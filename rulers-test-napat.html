The Ruler's Test — Jotham Napat, Prime Minister of Vanuatu

Subject: Jotham Napat — Prime Minister of Vanuatu since 11 February 2025. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Vanuatu is a parliamentary republic whose president (Nikenike Vurobaravu) is a largely ceremonial head of state, while executive power — the Council of Ministers, the budget, and the public service — sits with the prime minister. The holder named in the original brief still holds the seat: the verification run for this dossier found Napat in office continuously through November 2026, so there is no completed tenure to state as context; he is graded on roughly twenty-one months in office, and the file's thinness is stated where it matters. Napat, born in 1972, is the MP for Tanna and president of the Leaders Party of Vanuatu (LPV), first elected to Parliament in 2016 and previously a deputy prime minister and foreign minister in earlier coalitions. He came to the seat through the snap general election of 16 January 2025 — called after President Vurobaravu dissolved Parliament in November 2024 amid a no-confidence attempt against his predecessor Charlot Salwai, and held a month after the 17 December 2024 Port Vila earthquake (magnitude 7.3, more than a dozen dead) had devastated the capital. No party won a majority: the LPV took 9 of 52 seats, and on 27 January 2025 five parties — LPV, Vanua'aku Pati, Graon mo Jastis Pati (Land and Justice), Reunification Movement for Change, and the Iauko Group — agreed a coalition holding 32 seats. Parliament elected Napat prime minister unopposed on 11 February 2025, 50 votes to two void (IPU Parline; International IDEA election reporting, January–February 2025). He is Vanuatu's fifth prime minister in five years, in the Pacific's most turnover-prone parliament — more than thirty changes of prime minister since independence in 1980, twenty of them between 1991 and 2017 alone. His inheritance, and his central promise, are the same fact: the instability itself, and the constitutional stability amendments (Articles 17A and 17B, approved in the country's first-ever referendum on 29 May 2024) whose first full parliamentary term is his. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The one law was applied, fast and publicly, to a wealthy foreigner who had bought his way in. On 10 March 2025, within a day of international media reporting that Lalit Modi — the Indian cricket administrator who had obtained Vanuatu citizenship under the citizenship-by-investment (CBI) program while facing proceedings in India — held a Vanuatu passport, Napat personally directed the Citizenship Commission to begin cancelling it, stating that "holding a Vanuatu passport is a privilege, not a right," that Interpol had twice declined India's request for an alert notice, and that "we will not harbour fugitives or criminals. We have zero tolerance for those seeking to use our citizenship program to evade justice" (Prime Minister's Office press release, 10 March 2025). Modi's alleged offences in India are ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated; what is graded here is that the rule the government announced was applied to a paying customer of the state's own revenue program, at the cost of that revenue.
  • Parliament's own watchdog machinery ran against the executive's friends as well as its predecessors, in public. The Public Accounts Committee's report on the VT180 million Value Addition Investment Capital Fund (VAICF), tabled in Parliament on 4 November 2026, recorded seven critical findings: payments from grant recipients to a former Minister of Trade and his adviser; eight of eleven recipients assessed as ineligible; possible conflicts of interest involving an MP's family business; eight applications excluded from screening; businesses funded without valid industrial permits; Council-approved projects left unfunded; and no public advertisement or open competitive process (Public Accounts Committee report, as tabled 4 November 2026 and summarised in public reporting). The fund's administration substantially predates Napat — the payments it criticises went to a former minister — but the committee sat, reported, and was received by a Parliament his coalition controls, and the Speaker publicly called on the committees in November 2026 to conduct "rigorous, evidence-based scrutiny of government" and "proper follow-up on their recommendations" (Parliament of Vanuatu, 21 November 2026).
  • The stability amendments his government champions bind his own side first. Articles 17A and 17B — an MP who leaves or is expelled by their party vacates the seat; an independent or single-seat MP must join a larger party within three months or vacate — were applied after the January 2025 election to government and opposition MPs alike, with a declaration deadline of 11 May 2025 that MPs of coalition parties met on the same terms as everyone else (Vanuatu Electoral Office; 2024 referendum aftermath reporting). Napat defended the amendments on 23 October 2025 against an opposition legal challenge as a democratic mandate — "59% for Article 17A and 58% for Article 17B" in the 29 May 2024 referendum, passed by Parliament 47–0 in December 2023 — rather than as an instrument of his coalition (Prime Minister's Office, 23 October 2025).
  • The outcome measures are mid-table and stable rather than captured: Vanuatu scored 47 of 100 in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025 (63rd of 182), down from 50 in 2024, with TI's Pacific release noting "little to no change for the past several years" and citing weak oversight institutions — a stagnation finding, not a collapse finding (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released 10 February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The citizenship file shows two speeds, and the slower one ran for a man facing graver charges. Andrew Tate obtained Vanuatu citizenship under the same CBI program on 15 December 2022, two weeks before Romanian authorities detained him; he faces charges in Romania and the United Kingdom which remain ALLEGATIONs — he has not been convicted, and Vanuatu's Citizenship Act, as the Citizenship Office states it, permits revocation on a court finding of fraud in the application (s.14(2)) or a prison sentence of ten years or more (s.16). When OCCRP revealed the grant in 2025, a government spokesman first said authorities were "definitely looking into" revocation, then in June 2025 announced no further action because the original checks had cleared him — a sequence critics read as one law for the case the government wanted closed. The file then produced a documented contradiction between two arms of the same state: in September 2026 a government spokesman told Australia's ABC that Tate's citizenship and passport had been revoked; the Citizenship Commission publicly denied any such decision had been made, stating the evidence on file was insufficient and that Tate remains a citizen (IMI Daily and OCCRP reporting, June 2025 – September 2026). Whether Tate keeps or loses his citizenship is contested between the government's own spokesman and its own Commission; what is not contested is that the two said opposite things, and that the Modi cancellation took a day while the Tate file has run, unresolved and self-contradicting, for over a year.
  • The VAICF accountability the Opposition demands has not, on the record assembled here, arrived. The Opposition's November 2026 no-confidence motion charged that the handling of the VT180 million fund "shows that the PM has not taken sufficient steps to protect public money or hold those responsible to account" (Opposition motion, lodged 3 November 2026, PINA reporting). The PAC findings themselves are documented; what does not appear in the record assembled here is any prosecution, recovery action, or sanction flowing from them under Napat's government. The findings are recent (4 November 2026) and follow-up may yet come; the shelf records the absence to date, not a refusal.
  • The November 2026 motion also alleged, as ALLEGATIONs not findings, that Napat directed the Vanuatu Maritime Safety Authority to release the detained vessel LC Mahalia to transport public servants and equipment to Mota Lava for a Public Service Day event — executive interference, if it occurred, in a regulator's detention decision (Opposition motion, November 2026). The Prime Minister's Office responded through its PRO that some of the motion's grounds were "quite laughable" and could lead to legal consequences — a dismissal, not a documented rebuttal (PINA, 5 November 2026). The allegation is unproven and is recorded as an allegation; the absence of a factual answer to it is recorded beside it.
  • The portfolio complaint in the same motion — that Malekula, returning seven MPs, holds no ministerial portfolio — is a fact about coalition arithmetic (Malekula's MPs sit predominantly in opposition) rather than proof of unequal law, and is recorded at that weight under Test 4; it is noted here because the Opposition framed it as favouritism toward "political and personnel associates over the people," a framing the government has not answered with a published allocation rationale (Opposition motion, November 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts ruled against his own side's procedural shield, and the ruling was obeyed. In November 2025, facing a lodged no-confidence motion, the Speaker of Parliament — Stephen Felix, of Napat's own Leaders Party — closed the Second Ordinary Session on 14 November 2025 instead of allowing the motion to be debated. The Supreme Court (Justice Olivier Saksak) ruled the closure unconstitutional; the Speaker appealed; the Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal on 17 December 2025, Chief Justice Vincent Lunabek holding that Article 43(2) "does not specify who delivers the motion to the Speaker" and that the Speaker, once properly notified, could not close the session against it. Parliament reconvened on the courts' instruction on 19 December 2025, debated the motion, and defeated it 36–12 (Court of Appeal, Civil Appeal Case No. 3822 of 2025; PINA and Fiji Global News reporting, December 2025). The referees overruled the government's presiding officer, in the government's own chamber, on the question of whether the government could be challenged at all — and the government sat, voted, and accepted the process.
  • The courts likewise ruled against the government's own agency in the teachers' case, and the loss stood. In May 2025 the Supreme Court (Justice Edwin Goldsbrough) held the teachers' industrial action legal and quashed the Teaching Service Commission's suspension and termination of more than 600 striking teachers (Case No. 24/2752). The government announced an appeal (see Test 3 for the against-shelf weight of that), but the suspensions were not reimposed by executive action in defiance of the judgment, and the dispute was ultimately closed by negotiation, not by overriding the court (Supreme Court judgment, May 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, December 2025).
  • A statutory body publicly contradicted the executive's spokesman and kept its own line. When a government spokesman announced in September 2026 that Andrew Tate's citizenship had been revoked, the Citizenship Commission — chaired by Charlie Maniel — stated on the record that it had made no such decision, that the evidence was insufficient under the Act, and that citizenship decisions "must rest on verified information available when an application is determined rather than on allegations that emerge years later" (Citizenship Office statement via OCCRP and IMI Daily, September 2026). Whatever the merits of the underlying file (Test 1), a commission correcting the government's own announcement, in public, on a politically charged case, is the observable behaviour of a body that does not take the executive's dictation.
  • The media operate against the government in the open, and the prime minister has publicly thanked them for it. The Vanuatu Daily Post carried the PAC's VAICF findings, the no-confidence motions, and sustained criticism of the pace of earthquake recovery; at his three-month report in May 2025 Napat told the press, "We thank the media for keeping us all in check. Let's continue to work together for the betterment of our country" (Prime Minister's Office, May 2025). No documented case appears in the record assembled here of a journalist prosecuted, an outlet closed, or a licence withdrawn under his government. Vanuatu's broader environment is rated free by the standard monitors, with broadly respected civil liberties and a free press coexisting with its political instability (Freedom House, 2026; index summaries).

Evidence against:

  • The one documented attempt at a procedural grip came from inside his own house. The Speaker who closed Parliament on 14 November 2025 to keep a no-confidence motion from the floor is a member of Napat's party presiding over Napat's coalition; the closure was ruled unconstitutional by two courts. No documented statement appears in the record assembled here of Napat criticising, disowning, or disciplining the Speaker's manoeuvre — the correction came from the judiciary, not from the prime minister. The grip failed because the courts held, not because the seat declined to grip; the grade records both halves.
  • In the teachers' dispute, the government's first responses to losing included pressure as well as process. Its 3 June 2025 statement announced an appeal, complained of the Supreme Court proceedings, disclosed it had sought a private lawyer for the appeal, and carried a warning from the Education Minister and the Deputy Prime Minister urging teachers whose names were not in the judgment to refrain from joining the union action, "saying any such action could have very detrimental effect on their careers" (Prime Minister's Office, 3 June 2025). A warning from the finance minister to public servants about the career consequences of lawful industrial action — action the Supreme Court had just held legal — is pressure on the exercise of a right the court had vindicated, and it is recorded at that weight.
  • On two of the test's named referees, the record is thin to the point of silence, and the thinness is stated rather than filled. No documented instance appears in the record assembled here of Napat's government interfering with the Reserve Bank of Vanuatu, removing its governor, or directing monetary policy — and no documented stress test of the Bank's independence (a disputed appointment, a public disagreement, a resisted instruction) appears either. The same is true of the public prosecution: no documented case of a prosecution directed, halted, or punished under his government was found, and no documented case of the prosecution proceeding against the government's own against its wishes. Absence of interference is consistent with holding without gripping; it is also consistent with a file too small to grade on those two referees, and this dossier does not pretend otherwise.
  • The stability amendments themselves, whatever their democratic mandate, narrow one channel of parliamentary independence by design: an MP who is expelled by their party — a decision taken by a party president, not a court — now vacates their seat automatically under Article 17A, which comparative-politics scholar Jonathan Fraenkel assessed as poorly drafted and open to misuse by party leaderships (ConstitutionNet and International IDEA commentary, 2024). The amendments predate Napat's premiership and were approved by referendum; he is their implementer and chief defender, not their author, and the weight is recorded accordingly.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The defining wobble of his tenure was answered by submission to the process that threatened him. Ordered by the Court of Appeal to let Parliament sit and debate the motion that could have ended his government, Napat did not prorogue, dissolve, or defy: Parliament sat on 19 December 2025, the Opposition leader Ishmael Kalsakau spoke to the motion, and the government won the vote 36–12 on the floor (PINA, 22 December 2025). A second motion, lodged 3 November 2026 on six grounds (Test 1, Test 4), was processed under Standing Order 44, the Speaker confirming it met constitutional requirements — and the Opposition itself withdrew it on 9 November 2026 when the numbers were not there, the sitting showing 40 MPs on the government side and 12 on the Opposition's (Vanuatu Daily Post, 10 November 2026; RNZ Pacific, 9 November 2026). Both challenges were met inside the chamber, in the open, under the courts' and the Standing Orders' rules.
  • On the teachers' dispute — an eighteen-month strike inherited from his predecessor, over claims outstanding, on the government's own account, for eighteen years — the endpoint was apology and settlement, not victory. On 3 October 2025 Napat apologised in person to more than 400 teachers at Saralana Park "on behalf of past governments," acknowledging "prolonged suffering for almost 20 years" and "a lack of leadership at all levels"; his finance minister confirmed funds were allocated; a tripartite verification process (Teaching Service Commission, Ministry of Education, Teachers Union, and a CBA Taskforce the PM appointed) verified claims totalling VT537 million by 10 October 2025, expressly including VT372.5 million owed to teachers who were not union members. In December 2025 the strike ended under a collective bargaining agreement committing the government to pay VT4.2 billion (about USD 34.7 million) over four years (Prime Minister's Office releases, 3 and 10 October 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, December 2025). A parallel dispute — more than 300 Vila Central Hospital workers who stopped work on 10 December 2025 over an unpaid earthquake-risk allowance — was likewise resolved and staff returned on 12 December 2025 (CIVICUS Monitor, December 2025).
  • His largest foreign commitment was slowed down, visibly, in response to domestic unease. The Nakamal Agreement with Australia was initialled atop Mt Yasur in August 2025, but signing did not follow until 29 June 2026 in Canberra; the Prime Minister's Office states the government "took time to ensure proper domestic consultation," with engagement "since early 2025" across officials, ministers, and coalition party leadership, and formal Council of Ministers endorsement only in May 2026 (Prime Minister's Office, 29–30 June 2026). Delaying a signature a larger partner wanted, for the better part of a year, to consult at home first is adjustment to the wobble in its diplomatic form.
  • The disaster file was answered with presence rather than denial. Inheriting the December 2024 earthquake's wreckage, his government ran the recovery as its central stated task; when a magnitude 7.2 earthquake struck Santo on 30–31 March 2026, the response ran through the provincial emergency operations centre with damage assessment tabled to the Council of Ministers the same week, and the prime minister on the record about it immediately (Prime Minister's Office, 31 March 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The teachers' settlement came only after the government had first answered the street and the court with an appeal and a warning. The sequence is documented: strike from June 2024; Supreme Court rules for the teachers in May 2025; government announces on 3 June 2025 that it will appeal, criticises the judgment's handling, and warns non-party teachers off joining in; apology follows in October; settlement in December. The adjustment, when it came, was full and public — but it came after the doubling-down, and after the courts had removed the government's legal ground. The shelf records the order of events, because the order is the evidence.
  • The accountability wobble of November 2026 was met with derision before it was met with process. The Prime Minister's Office PRO publicly described grounds in the Opposition's motion as "quite laughable" and suggested they "could lead to legal consequences" for those raising them (PINA, 5 November 2026). The motion was nonetheless processed properly and withdrawn by its own movers for lack of numbers — the institution answered better than the spokesman's rhetoric did.
  • The December 2025 crisis itself was created by his own side's refusal of the wobble: the Speaker's unconstitutional closure (Test 2) was an attempt to make the challenge go away rather than answer it, and it took two courts to restore the sitting. Napat's personal role in the closure is not documented in the record assembled here — the act was the Speaker's — and is not attributed to him; but the adjustment, when it came, was the courts' work before it was the government's, and no documented word from the prime minister welcomed or even acknowledged the rulings that saved the process.
  • His flagship self-assessment instrument grades himself. The 100-day plan's reported results (Test 5) were published by his own office against his own dashboard, with no independent audit in the record assembled here; the wobble of public scepticism about the figures has no external referee to answer it.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the outer islands against the capital, the opposition's islands and voters, the teachers and the hospital staff, the climate-exposed, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The teachers' settlement counted the unorganised as well as the organised. The verified claims briefed to Napat and his deputy on 10 October 2025 split VT164.8 million owed to Teachers Union members and VT372.5 million — the larger share — owed to teachers who were not union members, and the government's stated commitment covered both: "the Government remains committed to settling all teachers' claims, including Vt372,511,020 owed to non-VTU members" (Prime Minister's Office, 10 October 2025). The December 2025 CBA closed an eighteen-month dispute that had kept over 600 teachers suspended or terminated under his predecessor's commission and classrooms across the country disrupted (CIVICUS Monitor, December 2025).
  • The climate file counts the people with the least margin, at the highest forum available. Vanuatu's campaign for an International Court of Justice advisory opinion on states' climate obligations — begun by Pacific law students and carried by successive Vanuatu governments — reached its conclusion under Napat: the ICJ's unanimous opinion of 23 July 2025 held that states have binding obligations to protect the climate system and that failure may carry legal consequences, including for the low-emitting, sea-level-exposed communities of Vanuatu's outer islands. His climate minister, Ralph Regenvanu, stated Vanuatu would press the opinion's implementation against high emitters including Australia; on 21 May 2026 the UN General Assembly adopted, by overwhelming support, the follow-up resolution Vanuatu introduced welcoming the opinion (The Guardian, 24–25 July 2025; Prime Minister's Office, 21 May 2026). A government of 320,000 people counting its most exposed citizens into international law is the count operating at full reach.
  • Inherited arrears owed to the state's own servants were picked up rather than disowned. In March 2026 the Council of Ministers approved a paper addressing longstanding financial liabilities facing the Vanuatu Police Force and the government dating back to 2016, Napat crediting his Internal Affairs minister and acknowledging that "previous administrations' lack of foresight had contributed to the situation" while his government took "a proactive approach" to settling them (Prime Minister's Office, 12 March 2026). The hospital workers' earthquake-risk allowance dispute was likewise settled in December 2025 rather than left to harden (CIVICUS Monitor, December 2025).
  • The referendum whose amendments define his term was itself counted broadly: the 2024 awareness campaign for Articles 17A and 17B reached, on the government's figures, 98% of the population across all six provinces and overseas communities, and the amendments were approved by 59% and 58% respectively (Prime Minister's Office, 23 October 2025; Vanuatu Electoral Office results). The campaign predates his premiership; the mandate he governs under was, on the record, put to everyone.

Evidence against:

  • The citizenship program's costs are counted onto everyone, including those who never sold anything. The European Union's revocation of visa-free access — made permanent in December 2024 after the program sold more than 10,000 passports with, on EU figures, 27 refusals from 1,988 applications in 2022–23 — applies to all Vanuatu nationals, born or bought: a natural-born ni-Vanuatu teacher now needs a visa for Europe because the state sold citizenship with screening the EU judged inadequate, and the United Kingdom has required visas since July 2023, with US visa bonds added in January 2026 (EU Regulation 2025/11; Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/222; CBI programme reporting, 2025–2026). The program predates Napat and he has tightened it (Test 5); but it continues under him as a major revenue source, and its price continues to be paid by the whole population while its revenue accrues to the state. That distribution — benefit at the centre, cost at the edges — is the count's hardest documented fact in this dossier.
  • Malekula counts as the Opposition's documented exclusion case. The November 2026 motion's first ground was "unfair allocation of ministerial portfolios," specifying that Malekula — Vanuatu's second-largest island, returning seven MPs — holds no ministerial position, and its sixth ground charged failure "to support infrastructure development outside Port Vila," alleging that VT1 billion allocated by Parliament for the refurbishment of Pekoa Airport on Santo was diverted to tarseal roads in and around Port Vila and had not been replenished in the 2026 budget (Opposition motion, November 2026, PINA reporting). The portfolio fact is a consequence of Malekula's MPs sitting in opposition; the Pekoa diversion is an ALLEGATION, not a finding — no court, auditor, or committee finding on it appears in the record assembled here, and the government has not published a rebuttal in that record either. Both are recorded at exactly that strength: a representation grievance that is arithmetically true, and a diversion claim that is unproven and unanswered.
  • The capital's recovery has been slower than the capital was promised, and the frustration is on the record. Twenty months after the December 2024 earthquake, public commentary carried in government-adjacent channels described Port Vila as still largely unfinished — "one big hole in the ground" — harming the tourism the recovery is meant to restore (public comment on Ministry of Internal Affairs reporting, August 2026). A single commenter's words are weak evidence of fact; they are recorded here as evidence of the perception the government has not dispelled, against its own earlier expectation-setting (Test 5).
  • Structural counts that predate him remain unclosed on his watch. The Parliament elected in January 2025 contains one woman among 52 members — Marie Louise Paulette Milne — the same as in 2022 (IPU Parline; International IDEA, January 2025). No documented initiative of his government addressing women's parliamentary representation appears in the record assembled here; the absence is recorded, not charged as an act.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 11 February 2025, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The stability promise — kept so far, in the hardest parliament in the Pacific. Napat's founding commitment, made as the fifth prime minister in five years, was a performance-based government that would last. Twenty-one months in, he remains in office; he defeated the December 2025 no-confidence motion 36–12 and saw the November 2026 motion withdrawn for lack of numbers, with analysts assessing in December 2025 that the coalition was likely to complete its four-year mandate to 2029 (PINA, 22 December 2025; Vanuatu Daily Post, 10 November 2026). In a seat that changed hands four times in 2022–23 alone, duration is not a formality; it is the promise.
  • The 100-day plan — launched on the date promised, and self-graded in public with the shortfall admitted. At his government's formation Napat promised a 100-day plan within two weeks; it was launched on 4 March 2025, covering 76 policy initiatives across five pillars, with all ministry directors-general directed to realign to it (Prime Minister's Office, 4 March 2025). At roughly the three-month mark he reported publicly that the government had "achieved 63% of our targets, I believe more could be done," projecting close to 80% by day 100 — a self-assessment, published by his own office, that records a miss against his own ambition rather than declaring victory (Prime Minister's Office, May 2025). The figures are the government's own (Test 3); the candour about the gap is documented in the same release.
  • The teachers' settlement — promised as a priority, delivered. His government named ending the teachers' dispute among its main priorities on taking office; the December 2025 collective bargaining agreement ended the eighteen-month strike with VT4.2 billion committed over four years, following the prime minister's public apology of 3 October 2025 (CIVICUS Monitor, December 2025; Prime Minister's Office, October 2025). The path ran through a court loss and an announced appeal (Test 3); the destination promised was reached.
  • Air connectivity — substantially restored as pledged. The 100-day plan committed to restoring the collapsed national airline's domestic service under the new company AV3 "within the next three months or so," with domestic routes the priority (Prime Minister's Office, 10 March 2025). By his first-anniversary account in the Vanuatu Daily Post (15 February 2026), the government recorded the rapid reopening of Port Vila's airport, two new Twin Otter aircraft acquired, and inter-island connectivity supplemented by partnerships with Air Caledonie and Solomon Airlines — his own account, recorded as such, with the domestic-first sequence matching the original pledge.
  • Citizenship tightening — partially delivered on schedule. The pledge to overhaul the CBI program produced new regulations signed off by the prime minister, strengthened Financial Intelligence Unit screening, mandatory biometric enrolment for all applicants from November 2025, and the Modi cancellation within a day of exposure (Prime Minister's Office, March 2025; programme reporting, 2025–2026).
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: Charlot Salwai (prime minister October 2023 – February 2025) carried the 17A/17B referendum to its vote and presided over the dissolution and snap election that transferred the seat on schedule; the earthquake that defined the election struck on his watch. The stability framework Napat governs under was substantially built by the predecessor he replaced — part of why the seat's word, this term, is being kept with inherited tools.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The CBD expectation — set for end-2025, not met on the visible record. In May 2025 Napat stated: "I am confident that by the end of this year, we will see the CBD get back up" (Prime Minister's Office, May 2025). By August 2026 — twenty months after the earthquake — public reporting still described central Port Vila as largely unfinished, with recovery works (demolition, the World Bank-supported VARS project) visibly in progress rather than complete (Ministry reporting and public comment, August 2026). The rebuild is real and ongoing; the date he attached to it passed without the result he described.
  • The 100-day final tally — never independently confirmed. The published record assembled here contains the launch, the 63% interim figure, and the projection of "close to 80%"; no final audited accounting of the 76 initiatives at day 100 appears in it. A government that promised a performance dashboard owes the final reading; its absence is recorded as an absence.
  • "Performance-based" — no documented enforcement. Napat's inaugural standard was explicit: the government would be "performance-based," and "anyone who does not perform as a public servant does not deserve to be in public office" (Prime Minister's Office, 4 March 2025). No documented ministerial dismissal for non-performance under that standard appears in the record assembled here. Coalition management may explain the silence; the standard was his, and the record does not show it applied.
  • The citizenship overhaul — the word outruns the file. The overhaul pledge sits against the Tate record (Test 1): a review announced, a no-action decision, a spokesman's revocation announcement the Commission then denied — the program's most visible case unresolved and publicly self-contradicted more than a year after the pledge, while the program itself continues as a key revenue source and its external costs (Test 4) remain unrecovered.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Jotham Napat's card is the mirror image of the strongman cards in this series: a prime minister whose grades are set less by what he has seized than by what the institutions around him have done — including to him. The defining sequence of his tenure ran through other people's rulings. His own party's Speaker tried to close Parliament against a no-confidence motion; the Supreme Court and the Court of Appeal declared the closure unconstitutional; the prime minister sat for the vote the courts ordered and won it 36–12. His government's own commission suspended 600 teachers; the Supreme Court quashed it; the government announced an appeal, then apologised, verified the claims — including the larger share owed to non-union teachers — and settled for VT4.2 billion. His spokesman announced a citizenship revocation his own Commission then publicly denied had ever been decided. In each case the referee held, and in each case the seat, after its first instinct, complied. That is a genuinely two-sided record, and the grades are MIXED on all five tests because both shelves are load-bearing: the grip that was attempted came from inside his house and was never disowned by him, and the compliance that followed was complete and never tested by defiance. What he has unmistakably delivered is the thing he promised first — time. Twenty-one months in the Pacific's most unstable seat, two motions survived or dissolved, a four-year mandate now plausibly in reach, is a kept word of a kind this series rarely gets to record. What he has not delivered is equally specific: the capital rebuilt by the date he named, a final accounting of his own 100-day dashboard, an accountability outcome on the VAICF file his own Parliament's committee documented, and a citizenship program whose costs stop falling on citizens who never sold a passport. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the PAC's VAICF findings produce prosecutions or recoveries, whether the Tate contradiction is resolved by a decision either way, whether Port Vila's CBD is standing by the next grading, and whether the coalition that the stability amendments protect completes its term — because in Vanuatu, finishing is itself the rarest evidence.
