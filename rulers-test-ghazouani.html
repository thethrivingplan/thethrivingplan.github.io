The Ruler's Test — Mohamed Ould Ghazouani, President of Mauritania

Subject: Mohamed Ould Cheikh El Ghazouani — President of the Islamic Republic of Mauritania since 2 August 2019. The seat graded is the presidency, which in Mauritania holds executive power: command of the government and the armed forces, appointment of the prime minister, and the pardon power. Ghazouani, a career general and former chief of army staff (2008–2018) and defence minister, was the candidate of his predecessor Mohamed Ould Abdel Aziz in 2019 and won with 52.01%; the handover was Mauritania's first transfer of power between two elected presidents. He was re-elected on 29 June 2024 for a second — and, under the constitution as it stands, final — term, winning in the first round. The verified 2024 figures, printed as their sources give them and never blended: the National Independent Electoral Commission (CENI) reported Ghazouani at 554,956 votes, 56.12%, anti-slavery campaigner Biram Dah Abeid second at 218,546 votes, 22.10%, and Hamadi Ould Sid'El Moctar of Tewassoul third at 126,340 votes, 12.78%, on turnout of 55.39% of 1,939,342 registered voters (CENI final results via IFES Election Guide and International IDEA, July 2024); the Constitutional Council's proclamation of 4 July 2024 gives 554,956 votes and 56.12% on turnout it states as 55.6% (Constitutional Council announcement via Mauritanian News Agency, 4 July 2024). Seven candidates stood. Abeid rejected the provisional results, alleging fraud — an ALLEGATION recorded as his, not a finding. Ghazouani's ruling party El Insaf holds 107 of 176 National Assembly seats from the 2023 elections. He chaired the African Union Assembly for 2024 (elected 17 February 2024; UN Secretary-General's UNOWAS report S/2024/521). He is graded on his whole tenure since August 2019, weighted to the second term, as the brief directs. Two live files frame the grading: the completed corruption prosecution of his predecessor Aziz, and the 2026 debate over a third term the constitution currently forbids. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Marks are lost only for how it is done. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The law reached a former president — the hardest direction for one law to run. A parliamentary commission opened a corruption inquiry into Mohamed Ould Abdel Aziz and 11 others in 2020; Aziz was charged in March 2021, convicted on 4 December 2023 of illicit enrichment and money laundering and sentenced to five years, and on appeal on 14 May 2025 the Nouakchott Court of Appeal raised the sentence to 15 years' imprisonment with a fine of about $3 million, upholding asset seizure and revocation of civic rights, after both the defence and the state had appealed — the state against the leniency of the first sentence (AP, 14 May 2025; Reuters, 15 May 2025; Ecofin Agency, 16 May 2025). Investigators estimated Aziz had accumulated over $70 million in assets while in power (AP, May 2025). In November 2025 the Supreme Court upheld the 15-year term, ending his appeals (Lawyard and West Africa Press reporting, 5 November 2025).
  • The same appeal court did not simply ratify the prosecution's case against everyone around Aziz: the May 2025 verdict cleared six senior officials of the former administration, while sentencing Aziz's son-in-law to two years for influence peddling (AP, 14 May 2025). Acquittals inside a flagship case are evidence the court weighed defendants individually, at least in part.
  • The institutional shelf was widened, on paper, for everyone: a special tribunal to hear hereditary slavery, trafficking, and migrant-smuggling cases was established and operationalized in February 2025, replacing the three earlier anti-slavery courts, and in September 2025 the National Assembly adopted a law consolidating the specialized courts into a single structure covering trafficking, smuggling, and slavery-related crimes (US State Department, 2025 Trafficking in Persons Report: Mauritania; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025, via ecoi.net).

Evidence against:

  • The flagship case is contested as score-settling, and the contest is named: Aziz's lawyers framed the trial as settling scores between Aziz and Ghazouani, his former ally and chosen successor, from whom he fell out after the handover; defence lawyer Mohameden Ichidou told Reuters the May 2025 decision "reflects the pressure the executive branch exerts on the judiciary" — an interested party's claim, recorded as an ALLEGATION-level characterization, not a finding (Reuters, 15 May 2025; AP, 14 May 2025). The Bertelsmann Transformation Index's 2026 country report records the same open question: "the possibility that the case against Ould Abdel Aziz is more motivated by politics than by a search for sweeping accountability" (BTI 2026 Country Report — Mauritania).
  • No comparable case against the current camp appears in the record assembled here. Mauritania's Corruption Perceptions Index score has been flat at 30 of 100 across 2022, 2023, 2024, and 2025 (Transparency International CPI, via the CPI country tables) — the Aziz conviction did not move the measured perception of public-sector corruption. In a televised plenary session in November 2026, opposition MP Biram Dah Abeid resigned his seat stating that corruption was "widespread yet there is not a single person in prison charged with corruption" under the current government — his characterization, an ALLEGATION as to the present camp, printed because it names the asymmetry this test grades (AFP via Legalbrief Africa and Africa Policy Observer, November 2026).
  • On the crime the law most distinctively exists to punish, enforcement is zero where it counts. The 2015 anti-slavery law criminalizes hereditary slavery with five to 20 years' imprisonment. In the period covered by the US State Department's 2025 Trafficking in Persons Report, the government identified 30 victims of hereditary slavery — the first such identifications in several years — but "did not report prosecuting or convicting any traffickers for hereditary slavery crimes," down from nine prosecutions and five convictions in the previous period; the report adds that some police, prosecutors, and investigative judges reportedly refused to investigate hereditary slavery crimes and that cases were settled through social mediation outside the formal justice system, a practice the law forbids (US State Department, 2025 TIP Report: Mauritania). The 2026 assessment, as summarized from the State Department's figures, again records trafficking prosecutions (28) and convictions (22) with "not one" for hereditary slavery, and three slavery cases at the new special tribunal still at the investigation stage (Restore Magazine summary of the US State Department 2026 assessment, November 2026). The law that jailed a president for enrichment has not, on this record, jailed a single slaveholder during the graded second-term window.

Why this grade: One law genuinely ran upward to the top of the previous regime and survived every appeal, with acquittals inside the case — that is more than theatre. But a law that gives by one measure must reach the giver's own camp and the crimes committed by the powerful against the powerless; on this record the enforcement map stops at the old regime, the corruption index has not moved in four years, and hereditary-slavery prosecutions stand at zero while the tribunal built for them sits at investigation stage. MIXED. Helped: the public record and the treasury's claim on Aziz's seized assets; future officeholders put on notice that a presidency can end in a courtroom; the six acquitted former officials, whose individual weighing shows the appeal court was not a stamp. Hurt: hereditary-slavery victims whose cases were mediated away or never investigated; citizens whose corruption environment, on the CPI's flat 30, is unchanged; Aziz himself, who maintains through counsel — ALLEGATION, contested — that his prosecution was political.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The electoral machinery ran and certified a contested result through its own channels: CENI conducted the 29 June 2024 election, the Constitutional Council proclaimed the final results on 4 July 2024, and the opposition — including the runner-up — sits in the National Assembly, where in November 2026 Abeid could resign on live television denouncing the government (CENI/Constitutional Council, July 2024; AFP, November 2026). Opposition protest has at times been permitted in the open: authorities authorized an opposition rally in Nouakchott for 10 May 2026 against price rises and declining freedoms (Sahel Research Group weekly brief, 1–7 May 2026, citing RFI/AfricaNews).
  • Courts have on occasion ruled in ways the executive did not script: the appeal court acquitted six Aziz-era officials (Test 1), reduced the two opposition MPs' sentences on appeal in July 2026 (Test 2, against shelf, for the full sequence), and the Constitutional Council ruled on 15 July 2026 that MPs Mariem Cheikh Samba Dieng and Ghame Achour Salem would retain their seats until the Supreme Court ruled (MENA Rights Group case files, 2026; IPU Committee decision, October 2026).
  • Critical monitors publish against the government from inside the record: CIVICUS Monitor, the Mauritanian human-rights associations cited by Human Rights Watch, and independent outlets such as Al Akhbar.info all operate and are cited throughout this dossier (CIVICUS Monitor, 2024–2025; HRW, August 2025).

Evidence against:

  • In 2026 the state prosecuted two sitting opposition MPs for speech, without first lifting their parliamentary immunity. Mariem Cheikh Samba Dieng was arrested on 9 April 2026, the evening she posted a live video denouncing violence and discrimination against activists of Haratin descent; Ghame Achour Salem was arrested the same day after posting support (IPU Committee on the Human Rights of Parliamentarians, decision adopted at its 181st session, Arusha, October 2026). Dieng was detained with her three-month-old baby and, on the complainant's account recorded by the IPU, held at a Police Academy detention centre described as created under Ghazouani primarily for political opponents — that description is the complainant's ALLEGATION, printed as such. The Justice Ministry's position was that the videos were offences in flagrante delicto, so immunity did not apply. On 4 May 2026 both were sentenced to four years' imprisonment under Law No. 2021-021 on the Protection of National Symbols, with their phones confiscated and their online accounts ordered closed; on appeal on 8 July 2026 the sentences were reduced to two years but a five-year deprivation of civil and political rights was added; Ghazouani pardoned them on 9 July 2026 and they were released on 10 July; on 29 September 2026 the Supreme Court upheld the appeal sentence and the flagrante-delicto route around immunity, and on 1 October 2026 the Constitutional Council confirmed the loss of their parliamentary seats (IPU decision, October 2026; MENA Rights Group, 2026; IFEX, 2026). The pardon freed the women; it did not restore their seats or their rights — the grip's legal consequences stand, granted relief running through the president's personal clemency rather than through the institutions' own correction.
  • The communications referee was switched off after the 2024 election: amid the post-election protests, authorities disrupted mobile internet nationwide for 22 days, from 1 July to about 24 July 2024 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2024; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025). A state that can silence the network for three weeks holds a grip no press law needs to spell out.
  • The press environment has measurably narrowed across the tenure. Mauritania's RSF World Press Freedom Index ranking fell from 33rd in 2024 to 50th in 2025 (score 67.52) and to 61st of 180 in 2026 (score 63.36, "problematic"), with RSF attributing the 2026 decline to delayed press reforms and the sector's financial precarity (RSF Index 2024–2026, via State Media Monitor and Sahel Research Group, April 2026). Human Rights Watch records that repressive laws on criminal defamation, "false information," cybercrime, and blasphemy "have been used by authorities to prosecute and jail human rights defenders, activists, journalists, and bloggers" (HRW Mauritania country page). On 2 September 2025 two Al Akhbar.info journalists covering a gas distributors' sit-in in Nouakchott were arrested after refusing police demands to delete their footage (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). Freedom House assesses that the judiciary lacks independence (Freedom in the World 2025).
  • The rules of the political game were rewritten mid-term over the opposition's objection: the National Assembly's law of 27 January 2025 amending the regulation of political parties was criticized for insufficient consultation and as potentially restricting democratic space, and an opposition coalition led by Abeid had denounced government interference in democratic processes on 30 December 2024 (UN Secretary-General's UNOWAS report S/2025/187, March 2025).

Why this grade: The referees still exist and occasionally rule against the script, which keeps this from being a closed system. But the test is whether independence is punished — and in 2026 two elected MPs were imprisoned for videos criticizing the president and the justice system, routed around their constitutional immunity, and stripped of their seats even after a presidential pardon; the internet was switched off for 22 days after an election; and the press ranking has fallen 28 places in two years. The grip has closed on the specific people who used the referees against the seat. FAILS. Helped: the presidency's control of the narrative after elections and over online dissent; officeholders who benefit from the national-symbols law's shelter. Hurt: MPs Mariem Cheikh Samba Dieng and Ghame Achour Salem — imprisoned, Dieng with her infant, then deprived of their seats and civil and political rights for five years; journalists arrested for filming a protest; every Mauritanian who lost mobile internet for 22 days in July 2024; voters in the two MPs' constituencies, whose representation was removed by a speech conviction.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The national dialogue is a real, open adjustment mechanism, run in public phases. A dialogue process was announced in early 2025; on 13 October 2025 Ghazouani received the preparatory report from Dialogue Coordinator Moussa Fall, concluding the first phase (UN Secretary-General's UNOWAS report, November 2025). When the dialogue's coordinator suspended proceedings on 30 March 2026 over the majority's proposed clause on "terms and mandates," the reference document was finally signed on 14 July 2026 with that clause dropped, and Ghazouani received the text on 24 July 2026 (MADAR, 17 October 2026, via CES Intelligence assessment, November 2026). Dropping the contested clause rather than forcing it is adjustment in the open.
  • On the third-term wobble itself, his stated position is constitutional: at a televised press conference on 24 July 2026 — only his second since 2019 — he said he would not support any political path that violated the Constitution or did not reflect the will of the Mauritanian people, described the 2029 debate as a distraction, and said his priority was the program he was elected on (SaharaMedia via Sahel Research Group weekly brief, 24 July–6 August 2026).
  • Street and cost-of-living pushback has drawn personnel and permission responses, not only force: authorities authorized the opposition's 10 May 2026 Nouakchott rally on prices and freedoms, and criticism of the government's handling of electricity outages in Nouakchott prompted a cabinet reshuffle on 10 October 2026, with Prime Minister Mokhtar Ould Djay reappointed at the head of a new 29-member government (ACLED expert comment, October 2026; Sahel Research Group, May 2026).
  • The July 2026 pardon of MPs Dieng and Salem, issued the day after their appeal sentences, was a partial walk-back under domestic and international pressure (IPU and IFEX records, July 2026) — partial, because the seat-stripping consequences were left to stand (Test 2).
  • Foreign pushback was answered diplomatically rather than by escalation: after Malian forces and their foreign partners reportedly killed Mauritanian civilians in cross-border operations in April 2024, Nouakchott protested formally through a defence-ministry delegation to Bamako and presidential-level engagement, without military retaliation (UNOWAS report S/2024/521; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The sharpest wobble of the tenure — the street's rejection of his re-election — was answered with deaths in custody, mass arrests, and a blackout, not adjustment. After provisional results were announced, protests erupted on 1 July 2024 in Bogué, Kaédi, and Nouadhibou and in parts of Nouakchott. The Interior Ministry stated on 2 July that three protesters had died — two in the detention facility in Kaédi and the third later in hospital — without giving causes of death, and said an investigation would be carried out; dozens were arrested in Nouakchott and Nouadhibou, and mobile internet was cut for 22 days (Reuters, 2 July 2024; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024; International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, July 2024). No published outcome of the promised investigation into the three custody deaths appears in the record assembled here; the toll is printed as the ministry's own figure of three, the only sourced count found, and it is not blended with any other.
  • If another option becomes available, the third-term file shows it not being considered openly. Through 2026, calls for a third term came from inside his own house — ministers, elected officials, and senior civil servants — and in October 2026 Commerce and Tourism Minister Zeinab Mint Ahmednah called a third term a "national necessity," provided it respects the constitution (SaharaMedia/CRIDEM via ACLED and Sahel Research Group, August–October 2026). Ghazouani's July 2026 answer stopped short of explicitly ruling a bid out (Sahel Research Group, July 2026). One assessment reports KAS as stating in July 2026 that he had made clear he would not seek a third term in 2029 (Conflict Tracker primer, November 2026, citing KAS) — the two characterizations are printed as they stand, contested and unreconciled: he has neither silenced his own ministers' campaign nor stated the plain refusal the opposition asks for. The Democratic Opposition Coalition rejected the calls on 19 August 2026, warning that amending or circumventing the limits — Articles 26 and 28 set the term and single re-election; Article 29's oath and Article 99 entrench them, on the opposition's reading — would threaten the peaceful transfer of power (Maghreb Insider and SaharaMedia, August 2026). On 21 October 2026 Tewassoul organized demonstrations across all 15 regions under the "three noes" — no third term, no restrictions on liberties, no corruption (ACLED, October 2026).
  • Everyday pushback from below has repeatedly been met with force across the tenure: police violently dispersed student protests in January 2024, farmers protesting a land redistribution in R'Kiz in February 2024 (tear gas, several injuries including one serious head injury), and medical interns in May 2024 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024).

Why this grade: The dialogue, the dropped clause, the authorized rally, the reshuffle, and the pardon are genuine open adjustments, and they are not nothing. Against them stand the tenure's defining pushback moments: an election contested in the street answered with custody deaths and a 22-day blackout whose promised investigation has no published outcome, and a succession question his own camp keeps raising, answered with ambiguity rather than the open consideration — acceptance or refusal, with reasons — this test asks for. MIXED. Helped: parties and citizens inside the national dialogue, whose contested clause was dropped rather than imposed; Nouakchott residents whose electricity complaints reached the cabinet table; MPs Dieng and Salem, freed by the pardon. Hurt: the three protesters who died in or after detention in Kaédi in July 2024, and their families, still without a published investigation outcome; farmers, students, and medical interns dispersed by force in 2024; voters seeking a settled 2029, who instead have a minister campaigning for a third term and a president who will not plainly close the question.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Haratin, Afro-Mauritanian communities, the opposition, migrants, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The social model built in the first term, and carried into the second, counts the poor in money and coverage, on the government's own reported figures. Campaigning in Gorgol on 19 June 2024, Ghazouani cited for that one region 11,000 families reached by health insurance and 13,000 by cash transfers through the Taazour social programs, alongside school, water, and road construction — figures printed as the candidate's own accounting, not independently audited here (Mauritanian News Agency, 19 June 2024). His 2024 program pledged to expand health-insurance beneficiaries and to complete the settlement of the "humanitarian liabilities" — the file of the 1989–1991 violence against Afro-Mauritanians (AMI, June 2024).
  • The state has begun, formally, to count slavery victims again: 30 hereditary-slavery victims identified in the 2025 TIP reporting period, the first identifications in several years, a national referral mechanism operationalized, and the special tribunal created (US State Department, 2025 TIP Report). A September 2025 government reshuffle increased representation of women and marginalized communities in key posts, as recorded by the UN (UNOWAS report, November 2025).
  • The country has kept its own security count inclusive in one hard respect: surrounded by coups and jihadist insurgency in Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger, Mauritania under Ghazouani has remained stable and has hosted large numbers of Malian refugees — a protection his government points to and outside monitors record (US State Department, 2026 Investment Climate Statement: Mauritania; HRW, August 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The Haratin — descendants of enslaved people, the country's largest and most subordinated community — are counted out at the point of law. Walk Free estimates 149,000 people in modern slavery in Mauritania, 3.2% of the population, the core of it hereditary servitude imposed on the Haratin (Walk Free figures via Restore Magazine, November 2026). Against that estimate stand the enforcement numbers from Test 1: zero hereditary-slavery prosecutions in the 2025 TIP period, zero again in the 2026 assessment, officials reportedly refusing to investigate, and cases diverted to the social mediation the law forbids (US State Department TIP Reports, 2025 and 2026 as summarized). The state maintains that slavery itself no longer exists and only its after-effects remain — a position printed here as the state's, contested by the abolitionist movement led by Biram Dah Abeid (AFP, November 2026).
  • The people who name that exclusion are themselves excluded. The two MPs imprisoned in 2026, Dieng and Salem, are IRA abolitionist lawmakers whose offence was a video denouncing discrimination against Haratin activists; both lost their seats and their civil and political rights for five years (Test 2). In February 2024, Haratin farmers in R'Kiz protesting the redistribution of land they had worked for centuries were met with tear gas (CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). Abeid — the runner-up for the presidency twice, with 22.10% of the 2024 vote — resigned his Assembly seat in November 2026 calling parliamentary debate pointless (AFP, November 2026). A fifth of the electorate's chosen voice leaving parliament as futile is a counting failure, whatever its theatrical element.
  • Migrants and Black Mauritanians were counted as a security problem in 2025. After the 2024 EU migration partnership (€210 million, per the European Commission via Reuters), expulsions nearly doubled: 28,125 people expelled in the first six months of 2025 alone, against roughly the same number in all of 2024, on figures the Mauritanian government itself provided to Human Rights Watch (Reuters, 18 December 2025). HRW's 142-page report of 27 August 2025 documented, for 2020 to early 2025, torture, rape and sexual harassment, arbitrary arrest and detention, racist treatment, extortion, and summary and collective expulsions by Mauritanian security forces against mainly West and Central African migrants and asylum seekers (HRW, "They Accused Me of Trying to Go to Europe," August 2025). The Mauritanian Association for Human Rights reported about 1,200 deportations in March 2025 alone, including nearly 700 people holding regular residence permits (AMDH via L'Osservatorio, November 2025). In September 2025, 15 political parties and civil-society organizations denounced "racial profiling" after the arrest of writer Ibrahima Ba, saying it was indicative of what "many sub-Saharan migrants and black Mauritanians" experience (RFI, 26 September 2025, via CIVICUS Monitor). HRW also notes recent government steps that may improve migrant protection; they are recorded, and they do not change the 2025 count.
  • The gas era's benefits are, so far, counted at the top. The Greater Tortue Ahmeyim (GTA) field, shared with Senegal and operated by BP and Kosmos, began production in December 2024 with first LNG exports in April 2025 (US State Department, 2026 Investment Climate Statement). The National Hydrocarbon Revenue Fund (FNRH) held about $214.5 million at end-2025; its 2025 receipts were $53.6 million, of which gas sales were $25.14 million (46.9%), and the Treasury drew a $50 million budget transfer on 9 September 2025 under the Finance Law (Ministry of Finance, FNRH Annual Report 2025). State Department projections of gas revenue exceeding $1.5 billion a year within five years are projections, printed as such. No documented distribution mechanism tying GTA income to the Haratin, Afro-Mauritanian, or rural poor beyond the general budget and the existing Taazour transfers appears in the record assembled here; the opposition's October 2026 mobilization ran explicitly on rising living costs and deteriorating public services alongside the term question (ACLED, October 2026).

Why this grade: The Taazour transfers and insurance expansion are real counting of the poor, and stability has protected everyone, including refugees. But this test's center is whether the historically counted-out are in the accounting — and on the record the Haratin's defining crime goes unprosecuted year after year, their elected advocates are imprisoned and unseated, a fifth of the vote's champion walks out of parliament, and 28,125 migrants were expelled in six months amid documented racist treatment. The count includes the poor as recipients; it excludes the subordinated as rights-holders. FAILS. Helped: Taazour cash-transfer and health-insurance beneficiary families; Malian refugees sheltered by a stable state; the treasury and budget recipients of the first $50 million GTA transfer. Hurt: Haratin victims of hereditary slavery, counted as 30 identified and zero prosecuted; MPs Dieng and Salem and the Haratin activists whose treatment they protested; the 28,125 people expelled in the first half of 2025, including, on AMDH's count, some 700 residence-permit holders in March 2025 alone; Black Mauritanians subjected, on the September 2025 parties' statement, to racial profiling in the migrant sweeps.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 2 August 2019, weighted to the second term.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Security and stability — the 2024 campaign's headline pledge, kept. Ghazouani campaigned on providing security and economic growth (AP/Reuters, June–July 2024). Through a period in which Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger fell to coups and insurgency, Mauritania held two elections, suffered no successful jihadist takeover of territory, and was described by the US State Department in 2026 as politically stable with the 2019 inauguration standing as the first democratic transfer between elected leaders, "further solidified" by the 2024 re-election (US State Department, 2026 Investment Climate Statement). Growth held: 6.3% in 2024, 4.2% in 2025, a fiscal deficit of about 0.3% of GDP in 2025 and public debt around 42% of GDP (US State Department, 2026).
  • The gas promise — delivered on the inherited schedule. The GTA project, contracted before his presidency, reached first gas in December 2024 and first LNG exports in April 2025 under his government, with first revenues paid into the FNRH from May 2025 and production at the FLNG's nameplate 2.7 mtpa by December 2025 (US State Department, 2026; Kosmos Energy Q4 2025 results, 2 March 2026; FNRH Annual Report 2025).
  • Youth and education priority — institutional motion, outcomes unproven. The 2024 program made education and youth employment the stated priority of the second term, including a flagship proposal for a large national agency for training, employment, education, and SME support modeled on Taazour (campaign reporting, June 2024; AMI, 19 June 2024). The machinery and the rhetoric are in place; no documented dent in youth unemployment appears in the record assembled here, and the opposition's 2026 mobilization ran on unemployment and poor services (ACLED, October 2026). Recorded as in motion, not kept.
  • Political appeasement and dialogue — kept in form. His governing brand since 2019 — calm, consultation, an open door to the opposition — produced the national dialogue whose first phase concluded in October 2025 and whose reference document was signed, minus its contested clause, in July 2026 (Test 3).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The anti-corruption word — kept against the predecessor, not kept as a system. The record is Test 1's: one completed prosecution, of the man he replaced and fell out with; a CPI score flat at 30 for four consecutive years; and the runner-up for the presidency resigning from parliament in November 2026 on the stated ground that no one is in prison for corruption under this government (Transparency International CPI; AFP, November 2026). The word was "fight corruption"; the delivered object is a single, if historic, case.
  • "A strong, just state of law" with an end to "discrimination, slavery, and factionalism" — the Gorgol words, unkept on their own terms. At Kaédi on 19 June 2024 the candidate pledged a state where the judiciary serves justice and committed to addressing the past "far from all hateful practices of discrimination, slavery, and factionalism," including completing the humanitarian-liabilities settlement (AMI, 19 June 2024). Two years on: zero hereditary-slavery prosecutions in both TIP cycles of the second term, two abolitionist MPs unseated for naming discrimination, and no documented completion of the humanitarian-liabilities settlement in the record assembled here (US State Department TIP Reports; IPU, October 2026).
  • The constitutional word — now in suspense, by his own choice of words. The 2024 mandate was universally described, including by his own camp, as his "second and final term" (CENI-era reporting; US State Department, 2026). He has not announced a third-term bid, and he said on 24 July 2026 that he would not support a path violating the Constitution. But he stopped short of ruling a bid out while his own ministers campaign for one, and one credible summary reports KAS reading him as having ruled it out — the contradiction is printed in Test 3 and left open, because it is open. A word that depends on which summary you read is not yet a kept word; it is not yet a broken one either. The 2029 file will grade it.

The four crossings, named: 1. Kept his word AND it helped: the security pledge — Mauritanians, including the Malian refugees the stable state shelters, have lived through the Sahel's coup-and-insurgency years without either; and the gas schedule, which put first revenues into a published sovereign fund rather than off-book accounts (FNRH Annual Report 2025). 2. Kept his word AND it hurt: the EU migration partnership — the commitment to curb irregular departures toward the Canary Islands was delivered (arrivals down 59% through October 2025, per Spain's Interior Ministry via Reuters), and the delivery ran through 28,125 expulsions in six months and the abuses HRW documented. The keeping earns the credit here; the hurt is recorded in Test 4's ledger in full. 3. Went against his word AND it helped: not documented in the record assembled here. No clear instance was found of a pledge broken in a way that demonstrably helped people; the July 2026 pardon of the two MPs softened a prosecution his own justice system had brought, but it executed no prior pledge and is recorded under Test 3, not counted here as a crossing. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt: the Gorgol pledge of a just state of law free of slavery and discrimination — broken in its enforcement half (zero slavery prosecutions; abolitionist MPs unseated), hurting Haratin victims and the communities the words were spoken to, in the same city, Kaédi, where three protesters died in or after detention a fortnight after he spoke there as a candidate.

Why this grade: The words about stability, growth, and gas were kept, and they are the words most Mauritanians voted on — under the mandate rule, carrying out the elected platform counts for him, and it does here. The words about justice — corruption as a system, slavery ended in fact, discrimination addressed — were spoken as plainly and have not been kept, and the largest word of all, the finality of the second term, he has chosen to leave ambiguous while his camp campaigns against it. A record that keeps the platform's material half and recedes on its justice half is MIXED. Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Mohamed Ould Ghazouani's Mauritania is the Sahel's exception in the ways that are easiest to count: no coup, no insurgent territory, two elections held and certified, a predecessor prosecuted to a final 15-year sentence, first gas exported, growth positive, debt moderate. The grades turn on what the counting leaves out. One law is MIXED because the law that jailed a former president — with acquittals inside his case — has produced no prosecution of the current camp and no prosecution at all for hereditary slavery, the crime committed by the powerful against the powerless; it helped the public record and hurt no one it should have protected, except by omission. Without gripping is FAILS because in 2026 the referees were routed around in fact: two opposition MPs imprisoned for critical videos without an immunity procedure, unseated even after the president's own pardon, atop a 22-day post-election internet blackout and a press ranking down from 33rd to 61st in two years; it helped the presidency's control and hurt the MPs, their voters, and every journalist working under the national-symbols law. The wobble is MIXED because the dialogue, the dropped term-limits clause, the authorized rally, and the reshuffle are real open adjustments, while the tenure's hardest pushback — a contested election in the street — was met with three custody deaths and a blackout whose promised investigation has no published outcome, and the 2029 question is answered with ambiguity. Counts everyone is FAILS because the poor are counted as transfer recipients while the Haratin are counted out as rights-holders — 149,000 estimated in modern slavery against zero prosecutions — and 28,125 migrants were expelled in six months of 2025 amid documented racist treatment; it helped Taazour families and the treasury, and hurt slavery victims, abolitionist lawmakers, and the expelled. His word is MIXED because the material platform — security, stability, gas, growth — was delivered as elected, which the mandate rule credits in full, while the justice words spoken at Kaédi in June 2024 remain unkept and the "second and final term" word hangs unresolved over 2029. The open files that will move these grades are named and dated: whether the third-term question is closed by a plain refusal or opened by an amendment; whether the special tribunal ever convicts a slaveholder; whether anyone from the current camp ever faces the law that reached Aziz; and whether the investigation promised into the three Kaédi deaths of July 2024 is ever published.
