AgricultureGrowing food on purpose, instead of only finding it.

Every other sheet on this tree sits on top of this one. Once people could grow more food than one family could eat, they could stay in one place, store the extra, and spend time on pottery, metal, writing — everything else. Agriculture is not a farming trick. It is the surplus that made all the rest possible.

LAYERS

Three layers, one idea

What it is

Deliberately planting, tending, and harvesting crops — and saving seed from the harvest to plant again. The loop, repeated every year, is the technology.

What it means

Food stops being only what the land happens to offer today. It becomes something you can plan, store, and count on — and that reliability is what lets a settlement exist.

What it could mean for you

Even one bed teaches the real system: soil feeds the plant, seed carries last year's choices into this year, and timing decides whether either one matters.

WHY → HOW → ACT

Why? → How? → Act.

WHY?

Found food runs out and moves around. A planted field stays where you put it — and can be made to yield more than your household needs.

→

HOW?

Feed the soil, choose the seed, get water to the roots, and plant on time. Four jobs. Every farming advance since is a better way of doing one of them.

→

ACT.

Start with one bed and one season (below). Watch what the soil, seed, and weather actually do. That watching is the original research method.

The working knowledge

1 · Soil — the pantry Tap to close

Documented · the foundation

Plants build most of their body from air and water — carbon from the air, hydrogen from water. What they must take from the soil is a short, specific list, and nitrogen is the one that runs out first:

  • Nitrogen — for leaves and growth. The limiting nutrient in most soils, most of the time.
  • Phosphorus — for roots, flowers, and seed.
  • Potassium — for stems, water control, and resilience.
  • Plus — calcium, magnesium, sulfur, and trace minerals in small amounts, and living soil life that makes all of it reachable.

Every harvest carries those nutrients away in the crop. Plant the same crop in the same ground year after year and you withdraw from the same account until it is empty. That single fact explains the two oldest fixes:

  • Rotation — different crops draw differently and root at different depths, so moving them around spreads the withdrawal.
  • Resting (fallow) — leaving a field unplanted lets weathering and soil life rebuild what was taken.
  • Legumes put nitrogen back — beans, peas, clover, and their kin host bacteria in root nodules that pull nitrogen from the air and leave it in the soil. Planting a legume before or alongside a hungry crop is a deposit, not just a different withdrawal. This is documented, and it is why beans sit beside corn in so many independent farming traditions.

Try the rotation: tap a year below and see what the field is doing.

Year 1 — Deposit. Legume roots fix nitrogen from the air into the soil. The harvest is modest; the real crop is what stays underground.
2 · Seed — the memory Tap to close

Documented · selection over generations

Nobody invented corn. Nobody invented wheat, rice, or potatoes either. People bred them — slowly, by saving seed from the plants that did best and planting that seed next year. Repeat for hundreds of generations and a wild grass becomes a crop.

  • Teosinte → corn (maize) — corn's wild ancestor in Mexico is a hard, small-kernelled grass that looks almost nothing like an ear of corn. The transformation is documented in the archaeological and genetic record: selection, generation after generation, for bigger kernels that stay on the cob.
  • The rule underneath it: the seed you save is next year's crop, in advance. Save from the earliest, healthiest, best-tasting plants and the field drifts that way. Save carelessly and it drifts back.
  • Why it matters on this tree: seed-saving is humanity's first long-term information storage — instructions written in living form, copied every season. Writing, later on the tree, is the second.
3 · Water — three sources Tap to open

One line each · irrigation gets its own sheet

  • Rain — the simplest source and the least controllable: you plant to match when it usually comes, and a late rain is a failed year.
  • Flood — rivers like the Nile rose on a schedule, watered the fields, and left fresh silt — water and fertiliser delivered together.
  • Irrigation — moving water to the field yourself, by ditch, channel, or lift: control, at the price of building and maintaining the works. Irrigation gets its own sheet later on the tree.

Too little water and the crop stalls; too much and roots drown and soil washes away. Drainage is water management too.

4 · The calendar problem Tap to open

Planting is timing · this is why Calendar is next

A seed planted too early rots in cold ground or is killed by a late frost. Too late, and it runs out of season before it ripens. The seed, the soil, and the water can all be right — and the year still fails on timing alone.

So farming creates a hard new question foraging never asked at this scale: how do you know, reliably, what day of the year it is — and when the rains or the frost will come? Watching the sun, the stars, and the seasons closely enough to answer that question is the next technology on the tree.

SHEET B

Tools of the era

No brands. These are shapes, not products.

The digging stick

A hardened, pointed stick — sometimes weighted. Pokes the hole, levers up roots and stones. The first farm tool, and still enough to plant a garden.

The hoe

A blade set at an angle on a handle — stone, then metal. Breaks ground, cuts weeds, and hills soil up around stems. The tool that turns poking holes into preparing a field.

The plough

A blade dragged through the soil — first by people, then by animals — cutting a continuous furrow instead of single holes. One pass does the work of hundreds of hoe strokes. Scale is the invention.

FULL SIZE

The honest negatives

Farming won on surplus and reliability — not on comfort.

The record says so, plainly: compared with foragers, early farmers generally worked longer and harder hours, ate a narrower diet built on a few staple crops, and — when one staple failed or disease moved through a settled, crowded village — carried risks foragers spread out by moving on. Skeletons from early farming communities show it: more signs of nutritional stress and repetitive labour than the foraging groups before them.

So why did it spread everywhere? Because a field can feed more people from the same land, and stored grain carries a community through a bad season in a way found food cannot. Agriculture was not an upgrade in daily life. It was an upgrade in numbers and staying power — and everything on this tree is built on that trade, good and bad together.

ACT

Starter practice — one bed, one season

A starting point, labelled as one.

The smallest real version of this sheet. Not a yield promise — a way to watch the system work with your own eyes. Kin page: the Growing Season page — it walks the season itself, week by week, where this sheet gives the why underneath it.

Pick one bed

Roughly a door-sized patch — about 1 m × 2–3 m — in the sunniest spot you are allowed to use. Sun you can see beats soil you have to guess at.

Loosen, don't rebuild

Fork the soil loose, pull the big weeds, mix in any compost you already have. You are opening the pantry, not renovating the house.

Plant one forgiving thing

Beans are the classic starter: they sprout visibly, grow fast — and as legumes, they leave nitrogen behind for whatever follows. Follow the seed packet's depth and spacing for your area; the packet knows your crop better than a general page does.

Water the roots, watch the leaves

Water at soil level, deeply and less often rather than a daily sprinkle. Then watch: colour, wilting, insects, which plants thrive. Write down the planting date — you have just started your own calendar.

Save seed from the best plant

At season's end, save dry seed from the healthiest, most productive plant — not just any plant. You have now run the full original loop: soil → seed → timing → selection.

What to watch — the whole lesson in four lines

  • Soil: does water soak in or run off? Does the bed dry out in a day or hold moisture?
  • Seed: how many days from planting to sprouting? (That number is timing data.)
  • Water: what changed after rain vs. after you watered?
  • Timing: what would planting two weeks earlier or later have changed?
Safety — plainlySun, heat, and water are real: hat, drinking water, and breaks are part of the work, not extras. Use tools carefully and store them safely. Check local rules before you dig — some places restrict digging, planting, water use, or what may be grown, and nothing on this page overrides local law. Wash hands after soil work and wash what you harvest before eating it. If you are unsure whether something you grew is safe to eat, don't eat it — ask someone local who knows.
