Wayfinder — GPS Navigation

A working navigator built only from free parts: your phone’s own GPS, OpenStreetMap maps, and the free OSRM routing service. No account. Use any name you like, or none at all — no name is ever asked for.

Free — always free. No ads, no sign-up, no locked features.

        Honest limits — read these at full size, because they matter

        • No live traffic. Traffic data is gated behind paid services, and we don’t pretend otherwise. Route times are the free-flow estimate: what the drive takes on a clear road. A jam will make it longer and this page will not know.
        • Maps and routes need an internet connection. The download door above carries this page’s shell, but map pictures, address search, and routes come from the network. No connection, no new map.
        • The routing server is a shared public service. OSRM’s free demo server can be slow or refuse at busy times. If it does, this page says so plainly — it never invents or fakes a route.
        • GPS can drift. In tunnels, parking garages, and between tall buildings the blue dot can jump or lag. Believe the road and the road signs first.
        • Your position stays on your device. It never leaves this page — except the start and finish points of a route request, which are sent to the routing service so it can draw your route. Address searches go to the search service (Nominatim) the same way, and nothing else is sent anywhere.

        How GPS knows where you are

        GPS satellites circle the Earth broadcasting the time, over and over, from atomic clocks. Your phone listens to several at once. Each signal arrives a fraction of a second late, and that delay tells the phone how far away that satellite is — because the signal travels at the speed of light, distance is just delay multiplied by that speed. Knowing you are exactly this far from one satellite puts you somewhere on a giant sphere around it. A second satellite gives a second sphere, and two spheres cross in a circle. A third sphere cuts that circle down to two points — and one of them is usually absurd (far out in space), so three satellites make a point. A fourth signal lets the phone correct its own cheap clock, which is why real fixes use four or more.

        Why the blue dot can jump

        Signals bounce. Between tall buildings a signal can reflect off glass and arrive late by a detour, and a late signal reads as “farther away,” so the dot slides. In a tunnel there is no sky at all, and the phone coasts on its last good fix and its motion sensors. Thin fixes — few satellites, wide spread — come with a wide accuracy circle, and you can see that circle around the dot on this map. A small circle means the phone is confident; a big one means it is guessing politely.

        What the route time knows — and what it doesn’t

        The time is distance divided by the typical speed of each road on the way, added up. It knows the road’s class and length. It does not know about traffic, weather, roadworks, school zones at bell time, or the train that crosses your route twice a day. Treat it as the clear-road estimate it honestly is.

        How the route is chosen

        OSRM lays the road network out as a web of junctions and road segments, each segment carrying a cost (mostly time). It then finds the chain of segments from your start to your finish with the lowest total cost — the same family of shortest-path maths used since the 1950s, sped up enormously by pre-computing shortcuts across the web. It prefers fast roads because they cost less time, which is why a route sometimes looks longer on the map yet arrives sooner.

        When the dot and the road disagree

        Believe the road. Maps age, roads close, new ones open, and GPS drifts. If a sign says the road is closed, it is closed, whatever the screen says. Slow down, follow the signed detour, and the route can be re-planned from wherever you actually are — tap “Use my location,” then plan again.
