The Ruler's Test — Claudia Sheinbaum, President of Mexico

Subject: Claudia Sheinbaum Pardo, President of Mexico (in office since 1 October 2024; elected June 2024 with ~59% of the vote; Mexico's first woman president; Morena party). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders; no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest corruption prosecution of her term reached into the armed forces and her own movement's founding administration. After the Navy seized the tanker Challenge Procyon at Tampico in March 2025 with ~10 million liters of diesel declared as tax-exempt additives, federal authorities arrested 14 people in September 2025 — including Vice Admiral Manuel Roberto Farías Laguna and, later, his brother Rear Admiral Fernando Farías Laguna (arrested in Argentina in April 2026 and returned to Mexico that August). Both are nephews of the Navy Secretary who served the whole of the previous administration. The charges are ALLEGATIONs awaiting trial; the arrests of serving and retired flag officers from the movement's own era are documented fact (AP, September 2025; FGR announcements, 2025–2026).
  • The Segalmex case — the food-security agency whose irregularities (over 15 billion pesos, per federal audit findings) occurred under her predecessor — was not buried when she inherited it. Prosecutions continued on her watch: former Liconsa operations deputy Hugo Buentello Carbonell was arrested in April 2025, and in October 2026 the federal auditor (ASF) filed two further criminal complaints with the Attorney General's Office (FGR), bringing the total to 13 (MCCI reporting, April 2025; ASF complaints, October 2026).
  • Inside her own government, she confirmed in April 2025 the removal of six officials at Birmex, the state medicines purchaser, amid investigations into irregular consolidated drug purchases, with at least 13 investigations reported under way (presidential statements, April 2025).
  • Her government has publicized recovering assets of a convicted official of an earlier, opposing era — nearly 200 million pesos recovered from former security minister Genaro García Luna (convicted in the United States) directed to schools in Guerrero (government announcement, November 2026). Enforcement against a predecessor-era convict is the easy direction; it is listed here because it is part of the record, and weighted accordingly.

Evidence against:

  • The Segalmex prosecution stops below the top: the agency's founding director, Ignacio Ovalle Fernández, has never faced criminal charges, and as of November 2026 the case has produced no final convictions against its principal accused (El Universal review, November 2026; ASF/FGR records).
  • A broader count is damning in aggregate: El Universal's November 2026 review counted at least 15 corruption, conflict-of-interest, or nepotism scandals implicating figures of the Fourth Transformation across eight years, with one resignation and no senior official in prison.
  • When a U.S. indictment (unsealed 2026) accused sitting Sinaloa Governor Rubén Rocha Moya — a Morena governor and longtime ally of her predecessor — of conspiring with the Sinaloa Cartel, Sheinbaum publicly demanded "overwhelming evidence" before any extradition and said the charges could only be considered "political" without more proof; Rocha denied wrongdoing and stepped aside temporarily only as a local investigation proceeded, amid a documented internal party fight in which the faction loyal to her predecessor opposed any action against him (Reuters, May 6, 2026; U.S. Department of Justice indictment — the charges are ALLEGATIONs, unproven, and Rocha's denial is on the record). No Mexican charges against Rocha are documented as of this grading.
  • In the October 2026 resignation of INE president Guadalupe Taddei Zavala amid reported conflicts of interest and irregular supplier contracts, Sheinbaum's public framing attributed the supplier network to administrations dating back decades and to her critics, rather than to the electoral authority's current leadership — a defense her critics characterize as protecting an ally; Taddei has faced no established criminal liability (presidential statements and Mexican press reporting, October–November 2026; the underlying contracting allegations remain under audit and investigation).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority (INE), the states, the media, and the military keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The judicial reform was carried out by constitutional amendment through Congress, where her coalition held the required supermajorities from the 2024 election — a legal process, not a decree or a court-packing by appointment (constitutional reform, September 2024; first judicial elections, 1 June 2025).
  • When her own electoral-reform package — restructuring proportional representation, cutting party financing, and changing how INE councilors are selected — failed in the Chamber of Deputies on 11 March 2026, short of the two-thirds required, she accepted the vote. Notably, the decisive defections came from her own coalition partners (the Green Party and Labor Party). She announced she would reintroduce the reform after the 2027 elections rather than force it through (Chamber of Deputies vote, March 2026; presidential statements, November 2026). A check that works, and is seen to work, belongs on this shelf.
  • On the press, her rhetoric is measurably less confrontational than her predecessor's: analysts cited by the LatAm Journalism Review note she generally avoids attacking independent journalists by name, a shift they describe as significant (LatAm Journalism Review, November 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The judiciary's independence was structurally reduced on her watch, by her choice to carry the reform forward. The September 2024 amendment — proposed by her predecessor in his final month — replaced career appointment with popular election for every federal judge, including the Supreme Court (reduced from 11 to 9 justices), and replaced the Federal Judiciary Council with a new administration body and an elected Judicial Discipline Tribunal. In the first judicial election (1 June 2025), turnout was about 13%, and voting guides ("acordeones") circulated directing voters to specific candidates, most of whom were then elected (Law Gazette/International Bar Association reporting, 2025; INE results).
  • Her own follow-up reform deepened rather than repaired the design. Her May 2026 judicial amendment was pushed through an extraordinary session in under 15 days; the think tank México Evalúa documented that it discarded the consensus points reached by academia, the judiciary, and civil society (competency certification, stronger suitability screening), while postponing the next judicial election to 2028 and adding a pre-selection-and-lottery mechanism for candidates (México Evalúa analysis, June 2026; Jones Day client briefing, July 2026).
  • The electoral authority is next in line: her (failed, but announced for reintroduction) electoral reform would end proportional representation as it exists, cut public party financing, and subject INE's structure to redesign — changes the Inter-American Dialogue's February 2026 panel of Mexican electoral experts warned could weaken pluralism and politicize the body that runs elections (CRS report R48859; Inter-American Dialogue, February 2026).
  • The transparency check was removed: the constitutional reform abolishing the autonomous transparency institute (INAI) and other autonomous bodies, passed in late 2024, was implemented under her administration, folding oversight functions into the executive (constitutional reform, December 2024).
  • The military's role expanded rather than contracted: the National Guard was placed constitutionally under the Defense Ministry (SEDENA), and the armed forces retain the ports, customs, and infrastructure roles accumulated under her predecessor — a centralization she has defended and extended (constitutional reform, September 2024; CRS R48859).
  • The press environment on the ground did not follow her softer tone: ARTICLE 19 recorded 451 attacks on the press in 2025, with public authorities behind about half, including 65 cases of stigmatization, 60% of them from authorities or political actors; RSF counted 18 journalists killed in her first two years (ARTICLE 19 annual report, 2025; RSF assessment, October 2026). Killings are overwhelmingly attributed to criminal violence and local actors, not federal direction — but the protection system she pledged to fix remains, per RSF, largely unimplemented (see Test 5).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, markets, the United States, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The 2025 tariff confrontation with the United States is the strongest documented case of adjustment under pressure. When the U.S. imposed 25% tariffs on 1 February 2025, she announced retaliation — then negotiated a one-month pause on 3 February (deploying 10,000 National Guard troops to the northern border), and after tariffs briefly took effect on 4 March, secured the USMCA-compliance exemption on 6 March and stood the retaliation down. The pattern repeated through the year (a further pause negotiated in late October 2025); by August 2025, 84% of Mexico–U.S. trade remained tariff-free, and Mexico never imposed the broad retaliatory package it had prepared (Federal Register notices; Argus, October 2025; trade chronologies, 2025).
  • She carried that negotiating posture into the USMCA joint review of 1 July 2026: Mexico sought the 16-year extension, did not get it (the U.S. declined), and her government framed and accepted the fallback the treaty itself provides — the agreement in force to 2036 with annual reviews — while continuing bilateral rounds (USTR statement, July 1, 2026; White & Case and Holland & Knight client alerts, July 2026).
  • After the March 2025 discovery by civilian searchers of the Izaguirre ranch in Teuchitlán, Jalisco — a cartel recruitment and training site where searchers reported cremation ovens and hundreds of personal items — she signed a decree strengthening the national search commission and sent Congress reforms (no 72-hour wait to open a disappearance investigation; clearer data rules), the first time a Mexican president declared the disappearance crisis a national priority (Reuters, March 2025; CIVICUS/CEPAD interview, 2025). Federal prosecutors later described the ranch as a CJNG recruitment and training center and disputed the cremation claim — the forensic characterization remains CONTESTED between the search collectives and the FGR.
  • When the homicide-statistics gap became undeniable — INEGI's death records counted 29,488 killings for 2025 against the security secretariat's (SESNSP) 24,348, a 5,140-death gap — she acknowledged the discrepancy publicly in November 2026 and announced a joint state-by-state review with INEGI, with results to be published (El Universal investigation, November 2026; presidential statements, November 2026).
  • After the 1 November 2025 assassination of Uruapan mayor Carlos Manzo — an independent mayor and vocal critic of her security strategy — she convened an emergency security cabinet, condemned the killing, pledged "zero impunity," and her government launched a dedicated federal plan for Michoacán (CNN, November 2, 2025; government statements, November 2025). She also acknowledged, on 28 October 2026, a rise in homicides in Sinaloa over the preceding two months rather than denying it (country-conditions reporting, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On disappearances, acknowledgment has alternated with dismissal. In March 2025 she publicly rejected the Catholic bishops' conference statement on the crisis, saying the episcopate "does not have the correct information" and that rising disappearances were "not true" (presidential press conference, March 13, 2025). In April 2026, when the UN Committee on Enforced Disappearances acted on Mexico — citing more than 4,500 clandestine graves and some 72,000 unidentified bodies — she rejected the decision, saying the committee relied on old data from four states (UN CED, April 2026; presidential statements, April 2026).
  • After the November–December 2025 protests triggered by the Manzo assassination — the largest drawing ~17,000 people in Mexico City on 15 November, with clashes at the National Palace — the government's response questioned the protests' authenticity, attributing them to opposition organization and inauthentic online promotion rather than engaging the security demands; the widow of the slain mayor herself distanced herself from the marches' political direction, a fact both sides used (Wikipedia's sourced chronology of the 2025 protests; Reuters reporting, November 2025). CONTESTED: the protests' origins are disputed between the government and the demonstrators.
  • On the security numbers themselves, she continued to headline the SESNSP series — claiming roughly a 50% fall in daily homicides by late 2026 — while the INEGI series showed a smaller decline (~13% for 2025) and the registry of the disappeared grew past 135,000 on her watch (SESNSP figures presented November 10, 2026; INEGI data; RNPDNO registry). The direction of the homicide trend is not in dispute; its size is, and her public claims consistently use the more favorable count.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Indigenous peoples, women, migrants transiting Mexico, the poor, her opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The poor are counted in, measurably. Multidimensional poverty fell from 41.9% (2018) to 29.6% (2024) — some 13.4 million people — driven principally by minimum-wage increases (up ~131% in purchasing power across the period) and direct transfers; Sheinbaum continued both policies, reporting welfare programs reaching ~43 million people and further minimum-wage rises into 2026 (CONEVAL/INEGI data as reported August 2025; government reports, 2025–2026). Critics note the poverty series is mostly her predecessor's term and that informality reached 55% of employment by late 2025 — the gains are real and their distribution is CONTESTED, not the headline figure (Project Syndicate/Ortiz, September 2026).
  • Women were given institutional form: she created the first federal Secretariat for Women, signed a constitutional gender-equality reform, and in July 2026 presented a general femicide law to standardize investigation nationwide, create a femicide prosecutor or unit in every state, and raise sentences from 40–60 to 50–70 years. Officially recorded femicides fell from 1,022 (2021) to 736 (2025), continuing downward on her watch (SESNSP figures; Guardian reporting on the Mexico City femicide unit she created as mayor in 2020; Senate bill reporting, July–November 2026).
  • Deported Mexicans were met with a named reception program ("México te abraza") and consular protection efforts, which she has repeatedly foregrounded (presidential statements, 2025–2026).

Evidence against:

  • The disappeared are the largest group counted out of the success story: the national registry grew by more than 20,000 names in her first two years, from ~115,000 (October 2024) to over 135,000 (June 2026), with search collectives reporting roughly 40 disappearances a day and rejecting the government's narrative of decline (RNPDNO registry; México Evalúa; search-collective statements, 2026).
  • Femicide's decline coexists with near-total impunity where it is worst: ~520 femicides were recorded in the first ten months of 2026; in Sinaloa, the state public security council documented a femicide impunity rate of 95.71%; independent counts put total intentional killings of women in 2025 at 2,798, most classified as homicides rather than femicides — the classification gap is itself disputed between the government and independent researchers (SESNSP via La Jornada, 2026; El Debate tracking, November 2026; ATHENA Women News).
  • Migrants transiting Mexico were counted as a bargaining variable. Under U.S. pressure, Mexico expanded enforcement collaboration; U.S. border encounters fell ~93% in early 2025. More sharply: Human Rights Watch documented (May 2026) that of 18,453 third-country nationals deported by the U.S. between January 2025 and March 2026, 12,977 (70%) were removed to Mexico — more than any other country — under an arrangement Sheinbaum has never acknowledged as an agreement, describing reception only as humanitarian; deportees were bused to southern states, and HRW found thousands of Cubans left effectively stateless, without the reception guidelines Mexico's own Supreme Court required in 2022 (HRW, "Casting Us Aside to Die," May 27, 2026; Refugees International, February 2026; CRS IF10215). The government's position — humanitarian reception, no secret agreement — is on the record; the opacity is documented by both NGOs.
  • Her opponents' voters face a proposed smaller count: the electoral reform she intends to reintroduce would restructure the proportional-representation seats through which minority parties — and their voters — enter Congress; her own allies blocked it in March 2026 partly on those grounds (see Test 2). It is a proposal, not an enacted exclusion, and is graded as such.

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since October 2024, with dates. Her central promise was continuity-with-change — the "second floor" of her predecessor's Fourth Transformation.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Continuity, as promised, was kept: the social programs, the daily morning conference, the constitutional-reform agenda (22 reforms in her first year, most originating with her predecessor), and the wage policy all continued, and her approval has held near 70% — the electorate's own running assessment that the offer matches the delivery (CRS R48859; polling reported November 2026).
  • The promised change in security doctrine was real and documented. Where her predecessor's doctrine was summarized as "hugs, not bullets" and avoided confrontation, her security strategy — under Security Secretary Omar García Harfuch — is intelligence-led: expanded investigative powers for the public security ministry, targeting of mid-level "violence generators," and mass arrests (40,000 detained for serious crimes, October 2024–December 2025), with officially recorded homicides falling steeply (CRS R48859; Viri Ríos analysis, May 2026). She diverged from her predecessor here openly, and the divergence was the campaign's own framing of "continuity with change."
  • The promised change in energy also arrived. Campaigning as a climate scientist, she pledged at her inauguration (1 October 2024) to "propel renewable energy," targeting ~45% renewable generation by 2030. Her March 2025 energy reform kept the state utility's guaranteed 54% share (continuity) while reopening private clean-energy investment her predecessor had frozen — 20 private projects worth $4.75 billion announced by December 2025, with targets of 6.4–9.5 GW of new private clean capacity by 2030 (energy ministry announcements, 2025; sector reporting). The same reform dissolved the independent energy regulators (CRE, CNH) into a centralized commission — the divergence on renewables and the continuity on state control are both in the same law.

Evidence against:

  • Her signed press-freedom pledge was not kept on schedule. As a candidate in 2024 she signed Reporters Without Borders' pledge of five pillars and 22 concrete actions against violence toward journalists; one year in, RSF reported none of the 22 fully implemented and only three partially advanced, with the protection mechanism still deficient (RSF, October 1, 2025).
  • The disappearance crisis was named a priority in March 2025, but the registry grew by more than 20,000 during her first two years (see Test 4), and her government moved in March 2026 to re-audit the register itself — announcing that 40,308 entries (31%) showed indications of having been located — a recount the search collectives dispute as definitional relief rather than resolution (Interior Ministry, March 27, 2026; collective responses, 2026). CONTESTED: whether the recount is correction or cosmetics is disputed between the government and the families' collectives.
  • On the judiciary, her word cut both ways: she promised in the campaign to implement the judicial reform and did — the promise was kept to her coalition and broken, in her critics' account, to the voters who were told the courts would remain an independent check; the 13% turnout in the first judicial election is the documented public response (INE, June 2025).
  • Her 2024 promise to defend the USMCA relationship ended in a lesser outcome than sought: Mexico asked for the 16-year extension at the July 2026 review and the United States declined, leaving annual reviews to 2036. The shortfall is Washington's decision, not her breach — it is recorded here because the promise was hers and the result is the result (USTR, July 1, 2026).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED.

Sheinbaum's record is the first in this series where the failing grade is structural rather than personal: she prosecuted corruption into her own movement's navy and kept her predecessor's signature case alive, yet let its top figure stand uncharged and answered a cartel indictment of a sitting governor of her party by demanding more proof. She negotiated the tariff confrontation and the disappearance decree like a ruler who answers the wobble, and rejected the UN's wobble on disappearances outright. What fails is the grip: an elected judiciary with 13% turnout, a transparency institute abolished, a military further embedded, and an electoral redesign waiting for a friendlier Congress — institutions that will not be able to steady her successors the way unsteady institutions steadied the system before her. The poor are counted in, measurably; the disappeared and the migrants in transit are where the count runs out.
