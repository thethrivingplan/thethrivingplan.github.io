    Learn the craft

    How a day works — every move is a tap

    1. Seat. A customer arrives at the door with a patience meter. Tap a clean table to seat the person who has waited longest — or tap the customer to seat them at the first clean table.
    2. Order. Seated customers decide, then their order ticket appears in the kitchen. Tap the ticket to start it cooking. The ticket shows its progress while it cooks.
    3. Carry. A finished dish lands on the pass. Tap it to pick it up. Your hands hold two dishes — filling both before you cross the room is the oldest trick in this trade.
    4. Serve. Tap the table whose dish you are carrying. Serving while patience is still high earns a bigger tip, and a customer counts as served happy if their patience was at 40 or above when the dish landed.
    5. Clear. After eating, a customer pays and leaves the table dirty. Tap the dirty table to clear it so the next party can sit.
    6. Chains. Doing the same action several times in a row — seat, seat, seat; serve, serve — builds a chain. From the third in a row onward, each chained action pays a growing bonus. Breaking to a different action restarts the chain at one.
    7. Walkouts. If patience reaches zero — at the door or waiting for food — that customer leaves angry, pays nothing, and a seated walkout still leaves you a dirty table to clear.

    Four strategies, honestly compared

    There is no single right way to run this floor. Each strategy below wins somewhere and costs somewhere; the Coach will point at the live moment where each one applies.

    Lowest patience first

    Always work on whoever is closest to walking out: serve their dish first, seat the longest-waiting first.

    Trade-off: fewest walkouts and the steadiest tips — but it breaks chains, because you keep switching actions to chase emergencies, and it can leave easy wins sitting on the pass.

    Finish the chain

    When you are two or three deep in one action — seating, serving, clearing — finish the run before switching, and let the chain bonus pay you.

    Trade-off: the bonus money is real and adds up over a day — but every second spent finishing a chain is a second someone else's patience is falling. A chain is worth money; a walkout costs money and a customer.

    Never cross empty-handed

    Batch your trips: pick up both dishes before crossing to the tables, clear a dirty table on your way back to the kitchen, start a ticket whenever you are standing at the pass.

    Trade-off: the fewest wasted steps and the calmest floor — but waiting to batch can delay one urgent dish by a few seconds. Batch the routine trips; break the rule for a genuine emergency.

    Seat fast, seat smart

    When a queue forms, seat people quickly — a seated customer’s patience drains no faster, and an empty clean table earns nothing. But keep one eye on the kitchen: seating four people at once creates four tickets at once.

    Trade-off: fast seating stops door walkouts and starts orders sooner — but a flooded kitchen means longer waits for food, which is where seated patience dies. If the kitchen is backed up, clearing and cooking may matter more than seating the next person this second.

    Where this game comes from

    The time-management game is a documented form: it was defined by a diner game released in 2003, in which one server seated customers, took orders, ran the kitchen, served, collected payment, and cleared tables against a ticking clock, with patience meters and bonuses for repeating the same action in a chain. That shape — one pair of hands, too many jobs, every customer on a timer — has been a recognised kind of game ever since.

    The Lunch Rush is our own version of that documented shape: our name, our café, our menu, our words, our numbers. Nothing here is copied from any commercial game — no art, characters, names, or text. The form is the inheritance; everything on this page was made for this page.

    Settings — the rules are yours to set

    The standard day is 90 seconds, 4 tables, standard patience. Changing any of these makes the day a labelled variant — the banner above the floor says so while you play it, and the record keeps the label with the day. Changes apply from the next day.
