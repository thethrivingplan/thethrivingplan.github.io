The Ruler's Test — Abiy Ahmed, Prime Minister of Ethiopia

Subject: Abiy Ahmed Ali — Prime Minister of Ethiopia since 2 April 2018. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Ethiopia's presidency is a ceremonial head-of-state office, while executive power — command of the government, the budget, and the armed forces as commander-in-chief — sits with the prime minister. Abiy came to the seat in April 2018 when the ruling EPRDF coalition, facing mass protests, replaced Hailemariam Desalegn; he dissolved the EPRDF into his own Prosperity Party in December 2019 (the Tigray People's Liberation Front refused to join), and parliament re-elected him after the elections of 2021 and of 1 June 2026. He was sworn in for a new five-year term on 5 October 2026. The premise in the original brief — that Abiy still holds the seat — is verified as of November 2026; what the brief could not carry is the shape of the current term: the June 2026 election was held with no voting at all in Tigray and disrupted voting in Amhara and Oromia, and within weeks of his swearing-in federal forces were fighting a renewed war in Tigray and diplomatic ties with Eritrea were severed. He is graded on the full tenure since April 2018, because the tests are about a record over time; the file is not thin, and where the record is contested — above all on war deaths — the competing counts are kept separate with their sources, as the method requires. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The opening year applied the law upward, into the security establishment he inherited. In November 2018 his attorney general announced the arrest of 63 intelligence officials, military personnel, and businesspeople: 27 from the military-run Metals and Engineering Corporation (METEC), whose former director general, Maj. Gen. Kinfe Dagnew, was detained near the Sudanese border, on allegations of corruption involving some USD 2 billion of procurements made without open tender, and 36 further officials on allegations of prisoner abuse including torture and killings under the previous government (Attorney General Berhanu Tsegaye, 12 November 2018; Associated Press, November 2018). These were ALLEGATIONs prosecuted through the courts, against men of the old order's army and intelligence services — the deepest insider network in the country.
  • The same opening freed the other side of the old law: several thousand political prisoners were released in 2018, terror charges against exiled opposition leaders were dropped, banned groups were permitted to return, and the state of emergency was lifted in June 2018 (contemporaneous reporting, 2018; Human Rights Watch, World Report 2019). For roughly a year, release and prosecution ran in both directions.
  • His government accepted and published a joint investigation that found against its own forces. The Ethiopian Human Rights Commission–OHCHR joint report of November 2021 concluded that all parties to the Tigray conflict — federal forces, Eritrean forces, Tigrayan forces, and Amhara militias — had committed violations that could amount to war crimes and crimes against humanity; Addis Ababa cooperated with the inquiry and published alongside it, while disputing some of its characterizations (EHRC–OHCHR joint report, November 2021).
  • At the retail level of the 2026 election, the electoral board applied its rules against its own officials: NEBE dismissed two election executives in Addis Ababa for unlawful acts and annulled an entire polling station's vote in Burji after its officials were caught filling in ballot papers themselves (NEBE chairperson's briefing, 2 June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • After 2020, accountability stops at the boundary of his own coalition and his own armed forces. The UN's International Commission of Human Rights Experts on Ethiopia (ICHREE) found reasonable grounds to believe that federal and allied forces, as well as Tigrayan forces, committed war crimes and crimes against humanity in the 2020–2022 war; Ethiopia rejected the commission's work, campaigned against the renewal of its mandate, and the mandate was allowed to expire in October 2023 (ICHREE reports, 2022–2023; UN Human Rights Council record). The transitional justice policy his cabinet adopted in April 2024 — the domestic substitute he offered in place of international investigation — had, by Human Rights Watch's assessment in its World Report 2026, "effectively stalled," with no special court established and no senior figure from any side prosecuted for the war's atrocities (HRW, World Report 2026). The prosecutor and the principal suspect sit in the same government; survivors' calls for international judges were declined (HRW, 2025–2026).
  • The flagship findings against forces allied to his government were answered with rejection, not process. Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch concluded in April 2022 that Amhara regional officials and forces, with the complicity or acquiescence of federal troops, had carried out a campaign against Tigrayan civilians in Western Tigray amounting to ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity; the federal government dismissed the report as biased and one-sided, and no prosecutions followed (Amnesty International/HRW, We Will Erase You From This Land, April 2022; Ethiopian government response, April 2022). The finding itself remains contested by the government; the absence of any judicial process on it is not contested.
  • Emergency law fell on one region's civilians at scale. Under the August 2023 – June 2024 state of emergency declared for the Amhara conflict, the emergency proclamation suspended judicial review of detentions, and Amnesty International documented thousands of Amhara civilians arbitrarily detained, including in military-run internment camps, most never charged (OHCHR reporting via UK Home Office country note, June 2025; Amnesty International, 2024). Arrests of suspected Fano sympathizers continued through 2024 and 2025 after the emergency formally lapsed (UK Home Office country note, June 2025).
  • Ethnicity itself functioned as a detention criterion at the war's height: in late 2021, as Tigrayan forces advanced, federal and Addis Ababa police detained thousands of ethnic Tigrayan civilians in the capital and elsewhere in sweeps documented by Amnesty International and the UN human rights office — people held for who they were, not for anything individually alleged (Amnesty International, November 2021; OHCHR, 2021).
  • The law also reaches inward against critics from his own camp. Taye Dendea, a former state minister for peace and Prosperity Party official, was arrested in December 2023 after publicly criticizing the government's conduct of the Amhara war, and charged over his social-media posts (contemporaneous reporting, December 2023). By the June 2026 election, the Associated Press recorded that most of the ruling party's prominent critics were "imprisoned, in exile, or had disappeared" (Associated Press, 21 June 2026). A law that prosecutes the old order's generals, exempts the current order's commanders, and detains its own former ministers for criticism is not one law.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The central bank was given a statutory spine, and has so far been allowed to use parts of it. The revised National Bank of Ethiopia proclamation adopted under the IMF-supported reform program made price stability the bank's primary objective and capped central-bank financing of the government; the IMF's fifth review records that through end-2025 there was "no new provision of advances to the government by National Bank of Ethiopia," with the program's quantitative criteria met (IMF staff report, Fifth Review under the Extended Credit Facility, 2026). The July 2024 float of the birr — surrendering the executive's discretionary control of the exchange rate — was itself a loosening of the grip on the economy's central lever (IMF, July 2024).
  • A court ruled against the police in a press case, and the ruling stood. In September 2025 the Federal Supreme Court upheld the Federal High Court's decision to release two detained Sheger FM journalists on bail, rejecting the police appeal that the journalists might destroy evidence; the journalists were released (Rorshok Ethiopia Update, 25 September 2025). The episode began with an arrest, which belongs on the other shelf; the ending — a supreme court freeing journalists over police objection — belongs on this one.
  • The electoral board's chair asserted institutional neutrality on the record and the board did annul a fraud-tainted station and dismiss its own officers in June 2026 (NEBE, June 2026; see Test 1). The board accredited more than 220,000 party agents and some 1,800 journalists for the 2026 vote (NEBE statements, May–June 2026).
  • The 2018 appointments were, at the time, structural loosening: an exiled former opposition leader, Birtukan Mideksa, was invited home to chair the electoral board; Meaza Ashenafi became the first woman president of the Federal Supreme Court; half the cabinet was female (contemporaneous reporting, 2018). The grip test grades what became of those appointments, which is the other shelf — but the appointments themselves were real.

Evidence against:

  • The media regulator is his appointee, and the law was rewritten to widen its powers. Amendments adopted in April 2025 expanded the authority of the Ethiopian Media Authority — whose director general is appointed directly by the prime minister — over licensing and sanctions (Human Rights Watch, September 2025). The sequence that followed is documented: the licence of Addis Standard was revoked in February 2026 (the outlet appealed); the correspondent of the French daily La Croix was expelled in June 2026; on 2 November 2026 the Authority suspended The Reporter — one of Ethiopia's oldest outlets and its last privately owned Amharic newspaper — indefinitely, for content said to contradict "national interests" (Ethiopian Media Authority announcement, 2 November 2026; HRW statement, November 2026). Ethiopia fell to 148th of 180 in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index, from 110th in 2019; RSF records five journalists currently detained, four of them held for nearly three years on terrorism charges (RSF, 2026).
  • Journalists have been taken, not just regulated. HRW documented, from August 2025: the abduction from his home of the editor-in-chief of The Reporter by masked men, some in military uniform (13 August 2025); the arrest of three Sheger FM staff after a report on a health workers' strike that the Media Authority had already ordered deleted — the station complied, and the arrests came anyway (3 September 2025); the June 2025 arrest of an investigative journalist accused of "spreading false information"; and the April 2026 seizure of Addis Standard's managing editor from his newsroom by plainclothes officers, with no charge filed (HRW, September 2025 and 2026; Addis Standard, May 2026). The government characterizes these as law-enforcement matters; the pattern — masked abduction, arrest after compliance, detention without charge — is recorded here as documented by the monitors.
  • Civil society's referees were suspended by a government body. Since late 2024 the Authority for Civil Society Organizations has suspended at least five prominent rights organizations, including the Ethiopian Human Rights Council, on grounds HRW describes as "vague and politicized" and inconsistent with Ethiopia's own 2019 civil society law (HRW, World Report 2026; FIDH/OMCT statement, 28 May 2026). The same FIDH/OMCT statement records the re-emergence of the Awash Arba military camp as a site of secret detention — an ALLEGATION documented by the Observatory from defender testimony, recorded here as such.
  • The independent-minded officeholders of 2018 did not finish their courses. Electoral board chair Birtukan Mideksa resigned in mid-2023; her successor, Melatwork Hailu, chaired the board through the 2026 election. Supreme Court president Meaza Ashenafi and her deputy left office in January 2023 — the official record states that parliament accepted their resignations — and were replaced the same day by Tewodros Mihret and Abeba Embiale; the judiciary has since been criticized as operating under the incumbent party's influence (Federal Supreme Court record; Wikipedia summary of the succession, 2023). The central bank governorship likewise rotated within his circle: Mamo Mihretu, the technocrat who ran the float, resigned in September 2025 and was replaced by Eyob Tekalign, his state minister of finance since 2018 and a close associate — an appointment The Reporter and analysts flagged with calls for a genuinely independent bank (Ecofin Agency, September 2025; The Reporter, 20 September 2025).
  • The 2026 election was run inside this architecture. African Union and IGAD observers were "confined mostly to Addis Ababa"; opposition candidates reported harassment and an overwhelming state advantage; a leading opposition figure who boycotted called the vote a "sham"; the board's chair maintained the board had acted "without interference" (Associated Press, 21 June 2026). Both statements are on the record; the structure — a board chaired by his appointee, media licensed by his appointee, rights groups suspended by his administration — is not a matter of statement but of design.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Sidama wobble was answered with a vote. When Sidama statehood demands — suppressed for years under his predecessors — pressed in 2019, his government held the referendum in November 2019, accepted the overwhelming yes, and Sidama became a regional state in June 2020 (contemporaneous reporting, 2019–2020). A street demand was converted into a ballot and the ballot was honored.
  • The largest adjustment of the tenure is the Pretoria Agreement. After two years of war — including a period in 2021 when Tigrayan forces advanced toward the capital and his government declared a state of emergency and called citizens to arms — he signed the Cessation of Hostilities Agreement at Pretoria on 2 November 2022 rather than fight to a finish, accepting a negotiated order: federal services restored to Tigray, a disarmament process begun, and in March 2023 an interim Tigray administration headed by Getachew Reda, a senior figure of the very front he had been fighting (African Union mediation record, November 2022; contemporaneous reporting, March 2023). Whatever its later unravelling (Tests 4 and 5), the signature and its first implementation were an open adjustment under maximum pressure.
  • The Somalia wobble was walked back in the open. His January 2024 memorandum with Somaliland — which Somalia's government treated as a violation of its sovereignty, a characterization Ethiopia disputed — brought the two countries to the edge of conflict; in December 2024 he accepted Turkish mediation and signed the Ankara Declaration, reaffirming Somalia's territorial integrity and converting the sea-access demand into a negotiated commercial question (Ankara Declaration, 11 December 2024). The adjustment was public, signed, and held through 2025.
  • On the economy he took the painful correction rather than the comfortable denial: the July 2024 float of the birr and the IMF program were adopted in full knowledge of the inflationary hit, defended publicly as necessary, and maintained through the cost-of-living pressure that followed (IMF program documents, 2024–2026).

Evidence against:

  • The founding wobble of the tenure was answered with war. When the 2020 elections were postponed — officially for COVID-19 and logistics — Tigray's government defied Addis Ababa and held its own regional election in September 2020; the federal parliament declared it unconstitutional, budget transfers were cut, and on 4 November 2020 the federal government launched a military operation. Who bears responsibility for the first shots is contested: the federal government states that Tigrayan forces attacked the Northern Command on the night of 3–4 November 2020; the TPLF stated its move was pre-emptive against a planned federal assault. The sequence that is not contested is that a constitutional dispute over an election date was settled by two years of war rather than by negotiation, referendum, or court (contemporaneous reporting, 2020; ICHREE, 2022).
  • Denial was the first answer to the war's gravest facts. His government denied for months that Eritrean troops were fighting inside Tigray — he acknowledged their presence only in March 2021, attributing it to border security — and federal officials denied famine conditions in Tigray during the 2021 blockade period even as UN agencies documented them (contemporaneous reporting, 2021; UN OCHA statements, 2021). The pattern repeated institutionally: rather than answer ICHREE's findings, his government worked to end the commission's existence, and succeeded (Test 1).
  • The Amhara wobble was answered with a timetable, then an emergency. The April 2023 decision to dissolve regional special forces into federal structures was pushed forward on schedule despite mass protests across Amhara; when Fano militias took up arms, the answer was a six-month state of emergency (extended to June 2024), suspension of judicial review of detentions, and a military campaign that continues into late 2026 — not a renegotiation of the integration that triggered it (contemporaneous reporting, 2023–2026). Peace contacts with Fano factions were episodic and produced no settlement.
  • The street's smaller wobbles draw the same answer. The June 2020 killing of singer Hachalu Hundessa was followed by unrest in which security forces killed large numbers of protesters — the federal police themselves reported 239 deaths in the unrest — and by the arrest of leading Oromo opposition figures, who were held for some eighteen months before release in a January 2022 amnesty (contemporaneous reporting, 2020–2022). In 2025, health workers striking over pay were answered in part by detaining the journalists who covered the strike (Test 2).
  • The most recent wobble was answered with escalation. When the TPLF reinstated Tigray's pre-war regional government in May 2026 and then joined the seven-group Alliance for Survival, federal forces fought a six-week campaign, retook Mekelle on 3–4 October 2026, and installed a caretaker administration of federal appointees (Africa Risk Consulting, October 2026 briefing; House of Commons Library research briefing, November 2026). In his swearing-in address on 5 October 2026 he renewed Ethiopia's claim to Red Sea access; Ethiopia and Eritrea severed diplomatic ties in early October, and on 19 October he warned Eritrea of "very severe consequences" if it backed his enemies (House of Commons Library, November 2026). Negotiation may yet follow — it has before — but the first answer, again, was force and pressure.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Tigrayan, Amhara, Oromo, Somali, Afar and other communities, the opposition's voters, the displaced, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The 2018 opening was, in design, a counting-in. A cabinet half female, the first woman president and the first woman chief justice, thousands of prisoners freed across every community, exiled movements invited home, and a peace with Eritrea that briefly reopened a border closed for twenty years — the program's premise was that the previously excluded would be counted (contemporaneous reporting, 2018).
  • After Pretoria, the machinery of counting Tigray back in was switched on, for a time: telecommunications, banking, and flights to Tigray were restored in late 2022 and 2023, humanitarian convoys resumed at scale, a rehabilitation commission began registering ex-combatants, and an interim administration including the former belligerents governed the region (contemporaneous reporting, 2022–2023; Associated Press, October 2026). The October 2026 caretaker administration he installed after the renewed fighting is tasked, on his office's own statement, with restoring services, returning the displaced, and preparing regional elections within six months (Prime Minister's Office statement, 16 October 2026). The statement is recorded here; its fulfilment is a Test 5 question, not yet a fact.
  • The national safety net and the development program continued to run for the counted: the Productive Safety Net Programme was maintained through the reform period, and the GERD — inaugurated on 9 September 2025 — was framed and substantially financed as a project of all Ethiopians, its electricity promise aimed at the roughly 45 percent of the population the World Bank counts as lacking power (World Bank figures via AFP, September 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Tigray's dead are counted by everyone except his government, and the counts — kept separate, as the method requires — describe the largest exclusion in this series. Ghent University's research group, combining violence, famine, and collapsed medical access, revised its estimate to 162,000–378,000 civilian deaths for November 2020 – November 2022, having earlier published 385,000–600,000. The African Union's chief mediator, Olusegun Obasanjo, publicly cited about 600,000 total deaths. A peer-reviewed University of London study calculated over 102,000 excess deaths in Tigray to mid-2022, 72 percent from violence. The Uppsala Conflict Data Program recorded over 100,000 battle-related deaths in 2022 alone. The Ethiopian federal government disputes the higher figures and has published no count of its own (Ghent University assessments, 2022–2023; Obasanjo via Financial Times, January 2023; Population Health Metrics, 2025; UCDP, 2022). Sexual violence figures are likewise contested and kept separate: the Tigrayan-appointed Commission of Inquiry on Tigray Genocide reports close to 300,000 women and girls victimized — a figure from an interested party's inquiry, recorded as its claim — while the Denis Mukwege Foundation's November 2022 review concluded the data suggested widespread and systematic conflict-related sexual violence by Ethiopian and allied forces; ICHREE independently found reasonable grounds that sexual violence was committed by all sides (CITG report, 2026; Mukwege Foundation, November 2022; ICHREE, 2023).
  • Western Tigray's Tigrayans were counted out of their homes, and stayed out. Amnesty International and HRW documented in 2022 a campaign of forced expulsion, detention, and killings of Tigrayan civilians by Amhara regional forces and officials with federal complicity — their characterization, "ethnic cleansing" and crimes against humanity, is rejected by the Ethiopian government and recorded here as contested. What is documented by monitors across the political spectrum is the outcome: Tigrayans continued to be forcibly displaced from Western Tigray after the Pretoria Agreement, and hundreds of thousands of Tigrayan displaced people remained in camps years later (HRW, World Report 2026; Amnesty/HRW, April 2022).
  • Amhara's civilians were counted as a battlefield. The drone counts are kept separate by source and period: the UN human rights office recorded at least 479 civilians killed by drones between August 2023 and January 2024; ACLED's data, as compiled in January 2026, counted at least 669 people killed in more than 70 drone strikes in the region; an Amhara advocacy group's strike-by-strike monitor records 1,291 killed in 210 strikes from August 2023 to November 2026 — an advocacy source, recorded as such (OHCHR, March 2024; ACLED via Ethiopian Tribune, November 2026; Amhara Association/AAGE Drone Strike Monitor, November 2026). Documented individual incidents include the 17 April 2025 strike on a primary school area in East Gojjam in which more than 100 people were reported killed, and November 2026 strikes reported by AFP — on a displaced-persons camp in Amhara and on a grain lorry in Tigray — killing at least 25 civilians, including children; the Ethiopian army did not respond to AFP's request for comment (contemporaneous reporting, April 2025; AFP, November 2026). The federal government states that its strikes target Fano combatants; the monitors' casualty records are the counter-evidence, and both are stated here.
  • Oromia's civilians are caught between two armed accountants. In the June 2022 Gimbi-area massacres, eyewitnesses reported more than 200 Amhara civilians killed; the government blamed the Oromo Liberation Army, the OLA blamed government-linked militias — attribution remains contested and is recorded as such (contemporaneous reporting, June 2022). In October–November 2026, attacks on Orthodox Christian villages in East Arsi killed dozens: the Ethiopian Human Rights Commission attributed 48 deaths in the paired incidents, 41 of them to the OLA (including 13 civilians by its breakdown); the Ethiopian Orthodox Church put the toll above 60 across two woredas; the OLA acknowledged its fighters' involvement while disputing that the dead were unarmed civilians, describing them as part-time combatants — each characterization recorded with its source, none adopted (EHRC findings via BBC Amharic, November 2026; EOTC statements, October–November 2026; OLA statement, 16 November 2026). ACLED recorded 3,792 fatalities from political violence in Oromia across 2024–2025 (ACLED via UK Home Office country note). The federal state's documented failure in Oromia is double: it cannot protect the counted, and its own counterinsurgency is among the forces the monitors record against civilians.
  • The 2026 election formalized the exclusion. Tigray did not vote at all: all 38 of its seats stood empty, and 61 of the chamber's 547 seats went unfilled for security reasons; 143 polling stations in Amhara and Oromia never opened; the parliament that swore him in for a new term contains no representative chosen by Tigray's voters (NEBE final results, 21 June 2026; Reuters, 21 June 2026). The displaced, meanwhile, are counted differently by different counters, and all the counts are large: UNHCR recorded approximately 1.9 million internally displaced persons as of December 2025; other UN-cited estimates run to about 3.3 million (FIDH/OMCT, May 2026; IOM-cited figures, 2025). Poverty moved the wrong way across the tenure: the share of Ethiopians under the USD 2.15-a-day line rose from 27 percent in 2016 to 32 percent in 2021 on war, drought, and inflation, and roughly 15 million people relied on food aid into 2025–2026 (World Bank data; humanitarian reporting, 2025).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 2 April 2018, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The dam — delivered. Completion of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam was a standing pledge of his tenure; the dam was inaugurated on 9 September 2025, at about 5,150 MW the largest hydroelectric plant in Africa, substantially self-financed as promised (AFP, September 2025). The project began in 2011 under his predecessors; the completion, and the refusal of foreign-loan conditions attached to it, are his.
  • The Pretoria signature — given and, for its first phase, performed. The November 2022 agreement's early obligations on his side — cessation of hostilities, restoration of services and humanitarian access to Tigray, establishment of an interim administration — were substantially carried out in 2022–2023 (AU monitoring statements, 2023). The agreement's later collapse is charged on the other shelf; the word was not empty when given.
  • The Ankara word — kept. The December 2024 declaration's commitments to Somalia were honored through 2025: no unilateral move on the Somaliland memorandum, and the question moved to a negotiating track (Ankara Declaration follow-up reporting, 2025).
  • Inflation — bent, as pledged. His reform program promised to bring down inflation that peaked at roughly 34 percent in 2022; by 2025–2026 consumer inflation had fallen to the mid-teens — about 15 percent in August 2026 on national statistics, with the IMF recording the program's monetary targets met through end-2025 (national statistics via country reporting, 2026; IMF Fifth Review, 2026). The cost — a birr that fell from 57 to the dollar before the July 2024 float to above 150 by late 2026 — was the stated price of the same program, not a concealed one (IMF program documents; country data, October 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The founding promise — a democratic Ethiopia — is the largest broken word in the file. He came to power in 2018 promising a democratic transition: free media, no political prisoners, elections Ethiopians could trust. The record at his third swearing-in: press freedom ranked 148th of 180, down from 110th in 2019; journalists imprisoned, abducted, and expelled; the two most prominent independent outlets shuttered by his regulator within the year of the vote; rights organizations suspended; and an election from which an entire region was absent and after which the AP recorded his leading critics as imprisoned, exiled, or disappeared (RSF, 2026; AP, June 2026; HRW, World Report 2026). The 2020 election itself was not held on schedule — the postponement that lit the fuse of the Tigray war.
  • The Nobel peace — reversed. The 2018 peace with Eritrea, for which he received the 2019 Nobel Peace Prize, was pledged as permanent reconciliation. Eritrean forces fought inside Tigray as his allies from 2020 — a fact he acknowledged only in March 2021 — never completed the withdrawal the Pretoria framework required, and by October 2026 the two states had severed diplomatic ties, with Eritrea accused by Addis Ababa of backing his enemies and his 19 October 2026 warning of "very severe consequences" on the record (House of Commons Library briefing, November 2026; contemporaneous reporting). A peace kept for roughly two years of a promised permanence.
  • Pretoria's unfinished half. The agreement's outstanding obligations on the federal side — full return of the displaced, resolution of Western Tigray's status, and Tigray's path back to elected government — were unmet when the deal collapsed: no Tigrayan regional election was held in the four years after the signature, Tigray was excluded from the June 2026 national vote, and the October 2026 promise of regional elections "within six months" under a caretaker administration is the same promise reissued after a war (Prime Minister's Office, 16 October 2026). It is recorded here so the record can grade it in April 2027.
  • Transitional justice — promised, designed, and shelved. Accountability for the war's crimes was pledged repeatedly from 2021 onward and institutionalized in the April 2024 transitional justice policy; by 2026 HRW records the process as effectively stalled, with no prosecutions of senior figures and the international mandate his government opposed gone (HRW, World Report 2026). The national dialogue promised since the commission's creation in December 2021 likewise remains unconcluded, with the armed movements and much of the opposition outside it (FIDH/OMCT, May 2026).
  • Growth — the numbers are contested, and the contest is the finding. His party campaigned in 2026 on growth "topping 10 percent"; the IMF's figures for the same economy run lower — 7.2 percent for 2024/25 in its program documents, 9.2 percent projected for 2026 in its data — and dollar GDP per head fell from roughly USD 1,500 to about USD 990 after the float (IMF, 2025–2026; World Bank/IMF data via country reporting, October 2026). Both sets of figures are stated; a word that depends on whose arithmetic you accept has not been kept in the way it was given.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Abiy Ahmed's file divides cleanly into an opening and a long aftermath. The opening — 2018 into 2019 — is genuinely on the record: thousands of prisoners freed, the old security establishment's generals arrested, women raised to half the cabinet and to the presidency and the chief justiceship, an exiled opponent brought home to run elections, a twenty-year war with Eritrea ended, a Nobel Prize awarded for it. Almost everything on the "for" shelves of the first four tests dates from those eighteen months or from the single year after Pretoria. The aftermath is the rest of the file: a constitutional dispute answered with a war whose civilian dead are counted — by a university research group, by the AU's own mediator, by peer-reviewed demography — in the hundreds of thousands, while his government publishes no count and worked to end the international commission that might have produced one; a second region governed through emergency detention and a drone campaign whose civilian toll three separate counters record; a third in an insurgency in which his state is one of the armed parties the monitors cite; a transitional justice process adopted and shelved; a media regulator, chaired by his appointee under a law widened in 2025, that revoked one independent licence in February 2026 and suspended the country's last private Amharic newspaper eight months later; and a parliament, elected in June 2026 with Tigray wholly absent, that returned him to office in October while federal forces retook Mekelle. The two MIXED grades are real: he has adjusted in the open when the alternative was worse — Sidama's referendum, Pretoria's signature, Ankara's walk-back, the float — and he has delivered the tenure's largest physical promise, the dam, while halving inflation from its peak. But the wobble adjustments cluster where the pressure was existential and the word-keeping where the promise was concrete; the promises that would have constrained the seat itself — free media, political prisoners, accountability for his own forces, Tigray's road back to the ballot — are the ones the record shows unkept. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the October 2026 six-month promise of Tigrayan elections is met by April 2027, whether any senior figure is ever prosecuted for the wars' atrocities, and whether The Reporter's suspension is lifted or made permanent.
