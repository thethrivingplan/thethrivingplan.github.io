The Ruler's Test — Robert Fico, Prime Minister of Slovakia

Subject: Robert Fico — Prime Minister of Slovakia since 25 October 2023; previously Prime Minister 2006–2010 and 2012–2018, resigning on 15 March 2018 amid the crisis over the murder of journalist Ján Kuciak. Chairman of Smer-SD (Direction – Social Democracy). His current government is a coalition of Smer-SD, Hlas-SD and the Slovak National Party (SNS). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts did convict a figure from his own era despite the legal changes: Peter Kažimír, finance minister in Fico's 2012–2019 governments and governor of the National Bank of Slovakia, was convicted of bribery at first instance on 29 May 2025 and fined €200,000 (Specialised Criminal Court, Pezinok). His case survived because the judge reclassified the offence as bribery damaging the EU's financial interests, to which the shortened limitation period did not apply. Kažimír denies wrongdoing, pleaded not guilty, and has appealed; the conviction is not final (Reuters/AP, May 2025).
  • The flagship "Očistec" (Purgatory) prosecution of his former police president Tibor Gašpar and businessman Norbert Bödör — accused by prosecutors of running a criminal group inside the police under earlier Smer governments — was not abolished by decree; it reached trial in 2026 and continues on its remaining counts. Both defendants deny wrongdoing; the charges are ALLEGATIONs being tried (Aktuality reporting, 2025–2026).
  • The penal-code reform itself was enacted by parliament and then reviewed by the Constitutional Court, which upheld its core on 3 July 2024 — the government legislated in the open and accepted constitutional review of the result (Constitutional Court, Pl. ÚS 3/2024).
  • Fico has himself been a defendant of the system he criticises: charged in April 2022 in the "Súmrak" (Twilight) case with abusing power as prime minister, he was never tried — parliament declined to lift his immunity in May 2022 and prosecutors discontinued the charges in November 2022, under a government politically opposed to him. The discontinuation is recorded here because it is part of the record of how the process treated him (Slovak press reporting, 2022).

Evidence against:

  • The reform's benefits ran, by design or in effect, to named members of his own circle. The February 2024 penal-code amendment — abolishing the Special Prosecutor's Office, cutting penalties for corruption and economic crime, and sharply shortening limitation periods — was reported at passage to mean that charged Smer figures including MP Tibor Gašpar and Peter Kažimír "will not be tried or may receive only a suspended sentence" (Slovak Spectator, February 2024). Gašpar, himself a defendant, participated in drafting successive versions of the amendment in parliament (China-CEE Institute briefing, March 2024).
  • In October 2026 the mechanism completed its work in open court: the trial court halted the prosecution of Bödör, Gašpar and a former police official on six acts in the Očistec case because the limitation period — shortened by the amendment Gašpar had voted for — had expired. The prosecutor in the case, Bohdan Čeľovský, stated in court that the change had helped the accused (Aktuality reporting, October 2026). The defendants then asked the court to hear the expired acts anyway; the main case continues (TA3/Aktuality, October 2026).
  • The Special Prosecutor's Office was abolished on 20 March 2024 while it was handling corruption cases against Smer-linked figures and while it served as the designated guardian of EU funds. The European Commission stated it "regrets the dissolution of the Special Prosecutor's Office without the introduction of safeguards to ensure investigations can continue effectively," and Justice Commissioner Reynders warned of "a risk of irreparable harm to the financial interests of the Union" (European Commission statements, February–March 2024).
  • Kažimír's prosecution was nearly extinguished by the same reform: the Supreme Court found in November 2024 that the shortened limitation applied in his favour, and the case survived only because prosecutors reclassified the charge as an offence against EU financial interests — after the government, under Commission pressure, had in July 2024 restored stricter penalties and longer limitation periods for EU-funds crimes (Reuters, July 2024; EconoStream, May 2025).
  • The investigators who had built the Smer-era cases were removed before the law changed. On 27 October 2023 — two days after the government took office — Interior Minister Matúš Šutaj Eštok placed the NAKA investigator group around Ján Čurilla out of service, although they held protected-whistleblower status for investigating pre-2020 corruption by officials of the now-governing parties. Courts later annulled the suspensions for lack of the Whistleblower Protection Office's consent; Čurilla returned to duty only on 19 November 2026, at a district directorate, his agency by then dissolved (court rulings and Slovak press reporting, 2024–2026).
  • The pattern predates this term. The 2011 "Gorilla" wiretap file — leaked recordings in which a Penta financial-group partner is heard discussing commissions with public officials and referring to Fico — remains an ALLEGATION as to Fico personally; he denied wrongdoing. The Penta partner was charged in 2020; in 2021 Prosecutor General Maroš Žilinka annulled the charges under the extraordinary §363 power, one of a series of §363 annulments (including that of the then-SIS director's charges in 2021) that terminated high-profile cases from the Smer era without trial (Slovak press reporting, 2011–2021).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do courts, prosecutors, media, the central bank, the intelligence service, and municipalities keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Constitutional Court still rules against his government, and its rulings take effect. On 17 December 2025 the Court struck down the government's NGO-disclosure law as unconstitutional (it lost effect on 4 February 2026) and suspended the law abolishing the Whistleblower Protection Office before it could take effect; in 2026 it struck down a coalition provision altering the rules for cooperating defendants in pending cases, and part of the post-assassination assembly law (Constitutional Court rulings, December 2025 and 2026). The government complied with each ruling.
  • The presidency is not a rubber stamp even in allied hands: President Peter Pellegrini, Fico's former deputy, vetoed the whistleblower-office law in December 2025 and earlier vetoed a lifetime-annuity law for the prosecutor general; parliament overrode the first veto, and the annuity law lapsed (presidential statements, 2025).
  • Independent and opposition media continue to publish, broadcast and litigate; protests proceed weekly without prohibition; the opposition organises freely and, by November 2026, Slovak media reported the coalition had lost its working parliamentary majority through defections — politics, not the executive, did that (Slovak Spectator, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The anti-corruption prosecution architecture was dismantled in sequence: the Special Prosecutor's Office abolished (20 March 2024); the National Criminal Agency (NAKA), the police unit for organised crime and corruption, dissolved on 1 September 2024 and replaced by new offices; the Whistleblower Protection Office targeted for abolition in December 2025 (see Test 3). The European Commission's 2025 Rule of Law Report records the closures and notes the Prosecutor General's power to annul lower prosecutors' decisions, which "has led to the termination of corruption investigations, including cases involving several high-profile defendants" (European Commission, 8 July 2025).
  • The judiciary was pressured at its head. Within days of taking office, before the government had even won its confidence vote, the coalition replaced three members of the Judicial Council. In March 2024, after the Constitutional Court suspended parts of the penal-code amendment, Fico publicly attacked Chief Justice Ivan Fiačan — accusing him of leaking the decision — and demanded he step down, saying the next president would dismiss him if he did not (Slovak press reporting, March 2024). Justice Minister Boris Susko subsequently opened disciplinary proceedings against a Supreme Court judge whose panel's conduct the European Court of Human Rights had criticised (MFRR and Slovak reporting, 2024).
  • The public broadcaster was dissolved and re-founded under government control. The Television and Radio Act, approved by the government in April 2024 over the stated objection that RTVS was biased, was passed on 20 June 2024 with the opposition boycotting the vote; RTVS ceased to exist on 1 July 2024 and was replaced by STVR, whose council structure gives the governing majority control of appointments, including the director-general. The International Press Institute concluded the move "allowed the ruling coalition to install its own leadership and exert greater political control over public media content" (IPI, 2024; Act No. 157/2024).
  • The intelligence service was steered to a defendant's family. Fico's government first amended the competencies law so that Tibor Gašpar — then under indictment in Očistec — could head the Slovak Information Service while keeping his parliamentary mandate; when President Čaputová made clear she would not appoint an indicted person, the government installed Gašpar's son Pavol Gašpar, a deputy justice minister with a thin security résumé, as SIS director in late August 2024. Police surveillance recordings from 2021 (the "hunting lodge" tapes) had captured Fico, then in opposition, meeting Pavol Gašpar and a lawyer to strategise over the Očistec case (Slovak Spectator reporting, 2024–2026). Opposition oversight-committee members state SIS under him "is not a trustworthy institution"; those characterisations are theirs, recorded as such.
  • Access and standing were withdrawn from critical media as policy: the government stopped inviting or answering reporters from Denník N, Aktuality.sk, Sme and TV Markíza at its press conferences, and Fico filed a lawsuit — characterised by press-freedom organisations as a SLAPP — against Aktuality.sk editor-in-chief Peter Bárdy and his publisher over the use of Fico's photograph on a book cover (MFRR reporting, 2024).
  • The cultural institutions followed the same pattern: in August–September 2024 Culture Minister Martina Šimkovičová dismissed the directors of the Slovak National Theatre (Matej Drlička), the Slovak National Gallery (Alexandra Kusá) and the Slovak National Museum, after earlier removals at the National Library, BIBIANA and Kunsthalle Bratislava; new laws gave the ministry control over the Arts Council (FPU) that distributes public arts funding. Petitions for the minister's dismissal gathered some 180,000–200,000 signatures (Eurozine/DW reporting, 2024).

The Kuciak record, stated exactly as documented. This belongs to this test because it concerns the same media whose autonomy is at issue.

  • Documented facts: Investigative journalist Ján Kuciak, who reported on tax fraud by businessmen connected to senior politicians and was investigating alleged 'Ndrangheta links reaching into the prime minister's office, was murdered with his fiancée Martina Kušnírová on 21 February 2018 — the first journalist murdered in Slovakia since independence. The killings triggered the largest protests in Slovakia since 1989. Interior Minister Robert Kaliňák resigned on 12 March 2018; Fico resigned on 15 March 2018, and Peter Pellegrini formed a new government from the same coalition (contemporaneous reporting; court records).
  • The courts' verdicts: The gunman Miroslav Marček pleaded guilty and was sentenced to 25 years; Tomáš Szabó to 25 years; intermediary Zoltán Andruskó to 15 years. Alena Zsuzsová was convicted on 19 May 2023 of ordering the murders and sentenced to 25 years. Businessman Marian Kočner, whom prosecutors accused of commissioning the murder, was acquitted of that charge on 3 September 2020 and again on 19 May 2023, the court holding his role was not proved beyond reasonable doubt; he remains in prison on a separate 19-year sentence for forging promissory notes. On 20 May 2025 the Supreme Court ordered the murder case tried again before a new panel; it is ongoing. No court has found that Fico or any member of his government ordered or knew of the murder, and no such finding is made here (Specialised Criminal Court and Supreme Court records, 2020–2025).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the EU, protesters, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest adjustment of his career was total: in March 2018, facing the biggest protests since the Velvet Revolution, Fico resigned the premiership rather than hold office against the street. Whatever else the record shows, the exit was made, and power passed within the constitutional order (March 2018).
  • Under EU financial pressure he amended the penal code within five months: after the Commission warned the reform endangered EU funds, parliament on 16 July 2024 raised penalties and lengthened limitation periods for crimes against EU financial interests (Reuters, 16 July 2024).
  • The whistleblower-office abolition ended in retreat. Parliament passed the law on 9 December 2025; President Pellegrini vetoed it on 11 December; the coalition overrode the veto on 12 December; the Constitutional Court suspended the law on 17 December; the Commission opened infringement proceedings and suspended €590 million of Slovakia's recovery-plan funds. In spring 2026 the cabinet approved repealing the law outright, Fico calling it a "legislative corpse" that was ineffective and should be cleared away (STVR/TASR reporting, December 2025 – 2026).
  • Confronted with the threatened collapse of hospital care, he settled. After more than 3,300 doctors filed resignations in October 2024, parliament first passed his government's emergency law (11 December 2024, by 74–70) allowing doctors to be compelled to work under threat of imprisonment; nine days later the government signed a wide-ranging agreement with the doctors' union LOZ conceding pay and reform commitments, and in February 2025 the government agreed to withdraw the emergency measure (AP, December 2024; government–LOZ agreement, 20 December 2024).
  • On sanctions he ultimately absorbed rather than broke the EU line: in July 2025, after conditioning his assent to the EU's 18th Russia sanctions package on gas-supply guarantees and receiving Commission assurances, he let it pass rather than veto it (EU Council reporting, July 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The penal-code reform itself was the refusal of a wobble: it was fast-tracked from December 2023 over weekly protests, expert objection, a presidential referral and the Constitutional Court's suspension of most of it, and was enacted essentially intact in August 2024 once the Court upheld its core (parliamentary record, 2023–2024).
  • The January 2025 protests — the largest of this term, with organisers estimating 60,000 in Bratislava and Denník N estimating at least 100,000 nationwide on 24 January 2025 — were answered not with engagement but with a counter-allegation: Fico, citing a classified SIS report read in parliament, accused the opposition and protest organisers of contact with foreigners plotting a coup. No evidence for the coup claim has been produced publicly; the protests remained peaceful throughout, as AP noted (AP/Reuters, January 2025).
  • After surviving an assassination attempt on 15 May 2024 in Handlová — shot five times at close range, four bullets striking him — Fico's first major statement (5 June 2024) forgave the gunman personally, said he felt no hatred and would take no legal action against him, and in the same address blamed the opposition, "anti-government media" and foreign-funded organisations for creating the climate of the attack. AP's reporting states there is no evidence linking the opposition or media to the shooter, who told investigators he acted alone out of disagreement with government policy — on Ukraine, the abolition of the Special Prosecutor's Office and culture policy (AP, June 2024 and October 2025). Juraj Cintula was convicted of a terrorist attack on 21 October 2025 and sentenced to 21 years; the Supreme Court upheld the conviction on 29 April 2026. Fico was not a claimant in the case.
  • The legislative answer to the shooting tightened assembly rather than loosening the rhetoric: the "lex atentát" package, fast-tracked and passed on 27 June 2024, banned gatherings near officials' homes and government seats, made municipalities liable for incidents at assemblies they permit, and granted lifetime salaries to prime ministers and speakers who served two terms — a category that includes Fico. Amnesty International Slovakia called it "an attempt to use security and public order as pretexts to crack down on the right to peaceful assembly" (Amnesty International, June 2024).
  • Where identity questions are concerned, the answer to pushback is escalation: the NGO-disclosure law was passed in April 2025 over the Council of Europe Human Rights Commissioner's written warning; the Beneš-decrees provision (Test 4) was defended by Fico personally after Hungary's opposition leader and Slovak Hungarian politicians objected; and the protests that resumed in November 2025 and continued into 2026 — over the whistleblower law, the Beneš provision and austerity — produced no policy change beyond the whistleblower retreat recorded above (protest chronologies, 2025–2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Roma, Hungarians in Slovakia, Ukrainians, journalists, LGBTQ citizens, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Social transfers were delivered broadly and without partisan test: the full 13th pension was paid to all pensioners in December 2024 as promised, and a 5% VAT rate on basic foods took effect on 1 January 2025 (Finance Ministry records, 2024–2025).
  • Ukrainians were sheltered at scale even as policy hardened: Slovakia maintained temporary protection for roughly a hundred thousand displaced Ukrainians throughout his term, and commercial — as distinct from state — arms and energy flows continued; the state did not expel or collectively strip protection from those already present (Interior Ministry figures, 2023–2026).
  • Roma inclusion machinery was not abolished: the office of the Government Plenipotentiary for Roma Communities continued under his governments, and Slovakia's formal Roma-integration strategies remained in force on paper throughout (government records).

Evidence against:

  • Roma children remain segregated on the state's own long record, most of it accumulated under his governments. The European Commission's infringement procedure, opened in 2015, was referred to the Court of Justice in April 2023 after the Commission found Slovakia's reforms insufficient; EU Fundamental Rights Agency data show 65% of Roma pupils aged 6–15 attending schools where all or most pupils are Roma. On 5 August 2025 the Advocate General opined that Slovakia has "systematically and persistently" failed to end the segregation of Romani children, in breach of the Race Equality Directive (CJEU, Advocate General Capeta's opinion, August 2025; FRA Roma Survey). The Court's judgment was pending at grading.
  • The Hungarian minority was counted out by criminal law in December 2025: an amendment to the Criminal Code, initiated by Smer, made publicly denying or questioning the post-war settlement — including the Beneš decrees, under which ethnic Hungarians were stripped of citizenship and property on the principle of collective guilt, and which the Slovak Land Fund still invokes in present-day land confiscations — punishable by up to six months in prison. On 30 January 2026 police detained Hungarian Alliance politician Örs Orosz at a Bratislava protest for wearing a vest reading "I question Beneš's decrees!"; he was released after two hours. Fico defended the decrees as "an indelible and integral part of the legal order of the Slovak Republic." Members of the European Parliament put the confiscations and the law to the Commission, which replied in April 2026 that it was monitoring the matter (European Parliament question E-005054/25 and Commission answer, December 2025 – April 2026; IEŚ commentary, February 2026).
  • Ukrainians were made a pressure instrument: after Ukraine let the Russian gas-transit contract lapse on 1 January 2025, Fico publicly threatened to halt Slovakia's emergency electricity exports to Ukraine and to "significantly reduce support for Ukrainian citizens in Slovakia" — the refugees his own state was sheltering — unless transit resumed or Slovakia was compensated for some €500 million in lost fees (Fico statements reported by Reuters, January 2025). His government had already halted state military aid in November 2023, and in June 2025 parliament passed a resolution instructing the government to oppose new sanctions on Russia in international forums.
  • Journalists are addressed as enemies from the seat: the government boycotts the country's main independent outlets at press conferences; the prime minister sued Aktuality.sk's editor-in-chief; the broadcaster those journalists' audiences relied on was dissolved (Test 2). The European Parliament's LIBE monitoring group reported in July 2025 that no measures had been taken to improve journalists' safety or working conditions in Slovakia, and UN experts warned in March 2025 of a deteriorating environment for expression and assembly (European Parliament DRFMG, July 2025; OHCHR, 10 March 2025).
  • LGBTQ citizens were written down in the constitution: the amendment passed on 26 September 2025 by exactly the required 90 votes defines sex as strictly male or female as determined at birth, restricts adoption to married heterosexual couples, bans surrogacy, requires parental consent for comprehensive sex education, and asserts the primacy of Slovak law in "cultural and ethical" questions — provisions the Venice Commission and the International Commission of Jurists warned could conflict with Slovakia's ECHR and EU obligations (National Council vote, 26 September 2025; Venice Commission, September 2025).
  • His opponents' voters are counted as a security problem: the January 2025 coup allegations (Test 3) were directed at peaceful protesters, and Fico responded to his party's expulsion from the Party of European Socialists on 17 October 2025 — a unanimous vote citing positions contradicting the family's values — by declaring himself "proud of this expulsion" (PES Congress, Amsterdam, 17 October 2025).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Signature promises and stated positions vs. the record across three premierships.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The Ukraine promise was kept to the letter. Campaigning in 2023, Fico pledged that Slovakia would not send "a single bullet" — state military aid — to Ukraine. Within weeks of taking office his government formally halted state military aid (November 2023), and no state aid package has followed; he pledged to block Ukraine's NATO accession for as long as he is prime minister, and has repeated the position since. Commercial arms sales by Slovak firms, which he did not promise to stop, continued (government decisions, November 2023 – 2026).
  • The social promises were substantially delivered. The full 13th pension was legislated and paid in December 2024; the 5% VAT rate on basic foods, medicines and books took effect on 1 January 2025; household energy prices were shielded by continued state support schemes (Finance Ministry and budget records, 2024–2025).
  • The foreign-policy reorientation was announced before it was executed. Fico told voters in 2023 he would pursue a "sovereign" foreign policy in all directions and end Slovakia's subordination, in his framing, to Brussels and Washington. The record matches the pitch: meetings with Vladimir Putin in Moscow on 22 December 2024 and at the Victory Day commemorations on 9 May 2025 and again on 9 May 2026 — the only EU head of government to attend — alongside continued EU and NATO membership, which he has also repeatedly affirmed (Kremlin and Slovak government readouts, 2024–2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The cost-of-living promise was financed by the taxes he said voters would not bear. Smer campaigned in 2023 on cheaper food and energy and against shifting consolidation onto ordinary people. In office his coalition raised the standard VAT rate from 20% to 23% (1 January 2025) and introduced a financial transaction tax of 0.4% on business transfers (from April 2025) — a levy without parallel in the EU — in three successive consolidation packages that employers' associations and trade unions jointly condemned, and which drove renewed protests through autumn 2025 (consolidation laws, October 2024 – 2025; China-CEE Institute review of 2025).
  • The restoration promise inverted. Fico returned to power in 2023 promising to end the "chaos" of 2020–2023 and restore a state governed by law rather than by prosecutors and media campaigns. The documented delivery is graded in Tests 1 and 2: the anti-corruption prosecutor abolished, the criminal agency dissolved, the limitation clock run out on his own police president's charges, and the European Parliament resolving in May 2026 that Slovakia's rule-of-law situation had deteriorated sharply (European Parliament resolution debate, 20 May 2026).
  • The earlier word on the press and minorities did not hold either. As a younger prime minister Fico presented himself as a standard European social democrat; his first government nonetheless adopted the 2009 State Language Act amendments — criticised by the OSCE for fining minority-language use and softened by the succeeding government — and the 2008 Press Act's right-of-reply provisions criticised by the OSCE and journalists' organisations. The trajectory from those measures to the 2024 broadcaster dissolution is one line, not a reversal (OSCE Representative on Freedom of the Media statements, 2008–2011).
  • The gas-transit word failed on outcome. Fico promised through 2024 to preserve Russian gas transit across Ukraine and the roughly €500 million a year it brought Slovakia. Transit ended on 1 January 2025 by Kyiv's decision; his Moscow diplomacy did not restore it, and the shortfall became a charge against Ukraine rather than an acknowledged miss (Fico statements, December 2024 – January 2025).

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Robert Fico's file is the record of a ruler who has been removed by the street once and shot once, and whose answer to both has been to narrow the space in which removal can be organised. The one-law failure is documentary and specific: a penal code rewritten in fast-track by a coalition whose own defendants — his former police president above all — then watched counts expire under the shorter clocks they had voted for, while the office built to prosecute such cases was abolished mid-docket. The grip failure runs the same way through every institution the tests name: prosecutors, police investigators, the whistleblower office, the public broadcaster, the intelligence service handed to an indicted man's son, cultural institutions purged by ministerial signature. Against that stand real counterweights, and they are on his shelf: he resigned when the street demanded it in 2018; he forgave the man who shot him and took no legal action against him; he has repeatedly, at cost, adjusted when EU money was the lever — the July 2024 code fix, the doctors' settlement, the whistleblower retreat — and his signature campaign promise on Ukraine was kept to the letter. But the adjustments come when the price is financial or systemic, never when the question is who counts: Roma children still sit in segregated classrooms under a judgment-in-waiting, Hungarian citizens can now be prosecuted for questioning the decrees that dispossessed their grandparents, and the refugees of a neighbouring war were threatened as a bargaining chip. The tests measure the seat, not the survival instinct, and on the evidence assembled here the seat gives by loyalty, grips by design, and counts the flock with named exceptions.
