Block Party

An original property-trading board game: roll two dice, walk the block, buy the street you land on, finish a colour set, build houses and a hotel, trade like you mean it — last neighbour solvent wins the block.

What happened — the table log

Trade evaluator — tap to open the pro tool

Price any trade honestly before you offer it. Pick who gives and who gets, tick the streets and type the cash on each side, and the evaluator prices both sides — face price, plus what a street is really worth when it finishes a set or blocks one. Works before a game too, with the full street list.

How strong players actually play — more than one way, on purpose

There is rarely one right move in this game, so the teaching here always shows the real alternatives with their trade-offs. A single way is named only where there genuinely is one.

Finishing a set vs taking the cash

Hold for the set: a lone street pays small rent; a finished set doubles the bare rent and unlocks houses, where the real money is. Trade-off: you may sit cash-poor while you wait. Take the cash now: selling your odd street funds houses on a set you already own. Trade-off: you may hand a rival the last piece of their set — price that danger in, every time.

Building: the three-houses sweet spot

Rent jumps hardest at the third house on a street — the step from two houses to three is the biggest single leap in the building table, so three houses across a set usually beats rushing one street to a hotel. Build even (the rules make you): steady income, money spread where dice actually land. Rush the hotel: one huge rent can end a rival — but it ties up cash, and a hotel with no cash reserve is one bad roll from a mortgage fire-sale. House-hoarding is a documented tournament tactic in the physical classic — its box holds a limited number of houses, so sitting on many small houses can starve rivals of buildings. It does not work on this page: Block Party's bank of houses never runs out, so hoarding here only caps your own rents. We name it so you recognise it in the classic's story — and so nobody wastes a game trying it here.

Block a rival's set vs complete your own

Complete yours: income now, building now. Block theirs: holding the one street a rival needs is worth more than its rent — it freezes their whole set, and its trade price only rises. Trade-off: a blocking street earns little while you hold it. Strong players check both questions before every trade: what does this finish for me, and what does it finish for them?

When a mortgage saves you

Mortgaging is a loan at a fair price: you get half the street's price now and pay 10% extra to lift it later. Mortgage to survive a rent: almost always right — a mortgaged street can be lifted; a bankrupted player can't un-lose. Mortgage the street that earns least first, and never mortgage a finished set's street if an odd street will cover the bill. Mortgage to build: occasionally right late, when the new houses will earn the lift-cost back within a few circuits — do the arithmetic in the evaluator first. The one single-answer rule in this game: if the choice is mortgage or go under, mortgage.

The Quiet Corner, late

Early, the Corner is a tax: turns lost while streets sell. Late, when rivals' hotels stand, it can be the safest seat on the board — some strong players stop paying to leave quickly once the board turns dangerous. Two honest strategies, opposite answers, both real: leave fast early, linger late.

Documented strategies — with their stories, not bare tips

Every strategy below is a documented strategy from real play of the classic property-trading game this one rests on — carried with its story: where it worked, where it didn't, and the rates where rates honestly exist. Dice rates are arithmetic (count the 36 ways two dice can fall). Where a number comes from this page's own simulation, it says so.

1. Buy the set that sits a dice-roll past the Corner

The story: frequency counts of the documented game — players tallying thousands of landings — kept finding the same thing: the colour set sitting 6 to 9 squares past the held corner collects the most rent on the board, because everybody leaving the corner throws the dice, and the likeliest throws are 6, 7, and 8 (5, 6, and 5 chances in 36). On this board that set is Ember — Market Hall Street, Cobbler Row, Tinsmith Lane, sitting 6, 8, and 9 squares past the Quiet Corner. Where it worked: long games, where that traffic compounds circuit after circuit. Where it didn't: short games and games where a rival hotel set ended things before the traffic could pay — position earns slowly; hotels earn suddenly.

2. Build to three houses, then stop and think

The story: documented building analysis of the classic game noticed the rent table's shape: the leap from two houses to three is the largest single jump in it — on this board's printed rents, three houses pay roughly 2.8× what two pay (check any street card: the factors run 5, 15, 42). Tournament players built to three across a set and only then weighed hotels. Where it worked: almost everywhere — it is the closest thing this game has to a proven default. Where it didn't: when hoarding cash mattered more — three houses on every street is a fortune that one hotel rent can still take; and a player one circuit from a rival's hotel sometimes rightly keeps the reserve instead.

3. The house shortage — a tournament tactic with an honest asterisk

The story: in the physical documented game the box holds a limited stock of houses, and tournament players learned to sit on many small houses and refuse hotels, starving rivals of buildings entirely. Where it worked: tournament tables, where it has decided real games. Where it doesn't work: here — Block Party's bank of houses does not run out, so the denial half of this tactic cannot bite on this page. The build-to-three half still applies in full. We print the asterisk rather than teach a tactic our own board defeats.

4. Linger in the Corner late

The story: documented endgame play: once rival hotels stand, experienced players stop paying to leave the corner quickly — every turn held is a turn no hotel can tax. Where it worked: endgames with two or more developed rival sets. Where it didn't: early, when the same patience costs you the streets still selling — the identical move is a blunder in the first half and a shield in the second. Timing, not the move, is the strategy.

5. Take the cheap sets seriously

The story: the documented game's first two sets look like consolation prizes, but their building costs are the lowest on the board, so their rents repay their cost faster than any prestige street — players who track payback periods, not rent sizes, documented this decades ago. On this board: Cedar and Sky, houses at 50 each. Where it worked: short games and cash-tight games. Where it didn't: long games against developed Fern or Harbour sets, whose single rents can erase a cheap set's whole evening.

So what's the best option?

Answered from the whole evidence base, with the evidence shown — never merely asserted:

  • Documented: the dice arithmetic (7 lands 6 times in 36; 6 and 8, 5 each) and the rent-table shape (the 2→3 house leap) are checkable facts, printed on every street card here.
  • Simulated: before shipping, the four bot levels' decision parameters — the same reserve, set-pricing, and building numbers the bots play with on this page — were run against each other in 6,000 simplified decision-model games (a numbers model of buy/build/trade choices, not full board games). The level that prices sets and blocks at full value (Pro) won 64.8% of four-way games against 2.4% for the level that mostly doesn't — set-pricing is the single biggest edge the model shows, and it is the model's edge, stated as such.
  • Yours: your own record appears here as you finish games.

Put together, the evidence-backed answer for a first game: finish a set — Ember if the dice offer it — build it to three houses before anything else, keep a reserve near the biggest rent on the board, and price every trade by what it finishes and what it blocks. Two honest alternatives stand beside it: the cheap-set sprint (better in short games) and the cash-hoard (better when a rival is one trade from a hotel set). The evidence favours the first; it does not erase the others.

How to play — tap to open

Turn: tap Roll. Two dice move your token. Doubles roll again; three doubles in a row sends you to the Quiet Corner. Passing Start pays 200 Block Bucks from the bank.

Landing: an unowned street, depot, or works can be bought for its printed price — or passed, which (under standard rules) sends it to auction for everyone. On somebody else's property you pay the rent on its card. Rent doubles on a finished, unbuilt set. Depots pay more the more one player holds; the two Works pay by the dice.

Building: own every street of a colour to build. Houses must go on evenly across the set; the fifth building is a hotel. Build and mortgage from your panel on your turn.

The Quiet Corner: land on “Head to the Quiet Corner,” draw a card that sends you, or roll three doubles. While held there you still collect rent. Leave by paying 50, using a Porch Pass card, or rolling doubles within three turns (the third failed try costs the 50 and you move).

Cards: Plot Twist squares and Block Letter squares each have their own deck, written for this game. Follow the card; some are kept until used.

Money trouble: mortgage streets (half price, lifted later at +10%) or trade. If you still can't pay, you go under: your streets go to the player you owed (or back to the bank), and — under the house rule chosen at setup — you stay at the table as banker, coach, or auctioneer instead of sitting out.

Winning: last player solvent, or richest by total worth (cash + streets at price + buildings at cost) when a short game's bell rings.

Design notes — the best kept and improved, the worst named and answered — tap to open

Block Party was built the house way: grade the classic documented game's features, keep and improve the best, name the worst and answer it — never silently inherit it. Both shelves, in plain words:

Best — kept, and improved

  • Trading. The heart of the classic — kept in full, and improved with an honest trade evaluator that prices both sides, including set-completion and blocking value, so a trade is a negotiation, not a ambush.
  • Auctions. The real classic rule most kitchen tables never knew: a passed street is auctioned. Kept as the standard default, run openly on the page.
  • Completing a set and building. The arc from scattered streets to houses to a hotel — kept whole, with the building math taught in the moment (the three-houses sweet spot).
  • The arc from poor to rich. Everybody starts equal; the board rewards patience, nerve, and arithmetic. Kept — and made readable: every rent, price, and worth is printed where you can see it before you decide.

Worst — named, and answered

  • Elimination leaves somebody sitting out for an hour. Answered the house way: a player who goes under takes a living role at the table — banker, coach, or auctioneer — or, if the table chose it in settings, a one-time second-chance loan. Sitting out survives only as an option a table picks on purpose; it is nobody's forced default.
  • The runaway leader nobody can catch. Answered with an optional catch-up setting: a small grant to the last-place player passing Start. Off by default — standard games stay standard — but the table can switch it on.
  • Games that never end. Answered with the short-game setting: 20 or 30 rounds, richest at the bell wins. The endless game remains available; it is a choice, not a trap.
  • The beloved bad part: the free-parking jackpot. It inflates the money supply and stretches games — and people genuinely love it. So it stays, as what it is: a labelled house-rule option in settings (here, the Community Porch jackpot), never the silent default.

The pattern: a bad part is left behind as a forced default, not as a possibility. What people love stays reachable; what the game forced on everyone is now a choice.

Bot honesty — how the four levels really differ — tap to open

The levels are not name tags. Each level is a different set of decision numbers, printed here: how much cash a bot insists on keeping before it buys or builds, how completely it prices set-completion and blocking in trades, how far it will chase an auction, and how often it simply misjudges.

Steady250SometimesUp to 1.1× valueSometimes

Pro80Always, at full value, and counts your set tooUp to 1.4× value when it finishes or blocks a setAlmost never

Simulation check: before this page shipped, the four parameter sets were run against each other in 6,000 simplified decision-model games (the same buy/build/trade parameters the bots play with here; dice luck shared equally). Four-way win rates separated cleanly: Learning 2.4% · Steady 7.8% · Sharp 25.0% · Pro 64.8%, and head-to-head Pro beat Learning 94.3% of games. Luck still beats skill in any single game — that's the game — but over many games the labels tell the truth.

Legal audit — what was checked before this shipped — tap to open

The standard applied: use nothing we are not certain is legal for us to use. Game mechanics and shapes are free for everybody; names, text, art, and trade dress that could be somebody's are not used unless certain. Uncertainty meant replace, not risk.

  • Game name: “Block Party” — our name for this game; the branded game's name appears nowhere on the board, in the money, or in any card.
  • Every street, depot, works, corner, and square label: written for this neighbourhood (Library Lane, Bakery Row, the four Depots, Sun Power Co-op, Rain Garden Water, the Quiet Corner, the Community Porch, Start). Checked one by one against the branded game's board: no name matches, and no name is a near-copy of one.
  • Replaced on suspicion alone: several first-draft labels were dropped even though they were probably fine, because “probably” is not the standard — a utility name in the style of the classic board's works squares, a prestige-street name in the style of its most famous square, a corner name in the style of its rest corner, both of its deck names, and its corner words for the start square and the held corner were all replaced with ours (Rain Garden Water, Lantern Heights, Community Porch, Plot Twist, Block Letter, Quiet Corner, Start). The replaced wordings are not reprinted here — naming what was avoided, in our words, is the audit; repeating their words would defeat it.
  • Card decks: every card's text was written for this game, in this game's words and numbers. Effects use the free mechanics (move, pay, collect); no card's wording was copied or paraphrased from any published deck.
  • Money, tokens, board art: Block Bucks are this game's own design; tokens are four everyday objects chosen for this game; the board's layout styling, colours, and centre panel are drawn for this page, not traced from any published board.
  • Rules: stated in our own words throughout. Where a rule is the documented classic rule (auctions, even building, mortgage at half price lifted at +10%), it is a mechanic — free for everybody — and the page says plainly that the game rests on the documented classic.
