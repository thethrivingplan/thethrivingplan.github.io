The Ruler's Test — Abdourahamane Tchiani, President of Niger

Subject: General Abdourahamane Tchiani (also spelled Tiani) — head of the National Council for the Safeguarding of the Homeland (CNSP) from 28 July 2023, and President of the Republic of Niger since 26 March 2025. The seat graded is the presidency, and the tenure graded runs from the coup of 26 July 2023 to November 2026. The premise in the original brief is confirmed with two precisions. First, Tchiani did not take the title of president in July 2023: he ruled for twenty months as head of the CNSP junta, and was sworn in as President of the Republic only on 26 March 2025, the day the Charter of the Refoundation was promulgated — a charter that replaced the suspended 2010 constitution and made him president for a five-year "refoundation" period that the charter itself describes as flexible and renewable (BBC, 26 March 2025; Britannica country file, updated November 2026). Second, the man he overthrew is part of this file's frame: Mohamed Bazoum, elected in 2021 in Niger's first handover from one elected president to another, has been detained with his wife Hadiza in the presidential palace in Niamey since the day of the coup — more than three years, without trial. His elected term would have expired on 2 April 2026; his lawyers argue the detention years should not count against it, a dispute recorded here as unresolved (AFP via Kapital FM, 1 April 2026). Tchiani, a career officer who commanded the presidential guard from 2011 to 2023, promoted himself to army general — the country's highest rank — on taking the presidency, and governs by ordinance through a government headed since August 2023 by Prime Minister Ali Mahaman Lamine Zeine. There is no elected institution at any level in Niger: the National Assembly was dissolved in 2023, local councils were dissolved, all political parties were dissolved by decree in March 2025, and an appointed 194-member Consultative Council of the Refoundation (CCR), installed 28 June 2025, advises the executive but cannot make laws (International IDEA country tracker, 2025; electos.net country file, November 2026). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform a ruler announced is not itself a failure: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out an announced program is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The single largest act of legal even-handedness on his record ran against the coup's own enemies list. On 1 April 2025, following recommendations of the February 2025 national conference, the authorities released around 50 people detained since the coup — including former ministers of the ousted government (former oil minister Mahamane Sani Issoufou, former defence minister Kalla Moutari, former finance minister Ahmed Jidoud, former energy minister Ibrahim Yacoubou), the president of Bazoum's former ruling party, a journalist, and — on the other side of an older divide — soldiers convicted over past coup attempts against a previous president, among them a former chief of staff sentenced in 2018 (France 24/AFP, 2 April 2025; International IDEA country tracker, April 2025). The releases were framed by Tchiani as "forgiveness and reconciliation," and they were real: men the junta had charged with conspiracy against state security walked free by the same door.
  • Some of his signature measures were, in form, universal. The dissolution of political parties in March 2025 dissolved all of them — there is no licensed ruling party sheltering under the ban — and the five-year refoundation framework applies to the whole state rather than carving out a CNSP exception in its text (BBC, 26 March 2025; Charter of the Refoundation as reported by Britannica, 2025).
  • The economic referees of the old order were left under one rule for everyone: Niger remained inside the West African Economic and Monetary Union and the CFA franc throughout, the IMF's Extended Credit Facility program — approved under Bazoum in 2021 — continued through its sixth and seventh reviews under Tchiani's government, and the oil-stabilization fund reform of June 2025 was adopted, per the IMF, to make the use of oil revenues more transparent and countercyclical (IMF press releases, 22 January 2025 and 14 July 2025).

Evidence against:

  • A second law exists, and it is written down. By decree, the junta built a parallel track for its opponents: a 2024 order creating a national terrorism database with, in Human Rights Watch's assessment, overbroad inclusion criteria and inadequate due process — and then used it to provisionally strip Nigerien nationality from named individuals, a status Niger's 1984 Nationality Code does not provide for at all. Nine people linked to Bazoum were stripped on 10 October 2024 (among them his former adviser Rhissa Ag Boula and two generals); seven more former officials followed in November 2024, including former foreign minister Hassoumi Massoudou; the citizenship of democracy activist Mariama Djibrine was revoked in June 2026 after she publicly called for Bazoum's release; and on 17 September 2026 five more were stripped, including former prime minister Ouhoumoudou Mahamadou and three former Bazoum advisers. The state news agency's own count puts the total at 25 people. Every documented target is an opponent, a critic, or a member of the ousted government; no member of the ruling council or its camp appears on the list. The opposition Alliance of Sahel Democrats notes the September 2026 decree stripped men who are still being prosecuted, although the 2024 order itself permits deprivation only after a conviction — the junta's own rule, not followed in the junta's own cases (Human Rights Watch, 15 October 2024 and September 2026; International IDEA country tracker, October 2024).
  • The detention track runs the same way. Since the coup, the authorities arbitrarily arrested at least 30 officials of the ousted government, charging civilians — former ministers and cabinet members — with "threatening state security" before a military court (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2025). The elected president himself is held without charge being brought to trial: the junta announced in August 2023 that it would prosecute Bazoum for high treason, a capital offence; a State Court lifted his immunity on 14 June 2024 in a proceeding Human Rights Watch records as failing basic due-process standards, including the right to a defence; and no trial has been held in the twenty-nine months since (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2025; European Parliament resolution, March 2026). One of his lawyers states the detention's function plainly — that Bazoum serves the junta as "a human shield" against foreign intervention. That is an interested party's characterization of motive, recorded here as an ALLEGATION, not a finding (AFP via Kapital FM, 1 April 2026).
  • The speech law is a second law on its face. Ordinance 2024-28 of 7 June 2024 reintroduced prison terms for online expression — one to three years for defamation, two to five years for disseminating data "likely to disturb public order or undermine human dignity," with mitigating circumstances expressly unavailable — and its Article 31 provides that the offence is constituted even when the facts reported are true. A law under which a true statement is punishable when the state dislikes its effects is not one law for the powerful and the powerless; it is a law whose content is the speaker's relationship to power (ordinance text as reproduced by Sahel Horizon, September 2026; State Media Monitor country file, 2025).
  • The amnesty side of the ledger completes the asymmetry: the February 2025 national conference recommended amnesty for the participants in the 2023 coup — the men who seized power by force — while the men they overthrew face military courts, denationalization, and indefinite detention without trial (ARIES Consult strategic intelligence report, April 2026, summarizing the conference recommendations; Human Rights Watch, 2026).

Why the grade lands here: Test 1 does not ask whether his laws are harsh; it asks whether there is one of them. The documented record shows two tracks operating side by side for three years: a general law for the country, and a personalized law — denationalization by decree, military courts for civilians, detention without trial, prison for true statements — reserved for the ousted government, its advisers, and the regime's critics. The April 2025 releases are genuine FOR-shelf evidence and are recorded; a release by grace, of people held without due process in the first place, does not make the track that held them one law. Helped: the ~50 detainees released in April 2025 and their families; the coup participants covered by the recommended amnesty; economic actors who kept a single monetary and fiscal framework (WAEMU membership, the continued IMF program). Hurt: the 25 people provisionally stripped of their nationality (several holding only Nigerien passports, and so exposed to statelessness — Human Rights Watch, September 2026); the at-least-30 arrested officials of the ousted government; Mohamed and Hadiza Bazoum, detained without trial since July 2023; every speaker in Niger who now writes under a law that punishes true statements.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The monetary referee was not gripped. Niger stayed in the West African CFA franc zone and the BCEAO system throughout the confrontation with ECOWAS — including through the sanctions period, when the central bank's freezing of Nigerien state assets was ECOWAS's instrument, not Niamey's — and the government continued to submit to IMF program reviews, accepting published staff appraisals of its own economy (IMF, January and July 2025). A ruler determined to hold every referee could have reached for the currency; on the documented record, he did not.
  • A consultative institution with broad social representation exists and sits for the full refoundation period: the CCR's 194 members, named in May 2025 and installed on 28 June 2025, include civilians and military officers drawn from the regions, traditional chiefs, trade unions, professional bodies, women, young people, and the diaspora (International IDEA country tracker, June 2025; electos.net country file, 2026).
  • The courts still function as courts in ordinary life, and one judicial act in the flagship case was at least routed through a courtroom: Bazoum's immunity was lifted by the State Court in June 2024 rather than by decree — the form of judicial process was used, even where monitors found its substance wanting (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Every elected referee was abolished, not merely gripped. The constitution was suspended on the night of the coup; the National Assembly and local councils were dissolved; in March 2025 every political party in the country was dissolved by decree; and the body that replaced parliament, the CCR, is appointed by the president, sits at his discretion, and "holds no legislative power" — the president "makes law by ordinance" (electos.net country file, November 2026; International IDEA, June 2025). There is no electoral commission function to grade: no elections have been held at any level since the coup, and none are scheduled.
  • The media referee was replaced by the executive's own instrument. The constitutional regulator, the Conseil Supérieur de la Communication, was superseded by an Observatoire National de la Communication created by presidential ordinance on 1 September 2025 under the charter (State Media Monitor country file, 2025). The independent press house, the Maison de la Presse, was suspended by the interior minister in January 2024 and placed under a management committee headed by the Interior Ministry's own secretary general (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2025). RFI, France 24, and later the BBC were banned from broadcasting (press reporting compiled by Burkina Times, November 2026).
  • The justice system's own organizations were dissolved by the executive. On 7 August 2025 the interior minister signed decrees dissolving four major justice-sector unions, including the Autonomous Union of Magistrates — the judges' own union (International IDEA country tracker, August 2025). In May 2024 the justice minister had already suspended all visits by human rights organizations to Nigerien prisons "until further notice" (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2025).
  • The external referees were exited, one after another. Niger's withdrawal from ECOWAS — announced 28 January 2024, effective 29 January 2025 — removed its citizens' access to the ECOWAS Court of Justice, the very court that had ruled on 15 December 2023 that Bazoum's detention was unlawful and ordered his release; the ruling was never complied with (ECOWAS Court ruling as reported by Human Rights Watch and the European Parliament, 2023–2026). On 22 September 2025 Niger, Mali, and Burkina Faso jointly announced withdrawal from the International Criminal Court (International IDEA, September 2025). The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found the detentions of Bazoum (February 2025) and of human rights defender Moussa Tiangari (May 2026) arbitrary and called for release; both men remain detained (Human Rights Watch, 23 July 2026).
  • The press's working conditions measure the grip in numbers: at least 13 journalists were arrested in the year to November 2025 under the revised cybercrime law; in October–November 2025 six journalists were arrested for circulating a public invitation to a government press briefing, three of whom spent nearly eight months in prison; journalist Serge Mathurin Adou was held 846 days before his release on 11 November 2026; Niger fell from 83rd to 120th in RSF's World Press Freedom Index in a single year, its worst position ever (International IDEA, November 2025; Sahel Horizon, September 2026; Burkina Times, November 2026). In November 2025 the Interior Ministry suspended hundreds of NGOs, authorizing only around 100 of more than 4,000 registered organizations to continue operating (International IDEA country tracker, November 2025; CIVICUS).

Why the grade lands here: the test is not whether institutions still exist with their names on the door — it is whether they can say no to the seat. On the documented record, every body that could check the presidency has been dissolved (parliament, parties, magistrates' union, press house), replaced by an executive-created substitute (the ONC, the appointed CCR), exited (the ECOWAS Court, the ICC), or chilled into self-censorship (the press). The one genuine FOR item — the untouched currency union and the continued IMF program — concerns referees outside his jurisdiction, and it is recorded; it does not offset a domestic board on which no referee remains his to lose to. Helped: the CNSP's freedom of action; the appointed members of the CCR and ONC, who hold seats that exist at the president's pleasure. Hurt: Nigerien voters, who have had no election at any level since 2023 and no parties to vote for; magistrates and justice-sector workers whose unions were dissolved; journalists — at least 13 arrested in a year, one held 846 days; the hundreds of suspended NGOs and the communities they served; every Nigerien who lost the ECOWAS Court as a place to be heard.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The first and largest wobble — ECOWAS's threat of military intervention in August 2023 — was met with engagement as well as defiance. Tchiani received the ECOWAS delegation in Niamey on 19 August 2023, stated on television that "neither the National Council for the Safeguard of the Homeland nor the people of Niger want war, and remain open to dialogue," and put a transition proposal on the table rather than refusing to discuss one (Al Jazeera, 20 August 2023). The intervention never came; the sanctions that replaced it were lifted by ECOWAS in February 2024 without Bazoum's restoration — the standoff ended in negotiation space, not in the war either side had threatened (Benin–Niger crisis record, Wikipedia compilation of contemporaneous reporting).
  • The American wobble was answered with an orderly process rather than a rupture. After Niamey declared the US military presence illegal in March 2024, the two governments negotiated a withdrawal agreement; US forces handed over Air Base 101 in July 2024 and Air Base 201 in Agadez on 5 August 2024, completing the departure by mid-September 2024 under a joint statement with Niger's defence ministry — a pushback absorbed through procedure (US Department of Defense joint statement, August 2024; contemporaneous reporting).
  • The regional break was later reopened by his own side. Niger participated in the first AES–ECOWAS ministerial meeting in Bamako on 22 May 2025, and in the Dakar ministerial meeting of 17 October 2026, where the two blocs agreed to establish joint working groups toward a framework cooperation agreement; in June 2026 Niger and Benin set up expert committees to examine reopening their common border — the border whose closure had strangled Nigerien trade since 2023 (ECOWAS–AES reporting, October 2026; African Development Bank assessment, June 2026). His foreign minister told diaspora audiences in New York on 27 September 2026 that the ECOWAS exit itself is "irreversible" while insisting the AES wants a legally framed partnership — a stated position, openly held, with the negotiation door left open beside it (African Press Agency via The Boston Report, October 2026).
  • A domestic wobble produced a documented adjustment: the February 2025 national conference recommended releasing the coup-era detainees, and on 1 April 2025 around 50 of them were in fact released, including senior figures of the ousted government (France 24/AFP, April 2025). When the conference's framework was adopted, the transition's length was set through that process and announced publicly, not smuggled (BBC, 26 March 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The courts' pushback was refused outright, at every level. The ECOWAS Court ordered Bazoum's release and reinstatement on 15 December 2023; Niger did not comply, and within six weeks had announced its withdrawal from the organization whose court had ruled. The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention's opinions (February 2025; May 2026) and the European Parliament's resolution of 12 March 2026 — passed 524 votes to 2 — met the same answer: no release, no trial, no stated legal path (European Parliament resolution RC-B10-0159/2026; Human Rights Watch, July 2026).
  • When the seat itself was struck, the first public answer was an accusation without evidence. On the night of 28–29 January 2026, Islamic State Sahel Province gunmen assaulted Niamey's international airport and Air Base 101 — the first major jihadist attack on the capital. Within hours, Tchiani publicly accused the presidents of France, Benin, and Côte d'Ivoire of having "sponsored" the attack; both governments rejected the accusation, and on 30 January the Islamic State claimed the assault through its Amaq agency. International Crisis Group records the accusation and the rejections; no evidence for the sponsorship claim has been produced on the record assembled here, and the claim is recorded as an ALLEGATION made by the seat, contradicted by the attackers' own claim of responsibility (International Crisis Group, January 2026; FDD Long War Journal, February 2026).
  • The August 2026 wobble came from inside his own army, and the documented response was force and silence about causes. On 28–29 August 2026, special-forces soldiers mutinied at Air Base 101, attacked the state broadcaster and the presidential palace, and were put down by the presidential guard with direct assistance from Russia's Africa Corps; state television broadcast images of arrests, media reported at least 27 mutineers killed — a figure the government has not confirmed with an exact toll of its own — and Crisis Group's account records the mutineers' grievances as service conditions and battlefield setbacks, with no public government accounting of those grievances in the record assembled here (International Crisis Group Q&A, 8 October 2026). Whether the incident began as a mutiny or as a coup attempt remains, on Crisis Group's assessment, unclear, and is recorded as contested.
  • On the other option, specifically: when another option became available, the record is split, and Test 3 grades the split honestly. He did look at some options in the open — the ECOWAS delegation was received, the American departure was negotiated, the AES–ECOWAS working groups were joined, the Benin border file was reopened. But on the single option that every court, mediator, and parliament kept placing in front of him — putting Mohamed Bazoum before a judge, or releasing him — he has refused to look for three years: no trial date, no charge tested, no stated reason beyond the accusation itself. Considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to bring the man to any forum at all is the refusal the rule names.
  • The no-good-choice file is recorded, with its origin. In August 2023, facing ECOWAS's ultimatum, the options actually on the table each carried real harm: restore Bazoum under threat of invasion (ending the CNSP and exposing its members to the treason charges they now hold over him), or defy the bloc and absorb sanctions on a landlocked country that imports its food, fuel, and electricity through its neighbours. The sanctions that followed were, on the WFP's record, severe: borders closed, some USD 1.2 billion in external aid undisbursed, liquidity frozen at the regional central bank, a 25 kg bag of rice nearly doubling in price within weeks (WFP Niger Annual Country Report 2023; contemporaneous price reporting). The constraint is recorded, not counted as a demerit. What remains gradable, and is graded in Tests 1, 2, and 5, is the origin: the trap existed because the seat had been seized by force nine days before the ultimatum — and the least-harm doors inside it (the negotiated American departure, the later ECOWAS working groups) show the seat could find them when it chose to.

Why the grade lands here: this is the one test where his record genuinely runs in both directions at once. Against foreign and regional pushback he has repeatedly adjusted in the open — negotiated withdrawals, working groups, a border file reopened, detainees released on a conference's recommendation. Against legal and internal pushback he has not adjusted at all: court orders refused without a counter-process, an evidence-free accusation against three heads of state after the capital was attacked, and an army mutiny answered with arrests rather than any public reckoning with its stated causes. The grade is MIXED because both halves are documented; it is not higher because the refusals cluster exactly where the wobble touches his own hold on the seat. Helped: Nigerien traders and consumers, as the Benin border file and the ECOWAS framework talks reopened (committees formed June–October 2026; normalization incomplete at grading); the ~50 detainees released in April 2025; both Niger and the United States, whose forces left on an agreed timetable without incident. Hurt: Mohamed and Hadiza Bazoum, for whom every forum's pushback has changed nothing; the presidents of France, Benin, and Côte d'Ivoire, accused by name of sponsoring a jihadist attack on evidence never produced; the mutineers of August 2026 and their families — at least 27 reported killed, dozens arrested, no public accounting; Nigerien consumers who bore the sanctions period's prices while the standoff ran.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the regions, the minorities, the displaced, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The displaced of other nations are counted in. Niger hosts more than half a million refugees and asylum-seekers — some 327,500 of them from Nigeria before 2026, with roughly 64,000 more arriving during 2026 — and UNHCR's representative publicly praised Nigerien authorities and host communities for "remarkable solidarity," including making land available for temporary shelter (UN News, November 2026). A state that counted only its own would not be carrying this, and the shelf records it.
  • Recognition was extended where the old order had not put it: the 2025 Charter of the Refoundation made Hausa — the language of the country's largest community — the national language, with French and English as working languages (Charter of the Refoundation, Article 12, as reported by Britannica, 2025).
  • The growth rebound, when it came, was broad in its drivers: GDP grew an estimated 10.3–10.6% in 2024 on the start of crude-oil exports and an exceptional agricultural harvest — the harvest being income in the hands of the rural majority — after growth of roughly 2% in 2023 under sanctions; the IMF projected continued growth of 6.6% for 2025 (IMF, January and July 2025; World Bank Niger Economic Update, 12 June 2025). After the catastrophic floods of June–October 2024 — at least 339 killed and 1.1 million displaced on contemporaneous counts — the government instituted relief measures cutting prices of essential commodities and services (contemporaneous reporting compiled by The Free Library, 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The western regions are not being protected, and the toll is the coup's own founding promise read back. ACLED recorded 1,939 conflict deaths in Niger in 2025, roughly 1,300 of them in the Tillabéri region alone, split about evenly between clashes and direct attacks on civilians (ACLED data via AFP, March 2026). The counts are kept separate, as the method requires: Human Rights Watch documented, separately, more than 127 villagers and worshippers summarily executed by Islamic State Sahel Province in five attacks between March and June 2025 — over 70 of them in a single mosque at Manda on 21 June 2025 (Human Rights Watch, 10 September 2025); HRW separately records ACLED's estimate of about 1,600 civilians killed by IS Sahel since the coup; and in February 2026 HRW reported 31 men and boys executed by IS fighters at Bossieye village (HRW testimony to the US House Tom Lantos Commission, May 2026). In November 2026 an ambush on market-goers near Téra killed at least 37 people, the attackers unidentified at reporting (AFP via The Hindu, 13 November 2026).
  • The state's own force has killed the people it counts as its own. In January 2026 a Nigerien military drone strike on a crowded market at Kokoloko village in Tillabéri killed at least 17 civilians, including children; Human Rights Watch called for an investigation and compensation, and no completed public accounting appears in the record assembled here (Human Rights Watch, 9 February 2026).
  • The humanitarian ledger widened on his watch. Forcibly displaced people inside Niger numbered 984,366 at the end of 2025, up about 12% year on year; more than 548,000 people were internally displaced by July 2026, about 20% more than a year earlier, with over 289,000 newly displaced in January–August 2026 alone, twice the same period of 2025 (OCHA Niger, 2025–2026; ACAPS, 16 September 2026). UNICEF counts 3.9 million people — 2.6 million of them children — requiring urgent assistance in 2026, with 2.4 million projected to face severe food insecurity and 1,097 schools non-functional, affecting some 93,676 children (UNICEF Humanitarian Action for Children, Niger 2026; Cadre Harmonisé, December 2025). In November 2025 his own Interior Ministry suspended hundreds of the NGOs that deliver that assistance, leaving around 100 of 4,000-plus authorized (International IDEA, November 2025) — a counting-out performed by the seat itself, against the counted.
  • Some Nigeriens were counted out of the nation altogether: the 25 denationalized exiles of Test 1, and the criminalization of consensual same-sex relations enacted under the refoundation, after which community health workers suspended outreach for fear of harassment (Human Rights Watch, 23 July 2026). Protesters and critics are counted as security files: at least 30 ousted officials arrested, journalists detained for sharing a press invitation, a prominent human rights defender — Moussa Tiangari, detained since December 2024 on charges HRW records as potentially capital — held under a detention the UN Working Group found arbitrary in May 2026 (Human Rights Watch, July 2026).

Why the grade lands here: a ruler counts everyone when the protection and the provision reach the people with the least power to demand them. Here the ledger runs the other way at scale: the region whose insecurity justified the coup is measurably less safe (ACLED's 2025 count), the displaced population grows by double digits year on year, the state's own drone killed at least 17 of its own market-goers without a public accounting, the aid organizations serving the hungry were suspended by the seat's own ministry, and a class of citizens was struck from the nation's roll by decree. The refugee solidarity and the 2024 rebound are real and are recorded on the FOR shelf; they do not reach the people this test is about. Helped: the more than half a million refugees hosted and given land for shelter; oil-sector and harvest beneficiaries of the 2024 rebound; Hausa speakers, whose language the charter raised to national status. Hurt: the people of Tillabéri, Diffa, Tahoua, and increasingly Dosso — roughly 1,300 dead in Tillabéri in 2025 on ACLED's count, 984,366 forcibly displaced nationwide by the end of that year; the at-least-17 civilians killed by the state's drone at Kokoloko market; the 3.9 million people UNICEF counts as needing urgent assistance in 2026, whose aid pipeline the November 2025 NGO suspensions narrowed; the 25 denationalized; LGBT Nigeriens, newly criminalized; detained defenders and officials.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 26 July 2023, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The sovereignty program — delivered, item by item, on the schedule he announced. Whatever else is graded elsewhere, the program Tchiani proclaimed from July 2023 was executed: French forces, ordered out after the coup, completed their withdrawal on 22 December 2023; the French ambassador was expelled and the embassy closed; US forces completed their withdrawal by mid-September 2024 after the presence was declared illegal in March 2024; the ECOWAS withdrawal, announced jointly on 28 January 2024, took effect exactly one year later on 29 January 2025, and the AES confederation was built on the announced timetable — mutual-defence charter September 2023, confederation treaty signed at Niamey on 6 July 2024, confederal parliament of 45 deputies installed at Niamey on 24 August 2026 (contemporaneous reporting; UK Parliament written evidence, September 2026). Under the mandate rule, carrying out an announced platform is FOR-shelf evidence here; the platform's costs are graded in Tests 1, 2, and 4, not re-graded as word-breaking.
  • The uranium word — kept, at a price recorded in the ledger below. He said Niger would dispose of its own resources; Orano's Imouraren permit was withdrawn in June 2024, its operational control of Somaïr ended in December 2024, Somaïr was nationalized in June 2025, and in November 2025 — after an ICSID tribunal had ordered Niger not to sell or transfer Somaïr's output — he announced on state television that Niger was putting its production on the international market "to whoever wishes to buy it," and a shipment left the site; a sale of 1,000 tons of yellowcake to Russia's Rosatom was reported by Le Monde, and draft decrees of May 2026 annulled the 1968 Arlit concession and created a successor company, TSUMCO SA (World Nuclear Association sequence via WISE Uranium, updated November 2026; Ecofin Agency, December 2025). Orano's characterization of the shipment as illegal under the ICSID order is recorded as the disputing party's claim; the tribunal's interim order itself is a matter of record.
  • The dialogue promise — eventually, partially, performed. He promised a national dialogue to found the refoundation; regional consultations were held, a national conference sat 15–19 February 2025, its recommendations (including the detainee releases) were substantially implemented, and the charter it produced was promulgated publicly on 26 March 2025 (International IDEA; France 24/AFP, 2025).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The central promise — broken in its own words. On 19 August 2023, after meeting the ECOWAS delegation, Tchiani stated on national television: "Our ambition is not to confiscate power," and announced that he had ordered proposals to "define the transition period, the duration of which cannot go beyond three years." Three years from the coup expired on 26 July 2026. By that date there had been no election at any level, no date for one, and Tchiani had instead been sworn in — sixteen months earlier — as president for a five-year refoundation period running to 2030, which the charter describes as flexible and renewable, with the national conference further recommending that the transition's own leaders be permitted to stand in whatever elections eventually end it (Al Jazeera and contemporaneous transcripts, August 2023; BBC, 26 March 2025; electos.net country file, 2026). The 30-day deadline he set in the same speech for the dialogue's proposals also passed unmet: the national conference sat in February 2025, eighteen months later.
  • The democracy he described — inverted. In the same August 2023 address he defined the goal himself: "a transparent system in which the opponents are not imprisoned or exiled, in which real transfer of power is possible and in which civil society can freely express themselves and demonstrate, without being worried." By November 2026 the record shows opponents imprisoned (Bazoum, Tiangari, at least 30 ousted officials), opponents exiled and then denationalized (25 people), all political parties dissolved, civil-society organizations suspended by the hundreds, and journalists imprisoned for circulating a press invitation (transcript via Aïr Info as republished, August 2023; Human Rights Watch, 2024–2026; International IDEA, 2025). Measured against other rulers' standards this would be argument; measured against his own sentence, it is a broken word in his own terms.
  • The security promise — the coup's stated reason, unmet on the monitors' counts. The CNSP justified the seizure of power by the deteriorating security situation and the ousted government's failures. Three years on: ACLED's 1,939 conflict deaths in 2025 with Tillabéri the deadliest civilian region in the central Sahel; the capital's airport attacked by jihadists twice in 2026 (28–29 January, claimed by Islamic State; and in June, attributed in regional reporting to JNIM); and his own special forces in mutiny in August 2026, with Crisis Group recording battlefield setbacks among their stated grievances (ACLED via AFP, March 2026; International Crisis Group, January and October 2026). The promise is graded on the outcome he himself chose as the standard: the country is, on the documented counts, less secure than the one he seized.
  • The reconciliation word — half-kept, and the unkept half is the named one. He spoke at his March 2025 inauguration of "forgiveness and reconciliation," and the April 2025 releases performed part of it. But the reconciliation never reached the one man whose case every mediator raised — Bazoum remains detained without trial — and the eighteen months after the releases brought fresh denationalizations (June and September 2026) rather than further releases (France 24/AFP, April 2025; Human Rights Watch, September 2026).

The two axes, printed independently: word-keeping and outcome are graded separately, and all four crossings are named where they occur. (1) Kept his word and it helped: the French and American departures and the ECOWAS exit were delivered as announced and were, on the documented domestic record, popular with the constituencies that had demanded them; the detainee releases matched the conference's word and freed some 50 people. (2) Kept his word and it still hurt: the uranium program was executed exactly as proclaimed — and the ICSID-defying sales left the nationalized mine, on 2026 reporting, largely unable to monetize its output, with buyers deterred by legal exposure; the sovereignty was kept and the revenue was not (Torch Media Africa analysis, 2026; The Africa Report reporting as summarized therein). The ECOWAS exit, likewise kept to the day, is the same act that removed his own citizens' access to the ECOWAS Court — the keeping earns credit here; the hurt stands in Tests 2 and 4 in full. (3) Went against his word and it helped: not documented on the record assembled here — no instance was found in which a broken commitment of his produced a documented benefit; the file records the absence rather than inventing one. (4) Broke his word and it hurt: the three-year, not-confiscating-power promise, broken into a five-year renewable self-presidency — the hurt is the electorate's: no vote cast at any level since 2023, no parties left to cast one for.

Why the grade lands here: a word is graded against the record the speaker himself set. Tchiani's sovereignty word was kept so exactly that it is the strongest FOR shelf in this dossier. But the promise that legitimized the seizure of power to the region and to his own people — we are not confiscating power; three years at most; opponents will not be imprisoned or exiled — has been broken on every clause, in writing, by his own decrees, and the security failure that was his stated reason for taking power has deepened on every monitor's count. A ruler who keeps the promises that enlarge him and breaks the ones that limit him has not kept his word over time; he has kept his program. The grade is FAILS. Helped: the constituencies for sovereignty, who got the departures, the exit, and the nationalization they were promised, on schedule; the ~50 released detainees; Russia's Africa Corps and the AES partners, whose position in Niamey his kept word built. Hurt: Niger's voters, whose three-year wait became a five-year renewable term with no election date; Mohamed Bazoum, whose promised prosecution has never become a trial; Somaïr's workforce and the treasury, holding a nationalized mine whose output the market fears to buy; the soldiers and civilians of Tillabéri, for whom the security promise was the whole bargain.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Abdourahamane Tchiani is graded on three years and four months: twenty months ruling as head of a junta that had promised the region it was not confiscating power, then — from 26 March 2025 — as president under a charter his own conference wrote, for five flexible, renewable years, with every political party dissolved and the elected president he overthrew still sitting in the palace, untried, as the fourth year begins. Test 1 fails because the law visibly runs on two tracks: a general law for the country, and a personalized one — denationalization decrees aimed exclusively at the ousted circle and its critics (25 people by the state agency's own count), military courts for civilian officials, prison terms for true statements — while amnesty was recommended for the men who seized power. The April 2025 release of some 50 detainees is the genuine counterweight and is recorded; grace is not one law. Test 2 fails because no referee survives him: parliament, parties, the judges' union, the press house, and the media regulator were dissolved or replaced by executive instruments; the regional court that ordered Bazoum freed was exited; the ICC withdrawal was announced; the press fell 37 places in a year. The untouched currency union and the continuing IMF program are recorded, and they concern referees outside his reach. Test 3 is MIXED, the dossier's one split grade: he negotiated the American departure, joined the AES–ECOWAS working groups, reopened the Benin border file, and released detainees on his conference's recommendation — and he refused every court's order on Bazoum without offering any forum of his own, answered the first jihadist attack on his capital by accusing three foreign presidents of sponsoring it on evidence never produced, and met his own army's mutiny with arrests and no public accounting. The August 2023 sanctions standoff is recorded under the no-good-choice clause — every door then open carried real harm — with its origin, the seizure itself, graded where it belongs. Test 4 fails on the counts: ACLED's 1,939 conflict deaths in 2025, roughly 1,300 in Tillabéri; 984,366 forcibly displaced by that year's end; at least 17 civilians killed by the state's own drone at Kokoloko market; 3.9 million people needing urgent assistance in 2026 while his ministry suspended hundreds of the NGOs that feed them. The half-million refugees Niger shelters, praised by UNHCR, stand on the FOR shelf and do not move the grade. Test 5 fails on his own sentences: "not to confiscate power," "cannot go beyond three years," "opponents are not imprisoned or exiled" — each broken by his own decrees, while the sovereignty program he proclaimed was kept to the day and to the item, which is precisely the pattern the test exists to catch: the word held where it enlarged the seat, and broke where it limited it. The crossings, stated once more in brief: he kept his word and it helped (the departures, the exit, the releases); he kept his word and it hurt (the uranium defiance that stranded the mine's revenue; the exit that closed his citizens' court); he broke his word and it helped — not documented, and the absence is recorded rather than filled; he broke his word and it hurt (the transition promise, and the security promise under which Tillabéri's toll doubled down). The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether Bazoum is ever brought before a judge or released; whether any election date is set inside the five-year charter; whether the denationalization decrees are revoked or extended; whether the Benin border and the ECOWAS framework talks conclude; and whether Tillabéri's count falls.
