The Ruler's Test — Sudan (a divided seat): Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, Chairman of the Transitional Sovereignty Council and Commander-in-Chief of the Sudanese Armed Forces

Subject: Sudan has two seats of power and a war between them, and this dossier grades the division as it actually stands in November 2026 rather than pretending otherwise. The recognised seat — graded here as the primary subject — is held by Lieutenant-General Abdel Fattah al-Burhan: commander-in-chief of the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF), chairman of the Transitional Sovereignty Council, and head of the government the United Nations and most states recognise as Sudan's. His government fled Khartoum in April 2023, governed from Port Sudan for most of the war, and formally returned to the capital in January 2026 after the army retook it; his prime minister since 31 May 2025 is Kamil Idris, a civilian former UN official whom Burhan appointed and whose cabinet formation Burhan personally overruled (below). The SAF holds the north, the east, the centre and the capital — the larger share of Sudan's roughly fifty million people — and since early November 2026 has been advancing westward through Kordofan toward the RSF's zone (Critical Threats Project, Africa File, 19 November 2026). The counter-seat is held by Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, "Hemedti," commander of the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) — a paramilitary built by the Bashir regime out of the Janjaweed militias of the 2003 Darfur war and made, by a 2017 law of that regime, a force answerable to the presidency rather than to the army. The RSF holds almost all of Darfur and most of West Kordofan, and since 2025 has governed its zone through a parallel administration, the "Government of Peace and Unity" of the Sudan Founding Alliance (Tasees), proclaimed from a charter signed in Nairobi on 23 February 2025, seated in Nyala, with Hemedti as president of its Presidential Council, the SPLM-N leader Abdelaziz al-Hilu as his deputy, and Mohammed Hassan al-Ta'ishi as its prime minister. No state recognises it. Like the Libya dossier, this one grades the recognised seat and prints the counter-seat's record at full strength inside every test, graded in the open. Three corrections to the brief's framing are recorded. First, the government is no longer in Port Sudan: it returned to Khartoum in January 2026, though Port Sudan remains the seat of the courts that have twice sentenced Hemedti to death in absentia in 2026 (below). Second, the division is not two clean halves: a third armed party, al-Hilu's SPLM-N, holds territory in South Kordofan and Blue Nile in alliance with the RSF's coalition, and the front in Kordofan is moving, not frozen. Third — and it shapes every test — both seat-holders carry formal determinations by the United States: on 7 January 2025 the US determined that the RSF and its allied militias committed genocide in Darfur and sanctioned Hemedti personally; on 24 April 2025 the US formally determined that the Government of Sudan — Burhan's — used chemical weapons in 2024, and sanctioned his government in two rounds (June 2025 and July 2026). Burhan himself has been under US sanctions since January 2025. The war began on 15 April 2023, out of the dispute over folding the RSF into the army under the December 2022 Framework Agreement both men had signed; which side fired the first shot that morning remains CONTESTED — the SAF states the RSF attacked its positions at Merowe and in Khartoum, the RSF states it acted against an army preparing to destroy it — and the contest is recorded, not resolved, here. Burhan is graded on his whole tenure at the top of the Sudanese state, which did not begin with the war: he has chaired the Sovereignty Council since August 2019, led the October 2021 coup jointly with Hemedti, and signed — in 2019, in July 2022 and in December 2022 — the three successive public commitments to hand power to civilians whose fate Test 5 grades. An older file hangs over the seat and is recorded as what it is: Burhan served as an army commander in Darfur during the 2003–2005 campaign, and Sudanese and international campaigners have long ACCUSED him of involvement in that campaign's atrocities against non-Arab civilians — an ALLEGATION he has never faced in any court and which this dossier does not grade, stated because the war he now fights is, in part, that war's continuation by other means. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that the seat passes, then the strongest evidence that it fails. Every test closes with a Why in plain words and a helped/hurt ledger naming who bore the acts on each shelf; a grade with no receipts means nothing, and no grade in this dossier is stated without them. The mandate rule applies: governing on the platform a ruler was sent to deliver is never itself a failure — carrying out a mandate is evidence on the for-shelf of Test 5 — and marks are lost only for how it is done: a second law for their own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not choose them (Test 4). It is recorded here that neither seat holds an electoral mandate: Burhan's claimed mandate is the 2019 Constitutional Declaration as amended — in February 2025, by himself — and the amendment is graded where it belongs, under Tests 2 and 5, not excused as platform. Test 3 also grades whether, when another option became available, the ruler at least considered it openly; considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence, and refusing to look at it is. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are separate axes and are printed independently — a kept word that hurt people earns its Test 5 credit and carries its hurt in the ledger in full, and a broken word that helped people is recorded the same way; neither launders the other. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, or none that appeared to be at the time, the dossier says so and grades it as the best possible choice available in the moment — naming the options that actually existed, why each carried harm, whether the ruler built the trap earlier, whether they were honest inside it, and whether they took the least-harm door actually open; hindsight options do not count as options refused. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Atrocity tolls are kept in separate sourced counts, with the disputants named; no toll in this dossier is merged with another. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The recognised state's courts did real judicial work against the counter-seat's leadership, in specific charges, through formal process. On 12 July 2026 the Anti-Terrorism and Crimes Against the State Court in Port Sudan convicted Hemedti, his brothers Abdel Rahim and Algoney Dagalo, and thirteen other RSF figures in absentia of genocide, crimes against humanity and war crimes for the 2023 El Geneina campaign and the killing of West Darfur's governor, Khamis Abakar, sentencing all sixteen to death by hanging and referring the verdicts to the Supreme Court's endorsement chamber for the mandatory review Sudanese law requires (Sudan War Monitor and Sudan Tribune court reporting, 12 July 2026). On 18 November 2026 the same court convicted Hemedti, his two brothers and eleven others in a second case — the looting and destruction of the National Medical Supplies Fund — ordering $396.2 million in joint compensation; that verdict is not yet final, and is recorded as such (Sudan Tribune via Anadolu, November 2026). The charges track documented events (Test 4); the forms — named articles of the 1991 Criminal Act, hearings held since April 2025, review reserved upward — were observed.
  • The army's command publicly submitted its own conduct to investigation at least once, at the moment it mattered most. When SAF and allied forces retook Wad Madani in January 2025 amid documented reprisal killings (against-shelf), Burhan ordered a committee of inquiry into the Camp Taiba killings and asked it to report within a week, and the SAF publicly condemned what it called "individual violations" by its own personnel (Burhan's order and SAF statement, 17 January 2025, via agencies).
  • Inside the recognised zone, one administration governs: courts sit, a civil service and central bank operate, and the government's writ — whatever its other failures — is a single published order rather than a patchwork of personal fiefs, which is more than the counter-seat offers its zone (below).

Evidence against:

  • The law's fire points one way. In three and a half years of war, the documented prosecutions from the recognised seat are of the enemy: the RSF's leadership twice condemned to death in absentia, RSF "collaborators" prosecuted in army-zone courts — while no SAF commander, and no commander of the SAF-allied Sudan Shield Forces or the al-Baraa ibn Malik Brigade, has faced any public process for the Gezira campaign documented on the shelf below. A law that reaches the other side's generals and never one's own is two laws wearing one robe.
  • The Gezira file is the centre of this test. After the SAF retook Wad Madani in January 2025, army units and allied militias conducted a months-long campaign against the Kanabi — non-Arab farming communities of Darfur origin settled in Gezira's irrigation camps — and against South Sudanese migrant workers, on the stated pretext of RSF collaboration. A joint CNN–Lighthouse Reports investigation (2026), built on hundreds of verified videos, satellite imagery, survivor testimony and SAF whistleblowers, documents ethnic targeting, mass killings, and bodies dumped in canals and mass graves across multiple camps, with attacks beginning in October 2024 and continuing for months after the city fell; its high-level sources state that the campaign's orders came from the highest ranks of the SAF and the Islamist networks around it — recorded here as an ALLEGATION as to command responsibility, untested in any court, and unanswered: the SAF and its intelligence service did not respond to the investigation's questions. Burhan's January 2025 committee of inquiry (for-shelf) was asked to report in a week; no public findings from it appear in the record assembled here, and no prosecutions followed in public. The Sudan Shield Forces deny targeting civilians by ethnicity and state their troops adhere to international humanitarian law; the denial is recorded.
  • The seat rewrote the constitution to protect itself from the law's oldest open file. The February 2025 amendments to the 2019 Constitutional Charter — enacted by Burhan's government, effective 23 February 2025 — dissolved the National Investigation Committee into the 3 June 2019 Khartoum sit-in massacre, in which security forces including the RSF killed more than a hundred pro-democracy protesters outside the army headquarters Burhan then helped command; the committee's report had never been published, its offices were raided and its documents seized after the October 2021 coup (per the committee's own account), and the amendment extinguished it entirely. The same amendments extended the transitional period by 39 months and, on the African Centre for Justice and Peace Studies' reading, granted the military an absolute majority across all three branches of government (ACJPS statement, 19 March 2025). Whatever the amendments' other purposes, one of their documented effects is that the single investigation into a massacre by the seat's own forces ceased to exist by the seat's own signature.
  • The counter-seat's record on this test is the floor of the series, and is printed at full strength. In RSF territory there is no law above the force: the El Geneina campaign of April–June 2023 (Test 4) was conducted by the RSF and its allied militias with no process of any kind; the November 2023 storming of the Ardamata garrison, where thousands of civilians had taken refuge, was followed by the massacre of more than a thousand of them; and after El Fasher the RSF's announced arrests of individual fighters — its leadership stated that men including the brigadier Al-Fateh Abdullah Idris, "Abu Lulu," filmed boasting of killing two thousand people, had been detained for investigation — have produced no public charge, trial or finding of any kind (RSF statements, October–November 2025; no proceedings documented since). The Tasees charter the RSF's coalition signed in Nairobi promises "a secular, democratic, decentralised state based on freedom, equality, and justice, without bias toward any cultural, ethnic, religious, or regional identity" (Sudan Founding Charter, 23 February 2025). In the territory that charter governs, the documented rule is the gun of the stronger, graded in Test 4.

Why: FAILS. The test asks whether the same rules reach the seat's friends and its enemies, and this seat's record answers in its structure: real courts, real charges and real process — aimed exclusively outward, at an enemy who is genuinely guilty of much of what he is charged with — while the seat's own documented ethnic campaign in Gezira drew a one-week committee whose findings never appeared, and the one investigation into the seat's own foundational massacre was dissolved by the seat's own constitutional amendment. Prosecuting a guilty enemy is not one law; it is the oldest second law there is. The counter-seat FAILS the same test without the furniture: it has no law to apply unevenly, only force.

Helped: The families of El Geneina's dead and of Governor Khamis Abakar, who hold two court verdicts naming the men accused of their killings — unenforceable while the condemned remain beyond reach, and recorded as verdicts of one belligerent's court, but the only judicial findings on those crimes any Sudanese institution has produced; Sudanese in the recognised zone, who live under one published legal order rather than competing armed sovereignties. Hurt: The Kanabi communities of Gezira and the South Sudanese farm workers killed, tortured and displaced in the 2024–2025 campaign by the seat's forces and allies, for whom no process has come from any direction; the families of the 3 June 2019 sit-in dead, whose investigation the seat dissolved by amendment; civilians in the army zone prosecuted as "collaborators" under wartime charges while the men who commanded the Gezira campaign face none — the asymmetry itself being the hurt this test measures; and, on the counter-seat's side of the ledger, every resident of RSF territory, for whom no law exists that the RSF itself must obey.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The seat appointed a civilian prime minister and let him govern in form. On 19 May 2025 Burhan named Kamil Idris — a former UN official and 2010 presidential candidate with no armed faction — prime minister, and Idris was sworn in on 31 May 2025 at the head of a cabinet Burhan had announced would be technocratic and civilian-led. The government under Idris ran the civil administration, returned to Khartoum in January 2026, restored services in the capital, and presented a budget; the central bank continued to operate a single currency and payroll across the recognised zone (Sovereignty Council decree, May 2025; government statements, January 2026).
  • The courts in the recognised zone visibly sit, hear evidence over months, and reserve their gravest verdicts for higher review (Test 1) — institutional forms that a pure military administration would not need and did not abolish.
  • The seat tolerated the documentation of its own side's crimes by Sudanese and international monitors operating in and reporting on its zone — the UN Fact-Finding Mission's and OHCHR's findings on SAF conduct, and the CNN–Lighthouse Gezira investigation, were compiled substantially from material gathered inside army-held territory and published without the seat suppressing them, whatever else it did (below).

Evidence against:

  • The constitution itself is now the grip. The February 2025 amendments (Test 1) extended the transition by 39 months from February 2025, removed the Constitutional Charter's references to the RSF and the civilian coalition, and — on the ACJPS reading of the enacted text — gave the military an absolute majority in the executive, legislative and judicial branches alike. The referee structure the test names — courts, prosecution, electoral machinery — sits, since those amendments, inside a document the seat wrote for itself, with its own tenure extended by its own hand. There has been no election of any kind in Sudan since 2015's predecessor regime polls, and none is scheduled inside the amended transition before 2028.
  • The civilian prime minister serves inside the grip, and the record shows the exact moment it closed. Idris attempted to form his cabinet on technocratic lines in 2025; Burhan overruled the formation and retained the armed-faction leaders Gibril Ibrahim (Justice and Equality Movement) and Minni Minnawi (SLM-MM) in their ministerial posts — coalition management by the commander, against his own prime minister's stated design (Horn Review Sudan Conflict Briefing, May 2026). Idris is the third head of government of Burhan's tenure arrangements since 2023 (after acting premiers Osman Hussein and Dafallah al-Haj Ali, the latter serving under three weeks), each appointed and removable by the same decree that made Idris. A referee who can be replaced by the player's signature is not holding the whistle; he is carrying it for him.
  • The judiciary that produced Test 1's verdicts is, structurally, a belligerent's court: an anti-terrorism and crimes-against-the-state bench sitting in the seat's wartime capital, trying the enemy's leadership in absentia on capital charges, while (Test 1) no equivalent process exists for the seat's own commanders and the one independent investigation into the seat's forces was dissolved by amendment. The forms are real; the autonomy is not demonstrated where it would cost the seat something.
  • Expression and civic space in the recognised zone are policed. The UN Fact-Finding Mission's report A War of Atrocities (presented to the Human Rights Council in 2025) documented arbitrary arrests, detention and torture by both parties, and Sudanese monitors — the Emergency Lawyers collective prominent among them — have documented through the war the army zone's detention of activists, aid workers and alleged "collaborators," including civilians held for social-media posts and for contact with relatives across the lines. Sudan's press, once the region's freest, operates in the recognised zone under wartime constraint, its Khartoum newsrooms destroyed or exiled in 2023 and its journalists working from Port Sudan and abroad under the security services' eye (UN Fact-Finding Mission, 2025; Emergency Lawyers reporting, 2023–2026).
  • The counter-seat grips totally, and its record is printed at full strength. The Government of Peace and Unity is headed by the RSF's commander; its deputy is a rebel ally; its prime minister is a former Sovereignty Council member serving under that command; the RSF's deputy commander, Abdel Rahim Dagalo, is simultaneously the family's Al Junaid gold company's head — the territory's war economy and its government in one family's hands. No court, commission, press or bank in RSF territory is documented to have acted against the RSF's interest at any point in the war. Where the recognised seat grips referees that exist, the counter-seat has never allowed referees to exist.

Why: FAILS. The test distinguishes a seat that holds the state from a seat that holds the referees, and Burhan's record since February 2025 is the deliberate conversion of the first into the second: tenure extended by his own amendment, the military written into a majority in all three branches by his own text, the investigation into his own forces' massacre dissolved by his own signature, and his own civilian prime minister overruled in public when the cabinet list displeased the coalition the commander manages. The furniture of a state — courts, a premier, a bank — stands and functions, and is printed on the for-shelf at full strength. But every piece of it is held at the seat's pleasure, and the seat has used that pleasure, on the record, exactly where its own accountability was concerned. The counter-seat FAILS the same test in its pure form: command, government and gold in one family, with no institution between the commander and the ruled.

Helped: Residents of the recognised zone who need a functioning state — litigants whose ordinary cases the courts still hear, employees and pensioners on the single payroll, patients and traders in a capital where ministries again sit; Prime Minister Idris's administration itself, which exists and governs day-to-day administration because the seat chose, in May 2025, to install a civilian face it could have refused to install. Hurt: Kamil Idris, overruled in his one documented attempt to choose his own cabinet — the hurt being to the office, and to every Sudanese told the appointment meant civilian government; the civilian political forces of 2019 — the Forces of Freedom and Change and the resistance committees — written out of the amended constitution and policed as suspected collaborators in the zone of the government that once governed beside them; journalists and activists detained in the army zone under wartime and collaboration charges; and, on the counter-seat's side, the entire population of RSF territory, who have no institution to appeal to that is not the force itself.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? When another option becomes available, does he at least consider it openly?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He has repeatedly entered processes rather than refusing every room. His representatives signed the Jeddah Declaration of Commitment to Protect the Civilians of Sudan on 11 May 2023, three weeks into the war, and the SAF attended the Jeddah talks through 2023; in October 2025, under international pressure after El Fasher's fall, Burhan yielded and sent representatives to indirect talks with the RSF in Washington — engagement by a seat that had spent two years insisting the RSF was not a party to talk to (EUAA country-of-origin reporting, 2026).
  • The clearest adjustment of his tenure was political and cost him something real: the February–May 2025 sequence in which he announced a civilian-led transitional government (10 February 2025) and then actually appointed a civilian prime minister with no faction of his own (Test 2) — an answer, however gripped, to three years of domestic and international pressure for civilian rule, delivered while the war was still being lost in the west.
  • On the 2026 truce text he engaged rather than walking away: through 2026 the army sought amendments to the US proposal for a 90-day humanitarian ceasefire — revised withdrawal sequencing and monitoring — instead of rejecting the file outright, and Burhan publicly welcomed President Trump's and Saudi Arabia's engagement in November 2025 even while rejecting the Quad's text (contemporaneous reporting, November 2025–2026). Seeking changes to an option is a form of considering it, and the record credits it as such.
  • On the chemical-weapons determination (Test 4), his government answered through the institutions that exist for the purpose: it formed a national technical committee in May 2025, circulated its interim report at the OPCW Executive Council (July 2026), and states that it proposed a joint mechanism and invited US experts to verify on the ground, which it says Washington did not answer. The committee found no evidence substantiating the allegations (Sudanese national committee interim report, July 2026; Sudan Tribune reporting). The answer's sufficiency is graded in Test 4; for this test, the answer was made in the open and through the referee's own building.

Evidence against:

  • The rooms he refused are as documented as the rooms he entered. In August 2024 the SAF boycotted the Geneva (ALPS) talks entirely — the RSF attended; the SAF stayed away, objecting to the format and to the UAE's presence — leaving a humanitarian process to proceed, and stall, without the recognised government. In January 2024 Sudan suspended its participation in IGAD, the regional bloc, after IGAD invited Hemedti to a summit — exiting the referee rather than answering it. And through the war his stated precondition for any ceasefire has been, in substance, the RSF's prior surrender: withdrawal from every city and home it occupies as a condition before talks, restated as late as January 2026, when he conditioned agreement to the Cairo-backed truce call on the RSF's effective surrender (EUAA, 2026). A precondition that the enemy first cease to exist as a force is not a negotiating position; it is the refusal of negotiation stated as one.
  • November 2025 is the test's heaviest single sequence, and it is printed whole. On 6 November 2025, days after El Fasher fell amid documented mass killing, the RSF announced its acceptance of the Quad's proposed three-month humanitarian truce; Burhan's Defence and Security Council answered the same day with a general mobilisation to "eliminate the rebel militias." On 23 November he rejected the Quad text as "the worst paper ever presented," on the stated grounds that it "cancels the presence of the armed forces, demands the dissolution of all security services, and keeps the rebel militia in its areas," and accused the UAE — a Quad member — of bias and of arming the RSF. On 24 November Hemedti announced a unilateral three-month humanitarian truce with a Quad- and AU-monitored field mechanism. The RSF's acceptance was itself compromised within weeks: it resumed drone strikes on army-held areas after announcing its agreement, a documented fact that materially supports Burhan's stated reason for distrusting the paper — and the clause analysis below grades the rejection by its mechanics, not by its optics. What is graded here, outside the clause, is the surrounding pattern: the seat's answer to the war's worst month was mobilisation, and its answer to the mediators was to accuse one of them of being the enemy's armourer.
  • The wobble from his own street has never been answered at all. Sudan's civilian anti-war forces — the coalition that became Somoud, successor to the Taqaddum alliance — offered from 2023 onward the option of a civilian political track that excluded both generals; the recognised seat's answer was to treat that centre as an RSF front, its leaders driven into exile and its members exposed to collaboration charges in the army zone (Test 2). The option was not considered and declined with reasons; it was criminalised unconsidered.

Why: MIXED — the weakest MIXED in this dossier, and the clause paragraph below is part of its earning. The seat demonstrably engages: it signs declarations, sends delegations, appoints the civilian it was pressed to appoint, seeks amendments to live proposals, and answers a chemical-weapons determination through the OPCW's own process. It also demonstrably refuses: it boycotted the one humanitarian table its enemy attended, walked out of its regional bloc for inviting that enemy, criminalised its own civilian centre, and has held for three years a precondition — the RSF's dissolution before any ceasefire — that no mediator has been able to move. Engagement without adjustment is this seat's signature: the wobble is answered with presence, paper and counter-conditions, and the position underneath them has not moved since April 2023. The counter-seat's record on this test is worse and differently shaped: the RSF accepts — Jeddah in 2023, the Quad truce in November 2025, a unilateral truce a day later — and then does not perform, resuming drone strikes after its own acceptance and converting every pause it is granted into position. Acceptance without performance is not answering the wobble either; it is using the answer as a weapon. The counter-seat FAILS this test.

Helped: Sudanese civilians in the windows the engagement bought — the Jeddah Declaration's brief aid openings of 2023, the indirect-talk channels kept alive into 2026, and the population of the capital, whose civilian administration (an adjustment under pressure) resumed services in 2026; the diplomatic process itself, which the seat's amendment-seeking kept from dying outright in 2026. Hurt: The population of El Fasher and Darfur in November 2025, for whom the truce that might have followed the city's fall was answered with mobilisation — graded with the clause below, and entered here because the hurt is real whatever the reasoning; Sudan's civilian political centre, exiled and criminalised rather than answered; aid-dependent Sudanese in both zones during the Geneva process of August 2024, which their recognised government declined to attend; and, on the counter-seat's side, every party — mediators, the SAF, and above all civilians in RSF areas — who organised around an RSF acceptance that its own drones then voided.

No-good-choice clause — applied, by its mechanics: The truce decision, November 2025. The options actually on the table were three, and each carried documented harm. (1) Accept the Quad text: a three-month truce leaving the RSF in its positions, with the Adré corridor open — the corridor through which, on the UN Fact-Finding Mission's September 2026 findings, a UAE-linked supply network was even then moving weapons to the RSF (the UAE denies arming the RSF; the network finding is the Mission's). The harm was not hypothetical: the RSF had accepted truces before and resumed operations — and did so again within weeks of this acceptance, resuming drone strikes on army-held areas. (2) Reject the text and fight on: the harm is the war's own ledger — famine conditions across Darfur and Kordofan (Test 4), the offensive and counter-offensive that have run since, and no relief window for El Fasher's survivors. (3) Engage and amend — the door the seat eventually took in 2026, seeking changes to the 90-day proposal; in November 2025 that door was open in principle and was not taken until later and under pressure. The trap's origin is graded honestly, because the clause requires it: the trap — a recognised army that cannot accept a truce without entrenching the force besieging its country, and cannot refuse one without starving that country — was built by both men. The two-army state was Bashir's construction; but the civilian framework that existed to dissolve it was destroyed by the October 2021 coup Burhan led with Hemedti beside him, and the December 2022 Framework Agreement that might still have settled the integration question was signed by Burhan and then left, by both signatories, to collapse into the April 2023 war — a war whose resort to force the clause does not protect, for either man: in April 2023 negotiated options were on the table and visible, so a hard choice was not a no-good choice. Inside the November 2025 trap, Burhan was honest about his reasoning in public and at length, and his stated reason — that the RSF would use the pause to rearm — was corroborated in part by the RSF's own conduct within weeks. His rejection of the November 2025 text is therefore graded as the best possible choice available to him in that moment, and is not held against him on this test. What remains graded, and what keeps this test at MIXED rather than higher, is everything around the moment: the Geneva chair left empty in 2024, the bloc walked out of, the civilian centre criminalised, and the least-harm door of November 2025 — amend, don't just refuse — taken a year late.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Including those who share their streets and the ones the count usually skips — the displaced, the detained, the foreign, the besieged, the hungry.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The recognised seat kept a state standing for the Sudanese inside its zone, and they voted with their feet toward it. Through the war, millions of the displaced fled to army-held territory — Port Sudan, the east, the north — rather than away from it, and the government there maintained the payroll, the ports and the aid corridor through which the World Food Programme reached more than four million people a month at its 2025 peak (WFP statements, 2025). In January 2026 the government returned to a destroyed capital and began restoring water, power, health and education services to the roughly 1.2 million residents who had already returned (UN return figures, 2025; government statements, January 2026). A seat that half a country runs toward is counting that half, and the shelf records it.
  • The seat's zone held the door for Darfur's aid when it finally opened it: the Adré crossing from Chad — the most direct route into Darfur — was authorised by the army-aligned authorities in August 2024 and aid did move through it thereafter, however obstructed (below), and the government's own humanitarian machinery continued to register and admit agencies across its zone through the war (UN OCHA access reporting, 2024–2026).

Evidence against:

  • The tolls are kept separate, as this dossier's method requires, and they are stated here once, in one place, because this test is where they belong. The war as a whole: US officials have estimated at least 150,000 dead since April 2023, and other analysts' estimates, counting indirect deaths from hunger and disease, run to roughly 400,000; both are estimates with wide uncertainty, and the UN's verified counts are far lower and known to be partial (US officials' estimates, 2024–2026; contemporaneous analysis). 2026 alone: the UN Human Rights Office counted 2,230 civilians killed between January and 17 November 2026, about three-quarters of them by drones — the weapon both sides now use most against the other's cities (OHCHR, November 2026). Displacement: some nine million people displaced inside Sudan and more than four million across its borders — the world's largest displacement crisis — with 33.7 million people, two-thirds of the country, in need of humanitarian assistance (UN OCHA, 2026 Humanitarian Needs and Response Plan). Hunger: the IPC's March–April 2026 analysis found 19.5 million people — 41 per cent of the population — in acute food insecurity at Phase 3 or above in February–May 2026, more than five million of them in Phase 4 Emergency and some 135,000 in Phase 5 Catastrophe, concentrated in North Darfur, South Darfur and South Kordofan, with fourteen areas at risk of famine and famine-level acute malnutrition detected in Um Baru and Kernoi in North Darfur (IPC Special Report, published 3 June 2026). Famine had already been formally confirmed in Zamzam camp in August 2024 and in further areas thereafter. Both seats govern halves of this; both seats' choices are inside every number on this shelf.
  • The chemical-weapons file. On 24 April 2025 the United States formally determined, under its Chemical and Biological Weapons Control and Warfare Elimination Act, that the Government of Sudan used chemical weapons in 2024, and imposed sanctions in June 2025 and a second round on 20 July 2026, stating that Sudan had not met the conditions — ending use and allowing international inspection — that could have averted them (US State Department determination and Federal Register notices, 2025–2026). A France 24 investigation (November 2025) reported strong evidence of chlorine use by the army in September 2024 near the al-Jaili refinery and the Garri base north of Khartoum. In September 2026 The New York Times and The Washington Post published separate investigations based on leaked Sudanese military documents, messages and recordings reporting a secret programme to manufacture, test and store chlorine munitions during 2024, including voice and text communications the Times reported as suggesting Burhan's direct knowledge — recorded here as an ALLEGATION as to his personal knowledge, drawn from leaked material reported by two newspapers, and denied by his government. Sudan's position is printed at full strength: the government denies any chemical-weapons use, its information minister called the accusations "political blackmail and a deliberate falsification of the facts," its national committee's interim report (July 2026) found no material, technical or documentary evidence substantiating the allegations in the locations examined, and its foreign ministry states it proposed joint verification and US expert access which Washington did not take up. The underlying US intelligence has not been published. What is not contested is the shape of the thing: a chemical weapon is a weapon that cannot count — it falls on a grid square, not a combatant — and the recognised seat of Sudan stands formally determined by a major power to have used one, with its answer, three years on, a committee of its own and access ungranted to the OPCW's inspectors. The determination is graded as a determination — a documented act of the US government — and the use itself as contested; the seat's refusal of the inspection that could settle the contest is graded as the seat's own documented act.
  • The Gezira campaign (Test 1) is also this test's evidence: the seat's forces and allies counted the Kanabi out of the country's protection by ethnicity — farming families of Darfur origin, resident in Gezira for generations, killed as presumptive enemies in a campaign the CNN–Lighthouse investigation documents as systematic — and counted South Sudanese migrant workers out with them, prompting South Sudan to summon Sudan's ambassador over its citizens' deaths (South Sudan Foreign Ministry, January 2025).
  • The seat administers hunger as a border. The army sealed the Adré crossing in February 2024 and reopened it in August 2024 under international pressure; UN agencies then complied with the army-aligned Humanitarian Aid Commission's directives because they recognised Port Sudan as the legitimate authority — and the Commission's bureaucratic impediments throttled the crossing to a fraction of need (38 trucks in the first eleven days after reopening, per aid reporting). In June 2024, army-aligned authorities stopped fifteen WFP trucks bound for Darfur — an incident documented in Reuters' reporting on WFP's internal probe. And on 28–29 October 2025, in the week El Fasher's survivors were fleeing a massacre, the seat's foreign ministry expelled the WFP's country director and its head of operations — persona non grata, seventy-two hours, no explanation given — from the country with the world's largest hunger crisis, an act the WFP said jeopardised operations supporting millions (WFP statement, 29 October 2025). The RSF's parallel record on aid — looted warehouses, detained convoys, the siege itself — is below; it does not move this shelf. A government that holds the pen over whether food crosses into the famine zone, and uses the pen this way, has drawn its circle at the front line.
  • The counter-seat's record on this test is the worst in this series, and it is printed at full strength, in its own counts. El Geneina, 2023: UN sanctions monitors estimated 10,000–15,000 people killed in that one city in April–June 2023 by the RSF and allied Arab militias in ethnically targeted violence against the Masalit — men separated at checkpoints and shot in the head when identified as Masalit, per the monitors' report to the Security Council (January 2024); Governor Khamis Abakar was killed on 14 June 2023 after being taken by RSF fighters; in November 2023 the RSF's storming of the Ardamata garrison was followed by the massacre of more than a thousand of the civilians sheltering there. The RSF denied organising the killings and characterised the violence as tribal conflict; the denial is recorded. Zamzam, April 2025: the RSF's assault on the famine-stricken displacement camp killed between 300 and 1,500 civilians, most of them women and children (UN Fact-Finding Mission, 2025). El Fasher, October 2025: after a siege of more than five hundred days that starved the city — a siege conducted against a population the IPC had already placed in catastrophe — the RSF's capture of the city on 26 October 2025 was followed by mass killing on a scale the counters state separately: the UN Human Rights Office documented more than 6,000 killed in the first days; the Yale Humanitarian Research Lab, from satellite imagery of body clusters, burn pits and disposal operations, estimates some 60,000 dead, within a stated range of 30,000–100,000; Darfur's governor Minni Minnawi, a SAF ally and an interested party, claimed 27,000 in the first days; the WHO reported some 460 patients and companions killed at the Saudi Maternity Hospital alone. RSF fighters filmed themselves executing civilians; the brigadier known as Abu Lulu boasted on camera of killing two thousand before losing count. On 19 February 2026 the UN Fact-Finding Mission reported that the RSF's campaign in and around El Fasher bore "the hallmarks of genocide": at least three underlying acts of genocide established, and genocidal intent — against the non-Arab Zaghawa and Fur above all — "the only reasonable inference" from the pattern (UN Fact-Finding Mission, Hallmarks of Genocide in El-Fasher, 19 February 2026). The United States had already determined, in January 2025, that the RSF committed genocide in Darfur. The RSF denies committing genocide or war crimes. Its system, on the Mission's findings, also ran on systematic sexual violence — rape, gang rape, sexual slavery — as a weapon of the campaign, and on the deliberate destruction of the means of survival: hospitals, markets, water systems, camps. This is the counter-seat's count: an ethnic subtraction, conducted by siege, bullet and hunger, against the peoples its own founding charter promises to protect without bias.

Why: FAILS — for the graded seat, with the distance to the counter-seat stated plainly, because the distance is the largest in this series and flattening it would be its own falsehood. Helped: The displaced who reached the recognised zone and lived — the millions sheltered in the east and north, the 1.2 million who returned to a capital with ministries again in it, the four-million-a-month the Port Sudan aid corridor fed at its peak; the residents of SAF-held cities, whose markets, hospitals and payrolls a functioning state kept open through the war. Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Promises made in the open against the record that followed — the dated word against the dated act.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

On 10 February 2025 Burhan announced that a civilian-led transitional government would be formed; on 19 May 2025 he appointed Kamil Idris, and the cabinet was sworn in on 31 May 2025. A civilian heads his government today. The keeping is documented; its thinning — the overruled cabinet, the amended constitution — is graded in Tests 2 and 5's against-shelf, because this dossier's axes are independent and a kept word is credited here even where its substance was cut elsewhere.
  • The state's continuity — the word underneath all the others, kept. From April 2023, Burhan's foundational public commitment was that the Sudanese state and its army would not fall. The state did not fall: the recognised government survived exile in Port Sudan, retook its capital, restored its ministries, kept its seat at the United Nations and its single currency and payroll. For the Sudanese who live under it, this is the promise that organises the rest, and it has not been broken.
  • The no-return word — kept to the letter. Burhan has stated, consistently since 2023, that the RSF will not return to power or partnership in Khartoum — that there is no going back to the arrangement of before 15 April 2023. He has kept it absolutely. What the keeping did is graded in the axes below; the keeping itself is this test's for-shelf, and it is the file's clearest crossing.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The 2019 word — the founding one. The Constitutional Declaration of August 2019, signed for the military by Hemedti and chaired into being by Burhan, set a 39-month transition to elected civilian rule, with the Sovereignty Council's chair passing from the military to a civilian after 21 months. The civilian chairmanship never came. On 25 October 2021 — weeks before the rotation fell due — Burhan dissolved the Sovereignty Council and the Hamdok government, arrested the ministers he governed beside, and declared a state of emergency, calling it not a coup but a correction. The African Union suspended Sudan; the promised elections of the transition were never held; and the man who chaired the transition became, by that act, its indefinite holder.
  • The November 2021 word. On 21 November 2021 Burhan signed a deal reinstating Hamdok, promising the release of political prisoners and a return to the constitutional track. Hamdok resigned on 2 January 2022, stating the agreement had failed to restore the transition; military rule continued uninterrupted. A word kept for six weeks, by the other signatory's own account of its collapse.
  • The July 2022 word — the plainest of the three. On 4 July 2022 Burhan announced that the military would withdraw from the political dialogue, that the Sovereignty Council would be dissolved once civilians agreed on a government, and that the army "will not stand in the way" of democratic transition. None of it was performed: no dissolution occurred, no civilian government outside his appointment was permitted to form, and he remained head of state — the office from which, nine months later, he went to war.

On 5 December 2022 Burhan and Hemedti signed the Framework Agreement, pledging a civilian-led transition and a single unified army; at the signing Burhan declared that soldiers belong in the barracks, that the army was committed to exit politics, and that the military recognised civilian political leadership. Four months later the two signatories were at war over the agreement's central question — the timetable for folding the RSF into the army — and every operative word in the document was dead. Both men broke this word; it is entered on both their files, and on Burhan's it completes a sequence — 2019, 2021, 2022, 2022 — in which the same promise was made four times and performed zero times.
