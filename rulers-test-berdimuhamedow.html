The Ruler's Test — Serdar Berdimuhamedow, President of Turkmenistan

Subject: Serdar Gurbangulyýewiç Berdimuhamedow — President of Turkmenistan since 19 March 2022. He succeeded his father, Gurbanguly Berdimuhamedow (president 2006–2022), in a snap election on 12 March 2022 in which the Central Election Commission reported 72.97% of the vote and 97.17% turnout. The seat is graded as it is actually held: since 21 January 2023 the father has held the constitutional title "National Leader of the Turkmen People" (Arkadag, "Protector") and chairs the Halk Maslahaty (People's Council), a body placed by the January 2023 constitutional laws above the unicameral Mejlis; the National Leader law grants him and his family immunity and inviolability of their property, and his initiatives are mandatory for consideration by state bodies. Serdar remains president and head of government in November 2026, and state media document him chairing the Cabinet and the State Security Council; they also document the father receiving foreign delegations, blessing flagship projects (the TAPI pipeline's Afghan section is named "Arkadagyň ak ýoly" — Arkadag's White Path), and setting strategic direction. Outside analysts dispute where final authority sits — a Carnegie Endowment analysis of October 2026 documents Serdar progressively removing his father's allies and relatives from posts, including the minister of national security, while projecting a united front in public — and that contest is itself part of the record graded here. Turkmenistan is among the most closed states in the world: no independent media operate inside it, the OSCE has never assessed any of its elections as free and fair, and much of the evidence below comes from exile-based outlets, UN bodies, and monitors working at the border of what can be documented. Where the record is structurally thin, the dossier says so rather than filling the gap. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Clemency is exercised at scale and on a regular calendar, and it is not confined to the connected: Serdar pardoned 736 convicted persons for Independence Day in September 2024, 276–308 for Neutrality Day in December 2024 (including six foreign citizens in the December 2023 round), 321–463 in successive Night of Power rounds in 2024–2025, and 247 in July 2025 for the International Year of Peace and Trust — several thousand releases across his tenure, drawn, per the reporting on the July 2025 round, mostly from people convicted of theft and traffic offences (state media decree reports, 2023–2025; Fergana News, July 2025).
  • Officials at the top of the security apparatus are not untouchable in form: in the largest personnel shake-up of his tenure, documented by the Carnegie Endowment in October 2026, Serdar dismissed the Minister of National Security — the country's top security official — with corruption inside the agency given as the stated ground, replaced the head of the Border Service, and removed relatives of his father from posts and property. The same analysis notes the Interior Ministry, headed by his own 2022 appointee, was untouched — the for-shelf fact and its limit sit in the same paragraph of the record.
  • The state's anti-corruption posture is stated on the record and routinized: cabinet sessions under both Berdimuhamedows regularly feature dismissals of officials for "shortcomings," and Turkmenistan participates in UN review processes (its fourth ICCPR review was held in March 2023) in which corruption and judicial independence were raised directly with its delegation (OHCHR meeting summary, March 2023).

Evidence against:

  • The law's source exempts itself, in writing. The Constitutional Law "On the National Leader of the Turkmen People," signed by Serdar on 21 January 2023, grants his father "and members of his family" the right of immunity and inviolability extending to their property, with state protection provided by the security services — a hereditary exemption from the law enacted by the son in favor of the family that includes the son (text of the law as published by Turkmen state media, 23 January 2023; Freedom House, Nations in Transit 2024). No comparable immunity exists for any citizen outside the family.
  • The succession itself was the first application of the law: Serdar rose through posts in his father's government (deputy foreign minister, deputy prime minister), was nominated by the ruling Democratic Party on 14 February 2022, and won a snap election held one month later in which the OSCE/ODIHR did not deploy an observation mission, citing Turkmenistan's failure to address previous recommendations on political pluralism and fundamental rights; no election in Turkmenistan has ever been assessed as free and fair (Congressional Research Service, 29 March 2022). The nine other candidates were not assessed by any observer body as genuine competitors.
  • The outcome measure is at the world's floor and flat: Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index scored Turkmenistan 19/100 in 2022, 18 in 2023, and 17 in both 2024 and 2025 (rank 165th, then 167th of 180) — the worst in Central Asia in every year of Serdar's tenure (Transparency International, CPI 2022–2025).
  • Everyday administration runs on payment, on the documented record: Freedom House's 2025 report records reports that citizens paid bribes to obtain a job, university admission, a new passport, "and even a pardon for jailed family members" — meaning the clemency on the for-shelf is itself reported to be purchasable at the retail level — and that public-sector officials and business managers in Balkan Province were compelled to buy wristwatches bearing the president's likeness (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025).
  • The pardons stop at the political line: the July 2025 round, per Turkmen.news as reported by Fergana, included no well-known political prisoners, and Human Rights Watch records that activists Murat Dushemov, Mansur Mingelov, and Myalikberdy Allamuradov remained imprisoned after closed, unfair trials while thousands of ordinary convicts were released (Human Rights Watch, 8 December 2025). One law for the thief who repents; another for the citizen who criticized.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, media, the electoral authority, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The formal architecture contains the standard guarantees, and the dossier records them because the tests grade the record, not the rhetoric alone: the constitution declares an independent judiciary, the 2012 Law on Mass Media prohibits censorship and pressure on journalists on its face, and Turkmenistan engages UN treaty bodies and admitted an ILO monitoring presence to its cotton harvest from 2023 (Law on Mass Media No. 355-IV; OHCHR, March 2023; ILO monitoring reports, 2024–2025).
  • One documented instance of a court ruling against the migration authorities on a citizen's behalf exists in the recent record: after several reviews, including a July 2025 decision overturning a lower court's travel ban, an Ashgabat court on 29 October 2025 ordered migration authorities to provide the legal grounds for the travel ban imposed on journalist Nurgeldy Khalykov. Human Rights Watch records in the same paragraph that the authorities had not responded and Khalykov remained unable to travel — the ruling happened; its effect, at the time of writing, had not (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026).
  • The central bank governorship and the state banking system have changed hands by ordinary appointment without a documented purge or public pressure campaign in Serdar's tenure; no documented case of the seat publicly instructing the central bank was located. The dossier records this as an absence of evidence, not as evidence of independence — the bank's chairman is a presidential appointee in a system with no published deliberations.

Evidence against:

  • The grip is constitutional, not incidental. The January 2023 laws placed the Halk Maslahaty — chaired for life-tenure in practice by the father, a permanent member — above the legislature as the "supreme" governing body, and the president is no longer able to veto laws proposed by it; the National Leader's initiatives are mandatory for consideration by all state bodies (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024; constitutional law texts, January 2023). Whatever autonomy any institution below that body possesses is held underneath a family chairmanship.
  • The judiciary scores zero, on the record, every year: Freedom House scores "Is there an independent judiciary?" at 0 of 4 and due process at 0 of 4 (Freedom in the World 2024 and 2025). The structural reason is documented and undisputed: the president appoints all judges for five-year terms and may dismiss them unilaterally, with no legislative review except for the Supreme Court chairman (CIA World Factbook; Nations Encyclopedia judicial summary). The electoral authority is likewise a presidential instrument: the Central Election Commission is appointed by the president and operates with little transparency (Freedom House, 2024).
  • The March 2023 Mejlis elections — the only parliamentary elections of Serdar's tenure — were assessed by the OSCE/ODIHR assessment mission as not held to international standards and lacking genuine competitiveness, with an election commission that lacks independence and no political pluralism; every registered party in Turkmenistan supports the president, and no opposition party may register (OSCE/ODIHR report, October 2023, via Freedom House Nations in Transit 2024).
  • Media autonomy does not exist in any documented form. Reporters Without Borders ranked Turkmenistan 177th of 180 in 2022, 176th in 2023, 175th in 2024, 174th in 2025, and 173rd in 2026 — last in Central Asia every year — with a 2025 score of 19.14/100 and the worst political-context sub-score in the world; RSF's assessment is that national media are state-controlled, news amounts largely to praise for the regime, and independent journalists work covertly at risk of prosecution, imprisonment, and torture (RSF World Press Freedom Index, 2022–2026). More than 122,000 internet domains are blocked; VPN use is prosecuted, and in April 2026 authorities raided homes and offices to confiscate Starlink satellite terminals, detaining some individuals suspected of installing them (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2025; IPHR/TIHR update, May 2026).
  • The prosecution's autonomy is answered by the prison system it feeds: the Prove They Are Alive! campaign has documented 162 cases of enforced disappearance in Turkmen prisons since 2002, at least half continuing, including dozens of people never released after their sentences expired; no international observer, including UN mandate holders, is permitted to visit Ovadan Depe, the prison where the disappeared are believed held (Prove They Are Alive! statement to the OSCE, 2026; Crude Accountability UPR submission, 2023).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, international bodies, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • In the external lane, documented adjustments exist and are recent. Amendments to the Law on Migration adopted in April 2025 created an electronic visa and abolished the letter-of-invitation requirement for tourists — for decades the single largest barrier to entering the country — and the e-visa portal went live on 1 October 2026, with tourist applicants no longer needing a Turkmen sponsor (State Migration Service announcement, 30 September 2026; migration-law amendments, April 2025). Eligibility lists and full functionality were still unpublished at launch, which the dossier records as the adjustment's stated limit.
  • Engagement with the United States produced a measurable concession from the other side: after Turkmenistan was placed under a partial US entry suspension in June 2025 (Proclamation 10949), the White House proclamation of 16 December 2025 lifted the suspension on nonimmigrant visas, stating that Turkmenistan "has engaged productively with the United States and demonstrated significant progress in improving its identity-management and information-sharing procedures"; the suspension on immigrant entry remains (White House fact sheet, 16 December 2025).
  • On forced labor, the government admitted the referee it had excluded for decades: ILO observers have monitored the cotton harvest since 2023 under agreed road maps (the latest covering 2026), and in May 2026 the ILO and EU launched a joint 2026–2027 project on child and forced labor in cotton — access granted and renewed under Serdar, even though the findings it produced (Test 4) were adverse (ILO monitoring reports; Times of Central Asia, November 2026).
  • A small, specific disclosure followed years of pressure on the disappeared: in early 2025 authorities disclosed the whereabouts of 12 forcibly disappeared individuals who had served their sentences — five of them confirmed dead (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026). Twelve names after two decades is recorded here at its exact size: a movement, and a measure of how much did not move.

Evidence against:

  • The founding denial of the era was never corrected. Turkmenistan remains the only sovereign state that has never reported a single COVID-19 case; the zero-case claim was maintained by the father's government through the pandemic — with the WHO's July 2020 mission repeating the government's "no confirmed cases" line while urging it to act as if the virus were present, and a senior WHO official conceding to the BBC in 2021 that it was scientifically "unlikely" the virus was not circulating — and Serdar's government, in office from March 2022, never revised the record (WHO mission reporting, July 2020; BBC/WHO, 2021; COVID-19 pandemic record through 2025). A state that will not correct a number the whole world can check has set its answer to every smaller wobble in advance.
  • Pushback on prisoners is answered with more prison. Civic activist Murat Dushemov, due for release in June 2025 at the end of a four-year sentence, was instead held on new charges — documented by rights monitors as fabricated — and sentenced on 16 September 2025 to eight additional years in a closed trial (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026 and 8 December 2025 statement).
  • Pushback on information is answered with a wider net: the 2025–2026 record shows the anti-VPN campaign extended to raids confiscating Starlink terminals (April 2026) and, per RSF and IPHR, state promotion of government-controlled VPNs as the permitted alternative — the response to citizens finding a door was to move the door inside the wall (IPHR/TIHR, May 2026).
  • The journalist who won in court was answered with silence: the October 2025 court order requiring migration authorities to state the legal grounds for Nurgeldy Khalykov's travel ban had received no response at the time Human Rights Watch closed its report (World Report 2026).
  • The 75-year-old journalist Soltan Achilova's attempts to travel to receive the Martin Ennals Award were blocked in 2023 by damage to her passport and on 20 November 2024 by forced hospitalization on an infectious-disease pretext; the UN Special Rapporteur on human rights defenders in April 2025 urged an investigation into an alleged November 2024 poisoning attempt against her. No investigation has been documented (Human Rights Watch, November 2024 and World Report 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — the poor, food-insecure households, labor migrants, ethnic minorities, women — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The social floor is universal in design and the increments are real decrees: the long-standing policy of raising public-sector wages, pensions, benefits, and stipends by 10% annually continued under Serdar (his 2023 program restated the 10% rise; the IMF notes the policy as a standing driver of its inflation projections), and a state-shop system sells subsidized staple foods to anyone, without a means or loyalty test (Program for Socio-Economic Development 2023; IMF Article IV statement, April 2024; Human Rights Watch/TIHR food-crisis report, 2020).
  • Serdar's September 2022 program promised, in his own figures, to raise salaries 1.8 times over 2022–2028, create some 30,000 additional jobs, and build 40 preschools, 54 schools, four higher-education institutions, and 10 hospitals (state media report of the State Council address, September 2022). The promise is graded under Test 5; its universalist design belongs here.
  • The pardons (Test 1) reach ordinary prisoners in the thousands each year, and the 2025 disclosure of 12 disappeared prisoners' whereabouts — however small against 162 documented cases — was information given to families who had been given none (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The cotton harvest counts the flock as a labor reserve. The ILO's 2025 monitoring — conducted with the government's permission — found observers saw children in the fields during 38% of visits, up from 23% in 2024, with children observed in 59% of fields visited in Dashoguz region, and reported interference in 35% of field visits; the Cotton Campaign's report on the 2025 harvest (May 2026) found all categories of public-sector employees required to pick cotton or pay for replacements, with Turkmen.news documenting teachers, bank staff, and oil workers paying levies of up to roughly 17% of a monthly salary to buy their way out, military conscripts picking under daily quotas of 40–50 kg, and replacement pickers as young as 12 (ILO 2025 monitoring report; Cotton Campaign, May 2026; Times of Central Asia, November 2026). The government denies the systematic use of forced labor; the ILO's own observers' figures are the documented record, and they rose on Serdar's watch.
  • The poor are not counted because, officially, they do not exist: the government does not acknowledge poverty, the World Bank does not publish key economic data for Turkmenistan for lack of reliable statistics, and Human Rights Watch records that through 2024 the availability of subsidized staples continued to shrink while prices rose, with sporadic bread shortages and tightly controlled queues in state stores; in February 2024 security services reportedly interrogated residents in Mary region who had tried to meet their mayor over flour and bread price increases — the complaint itself treated as the offence (Human Rights Watch, World Reports 2024 and 2025).
  • Two prices for one currency count insiders and outsiders differently: the manat has been officially pegged at 3.5 to the US dollar since 2015, while the parallel market rate stood at roughly 19 to the dollar in 2026 (and was reported as high as 32 in January 2021) — access to foreign exchange at the official rate is a privilege the state allocates, and everyone outside the allocation pays fivefold (IMF and market reporting; 2026 travel-market compilations of the parallel rate).
  • Women are counted as a category to be managed: voluntary abortion is banned after five weeks of pregnancy; in August 2024 authorities warned medical practitioners they would lose their diplomas for performing abortions; HRW documents arbitrary requirements including a minimum age of 35 for a driver's license and, in Mary and Balkan regions, mandatory gynecological examinations of schoolgirls; the restrictions on women's appearance, dress, cosmetics, and riding in cars with unrelated men introduced in April–May 2022 — weeks after Serdar took office, with no official announcement — were enforced by police and employers; in March 2025 Ashgabat authorities reportedly imposed yellow headscarves for unmarried women and yellow dresses for married women in the public sector under threat of dismissal. Turkmenistan has no domestic-violence law (Human Rights Watch, World Reports 2025 and 2026; Library of Congress Global Legal Monitor, June 2022; CEDAW sixth periodic review, February 2024).
  • Exit is rationed and kin are counted for each other's offences: authorities bar citizens from boarding international flights on shifting pretexts, refuse passport renewal at consulates abroad — forcing labor migrants to return and risk being trapped — ordered public-sector workers and newly admitted university students in 2024 to surrender their passports, and imposed travel bans on the brother and sister of the imprisoned activist Murat Dushemov "in apparent retaliation for his activism" (Human Rights Watch, World Reports 2025 and 2026).
  • Ethnic and religious minorities stand in the documented margins: Baluch activist Mansur Mingelov, imprisoned since 2012 on a 22-year sentence, has been denied early release despite serious illness (Human Rights Watch, December 2025); conscientious objectors were again sentenced in December 2024 and February 2025 — the first such convictions since the 2021 amnesty released 16 Jehovah's Witnesses (documented case reporting, 2024–2025); consensual same-sex conduct between men remains a crime punishable by two years' imprisonment (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026).
  • The disappeared are the count's floor: 162 documented enforced disappearances since 2002, dozens held past the end of their sentences, families given no information for decades, and no independent access to the prisons — a population the state's accounting simply omits (Prove They Are Alive!, 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record since March 2022, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Continuity — kept, in form and in fact. Serdar's central inaugural commitment (19 March 2022) was to continue the course of the previous 30 years and implement the National Program for 2022–2052 — a program prepared on his father's initiative and adopted in February 2022, before Serdar took office. He has governed inside it, and the January 2023 settlement formalized the continuity he promised: the father retained, the program retained (inaugural address, 19 March 2022; constitutional laws, 21 January 2023).
  • The annual increments — kept as decreed. The standing pledge of yearly increases to wages, pensions, benefits, and stipends was restated in his 2023 program (10%) and is treated by the IMF as an operative policy of his government through 2024–2026 (Program for Socio-Economic Development 2023; IMF Article IV statements, 2024–2026).
  • TAPI — in motion, on his stated schedule so far. The Serhetabat–Herat section of the Turkmenistan–Afghanistan–Pakistan–India pipeline — construction blessed by the National Leader on 11 September 2024 — was reported in late October 2026 at roughly 80% welded (124 of ~153 km), with Turkmen officials pledging completion by the end of 2026 and satellite imagery published by RFE/RL on 7 November 2026 showing the line about 22 km from Herat; the section is financed by the Turkmen government at a reported ~$600 million. Volumes and pricing for the gas itself remain unnegotiated, and the pledge's deadline had not yet fallen due at grading (OilPrice/RFE/RL reporting, November 2026).
  • Neutrality and international obligations — kept in the form he states them. Turkmenistan's permanent neutrality was maintained, its UN engagement continued (the UN declared 2025 the International Year of Peace and Trust at Turkmenistan's initiative), and the methane pledge made at COP28 in December 2023 was formally acceded to (inaugural address, March 2022; UN and COP28 records).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or unverifiable by design):

  • The rights guarantee — not kept. The inaugural address promised "constitutional guarantees for the protection of the rights of citizens" and strengthened "social and legal protection of the population" (19 March 2022). The record since is Tests 1, 2, and 4: Freedom House's Freedom in the World score fell from 2/100 in the 2024 report to 1/100 in 2025 — the decline attributed to morality enforcement against women and couples — making Turkmenistan, on that measure, the least free country scored on earth (Freedom House, 2024–2025).
  • Food abundance — not kept on the documented record. "Ensuring the country's food abundance is one of the most important tasks," the inaugural address said. Subsidized staples shrank, prices rose, bread queues were policed, and complainants were interrogated (Test 4; Human Rights Watch, 2024–2025). The government does not publish the poverty data that would let its own promise be checked.
  • The numbers themselves cannot be verified — and the referee says so. State media report GDP growth around 6.2–6.5% in Serdar's programs and cabinet reports; the IMF declines to use the official figures, stating in June 2026 that "the narrative underlying official GDP growth estimates is hard to reconcile with other available data; staff uses its own GDP estimates" — its own estimates being 5.3% for 2022, 2.0–4.5% for 2023 (revised across vintages), 3.4% for 2024, and 3.6% for 2025 — and an IMF working paper (No. 2023/207) uses Turkmenistan as its case study for national accounts with "serious shortcomings… often manifested as overestimated growth rates." The 1.8-times salary pledge for 2022–2028 is denominated in a currency with two prices (Test 4); whether it has been kept in terms a citizen can spend is NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE on the published record — which is itself the finding (IMF Article IV statements, 2024–2026; IMF Working Paper 2023/207).
  • The monument outran the promise. The signature construction of the era is Arkadag, the "smart city" named for his father, opened on the father's 66th birthday, 29 June 2023, at a reported $3.3 billion for the first phase and ~$4.8–5 billion in total for a planned 73,000 residents — in a country whose government does not acknowledge the poverty documented in Test 4, and where how many people actually live in the city cannot be estimated from any published figure (Reuters, 29 June 2023; construction officials' figures via project reporting, March 2023). Housing "for each family" was the inaugural promise; the housing that can be verified is a city named for the family.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

The Turkmen seat is the only one in this series held, by constitutional design, by two men at once — and the design is the grade. A son holds the presidency; a father holds a National Leader law that exempts the whole family from prosecution and from seizure of its property, chairs a council placed above the parliament, and lends his name and his birthday to a five-billion-dollar city and a pipeline. Under that roof, the law pardons thousands of ordinary prisoners a year and is reported to sell the pardons; it imprisons the activist past his sentence's end and disappears 162 people from its own accounting; it sends the state's own employees — and, on the ILO's rising count, their children — into the cotton fields, and interrogates the citizens who queue for flour and complain. Against that record stand real, smaller things, printed at their size: a referee admitted to the cotton harvest, an e-visa door opened in October 2026 after decades of invitation-only entry, twelve disappeared men finally named to their families, five of them dead. The wobble grade is mixed because those adjustments happened; it is not higher because every one of them opened outward, toward foreigners and monitors, while every pushback that came from inside — a journalist's court order, an activist's completed sentence, a region's complaint about bread — was answered with silence, a raid, or more prison. The word was kept where it promised continuity, increments, and neutrality; it was not kept where it promised rights and food; and on the numbers in between, the state's own referee declines to certify the arithmetic. We do not pick who lands where they land. On the documented record, this seat gives by family, grips by constitution, and counts its flock — in the cotton season, literally — as a resource, not as the point.
