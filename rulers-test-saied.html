The Ruler's Test — Kaïs Saïed, President of Tunisia

Subject: Kaïs Saïed — President of the Republic of Tunisia since 23 October 2019, re-elected on 6 October 2024 and sworn in for a second five-year term on 21 October 2024. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: under the constitution Saïed himself promulgated in 2022, the president is the executive — he appoints and dismisses the head of government and the ministers, commands the armed forces, appoints the members of the electoral authority and senior judges, and can legislate by decree-law. The brief's premise was verified before grading: Saïed still holds the seat in November 2026 — he was receiving ministers at Carthage and issuing statements in the first week of November 2026 (TAP, 3 November 2026) — and no premise correction is required. Saïed, a former constitutional-law professor who ran in 2019 as an independent with no party, won the 2019 runoff with 72.71% of the vote. On 25 July 2021, citing Article 80 of the 2014 constitution amid a collapsing COVID-19 response and a deadlocked parliament, he dismissed the government of Hichem Mechichi, suspended parliament (dissolving it outright on 30 March 2022), and took the public prosecution under his own authority. He then ruled by decree-law under Decree 117 of September 2021, dissolved the elected Supreme Judicial Council in February 2022, dismissed 57 judges by decree in June 2022, and put a new constitution to referendum on 25 July 2022 (official result: 94.6% in favour on 30.5% turnout). Legislative elections under that constitution in December 2022–January 2023 drew 11.22% turnout in the first round. In the October 2024 presidential election, the electoral authority (ISIE) admitted only three candidates from seventeen applicants — Saïed, Ayachi Zammel, and Zouhair Maghzaoui — refusing to implement Administrative Court rulings reinstating three others; Zammel campaigned from prison and was sentenced to a cumulative thirteen years and eight months for sponsorship offences during the campaign period. Saïed was declared re-elected with 90.69% on 28.8% turnout (ISIE figures). He is graded on the full tenure, October 2019 to November 2026, with the weight on 2021–2026, when the seat took its present shape. Four heads of government — Najla Bouden, Ahmed Hachani, Kamel Maddouri, and Sara Zaafarani — have served under him since October 2021, each appointed and each dismissed or replaced by him. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic of the Test, in Tristan's words: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Three standing rules of the method apply throughout. First, the mandate rule: governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one — carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the for-shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how it is done. Second, the two axes are independent: keeping his word and helping people are graded separately, and neither launders the other; the crossings are named where they occur. Third, the no-good-choice clause: where the record shows a situation with no good choice — or none that appeared to be at the time — the dossier says so, names the options that actually existed and the harm each carried, and grades the choice as the best possible available in the moment; that cannot be held against him, though whether he built the trap himself, was honest inside it, and took the least-harm door actually open remain gradable. Every test closes with why the grade lands where it lands, and with who his actions on that test helped and who they hurt.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution net has caught people from his own orbit, not only his enemies'. The single heaviest sentence in the February 2026 appeal rulings in the second "conspiracy against state security" case fell on Nadia Akacha — Saïed's own former chief of staff, who broke with him and left the country — sentenced in absentia to 35 years (Reuters, 3 February 2026). The same case sentenced a former intelligence chief and a former foreign minister of earlier governments. Whatever else these trials are (Test 2 and the against-shelf below), the documented record does not show a protected inner circle around his person: a chief of staff who fell out with him received no shelter.
  • No documented record of personal enrichment by Saïed or his family appears in the sources assembled here, and none of his monitors — including the monitors most hostile to him — rests its case on his personal corruption. His anti-corruption machinery has been aimed, at least in its stated targets, at networks of the Ben Ali era and of the post-2011 party governments he defeated, and his November 2026 public position on the penal-reconciliation file remains that there will be "neither tolerance nor concession over a single millime" owed to the Tunisian people (TAP, 3 November 2026).
  • Some enforcement has run against economic actors with no opposition profile: merchants and warehouse holders detained in recurring "speculation and hoarding" operations, and businessmen of the old regime's orbit summoned into the penal-reconciliation process rather than simply left alone (presidency and TAP reporting, 2023–2026; Noria Research, June 2025, updated June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The criminal law's weight falls, with documented consistency, on one side of the political line. In the first "Conspiracy Case," a Tunis court on 19 April 2025 sentenced 37 defendants — opposition politicians, lawyers, activists, researchers, and businessmen — to between 4 and 66 years for conspiracy against state security and terrorism offences; on 27 November 2025 an appeals court confirmed sentences against 34 of them (5 to 45 years), acquitted three, and the authorities then arrested three prominent convicted activists who had been at liberty — Chaima Issa, Ayachi Hammami, and Ahmed Nejib Chebbi (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026). In the second conspiracy case, appeal rulings of 2–3 February 2026 upheld and increased sentences against at least 20 opposition figures; Rached Ghannouchi, the 84-year-old Ennahda leader and former speaker of the parliament Saïed dissolved, received 20 years in that case, bringing his cumulative sentences across more than a dozen cases to some 50 years (Reuters, 3 February 2026). The defendants deny the charges and say the cases are fabricated to silence opposition; UN and NGO monitors describe the April 2025 trial as marred by fair-trial violations. The charges themselves are recorded here as ALLEGATIONs that courts acting under the conditions described in Test 2 have upheld — not as facts this dossier finds.
  • The same pattern runs down the ladder of dissent. Abir Moussi, leader of the Free Destourian Party, was sentenced on 12 June 2025 to two years for criticising the electoral commission, and on 12 December 2025 to a further 12 years for seeking "to change the form of government" (HRW, World Report 2026). Former president Moncef Marzouki, former minister Abderrazak Kilani, and former MP Imed Daïmi — the last a prospective 2024 candidate — were sentenced in absentia to 22 years on 20 June 2025 (HRW). On 1 October 2025 a Nabeul court sentenced a private citizen, Saber Ben Chouchane, to death for peaceful Facebook posts criticising the president; he was released on 7 October 2025 after a presidential pardon when the sentence sparked public outrage (HRW). A law under which a Facebook post can draw a death sentence, and criticism of the electoral commission draws prison, is not one law for the critic and the supporter alike.
  • Decree-Law 54 of September 2022 — his cybercrime decree, adopted without consultation — has been used overwhelmingly against journalists, commentators, and lawyers rather than against any comparable speech from his own camp: broadcasters Mourad Zghidi and Borhen Bsaies, arrested in May 2024 over commentary critical of the president, had three-and-a-half-year sentences upheld on appeal in mid-2026; lawyer-commentator Sonia Dahmani has faced at least five prosecutions over media appearances, receiving an 18-month sentence in April 2026; editor Ghassen Ben Khelifa was sentenced to two years in March 2026; Sihem Bensedrine, former president of the Truth and Dignity Commission, was sentenced on 26 June 2026 to 25 years in a case arising from the Commission's final report; anti-racism association president Saadia Mosbah received eight years on appeal in June 2026 (press-freedom and rights reporting, 2026; HRW; SNJT statements). His supporters, including those who physically attacked the UGTT headquarters in August 2025 (Test 2), face no comparable documented prosecutions.
  • The 2024 ballot itself was cleared by a rule applied to one side. ISIE — whose members Saïed appoints — admitted three of seventeen presidential applicants, convicted or detained eight other prospective candidates during the electoral period, and refused to implement the Administrative Court's binding rulings reinstating Mondher Zenaidi, Abdellatif Mekki, and Imed Daïmi (ICJ statement, October 2024; Administrative Court and ISIE statements, August–September 2024). The one admitted challenger with momentum, Ayachi Zammel, was arrested the day the final list was announced and accumulated thirteen years and eight months of sentences for sponsorship offences before the vote; the sponsorship-falsification charges are ALLEGATIONs as to his intent, but their timing and accumulation are documented facts (ICTJ and AP reporting, September 2024).
  • For those with money, a second, discretionary track exists beside the criminal law. His penal-reconciliation mechanism (Decree-Law 13 of March 2022) lets businessmen accused of financial wrongdoing settle by payment or development projects instead of facing verdicts; after the commission's first year produced almost nothing (below, Test 5), Saïed amended the law to move final arbitration to the National Security Council — a body he chairs — whose decisions are not subject to appeal, while dozens of businessmen were arrested and summoned to "negotiate" (Noria Research, 2025–2026). The guilt of every businessman in that process is an ALLEGATION; what is documented is the structure: prison or payment, at the discretion of a council the president chairs, with no appeal. That is a second law for the rich running parallel to the first law for everyone else — and it is the same structural risk this series flagged in Baghdad, except that there it was a risk and here it is the written design.

Why: A one-law record does not require that no opponent ever be guilty of anything; it requires that the law's incidence not track political alignment. Here it tracks it almost without exception: every mass trial, every Decree 54 prosecution of note, every candidacy exclusion, and the only death sentence in the file fall on critics, opponents, and the institutions that checked him, while the discretionary exit — reconciliation by payment, arbitration without appeal — is reserved for men with money. The for-shelf is real: his former chief of staff got no protection, and no monitor documents his personal enrichment. But a net that catches a defector from his own palace while his street supporters go unprosecuted is not one law; it is one law for the aligned and another for everyone else. Helped: his governing camp and its supporters, who operate without legal exposure for comparable acts; wealthy accused businessmen, who hold a purchasable alternative to trial. Hurt: the imprisoned opposition across every party family (Ennahda, the Free Destourian Party, the secular Salvation Front parties), their lawyers, critical journalists and commentators, the 2024 electorate whose candidate list was cut from seventeen to three, and ordinary social-media users, one of whom received a death sentence for Facebook posts before being pardoned.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The referees still speak, even where they are not obeyed. The Administrative Court suspended most of the June 2022 judicial dismissals in August 2022 and ruled in August 2024 to reinstate three presidential candidates — rulings issued against the executive's position, in the open (Administrative Court decisions, August 2022 and August 2024). The judges' and lawyers' associations still strike — a lawyers' strike in June 2026 aired the breakdown of trust publicly (Intersection Association report, 28 July 2026). The UGTT still exists, still mobilises thousands (21 August 2025), and still publishes positions against the government; independent outlets such as Nawaat still publish investigations of his flagship projects. The grip documented below is a grip on outcomes, resisted daily — not yet a silence.
  • Parliament, though elected on 11.22% turnout under his constitution, sits, debates his budgets in plenary (the 2027 finance bill debate opened 9 November 2026), and processes the government's financing bills through committee (ARP proceedings, November 2026). The Central Bank still publishes the statistics that document the state's dependence on it, and took its December 2025 rate cut (8% to 7%) on its own calendar (BCT statistics bulletin, 2026; economic reporting, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The judiciary was dismantled as an independent power by his own hand, in sequence. He dissolved the elected Supreme Judicial Council on 12 February 2022 and replaced it with a provisional council whose members he appoints; on 1 June 2022 a decree gave him the power to dismiss judges summarily, and he dismissed 57 the same day, accusing them collectively of corruption and obstruction — accusations recorded here as ALLEGATIONs, never adjudicated individually. When the Administrative Court suspended most of the dismissals in August 2022, the ruling "was not effectively implemented," and the judges were not reinstated (Intersection report, July 2026; legal monitoring, 2022–2026). The 2022 constitution then demoted the judiciary from a power to a "function," and the mass trials of Test 1 were conducted by the bench that remained. In 2025 Tunisia also withdrew its declaration accepting individual petitions to the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights — the court that had issued provisional measures for Ghannouchi and other detainees — effective March 2026 (HRW, World Report 2026): an external referee removed from the board entirely.
  • The electoral referee is his appointee and acts like it. Decree-Law 22 of April 2022 gave him appointment of ISIE's members; ISIE then refused the Administrative Court's binding reinstatement rulings in September 2024 (Test 1), and on 27 September 2024 — nine days before the vote — parliament, sitting in his majority, stripped the Administrative Court of electoral jurisdiction altogether and transferred it to the Court of Appeal (ICJ, October 2024). In September 2026 he amended ISIE's organic law again by decree-law (Decree-Law 2026-3), one of four decree-laws issued on 30 September 2026 while a parliament elected under his constitution exists — the first decree-laws since that parliament convened in March 2023, their constitutionality questioned by the International Commission of Jurists (ICJ Q&A, November 2026).
  • The central bank's independence has been legislated away in instalments. Parliament twice derogated from Article 25 of the bank's 2016 statute to authorise direct, interest-free treasury financing — some USD 4.45 billion authorised by April 2026 (USD 2.25 billion in February 2024 and USD 2.2 billion in December 2024) — and the bank's direct advances to the state climbed from 1.4 billion dinars at end-2023 to 7.6 billion at end-2024, 13.55 billion at end-2025, and 16.25 billion by June 2026, making the Treasury, not the banking system, the central bank's biggest borrower (BCT financial statistics bulletin, 2026; BTI 2026 country report). The 2027 finance bill would authorise a further 9.8 billion dinars on the same terms. A central bank that must lend to the treasury without interest, in amounts set by the borrower's budget, is not holding its own mandate.
  • The last large independent institution outside the state, the UGTT — the labour confederation of roughly one million members and co-winner of the 2015 Nobel Peace Prize — has been pressed by every instrument short of dissolution. After its July 30–August 1, 2025 transport strike, the government suspended all negotiation and conciliation sessions with it; on 7 August 2025 his supporters attacked its Tunis headquarters demanding its dissolution, and Saïed publicly defended the attackers while alluding to investigations into the union's leadership; a 14 August 2025 decree cancelled paid union leave for public employees; the government moved against automatic dues deduction and secondments and arrested prominent union figures (UGTT statements; Arab Reform Initiative, 2025; Reuters Connect, 21 August 2025). The union's planned 21 January 2026 general strike — its first since 2021 — collapsed amid its own internal fractures (its secretary-general resigned in December 2025 and withdrew the resignation in January 2026), which this dossier records as the union's own record, not his act; what is his act is the documented sequence of punishment that preceded it.
  • Civil society's remaining organisations are being switched off administratively: in late October 2025 the authorities suspended the Tunisian Association of Democratic Women (ATFD) for a month — closing its counselling centres for survivors of violence — and the Tunisian Forum for Economic and Social Rights (FTDES), citing administrative and financial irregularities (International IDEA democracy tracker, October 2025; FIDH, October 2025). The irregularity findings are the authorities' claims, recorded as such; the suspensions are documented facts.

Why: Test 2 does not ask whether any independent voice survives — some do, and the for-shelf records them. It asks whether independence is punished and control centralised. On the documented record, every referee that ruled against him was dissolved (the judicial council), purged (57 judges), stripped of jurisdiction (the Administrative Court, twice — its rulings ignored, then its electoral competence removed), defunded by statute (the central bank), withdrawn from (the African Court), or physically attacked by his supporters with his public defence (the UGTT). That is not a seat holding the centre while members keep their spin; it is a seat that has taken the referees' whistles one by one. Helped: the presidency itself, which now appoints or disciplines every referee that could check it; the treasury in the short run, which borrows from its own central bank at zero interest. Hurt: the dismissed judges and the litigants before a bench that knows the price of ruling wrong; voters, whose electoral referee answers to a candidate; the UGTT's million members and the workers whose wage negotiations were suspended; women using ATFD's closed counselling centres; and every dinar-holder, whose currency now backs 16.25 billion dinars of direct state advances.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, when another option became available, he at least considered it openly; considering another option and declining it with stated reasons is not evidence against, refusing to look at it is.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The October 2025 death sentence was reversed by his own hand within six days. When the Nabeul court's death sentence against Saber Ben Chouchane for Facebook posts sparked public outrage, Saïed pardoned him and he was released on 7 October 2025 (HRW, World Report 2026). The adjustment was individual rather than legal — the statutes that produced the sentence stand — but it was fast, open, and in the direction the street demanded.
  • After four years of domestic and international pressure over Decree 54, he softened it himself: Decree-Law 2026-1 of 30 September 2026 repeals imprisonment under Article 24 for first-time offenders and lowers the fine to 10,000–20,000 dinars. The International Commission of Jurists calls the removal of imprisonment and the lower fines "positive steps," while finding the amendment insufficient — the offence of spreading "false news" remains, and imprisonment remains available for repeat offenders (ICJ Q&A, November 2026). It is a partial adjustment, openly made, in the direction his critics had demanded since 2022.
  • On the migrants file, one early pushback was answered with a concrete reversal: after the July 2023 collective expulsions to the Libya border drew continental condemnation, the authorities on 10 July 2023 relocated more than 600 of the expelled people back into Tunisia to shelters (HRW letter to the AU and ECOWAS Commissions, July 2023). The expulsions later resumed (Test 4); the reversal itself is documented and belongs on this shelf.
  • On Gabès (Test 4), the government's answer to the October 2025 uprising included an announced adjustment alongside the arrests: a promise to relaunch six stalled environmental clean-up projects and to end the dumping of phosphogypsum at sea (Carthage Magazine's running account, 2025–2026). Implementation is another matter — a court refused in February 2026 to suspend the plant's operations and a new gas leak hit in July 2026 — but the promise was made in response to the pushback, which is what this test measures.
  • The IMF option was considered openly and declined with stated reasons. A USD 1.9 billion staff-level agreement was reached in October 2022; Saïed then rejected it publicly, in April 2023 and repeatedly since, on stated grounds — that its subsidy and wage conditions were foreign "diktats" whose social cost Tunisia would not pay. Under this test's standing rule, considering another option and declining it with reasons given in the open is not evidence against him, and it is not entered as any here. What is graded elsewhere is the honesty of the alternative he promised instead (Test 5) and the instrument he chose (Test 2).

Evidence against:

  • The courts' pushback has been answered, at every step, with refusal and then removal. The Administrative Court's August 2022 suspension of the judicial dismissals was not implemented; its August–September 2024 candidate reinstatements were refused by his electoral authority on a notification pretext the Court publicly denied, and parliament then abolished the Court's electoral jurisdiction (ICJ, October 2024). No ruling against his core project has ever been complied with and then argued; each was defied and the defiance then legalised.
  • The voters' pushback — abstention on a scale that is itself a message — has never been answered with any adjustment at all. Turnout fell from 30.5% at his 2022 referendum to 11.22% at the December 2022 legislative elections, roughly 11.7% at the December 2023 local elections, and 28.8% at his own 2024 re-election (ISIE figures). After each, the project proceeded unchanged and the abstainers were addressed, in his October 2024 swearing-in speech, in the language of treason: "there is no place for traitors" (swearing-in address, 21 October 2024).
  • A negotiated national-dialogue option became available and was refused without consideration. In 2023 the UGTT, the Bar Association, and the Human Rights League — three of the four organisations that had won the Nobel Peace Prize for brokering Tunisia's 2013 dialogue — put forward a national-dialogue roadmap to address the crisis. Saïed declined to join or receive it, having ruled out dialogue with parties and figures he designates corrupt; there is no documented instance of the proposal being weighed and answered on its terms. Under the standing rule of this test, an option refused without being looked at is the precise failure the test exists to catch.
  • The street's institutional pushback was answered with punishment, not adjustment. The UGTT's 2025 transport strike was met with suspended negotiations, the headquarters attack he defended, cancelled union leave, and arrests (Test 2). The Gabès uprising of October 2025 — the largest popular mobilisation of his tenure — was met in parallel with more than 150 arrests in under two weeks, at least 44 pre-trial detentions, and 89 people, including around 20 minors, brought before prosecutors between 14 and 17 October alone (FIDH, 23 October 2025; Article 19, October 2025; Gabès Bar Association via TAP). The clean-up promises on the for-shelf were made; the arrests were larger, faster, and documented in greater detail.
  • Events themselves are answered with accusation rather than revision. Facing the summer 2026 wildfires (more than 2,000 hectares burned in Siliana alone) and a water crisis that left an estimated 300,000 people without drinking-water access in January 2026, Saïed in November 2026 publicly attributed the disruptions to deliberate criminal interference and former political networks "manufacturing crises," and called for investigations (TAP and Tunisie Numérique via Maghrebi, 9–10 November 2026). The sabotage claims are ALLEGATIONs — no findings are documented — and their function in the record is consistent: every wobble is a plot, so nothing in the policy needs to move. The same answer has covered the reconciliation shortfall ("sabotage attempts," presidency statement, November 2026) and the community-company stall (obstacles created by "conspiracists within the state," per his own public remarks).

Why: The for-shelf is not empty — a pardon in six days, a real if partial softening of Decree 54, one migrant reversal, announced clean-up promises, and an IMF refusal that this test expressly does not count against him. But set against the whole record, every pushback that touched the structure of his power — court rulings, electoral abstention, a Nobel-laureate dialogue offer, a union strike, a city's uprising — was refused, punished, or explained as treason and sabotage, while the adjustments cluster at the level of individual mercy and marginal amendment. A ruler who pardons one man and keeps the law that condemned him has answered the outrage, not the wobble. The grade is FAILS because the pattern is not mixed: on the documented record, the structural answer has been the same for five years. Helped: Saber Ben Chouchane, freed; first-time Decree 54 defendants, who no longer face prison under Article 24; the 600-plus migrants relocated to shelter in July 2023. Hurt: the dismissed judges whose court-ordered remedy was never implemented; the reinstated-then-excluded candidates and their voters; the UGTT's members after the 2025 strike; the Gabès detainees, including minors; and the public in the water-stressed regions, whose crisis was answered with a sabotage investigation rather than a published remedy.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's voters, the regions, Black Tunisians, migrants and refugees, women, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The subsidy shield was held for the poor at real fiscal cost. His refusal of the IMF programme (Test 3) was, in its stated and plausible substance, a refusal to cut the food and fuel subsidies on which poor households depend; through 2026 Tunisia serviced its debts without default — repaying a €700 million eurobond on 15 July 2026 — and without the subsidy shock the programme's conditions were expected to bring (BCT and Finance Ministry reporting, 2026; economic reporting, 2026). Whatever the financing method's risks (Test 2), the counted-in here are the households that did not face an overnight doubling of bread and fuel prices.
  • His two signature social instruments were aimed, by design, at the counted-out regions. The penal-reconciliation law directs recovered funds to development projects in the most deprived areas, and the community-companies law (Decree-Law 20 of March 2022) targets collective lands and local employment in the interior; a secretary of state for community companies was appointed and the count of such companies rose from 70 in August 2024 to 160 by February 2025 (RNE figures via Noria Research, 2025). The scale is graded in Test 5; the direction — toward the regions every previous government is accused of abandoning — is this test's for-evidence.
  • The bottom-up architecture he promised was actually built and voted in: local councils were elected across the country on 24 December 2023, and the second chamber they feed, the National Council of Regions and Districts, was seated in April 2024. Turnout was roughly 11.7% (ISIE), which belongs to Test 3's shelf; the existence of an elected local tier where none functioned before belongs here. He also appointed the Arab world's first woman head of government, Najla Bouden, in 2021, and another woman, Sara Zaafarani, in 2025.

Evidence against:

  • Black African migrants and refugees were counted out from the presidency's own mouth, and then in fact. On 21 February 2023 Saïed told his National Security Council that "hordes" of sub-Saharan migrants were part of a criminal plot to change Tunisia's demographic composition — a replacement-theory claim stated as fact by the head of state, recorded here as his claim, for which no evidence was produced. What followed is documented by Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International: a wave of attacks and evictions, then collective expulsions — up to 1,200 people in July 2023 alone, including children and people with legal status, dumped in desert border zones with Libya and Algeria without due process (HRW, July 2023). Amnesty's 2025 investigation documents the pattern continuing into 2024–2025 — dangerous interceptions at sea, collective expulsions amounting to refoulement, and a May 2024 crackdown on the organisations that aid migrants — and concludes Tunisia is not a safe country for them (Amnesty International, MDE 30/0180/2025). The July 2023 EU memorandum made Tunisia Europe's border guard in the same season; the counted-out were, in the plainest sense, the price of the partnership.
  • Black Tunisians' own advocates were prosecuted. Saadia Mosbah, president of the anti-racism association Mnemty, was arrested in May 2024 in the crackdown on migrant-aid organisations and sentenced on appeal in June 2026 to eight years (rights reporting, 2026). The association's offence, on the documented record, was receiving foreign funding to assist migrants; the financial charges are ALLEGATIONs the courts upheld. A state that imprisons its leading anti-racism campaigner while its president speaks of demographic plots has told a whole community where it stands in the count.
  • Gabès was counted as a cost centre for fifty years and then policed when it objected. The state chemical complex at Ghannouch has pumped phosphogypsum into the Gulf of Gabès since 1972 — 14,000–15,000 tonnes a day, over 500 million tonnes cumulatively, per a June 2026 Inkyfada investigation — destroying the fishery of what was once among the Mediterranean's richest waters. In September–October 2025, gas leaks poisoned more than 200 residents, including 69 schoolchildren in their classrooms in one episode; some 40,000 marched on 15 October and roughly 130,000 joined a general strike on 21 October, in a city of 150,000 (Inkyfada, June 2026; Carthage Magazine running account; FIDH, October 2025). The state's first answer was the arrests recorded in Test 3. The region that breathes the country's fertiliser exports is, in the accounting that matters, still a sacrifice zone.
  • The opposition's voters were counted out of the count itself. The parties that together won a majority of votes in 2019 have their leaders in prison or exile (Test 1); the 2024 presidential ballot offered their voters three names, one of them imprisoned; and the 2022 electoral law's individual-candidacy system cut women's share of parliament to roughly one seat in seven, from more than a quarter under the list system — a documented structural effect, recorded here because counting includes who can plausibly be chosen (electoral reporting, 2023). The 71.2% who abstained in 2024 are also part of this ledger: a ruler's count that proceeds identically whether a third or a tenth of the country participates has stopped counting the absent.
  • The interior's basic services deteriorated on his watch without entering his accounting as his responsibility: an estimated 300,000 people without drinking-water access in January 2026 (Maghrebi, November 2026), recurrent shortages of subsidised staples through 2022–2025, unemployment at 14.9% in mid-2026 with youth unemployment at 35.4% and female graduate unemployment at 35.6% (INS figures via economic reporting, 2026) — the young graduates whose 2019 hopes were his first electorate, leaving by boat in documented thousands each year.

Why: Every ruler's file has a for-shelf, and his — subsidies held, debts paid, a local tier built, flagship instruments aimed at the interior — is genuine. But Test 4 is failed at its centre, not its margins: a whole population present on the territory was named a demographic plot by the president and then expelled to deserts in documented collective operations; the citizens who defend them were imprisoned; the country's most poisoned city was answered with mass arrests; and the majority of the electorate that chose his opponents, or chose absence, was written out of the story the seat tells itself. Counting everyone is the one test his project, which claims to speak for "the people" as a single body, cannot pass while defining part of the people as the enemy. Helped: subsidy-dependent households, shielded from an IMF-style price shock; eurobond holders and the state's creditors, paid in full and on time; the European Union's border policy, which gained a partner; the interior regions named as beneficiaries of his flagship funds, to the small extent those funds have flowed (Test 5). Hurt: Black African migrants and refugees — expelled, intercepted, and abandoned in border deserts in documented operations; Black Tunisians, whose leading anti-racism voice is in prison; the people of Gabès, poisoned and then arrested; opposition voters and abstainers, unrepresented by design; and unemployed graduates, whose numbers did not move.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign pledges and public statements vs. the record since October 2019, with dates. Governing on his elected platform is recorded on this test's for-shelf, per the mandate rule; the two axes are kept separate — a kept word that hurt people is credited here and charged in the ledgers above, and a broken word that helped people is charged here and credited there.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The refounding timetable — kept to the month. In December 2021 Saïed published a roadmap: a national consultation, a constitutional referendum on 25 July 2022, and legislative elections on 17 December 2022. Each happened on the date announced (referendum 25 July 2022; elections 17 December 2022 and runoffs 29 January 2023). The content of that refounding is graded in Tests 1, 2, and 4; the dates were his word, and the dates were kept.
  • The IMF refusal — kept for five years, at cost. He said Tunisia would not accept foreign "diktats" and would not sign a programme whose conditions cut subsidies and the wage bill. The October 2022 staff-level agreement was never signed; there has been no IMF programme since; and the state has met its external maturities, including the €700 million eurobond repaid 15 July 2026 (Finance Ministry and BCT reporting, 2026). This is the file's clearest crossing of the two axes, and both sides are printed: crossing 1 in part — the word was kept and poor households were spared the subsidy shock (Test 4's for-shelf) — and crossing 2 in part — the word was kept and the keeping was financed by 16.25 billion dinars of central-bank advances whose inflation risk falls on every holder of the currency (Test 2's against-shelf). The keeping earns its credit here; neither consequence is hidden there.
  • The penal-reconciliation mechanism — built as promised, and still being amended in 2026. Decree-Law 13 of 20 March 2022 created the commission he had championed since his professorship; Decree-Law 2 of 30 September 2026 amended it again, and in November 2026 he was publicly pressing its president for full recovery (TAP, 3 November 2026). The mechanism exists and operates. The money promised through it is the against-shelf's first item.
  • The bottom-up building — delivered in form. The local councils and the National Council of Regions and Districts that were the institutional heart of his 2019 "building from the base" platform exist and sit (Test 4). Form is not nothing: he is one of the few rulers in this series whose signature constitutional architecture was actually constructed rather than announced and abandoned.
  • No default, no Paris Club — kept. Through the 2023–2026 financing squeeze his governments repeated that Tunisia pays its debts; on the documented record through November 2026, it has — domestic debt and central-bank advances absorbed the strain instead (BCT bulletin, 2026; Fitch commentary, September 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The founding word — the temporary exception — was not kept. Saïed took his 2019 oath on the 2014 constitution, and in July 2021 he framed the Article 80 measures as exceptional, temporary, and aimed at restoring the normal functioning of the state — the constitution he invoked forbade dissolving parliament during the exception and required the assembly to remain in session. The 30-day mark passed; on 23 August 2021 he extended the measures "until further notice"; in September 2021 Decree 117 made his decree-power supreme over the constitution; on 30 March 2022 he dissolved the parliament the article protected; in July 2022 the constitution itself was replaced by one written under his authority. His stated defence — that Article 80 authorised the measures and that the people ratified the outcome by referendum — is recorded as his position; it is contested by the large majority of Tunisian constitutional scholars and by the referendum's own arithmetic (30.5% turnout). A word about temporariness that ends in a new constitution concentrating power in the speaker's office is the heaviest single item on this test's against-shelf, and it is crossing 4 in full: the word was broken, and the breaking is the doorway through which every hurt in Tests 1, 2, and 4 walked in.
  • The 13.5 billion dinars — a decade-old promise, 0.2% delivered. Since his professorship, through the 2019 campaign and the presidency, Saïed has anchored his anti-corruption project on recovering 13.5 billion dinars he says were stolen from the state — a figure a minister asserted in 2012 without evidence, drawn from a misreading of the Amor commission's report, which estimated revenue losses and never quantified anyone's gains (Noria Research, 2025–2026). The reconciliation commission began work at the start of 2023 mandated to gather that sum within a year; by January 2024 it had recovered 26.8 million dinars. He dismissed commission members, moved arbitration to the National Security Council, and in November 2026 — nearly four years after the commission opened — was still announcing that files were being settled and the whole sum would come (TAP, 3 November 2026; social-media reports of individual settlements, November 2026, unverified in detail and recorded as such). The promise also did collateral work: he repeatedly derailed his own ministers' IMF negotiations on the ground that the reconciliation money would make a loan unnecessary (Noria Research). The money was not there; the negotiations were real.
  • Community companies — the people's engine, at walking pace. The March 2022 law was to seed locally owned companies across the country as the economic arm of the bottom-up project. The National Business Register counted 70 created by August 2024 and 160 by February 2025, in a country of 12 million with some 600,000 unemployed; the iDrak/IWatch two-year assessment is titled "Two Years Between Illusion and Reality" (Noria Research; IWatch, 2024). Not a broken date — he set none — but a flagship whose promised transformation has not occurred, receding rather than approaching.
  • The 2019 self-portrait — the professor above the fray. He campaigned as an independent with no party, no programme in the conventional sense, and no personal ambition for power, presenting himself as the constitution's guardian executing the people's will. The record since is of a presidency that rewrote the constitution to expand its own office, stood for re-election under rules administered by its own appointees, and won 90.69% in a field its institutions had cleared. The distance between the portrait and the record is itself a word-item, and it is charged here rather than in the tests above because it is a promise about who he would be.

The no-good-choice clause, applied where it belongs: Two moments in this file are graded under the clause, and their boundaries are stated exactly. (1) The night of 25 July 2021. The options actually open were these: leave the Mechichi government in place while the COVID response collapsed — Tunisia that month had among the world's highest per-capita death rates, hospitals out of oxygen, the health minister dismissed on 20 July — and a parliament deadlocked for a year, which had failed for a decade to seat the Constitutional Court; or invoke Article 80 for an exceptional, time-limited correction, the door the constitution itself provided; or what he in fact built over the following year, an open-ended refounding. The clause covers the decision that the status quo of that week could not simply continue — a genuine trap, though one he had helped build: his refusal through 2021 to swear in Mechichi's reshuffled ministers was one of the deadlock's moving parts, and that contribution is charged in Tests 1 and 5 above. The clause does not cover the choice of the third door over the second: the 30-day return path existed, was his to take, and its refusal is graded, not excused. (2) The IMF fork of 2022–2023. The options were to sign a USD 1.9 billion programme conditioned on subsidy and wage-bill cuts — in a country whose 1984 bread riots are living memory and whose union confederation had already struck nationally on 16 June 2022 against exactly those cuts — or to refuse and self-finance through the central bank, with the monetary consequences Test 2 records. Both doors carried real harm, and this dossier grades the fork itself as the best possible choice available in the moment being genuinely arguable either way; it is not held against him. What remains gradable, and is graded in Test 5, is the third door he told the public existed — 13.5 billion dinars of reconciliation money that would make the choice painless. It did not exist at that scale, and the arithmetic was knowable when he said it.

Why: His word divides cleanly in two, and the dividing line is the direction of the promise. Every dated commitment about his own project's construction was kept — the roadmap to the month, the institutions built, the IMF line held for five years, the debts paid. Every commitment that limited the seat was broken or receded: the temporary exception became a permanent refounding under a constitution of his own drafting; the oath to the 2014 text was answered by replacing it; the 13.5 billion that justified the whole economic strategy delivered 26.8 million in its first mandated year and is still being announced in November 2026. That composition — word exact where it enlarges the seat, elastic where it binds it — is this series' classic MIXED on Test 5, and the oath item is the heaviest single entry on any MIXED word-card in the series to date. Helped: the households that kept their subsidies and the state that kept its perfect payment record — the kept words had real beneficiaries, named in Test 4's for-shelf. Hurt: the voters of 2019 who were promised a guardian of the constitution they had; the public that budgeted, in his telling, on 13.5 billion dinars that did not arrive; and the ministers and negotiators whose IMF work was repeatedly set aside for a revenue source that existed mainly in his speeches.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Kaïs Saïed is the only ruler in this series who arrived as a constitutional-law professor promising to execute the people's will within the text, and then replaced the text. The whys, test by test, with their ledgers in brief. On one law: the criminal law's incidence tracks political alignment almost without exception — mass conspiracy trials (37 sentenced in April 2025, up to 66 years; Ghannouchi's cumulative sentences now near 50 years), Decree 54 falling on critics, a presidential ballot cleared from seventeen names to three by his own appointees defying the Administrative Court — while a purchasable, unappealable reconciliation track runs beside the courts for men with money; helped, his aligned camp and wealthy accused; hurt, the imprisoned opposition, critical journalists, and the 2024 electorate. On gripping: every referee that ruled against him was dissolved, purged, stripped of jurisdiction, or withdrawn from — the judicial council dissolved, 57 judges dismissed and their court-ordered remedy ignored, the electoral court's competence abolished nine days before the 2024 vote, the African Court exited, the central bank converted by statute into the treasury's zero-interest lender (direct advances up from 1.4 to 16.25 billion dinars in thirty months), and the UGTT's headquarters attacked by his supporters to his public applause-by-defence; hurt, judges, voters, union members, and every dinar-holder. On the wobble: the adjustments are real but marginal — a death sentence pardoned in six days, Decree 54 softened for first-time offenders in September 2026, one migrant convoy brought back to shelter — while every structural pushback (court rulings, 89% abstention, a Nobel-laureate dialogue offer, a union strike, Gabès's uprising) was refused, punished, or named treason and sabotage; the IMF option, considered openly and declined with stated reasons, is expressly not counted against him. On counting: a president who speaks for "the people" as one body named a whole population on his territory a demographic plot in February 2023 and his state then expelled its members to desert borders in documented collective operations; the anti-racism leader who defended them is in prison; Gabès, poisoned for fifty years, was answered with 150 arrests; against that stand real entries on the other shelf — subsidies held and debts paid for the poor, a local elected tier built — and they do not carry the test. On his word: the two axes cross in full view — crossing 1 and crossing 2 in the same act (the IMF refusal: word kept, the poor shielded from a price shock, and the keeping financed by printing-press advances whose risk every dinar-holder carries), crossing 4 at the foundation (the "temporary" exception that became a permanent refounding), and a signature promise, 13.5 billion dinars of recovered theft, that delivered 26.8 million in its mandated year and was still being announced in November 2026. The no-good-choice clause is applied twice and bounded both times: the trap of July 2021 was real, though he had helped build it, and it excuses the intervention, not the permanence; the IMF fork was a genuine both-doors-harm choice, and what is graded is not the fork but the painless third door he described that did not exist. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the September 2026 Decree 54 amendment ends the journalist prosecutions in practice or merely reroutes them; whether the reconciliation commission ever publishes a recovered total within sight of its promise; whether the 2027 budget's further 9.8 billion dinars of central-bank financing proceeds; and whether any of the conspiracy-case sentences are revisited by a court the executive does not appoint.
