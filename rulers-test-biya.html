The Ruler's Test — Paul Biya, President of Cameroon

Subject: Paul Biya — President of the Republic of Cameroon since 6 November 1982, and still the holder of the executive seat in November 2026. The premise in the brief is verified, with three corrections of detail recorded here. First, the counting of his terms: Biya first took the presidency without an election, by constitutional succession on the resignation of Ahmadou Ahidjo in November 1982; he has since won eight presidential elections (1984, 1988, 1992, 1997, 2004, 2011, 2018, 2025), and the mandate proclaimed on 27 October 2025 is his eighth elected term, running to 2032. Second, his age: he was 92 at the election of 12 October 2025 and turned 93 on 13 February 2026 — the world's oldest serving head of state, and the longest-serving non-royal head of state in the world. Third, the succession question is no longer entirely open in the form the brief assumed: a constitutional amendment promulgated on 14 April 2026 created an appointed vice-president who would automatically complete his term in the event of vacancy — but as of this grading, more than seven months later, no vice-president has been named, so the mechanism exists and the person does not. He is graded on the whole tenure since November 1982, weighted to the most recent term and to the 2025–2026 record, which is where the freshest evidence on every test sits. The prime minister (Joseph Ngute, in office since January 2019) heads the government under him; in Cameroon's system the presidency is unambiguously the executive seat — it appoints and dismisses the government, the electoral board, the Constitutional Council, the senior judiciary, the governors, and the heads of the security services — and it is the presidency that is graded. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic of the Test, printed on the card above the five tests, governs this dossier: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one — carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how the holding is done — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), refusing to look when another option became available (Test 3), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4).

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Operation Sparrowhawk (Opération Épervier), the anti-corruption drive launched in 2006, has genuinely jailed men who were once untouchable: over two decades it produced convictions of former ministers, former heads of state corporations, and a former prime minister, Ephraïm Inoni — the most ambitious anti-corruption drive in Cameroon's history, and one whose early targets were insiders of the regime, not opposition figures (US Embassy Yaoundé assessment, 2008; Cameroonian and international reporting across the operation's twenty years).
  • The state's own anti-corruption commission publishes damaging numbers against the state itself: the National Anti-Corruption Commission (CONAC) recorded more than FCFA 228 billion in financial losses linked to corruption between 2019 and 2025, including a record FCFA 114 billion in 2023 — the figures are the government's own, published, not leaked (CONAC annual figures, reported 2026).
  • In February 2026, for the first time in the Anglophone conflict, Cameroonian soldiers were convicted for killing civilians: a Yaoundé military court sentenced three security-force members and a pro-government militia member to between five and ten years for the 14 February 2020 Ngarbuh massacre, in which government forces and allied militiamen killed 21 people, including 13 children and a pregnant woman (Human Rights Watch, 24 February 2026). The trial happened, in public, against the state's own soldiers.

Evidence against:

  • Sparrowhawk's pattern over twenty years is that prosecution follows the loss of favor, not the commission of the act. The operation is a special unit placed under the authority of the presidency itself; its prominent defendants were overwhelmingly officials who had fallen from grace or were seen as potential rivals, and no figure of the sitting inner circle has ever been its target. The Cameroonian public verdict, recorded in 2026 commentary, is that it "only arrests the chicks… not the roosters or the hens" (Dash Talk discussion, May 2026; the selectivity criticism runs from the US Embassy's 2008 assessment — "allegations of political undertones" — through Transparency International commentary to the present). A sitting minister of territorial administration, Paul Atanga Nji, summoned by the Supreme State Audit, refused to appear, saying he was waiting for the president to tell him to — and the president never did (Cameroonian press reporting, recounted 2026). That is the second law stated in one sentence, by its beneficiary.
  • The Glencore file is the limiting case. In November 2022 Glencore's UK subsidiary pleaded guilty in London to paying some €10.5 million (about FCFA 7 billion) in bribes to officials of Cameroon's state oil company SNH and refinery SONARA between 2011 and 2016 to secure preferential crude prices; Glencore paid over £281 million in UK penalties and further sums in the United States, and its former executives face trial in London in 2027. Four years after the payer's conviction, no Cameroonian official has been charged. SNH filed a complaint before the Special Criminal Court in November 2023 asking it to "identify the Cameroonian accomplices"; the recipients remain unnamed under an anonymity clause, SNH's board is chaired by the secretary-general of the presidency, Ferdinand Ngoh Ngoh, and both companies deny wrongdoing (UK Serious Fraud Office case record, 2022; EITI International Secretariat statements; Cameroonian reporting through 2026). The identity of any individual recipient is an ALLEGATION and is recorded as such here; what is documented is the asymmetry — the bribe-payer convicted abroad, the bribe-takers' side of the ledger untouched at home.
  • The one-law failure is written into the constitution for his own person. The April 2008 amendment that abolished presidential term limits also granted the president immunity from prosecution for acts committed in office, and raised the threshold for any treason proceeding to a four-fifths vote of both houses (constitutional revision of April 2008; US Embassy cables 08YAOUNDE346 and 08YAOUNDE1169). Whatever else it did, the revision placed the seat-holder himself above the law he administers.
  • The 2025 election cycle ran the same two tracks. Maurice Kamto — the runner-up of 2018 and the incumbent's principal challenger — was excluded from the October 2025 ballot because the electoral board ELECAM held that his sponsoring party, MANIDEM, had sponsored two candidates; MANIDEM's president Anicet Ekane stated the party sponsored only Kamto, and the documented mechanism was the Ministry of Territorial Administration's recognition of a rival claimant to the party's leadership as the lawful one. The Constitutional Council rejected Kamto's appeal as "unfounded" on 5 August 2025 and its ruling could not be appealed (Human Rights Watch, 30 July and 6 August 2025; Reuters, 5 August 2025). After the vote, the prosecutions ran one way: more than 50 Kamto supporters were arrested outside the Constitutional Council in August 2025; Ekane himself was arrested on 24 October 2025 on "insurrection" and "incitement to rebellion" charges and died in military custody on 1 December 2025 (below, Test 4); and as of this grading, no member of the security forces has been charged over the protest deaths of October–November 2025, on any of the counts in circulation.

Why the grade lands here: One law does not require that every case be won; it requires that the same instrument reach the same conduct regardless of whose it is. The documented pattern across forty-four years is that Cameroon's enforcement instruments — Sparrowhawk, the electoral code, the security charges — reach fallen insiders, opponents, and soldiers in the field, and stop, every time, at the door of those currently in favor: an audit summons a minister can decline, a bribery conviction abroad with no domestic counterpart, an immunity clause for the seat itself. The Ngarbuh convictions and CONAC's published figures are real, and they are on the shelf above; they do not change the direction of the pattern.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: the treasury and the public record, where Sparrowhawk recovered assets and CONAC published the losses; the Ngarbuh victims' families, partially — a verdict exists, though the court rejected their compensation claim (Human Rights Watch, February 2026).
  • Hurt: Maurice Kamto and the voters who would have chosen him, excluded by a party-paperwork ruling; Anicet Ekane, prosecuted under security charges for political support of a candidate; every Cameroonian official who did not take Glencore money and works under the suspicion the unprosecuted file leaves on all of them; the public, whose FCFA 228 billion in documented corruption losses (2019–2025) produced no accounting at the level where the money concentrates.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Supreme Court broke ranks in the state's flagship Anglophone case, on its own motion. On 19 March 2026 the Court — presided over by its president, Justice Marie Louise Abomo — raised a procedural defect suo moto, quashed the appellate judgment that had upheld life sentences for the ten separatist leaders known as the Nera 10 (arrested in Nigeria in January 2018, sentenced by a military tribunal in August 2019), and sent the case back for a fresh appeal hearing before a differently constituted panel (RFI and Cameroonian reporting, 19 March 2026; defence counsel Akere Muna's account). The executive did not overturn or publicly attack the ruling.
  • International referees were admitted to the 2025 election and said what they said: African Union and ECCAS observer missions found the vote "conducted largely in accordance with international standards," a conclusion the opposition disputes and the government did not write (International IDEA, October 2025).
  • One referee is structurally beyond his sole grip: Cameroon shares its central bank, the BEAC, with five other CEMAC states, and its currency with the regional bloc — monetary independence from Yaoundé is built into the arrangement and has never been breached by him.
  • The April 2026 vice-presidency amendment, whatever its purpose (Test 5), contains two written limits on the successor it creates: an appointed vice-president who succeeds may not initiate constitutional changes and may not run in the subsequent presidential election (Reuters, 4 April 2026, from the bill text).

Evidence against:

  • The referees of the 2025 election were his appointees at every step. ELECAM's board is appointed by presidential decree; the Constitutional Council's members are appointed by the president and chaired by Clément Atangana, a presidential appointee. It was this Council that upheld Kamto's exclusion in August 2025, dismissed all eight post-election petitions (for insufficient evidence or lack of jurisdiction), and proclaimed the result on 27 October 2025 (Reuters; BBC; International IDEA, 2025). The opposition's refusal to file or accept — Tchiroma declined to petition a council he considered compromised — is recorded as their position; the structural fact, appointment by the incumbent whose election is being judged, is not contested by anyone.
  • Parliament's own mandate was extended rather than renewed. Legislative and municipal elections due in 2025 were postponed twice by his government; on 23 March 2026 the National Assembly voted to extend its own mandate to 20 December 2026, and only on 9 November 2026 did a presidential decree set the elections for 7 February 2027 (International IDEA country tracker, March 2026; Reuters/AFP reporting, November 2026). For two years Cameroonians have been represented by a legislature whose electoral mandate expired — extended under a presidency that benefits from its compliance.
  • The April 2026 constitutional amendment — the most consequential change since 2008 — was rushed through a ruling-party-dominated joint session over the Easter weekend: the special commission had under two hours to examine the text, no opposition amendment was adopted, and the bill passed 200–18 with an opposition boycott; Biya signed it on 14 April 2026 (International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, April 2026; Reuters, 4 April 2026; Cameroonian parliamentary reporting). A succession rule for 30 million people was written at the speed of a decree.
  • The media's autonomy is bounded by explicit prohibition. On 9 October 2024, after the president had not been seen in public for over a month (he was in Geneva; death rumors circulated), the Minister of Territorial Administration banned all media debate on the president's health as a "national security" matter, ordered governors to set up monitoring cells for private media and social media, and warned that offenders would "face the full force of the law" (Committee to Protect Journalists, 10 October 2024; Al Jazeera, October 2024). The ban remains in force in 2026 — reporting on the fitness of a 93-year-old head of state is, by ministerial order, illegal. The unresolved emblem of the press file is Martinez Zogo, a radio host who reported on corruption, abducted and murdered in January 2023; a prominent businessman and intelligence officers were charged — the charges are ALLEGATIONs, untried to conclusion — and the trial has dragged for years without a verdict (CPJ and Paradigm Initiative Londa reporting, 2023–2026).
  • The internet is treated as a switch the state may throw. In 2017 the government cut internet access in the two Anglophone regions for 93 days. In October–November 2025, nationwide disruptions began on 23 October, and NetBlocks and OONI documented restrictions on TikTok, YouTube, Facebook, Instagram, and Telegram, plus blocking of VPN providers' websites, through the post-election crackdown; the government attributed the disruption to a submarine cable fault, a claim monitors treated as inconsistent with the platform-specific pattern (NetBlocks, October–November 2025; Reuters, 23 October 2025; Paradigm Initiative, Londa 2025 report, April 2026). The dispute over cause is printed as the government states it; the restrictions themselves are measured fact.
  • In October 2026 the authorities suspended the FSNC — the party of the runner-up, Issa Tchiroma Bakary — removing the principal challenger formation from legal political life a year after the election (AFP/Reuters reporting via Cameroon Online, November 2026).

Why the grade lands here: A referee who owes his appointment to one player, and whose rulings in the decisive matches all fall one way — exclusion upheld, petitions dismissed, result proclaimed — is being gripped whether or not any instruction is ever documented; the appointment structure is the grip. The Supreme Court's Nera 10 ruling shows the judiciary is not wholly inert, and it is credited at full strength; but the same men's retrial has been adjourned repeatedly through 2026 (the August 2026 hearing was the third consecutive adjournment) and they remain in Kondengui prison, eight years after arrest. Against one procedural correction stand the health-discussion ban, the platform blocks, the suspended party, the self-extended parliament, and a succession amendment passed in an Easter weekend. The holding is a grip.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: the ruling CPDM, whose appointees judged its elections and whose parliament extended itself; the presidency, which acquired a succession instrument of its own choosing.
  • Hurt: the Nera 10, still detained through an adjourned retrial; Cameroonian journalists, working under a standing ban on the most newsworthy subject in the country and the unclosed Zogo file; voters in the postponed legislative elections, unrepresented by anyone they chose for a current term; the FSNC's members, whose party was suspended by administrative act.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The 2019 Major National Dialogue was a real adjustment, and its outputs were delivered. After three years of Anglophone crisis, the government convened the dialogue in Yaoundé (30 September – 4 October 2019); it produced the December 2019 law granting the North-West and South-West a special status within the decentralization framework, and the release of detainees — 333 Anglophone-related prisoners freed, and Maurice Kamto and his co-accused released on 5 October 2019, ending the prosecution that had followed his 2018 victory claim (African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights session statement, October 2019; contemporaneous reporting). The pushback was answered with a table, a law, and open prison doors — once.
  • In April 2026 he received Pope Leo XIV's visit, which included the Anglophone regions and raised documented hopes of renewed peace talks (International Crisis Group, 16 April 2026). The visit was welcomed, not obstructed.
  • The electoral calendar, after two postponements, was finally restored by his own decree: on 9 November 2026 legislative and municipal elections were set for 7 February 2027 (Reuters/AFP, November 2026). A deadline he had twice moved, he has now publicly re-set.
  • The Supreme Court's March 2026 annulment of the Nera 10 sentences (Test 2) was an institutional pushback against his own security state's flagship prosecution, and it stood — no retaliation against the court appears in the record.

Evidence against:

  • The founding wobble of the tenure's second half was answered with force and then with the change the street was protesting. When riots driven by prices and by his announced intention to abolish term limits swept the country in February 2008, the response killed at least 40 people on the government's own count and over 100 on the counts of human-rights organizations and the Catholic Church — two counts, printed separately, never reconciled — with over 1,600 arrested; parliament then passed the amendment in April 2008 with virtually no debate (US Embassy cable 08YAOUNDE1169; Fomunyoh testimony to the US Senate Foreign Relations Committee). The adjustment made was to the constitution, not to the grievance.
  • The Anglophone wobble began peacefully and was answered into a war. In late 2016 lawyers, teachers, and students struck over the erosion of the common-law and English-education systems; the response was arrests of the movement's leaders, a 93-day internet shutdown, and the banning of its organizations — and by late 2017 Cameroon had an armed separatist conflict that has now run ten years (US House Human Rights Commission briefing, June 2018; International Crisis Group reporting). The 2019 dialogue, credited above, excluded the armed separatists and the exiled leadership by design, and the war intensified after it. When another option became available — internationally facilitated talks, including the Swiss process and the Canadian-facilitated process announced in January 2023 — the government let the Canadian process collapse within months, disputing that it had ever mandated it, and has never openly considered negotiation with the separatist leadership; the official position remains that the crisis is a law-enforcement matter against "terrorists." Refusing to look is what Test 3 grades; the look was refused, repeatedly, over ten years.
  • The 2024 wobble — a president unseen for six weeks — was answered by denial and prohibition: officials insisted he was in "excellent health," banned the subject from the media (Test 2), and he returned on 21 October 2024 behind a heavy military deployment in the capital (CPJ, October 2024; Georgetown Security Studies Review). The question was legitimate — he missed the UN General Assembly and the Francophonie summit — and it was never answered, only forbidden.
  • The 2025 election wobble was answered with live fire and exile. When Tchiroma claimed victory on 14 October and his supporters protested the proclaimed result, security forces responded with live ammunition across several cities; snipers were filmed on rooftops near Tchiroma's Garoua home, where supporters had massed to protect him from arrest (International Crisis Group, October–November 2025). In his 6 November 2025 inaugural address Biya offered condolences, blamed "irresponsible politicians," praised the security forces' "professionalism," and assured the country that "order will reign" — he did not address the complaints of excessive force, order an inquiry into the protest deaths, or concede any defect in the process, which he called "satisfactory" (BBC, 7 November 2025; swearing-in speech, Presidency of the Republic). Tchiroma fled to The Gambia in late November 2025, hosted "purely on humanitarian grounds" (Gambian government statement via BBC, 24 November 2025). The pushback's leader is in exile, its party suspended (October 2026), its dead uninvestigated.
  • The no-good-choice clause was considered for the two hardest files and does not apply to either. For October 2025: the options actually available included an independent audit or recount of polling-station tallies (which civil-society groups were publishing), a public inquiry into the shootings, or the course taken. The first two carried political cost but no real harm to anyone; they were not taken, and no hindsight is needed to see them — they were demanded in the moment, in the open. For the Anglophone crisis: the 2016 strikers' demands (keep common-law courts and English-language education intact) were concedable at no one's expense; the trap — a decade of war — was built by the response, and its origin is part of the record. Where he did face a genuine fork with harm on every side — the 1984 coup attempt's aftermath — the record credits him: he commuted the death sentence of his predecessor Ahidjo to life imprisonment rather than executing him (documented in the standard histories). The clause protects choices among harms; it does not protect the refusal of harmless doors.

Why the grade lands here: Across four decades the pattern is one movement: pushback is treated as a threat to be ended, not information to be answered. The exceptions are real — the 2019 dialogue above all — and they are the proof that adjustment was available to him when he chose it; after the dialogue, on every major wobble since (2023 talks, 2024 absence, 2025 election), the choice was denial, prohibition, or force. A ruler who adjusts once in ten years of wobbles, and doubles down on the rest, fails the test the exceptions define.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: the 333 released Anglophone detainees and Kamto's co-accused of 2019, freed by the one genuine adjustment; Ahidjo, whose life the commutation spared in 1984.
  • Hurt: the February 2008 dead (40 by the government's count; over 100 by rights groups' and the Church's — separate counts, unreconciled); the October–November 2025 protest dead (counts at Test 4) and the up-to-2,000 detained (lawyers' estimate via Human Rights Watch); Tchiroma, exiled; the Anglophone public, whose 2016 grievances were answered into a war now in its tenth year.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Anglophone minority, the north, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The whole map is, at the level of the state's formal claim, counted: the 2019 special-status law recognized the Anglophone regions' distinctiveness in law for the first time; a presidential reconstruction plan for the North-West and South-West followed in 2020; the Far North's war against Boko Haram has been fought with state resources and regional allies for a decade, and Cameroon hosts several hundred thousand refugees from the Central African Republic and Nigeria alongside its own displaced (UNHCR country reporting).
  • The infrastructure of the recent term is national, not regional: the 420 MW Nachtigal hydropower plant — the country's largest — entered service progressively through 2024–2025 and feeds the national grid; the Kribi deep-sea port and the road program Biya cited at Maroua on 7 October 2025 (Mora–Dabanga–Kousseri, Ngaoundéré–Garoua) serve the poorest region, the Far North (presidency campaign speech, 7 October 2025; project reporting). GDP growth ran at about 3.5 per cent in 2025 (Paradigm Initiative, Londa 2025 report).
  • Cameroon under his tenure avoided the fate of several neighbors: no successful coup since 1984, no civil war in the francophone majority regions, and the Bakassi Peninsula dispute with Nigeria settled by law and completed by peaceful transfer in August 2008 rather than by war.

Evidence against:

  • Two of the country's ten regions have been at war for ten years, and their people are counted in the tolls, not in the polity. The International Crisis Group put the Anglophone conflict's death toll at over 7,000 as of April 2026 (an earlier ICG-cited figure, January 2026, was over 6,000 — the counts are cumulative estimates and are printed as their sources date them); OCHA counts 493,402 internally displaced in the two regions in 2026, with more than 76,000 refugees in Nigeria, and about 45 per cent of schools in the two regions not functioning as of February 2026 (OCHA via Cameroonian reporting, 2026). Separatist abuses against civilians are documented and are the separatists' own; the state's share — village burnings documented since December 2017, the Ngarbuh massacre, incommunicado detention and torture at the SED facility in Yaoundé (Human Rights Watch documented 26 such cases in 2018–2019 alone) — belongs to the seat that commands the army. The 2025 official results credited Biya with 86.31 per cent in the North-West and 68.79 per cent in the South-West, regions where separatist lockdowns suppressed voting and his support is, in the Chatham House assessment carried in the April 2026 strategic reporting, "functionally nonexistent" — a count that counts the region as his while its schools stand closed.
  • The post-election dead are counted three ways, and the state's way is the smallest. For the October–November 2025 crackdown: the government acknowledged 16 killed; Cameroon's National Human Rights Commission reported at least 14 killed and more than 1,200 arrested; UN sources put the dead at 48; opposition sources told Human Rights Watch 55. Arrests: the government told the UN Human Rights Committee in October 2026 that police arrested 985 people, of whom courts convicted 66; lawyers cited by Human Rights Watch put detentions at up to 2,000, with some detainees still in custody a year later; the UN-cited figure for arrests exceeds 2,500 (Reuters and UN reporting, October–November 2025; UN Human Rights Committee review of Cameroon, October 2026; Human Rights Watch, 2025–2026). These counts are printed separately and not blended; what no count contains is a single prosecution of a security-force member for any of the deaths.
  • Anicet Ekane's death is the counting failure in one file. The 74-year-old MANIDEM president — a public figure of five decades' standing — was arrested in Douala on 24 October 2025 for supporting Tchiroma, held at the SED in Yaoundé on insurrection charges, and died there on 1 December 2025. His family and lawyers state his oxygen equipment was taken at arrest and he never saw a judge; the government spokesperson stated he died "following an illness" and had received proper medical attention; the government announced an investigation. Human Rights Watch and the UN Human Rights Office called for an impartial investigation and the release of arbitrarily detained protesters; no independent finding on the death appears in the record as of this grading (Human Rights Watch, 3 December 2025; OHCHR statement, 5 December 2025). A man can be arrested for his politics, die in the state's custody, and be answered with a press release: in the accounting of this seat, he did not count.
  • The young are the country's majority and its counted-out class. Cameroon's median age is about nineteen; youth unemployment and underemployment are the grievance Biya himself names in every campaign ("I am well aware of the problems that are of concern to you… the unfulfilled expectations that make you doubt the future," Maroua, 7 October 2025). The October 2025 protests were youth-led, and the state's answer to its young majority was the crackdown above — and a ban on discussing the 93-year-old president's health, the question of their lifetimes. Around two in five Cameroonians live below the national poverty line (World Bank estimates), a share that four decades of growth figures have not brought down decisively.

Why the grade lands here: Counting everyone is tested where counting costs something: in the regions that did not vote for him, in the crowd that disputed his result, in the cell of a detained opponent. On each, the record shows the same arithmetic — the Anglophone regions counted as electoral assets and security problems, not as citizens with a negotiable grievance; the protest dead counted lowest by the state that shot them and not at all in its courts; the detained party leader counted as a security file until he died. The national achievements on the FOR shelf are real, and they are the achievements of the counted; the test is about the rest.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: electricity consumers on the national grid (Nachtigal); the Far North's population, defended against Boko Haram and named first in his campaign; refugees hosted under his state's protection.
  • Hurt: the North-West and South-West — over 7,000 dead on the Crisis Group's April 2026 count, 493,402 displaced on OCHA's 2026 count, a generation's schooling broken (45 per cent of schools not functioning, February 2026); the October–November 2025 protesters — 16 dead on the government's count, 48 on UN sources', 55 on the opposition's, never reconciled and never prosecuted; Anicet Ekane, dead in custody; the young majority, counted as a risk to be managed.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 6 November 1982, with dates; the founding promises, the 2018 and 2025 campaign words, and the eighth term's first year.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The Bakassi word — kept, at cost. Cameroon accepted the International Court of Justice's 2002 judgment and the UN-backed Greentree process, and the Bakassi transfer was completed peacefully in August 2008. A territorial word given to an international process was honored to the end.
  • The 2019 dialogue outputs — delivered as announced. The dialogue was held when promised; the special-status law passed in December 2019; the announced releases happened (Test 3).
  • The 2025 election date — kept. Whatever else is graded elsewhere, the presidential election was held on its constitutional date, 12 October 2025, not postponed — in a term when the legislative elections were twice delayed, the presidential calendar was honored.
  • Nachtigal — delivered. The hydropower program he cited across two campaigns reached service in 2024–2025; the 420 MW plant is generating.
  • The newest words are dated and checkable, and are recorded here so the record can grade them: at his 6 November 2025 inauguration he pledged that youth and women would be "at the heart of my action throughout this seven-year term," announced a Special Plan for the Promotion of Youth Employment with tax facilities for firms hiring the young, promised to intensify the fight against corruption, and assured the country "order will reign" (swearing-in speech, Presidency of the Republic, 6 November 2025). The order, on his definition, was delivered — by the means graded at Tests 3 and 4.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The founding word — "rigour, moralisation, integrity" — is the tenure's broken promise. Biya took office on 6 November 1982 presenting a program of rigour, moralisation, and integrity. Forty-four years on: Cameroon's own anti-corruption commission records FCFA 228 billion in corruption losses in just 2019–2025; the Glencore bribes were paid and never prosecuted at home (Test 1); Cameroon scores 26 of 100 on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI 2024). The word was not merely unkept in a detail; the system the word promised against is the system the record shows.
  • The Anglophone word — given repeatedly, unkept for a decade. Across the 2018 campaign and after, he pledged to resolve the Anglophone crisis and to implement decentralization — the promise on which the 2019 dialogue and special status were partial down-payments. Ten years after the first strikes, the war continues, the special status has not been matched by elected regional government with real powers in the two regions, and no negotiation with the other side has ever been opened (Test 3).
  • The 2008 word to the street — the term-limit safeguard. The 1996 constitution's two-term limit was part of the democratic settlement under which he was re-elected in 1997 and 2004. His 1 January 2008 message announced he would remove it; the removal was carried out over the February riots (Test 3). This is the kept-word-and-hurt crossing in its purest form in the series: he said he would do it, and he did it — the keeping is recorded, and so is what the kept word cost.
  • The reshuffle word — announced, unformed. After the inauguration he announced a government reshuffle; nearly a year later the new government has still not been appointed, and the administration runs on the pre-election cabinet (AFP/Reuters reporting, November 2026).
  • The vice-presidency word — the mechanism kept, the act deferred. The April 2026 amendment was presented as ensuring continuity; seven months on, the post stands empty. A succession instrument whose holder is never named is a promise of continuity that has not yet been performed — recorded as receding, not yet as broken: the term runs to 2032.
  • The electoral word on the legislature — broken twice, now re-given. Deputies and councillors elected in February 2020 for five-year terms sit in 2026 under extensions; the elections were postponed twice. The 9 November 2026 decree setting 7 February 2027 is the third date given for the same promise.

The four crossings, named: 1. Kept his word and it helped: the Bakassi process completed as agreed (2008); the 2019 dialogue's announced releases carried out. 2. Kept his word and it hurt: the 2008 constitutional revision — announced on 1 January 2008 and delivered in April 2008, abolishing the term limit that was the public's safeguard and adding his own immunity. 3. Broke his word and it helped: not documented. No instance appears in the record assembled here of a broken Biya pledge that produced a documented public benefit; the claim is sometimes made for the election postponements (stability), but the benefit is asserted by the government, not documented, and is recorded as assertion, not crossing. 4. Broke his word and it hurt: the founding triad of rigour, moralisation, and integrity against the corruption record; the pledges of an Anglophone settlement against ten years of war.

Why the grade lands here: Test 5 does not require a ruler to keep every word; it grades the pattern of the words that mattered most. Biya's kept words are real and are shelved above — an international process honored, a dialogue's outputs delivered, an election held on its date. But the three words on which his holding was sold to the public across forty-four years — clean government (1982), a settled Anglophone question (2018 and after), and a constitution whose limits bound him too (1996, removed by his own hand in 2008) — are each broken on the documented record, and the eighth term's first year added smaller receding words (a reshuffle unformed, a vice-presidency unfilled, a legislature twice unelected) in the same direction. The trajectory, not any single moment, is the grade.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: Nigeria–Cameroon relations and the Bakassi population, by a transfer completed without war; the beneficiaries of the 2019 releases; grid consumers, by Nachtigal's delivery.
  • Hurt: every Cameroonian who extended trust on the 1982 triad and the 2018 settlement pledges; voters whose 2020 deputies and councillors still sit in 2026; the public that was promised a new government after the 2025 inauguration and, a year on, has the old one.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Paul Biya is the sixth straight-fail card in this series, and the first belonging to a ruler who arrived by constitutional succession, has faced voters eight times, and has never once lost control of his state to a coup, a war in his capital, or a rival government. That is precisely what the card measures: forty-four years of uninterrupted holding, and the holding is the failure. On one law, his enforcement instruments — Sparrowhawk, the electoral code, the security charges — reach the fallen, the opposing, and the expendable, and stop at the door of the favored: an audit summons a minister may decline, a bribery conviction in London with no counterpart in Yaoundé, an immunity clause written for the seat itself in 2008. Without gripping, the referees are his appointees — ELECAM and a Constitutional Council that excluded his main challenger on a party-paperwork ruling, dismissed every petition, and proclaimed him winner; the press works under a standing ban on discussing his health; the internet is restricted at the moments of maximum public interest; parliament extended its own expired mandate; and the one court that broke ranks, annulling the Nera 10 life sentences in March 2026, watches its retrial adjourn through the year with the men still in prison. On the wobble, the exceptions prove the capacity and convict the pattern: he could convene the 2019 dialogue and free its prisoners, and he chose — in 2008, in 2016, in 2024, in 2025 — the riot police, the shutdown, the prohibition, and the live round instead; when another option became available, in the Swiss and Canadian processes and in the Pope's April 2026 visit, it was never openly considered. On counting everyone, the tolls are the ledger: over 7,000 dead in the Anglophone war on the Crisis Group's count, 493,402 displaced in two regions on OCHA's, 45 per cent of their schools shut; the 2025 protest dead counted at 16 by the government, 48 by UN sources, 55 by the opposition, and prosecuted at zero; a 74-year-old party leader dead in military custody, answered with a press release. On his word, the crossings are named in the dossier: Bakassi and the 2019 releases kept and helping; the 2008 revision kept and hurting; a broke-and-helped crossing not documented; and the founding words — rigour, moralisation, integrity (1982), an Anglophone settlement (2018) — broken and hurting, against FCFA 228 billion of his own commission's documented losses and a war in its tenth year. The eighth term's open files are the ones that can still move this card: whether the 7 February 2027 elections are held and how; whether a vice-president is named and by what right he would hold the seat; whether anyone is ever charged — for the Glencore money, for the October 2025 shootings, for the death of Anicet Ekane. The Sun holds on all five. At 93, with a term running to 2032 and no successor named, Paul Biya's card is what a lifetime of holding without letting go looks like when it is finally written down.
