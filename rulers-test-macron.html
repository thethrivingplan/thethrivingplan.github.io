The Ruler's Test — Emmanuel Macron

Subject: Emmanuel Macron, President of the French Republic (in office since 14 May 2017; second and final term runs to 2027) Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Both shelves are shown for every test: the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION; contested findings are described with who disputes them.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Does he apply the same rules to allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders, with no favorites at the source?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His government passed the "trust in political life" laws of 15 September 2017, which banned parliamentarians from employing family members, tightened expense rules and created new conflict-of-interest controls — ethics rules applied to his own majority first (Law No. 2017-1339, 15 September 2017).
  • In the Benalla affair's judicial track, the law did run against the president's own aide: Alexandre Benalla was fired on 20 July 2018, placed under formal investigation, tried, and convicted by a Paris court on 5 November 2021 (three years' imprisonment, two suspended) for his violence against protesters (Élysée announcement, 20 July 2018; Paris criminal court judgment reported by Reuters/AP, November 2021).
  • A sitting justice minister was put on trial by the Cour de Justice de la République: Éric Dupond-Moretti was tried in November 2023 for alleged unlawful conflict of interest and acquitted on 29 November 2023 — the process ran to a verdict against a member of Macron's own government (CJR record; Reuters, 29 November 2023).
  • The Constitutional Council repeatedly ruled against his government's texts and the rulings were accepted: it struck six provisions from the 2023 pension law (14 April 2023) and had earlier struck down the Global Security law's controversial article criminalizing dissemination of police images (May 2021) (Constitutional Council decisions, 2021 and 2023; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022).
  • The Conseil d'État ordered his government to take additional climate measures (Grande-Synthe decision, 1 July 2021), and the government responded within the court's framework rather than defying it (Conseil d'État, 1 July 2021).
  • The 2018 replacement of the ISF wealth tax by the real-estate-only IFI, and the 30% flat tax on capital income, were general laws voted by Parliament and applied by category, not by named beneficiary (Finance Act 2018, Law No. 2017-1837, 30 December 2017).

Evidence against:

  • The Benalla affair began with insider protection: after Benalla was filmed beating a protester on 1 May 2018, the Élysée imposed only a 15-day suspension (served with salary docked against leave, per Senate testimony), reassigned him, and did not report him to prosecutors as Article 40 of the criminal procedure code contemplates; he was fired only after Le Monde published the video on 18 July 2018 (Senate commission of inquiry record, 2018–19; Le Monde, 18 July 2018).
  • The Senate's seven-month inquiry concluded that Macron's senior aides had withheld information from it, and prosecutors opened perjury investigations into Benalla, his associate Vincent Crase, and Élysée chief of staff Patrick Strzoda (Senate commission report, February 2019; Paris prosecutors' statement, 2019).
  • Macron broke his own stated rule for insiders: as a candidate in March 2017 he declared that "a minister placed under investigation must resign," a rule applied in 2017 when François Bayrou left the government amid a preliminary inquiry — but Justice Minister Dupond-Moretti was kept in post after his indictment in July 2021, and Élysée Secretary-General Alexis Kohler was kept in post after the financial prosecutor put him under formal investigation for unlawful taking of interest on 3 October 2022 (candidate statement, March 2017; PNF statement, 3 October 2022; both men deny wrongdoing — ALLEGATION status in Kohler's case, which remained an investigation, not a verdict).
  • His first fiscal package was asymmetric in effect: the ISF-to-IFI conversion cut the number of wealth-tax households from 358,198 to 132,722, and retrospective assessments by the Institut des Politiques Publiques put the annual gain for the richest 10% at roughly €2,600 against roughly €600 for the poorest 10% (largely via activity bonuses), while a €5 monthly cut to the APL housing benefit hit students in the same first autumn — the sequence that fixed the "president of the rich" label on him (Wealth Tax Commission background paper on the ISF/IFI reform; IPP figures as reported in INSEE-based retrospectives, October 2026).
  • A Senate inquiry into consulting firms (March 2022) found that McKinsey — a firm closely associated with Macron's 2017 campaign volunteers — had paid no corporate tax in France for at least ten years and flagged a "phenomenon of dependence" of the state on private consultancies; Macron dismissed the affair's significance (Senate commission of inquiry report, March 2022).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do Parliament, the courts, local government, and his own camp keep their autonomy, or does he govern by decree and punish independence?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The checking institutions visibly worked throughout: the Senate (opposition-controlled) ran hostile inquiries into Benalla and McKinsey; the Constitutional Council and Conseil d'État ruled against his government repeatedly (see Test 1); no court was packed or judge removed (Senate and court records, 2018–2023).
  • After losing his absolute majority in June 2022 (245 seats against the 289 needed), he had to negotiate bill by bill, and some texts passed only with opposition support or were amended against his government's preference (Reuters analysis, 19 June 2022).
  • After the 2024 dissolution he appointed prime ministers from outside his own party — Michel Barnier of Les Républicains and François Bayrou of MoDem — and when the Assembly censured Barnier (4 December 2024) and refused Bayrou confidence (8 September 2025), he accepted the falls and appointed successors rather than obstructing the constitutional process (National Assembly votes, 4 December 2024 and 8 September 2025).
  • Dissent inside his own camp was not criminalized and eventually prevailed on the biggest question: in November 2025 his own prime minister, Sébastien Lecornu, proposed suspending Macron's flagship pension reform, and the Assembly — including votes from the presidential camp — adopted the suspension on 12 November 2025 (Euronews, 12 November 2025; France 24, 16 December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Governing without a vote became routine: Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne used Article 49.3 — which adopts a text unless the Assembly censures the government — 23 times in 20 months, the heaviest use since Michel Rocard, including on every budget (National Assembly's official count of 49.3 uses, closed September 2025).
  • The 2023 pension reform, opposed by roughly two-thirds of the public in polls, was embedded in a social-security budget bill to shorten debate and adopted via 49.3 on 16 March 2023 without an Assembly vote; the government survived the ensuing censure motion by nine votes (Constitutional Council decision summary, 14 April 2023; Al Jazeera/CNN reporting, March 2023).
  • His first-term labour reform was enacted by ordonnances (government decrees authorized in advance) in September 2017, setting the top-down template Reuters described as reforms "drafted by his aides at the Élysée palace without consulting lawmakers or outside stakeholders" (ordonnances of 22 September 2017; Reuters, 19 June 2022).
  • The June 2024 dissolution was a solitary act: announced the night of the European elections, 9 June 2024, with consultations described as "very short" and doubt over whether even his prime minister knew in advance; it produced a hung Assembly of three hostile blocs and a crisis "unprecedented in the Fifth Republic" (Cambridge Political Data Yearbook, France 2024; record of the 2024–2025 political crisis).
  • He then overrode the election's result in choosing the government: on 26 August 2024 he announced he would not appoint Lucie Castets, the candidate of the New Popular Front — the bloc that had won the most seats — departing from cohabitation practice, and kept a caretaker government for 51 days before naming Barnier from the fourth-place bloc (record of the 2024–2025 political crisis; Élysée statement, 26 August 2024).
  • The decree habit outlasted his own camp's promises: PM Lecornu pledged in autumn 2025 not to use 49.3, then reversed the pledge and used it three times in January 2026 to pass the 2026 budget (Boursorama/AFP reporting, January 2026; National Assembly 49.3 tally).
  • Local government's fiscal autonomy was narrowed from Paris: the abolition of the housing tax (taxe d'habitation) on main residences, completed in 2023, removed a tax whose rate mayors set, replacing it with state compensation — a longstanding complaint of mayors' associations (Association des maires de France statements; Finance Acts 2018–2023).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When the street, the voters, or events push back, does he adjust openly, or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The gilets jaunes forced a genuine, costly reversal: after weeks of insisting the fuel-tax rise would go ahead, the government abandoned it entirely on 5 December 2018, and Macron's televised address of 10 December 2018 added a €100 monthly minimum-wage boost, tax-free overtime and bonuses, and cancellation of a pension tax rise — a package of roughly €10–17 billion (AP, 5 and 10 December 2018; Rabobank and PPE Sydney analyses of the concession package).
  • He then submitted himself to the Grand Débat national (January–March 2019), a two-month national consultation of thousands of town-hall meetings which he personally fronted for hours at a time, followed by an income-tax cut of about €5 billion and re-indexation of small pensions (Grand Débat record, 2019; Cambridge University Library reading list summary).
  • His longest-held reform eventually bent: after defending the pension age rise through 2023–2025, he let PM Lecornu suspend the reform until after the 2027 election; the Assembly adopted the suspension on 12 November 2025 and it was folded into the 2026 social-security budget adopted 16 December 2025 (Euronews, 12 November 2025; France 24, 16 December 2025).
  • In New Caledonia, after the May 2024 riots he suspended the contested electoral reform on 12 June 2024 rather than forcing the constitutional congress, and PM Barnier formally scrapped the bill in October 2024 (record of the 2024 New Caledonia unrest; Barnier statement to the National Assembly, October 2024).

Evidence against:

  • On pensions in 2023 he doubled down completely: facing months of strikes and protests of over a million people (first day: 19 January 2023), he refused union pleas to withdraw or pause the reform, and promulgated it in the Official Journal in the night hours immediately after the Constitutional Council's 14 April 2023 ruling — a speed opponents across the spectrum called deliberate provocation (Al Jazeera/AFP, 15 April 2023).
  • His first response to the gilets jaunes was defiance: he publicly ruled out "giving in" through November 2018 while protests spread, conceding only after the 1 December 2018 Paris riots — the worst in the capital since 2005 (AP reporting, November–December 2018).
  • The voters' clearest pushback, the June–July 2024 elections he himself called, was answered by refusal: he declined to appoint the plurality bloc's candidate for prime minister (26 August 2024) and has consistently refused calls to resign, which the constitution does not require but which framed his response as denying the result's meaning (Élysée statement, 26 August 2024).
  • In Mayotte after Cyclone Chido, confronted on 19 December 2024 by residents still without drinking water a week after the storm, he answered angrily on camera — "If it wasn't for France, you would be in way deeper shit, 10,000 times more" — remarks opposition lawmakers across the spectrum called undignified, and which he defended the next day rather than retracting (Reuters, 20 December 2024; AP, 20 December 2024).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Who is inside his count — banlieue residents, Muslims, overseas territories, the rural periphery, the poor — and who is outside it?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The jobs record reached the bottom of the ladder for most of his tenure: unemployment fell from 9.7% when he was elected to 7.1% in 2023, a historic low, driven in part by an apprenticeship boom that disproportionately serves young people without degrees, and the employment rate rose from 65.5% to about 69% (INSEE figures reported by Le Monde, February and August 2026).
  • During COVID-19 his "whatever it costs" policy (March 2020) funded furlough for millions and is credited by INSEE with holding the poverty rate roughly stable through 2020–2021 despite the recession (INSEE poverty analyses, 2021–2022).
  • On New Caledonia, his government's reform had a one-person-one-vote rationale: the "frozen" electorate excluded roughly a fifth of residents — some 25,000 people, including residents of over ten years — from provincial elections, an arrangement the European Court of Human Rights had accepted in 2005 only as a provisional decolonization measure (record of the 2024 New Caledonia unrest; ECHR position of 2005).
  • On Mayotte, after Chido the state passed an emergency reconstruction law (25 February 2025) and a "refoundation" law published 12 August 2025 committing nearly €4 billion over six years to water, housing, health and education in France's poorest department (Mayotte refoundation law, 12 August 2025).
  • For the rural periphery, his government built out France Services public-service houses (over 2,500 by 2024) to restore state presence in areas that produced the gilets jaunes, and the Grand Débat concessions were aimed squarely at rural and peri-urban purchasing power (government France Services reporting; Grand Débat record, 2019).
  • He gave French Muslims an institutional seat rather than only a police file, creating the Forum of Islam of France (February 2022) as an interlocutor body, and framed the separatism policy as targeting Islamist extremism, not Islam itself (Élysée, Les Mureaux speech, 2 October 2020; Forum of Islam of France launch, 2022).

Evidence against:

  • Poverty rose on his watch to a modern record: INSEE put the poverty rate at 15.4% in 2023 — the highest since the series began in 1996, roughly 9.8 million people — with economists noting France was nearly alone in Europe in combining rising employment with rising poverty, because many new jobs are precarious (INSEE, July 2025; Le Monde, August 2026).
  • The banlieues got a burial, not a plan: in May 2018 Macron publicly discarded the €38-billion Borloo report he had himself commissioned for the priority neighbourhoods, announcing no funded plan in its place; mayors of the affected towns described leaving "empty-handed" (Macron speech at the Élysée, 22 May 2018; reaction of suburban mayors reported at the time).
  • The 2021 "separatism" law (Law No. 2021-1109 of 24 August 2021) — which expanded state power to monitor and dissolve associations and required a "contract of republican values" for subsidies — is formally religion-neutral, but the US State Department's religious-freedom report records that critics say it targeted and stigmatized Muslims, noting Macron proposed it against "Islamist separatism"; the Council of Europe's anti-racism rapporteur warned it "inherently stigmatises" Muslims, and the law's framework was used to dissolve the CCIF, France's main anti-Islamophobia collective (Council of State upheld the dissolution, September 2021). The government disputes the stigmatization charge, maintaining the law targets extremism, not a faith (US State Department International Religious Freedom Report 2022; PACE rapporteur statement, 2021; Freedom House, 2022).
  • New Caledonia's Kanak population experienced his counting as dilution: Paris pushed the electoral reform through the Assembly on 14 May 2024 despite Kanak leaders' objections that it broke the Nouméa Accord's spirit; the ensuing unrest killed 14 people, caused about €3 billion in damage, and ended only with the reform's suspension and scrapping (record of the 2024 New Caledonia unrest).
  • Mayotte's residents experienced the count as neglect before Chido and as condescension after it: the archipelago entered the storm as France's poorest department with chronic water shortages, the official death toll (35–40) was widely disbelieved locally because shantytown residents were unaccounted for — the true toll remains CONTESTED — and Macron's December 2024 visit produced the "10,000 times more" exchange rather than an acknowledgment (Reuters/AP, December 2024; Mayotte toll reporting, December 2024–2025).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

2017 promises vs. the record, across both terms.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His European word was substantially kept: the September 2017 Sorbonne agenda's centerpiece — a common eurozone financial capacity — was realized beyond its original form in the €750 billion Next Generation EU recovery fund agreed in July 2020, financed by common EU debt, after Macron persuaded Chancellor Merkel to co-sponsor it; "European sovereignty" became standard EU doctrine on his watch (Sorbonne speech, 26 September 2017; European Council agreement and Macron–Merkel press conference, 21 July 2020; UK in a Changing Europe assessment, 2024).
  • The 2017 economic transformation he campaigned on was largely enacted as promised: labour-code reform (2017), corporate tax cut from 33% to 25%, the ISF-to-IFI conversion and capital flat tax, and unemployment insurance and apprenticeship reforms all passed in recognizable form (2017 programme; Finance Acts 2018–2022; Le Monde retrospective, 2026).
  • On pensions he did, in the end, deliver an enacted rise in the retirement age — his 2022 manifesto promised 65; the 2023 law delivered 64 — rather than abandoning the commitment under protest (2022 manifesto; Law of 14 April 2023).
  • He kept the procedural promise attached to the Citizens' Climate Convention in part: of its 149 proposals he publicly accepted 146 in June 2020, using only his three announced "jokers," and a Climate and Resilience Law carrying many of them was enacted on 22 August 2021 (Élysée response to the Convention, 29 June 2020; Law No. 2021-1104).

Evidence against:

  • The exemplary-republic promise was broken by his own hand: candidate Macron's March 2017 rule that an indicted minister must resign was not applied to Dupond-Moretti (2021) or Kohler (2022), both kept in post (see Test 1).
  • The climate "without filter" promise was diluted in the keeping: by Reporterre's March 2021 count, only about 15 of the 149 proposals were taken up unchanged and roughly 54% were dropped or deeply altered; the Convention's own members graded the government's response 3.3 out of 10, and the promised constitutional referendum on climate was abandoned on 6 July 2021 after the Senate refused the wording (Reporterre count, March 2021; Convention members' evaluation, February 2021; Justice Ministry statement, 5 July 2021).
  • The style promise was not kept: in the 2022 campaign he pledged a "new method" of governing with greater consultation, yet his second term opened with the 49.3 pension adoption, and Reuters' verdict on the first term — reforms drafted at the Élysée "without consulting lawmakers" — remained the standard description of the second; the consultative National Council of Refoundation he created in September 2022 faded without changing the method (Reuters, 19 June 2022; record of the CNR, 2022–2023).
  • The full-employment pledge failed and was quietly dropped: his 2022 goal of ~5% unemployment by 2027 disappeared from the government agenda without announcement, while INSEE recorded the rate climbing back to 7.9% by late 2025 and 8.3% by mid-2026 — above where it stood in 2023 and with youth unemployment back above 22% (Le Monde, 10 February 2026 and 20 August 2026, reporting INSEE data).
  • His pension word did not hold across time in either direction: the 2017 programme proposed a universal points system with no headline age rise; the 2019 points plan was shelved in 2020; the 2022 campaign promised 65; the 2023 law set 64; and in 2025 he accepted the reform's suspension until after 2027 — five positions in nine years on the same question (2017 and 2022 programmes; Law of 14 April 2023; suspension adopted 12 November 2025).
  • The 2022 promise to voters who re-elected him as a barrier against the far right — "this vote obliges me" (24 April 2022) — sat alongside a 2022 legislative campaign in which his camp refused a clear "republican front" against National Rally candidates, after which the RN entered the Assembly with 89 seats and grew to 142 in 2024 (victory speech, 24 April 2022; legislative election results, 2022 and 2024).

Overall Summary

Macron's record splits cleanly by direction: outward and downward, his word largely held — the European commitments were delivered beyond their letter, and the 2017 economic programme was enacted much as written — while inward, toward his own institutions, the grip tightened over time: 23 uses of Article 49.3 under Borne alone, a flagship reform adopted without a vote and promulgated overnight, a dissolution decided alone whose result he then declined to honor in choosing a government, and his own ethics rule waived for the two closest men around him. He does answer the wobble — the fuel tax, the Grand Débat, the scrapped Caledonian reform and the suspended pension law are real concessions — but characteristically late, after defiance, and at the price of the crisis deepening first. On counting everyone, the ledger is genuinely two-sided: historic lows in unemployment and real money for Mayotte and rural services, against a record poverty rate, a shelved plan for the banlieues, and overseas citizens who met the state first as an electoral reform or a disaster lecture.
