The Ruler's Test — Gaston Browne, Prime Minister of Antigua and Barbuda

Subject: Gaston Alphonso Browne — Prime Minister of Antigua and Barbuda since 13 June 2014, and simultaneously Minister of Finance, Corporate Governance and Public Private Partnerships. The seat graded is the premiership: Antigua and Barbuda's Governor-General is a ceremonial head of state, while executive power — the budget, the public service, the security forces, and the finance ministry Browne holds personally — sits with the prime minister. Browne, a former banker who has represented St. John's City West in Parliament since 1999, led the Antigua and Barbuda Labour Party (ABLP) to victory in 2014 (14 of 17 seats), 2018 (15), and January 2023 (9 — a near-loss that left him governing on a two-seat margin). He then called a snap general election nearly two years early — announced on 21 March 2026, Parliament dissolved on 1 April, voted on 30 April 2026 — and won a fourth consecutive term with 15 of 17 seats and 60.79% of the popular vote on 62.41% turnout, the first Antiguan prime minister to win four consecutive general elections (Antigua and Barbuda Electoral Commission results; Reuters, 1 May 2026). He was sworn in on 2 May 2026; his cabinet was sworn in on 5 May 2026 under an oath from which references to the monarchy had been removed. He is graded on the full twelve-year tenure, including the first six months of the fourth term — a period in which his government hosted the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in St. John's (1–4 November 2026) and was simultaneously negotiating the end of the Citizenship by Investment Programme under European and American pressure. Two features of the seat frame every test below: it is a very small state, in which the prime minister's household is itself part of the government (his wife, Maria Browne, is an elected MP and the Minister for Housing, Works and Urban Renewal), and it is a twin-island state, in which the smaller island — Barbuda, some 1,800 people — holds a land system and an elected council of its own that the central government has spent a decade legislating around. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The law has reached his own camp's personnel, repeatedly, at the moment it cost him. Asot Michael, his Minister of Tourism, Economic Development, Investment and Energy, was arrested at Heathrow in October 2017 and questioned in a British investigation touching an alleged bribery plot (Michael denied wrongdoing; no charge against him resulted in the record assembled here); Browne removed him from the cabinet, and the party later expelled him in 2021 — Michael won his St. Peter seat back as an independent in January 2023 and remained the government's sharpest parliamentary critic until his murder in November 2024 (Antigua Observer reporting, 2017–2018; Wikipedia, 2025 St. Peter by-election). In 2020 his Education Minister was relieved of his ministerial position after being charged with a criminal offence — the charge recorded here as an ALLEGATION, denied by the minister (Antigua Observer, December 2020).
  • His own Director of Public Prosecutions was not protected. When DPP Anthony Armstrong — in post since 2005 — was found guilty of professional misconduct by Jamaica's General Legal Council in February 2022 (false attestation of a property document) and then criminally charged in Jamaica over the underlying property sales (charges recorded as ALLEGATIONs; Armstrong's defence denies fraud and notes the disciplinary process upheld no fraud finding), Browne confirmed publicly that Armstrong would not return as DPP and that he had agreed to retire: "My government could not allow Armstrong to continue to serve in this position given the accusation against him" (Antigua Observer, 2022–2023; Jamaica Court of Appeal upheld the misconduct finding, May 2026).
  • The citizenship programme's one famous beneficiary was pursued, not sheltered. Mehul Choksi, an Indian businessman who obtained Antiguan citizenship through the Citizenship by Investment Programme in November 2017 and left India days before the Punjab National Bank fraud became public in January 2018, was made the subject of citizenship-revocation proceedings initiated by Browne's government in October 2019 on allegations of concealment in his application; Browne publicly called him a "crook" in 2021 and said he would be extradited once legal processes ran (IACHR Report No. 237/23, October 2023; Indian and Caribbean reporting, 2019–2021). Choksi's alleged fraud is an ALLEGATION — he denies it and has not been convicted — but the revocation file is a documented act of the seat against a man who had paid into its own programme. He was arrested in Belgium in April 2025; in April 2026 the Antwerp appeal court advised extradition on most counts, with the Belgian government's decision still pending in August 2026 (Amar Ujala and Indian reporting, 2025–2026).
  • On the tenure's largest corruption allegation — the Alfa Nero proceeds (below) — the strongest judicial test so far ran in his favour: on 30 March 2026 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit upheld the quashing of subpoenas that had sought American banking records for Browne, his wife, his son, and Antiguan officials, ruling on the procedural usability of the evidence and making no finding on the merits of the underlying claims (SuperyachtNews, 2 April 2026). No court, in any jurisdiction, has found that Browne or his family took money from the sale.
  • On a smaller documented discretion — duty-free vehicle concessions, long criticised as a favour bank — Browne stated in 2026 that he has never refused an eligible applicant and signalled willingness to codify the policy in law (Antiguan Herald, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Alfa Nero file: the state seized the 82-metre superyacht in April 2023 under legislation Parliament passed to declare it abandoned, after its Russian-linked beneficial owner, Andrey Guryev, was sanctioned over the invasion of Ukraine. A US$67.6 million purchase agreed in 2023 collapsed; at auction in July 2024 the vessel sold for US$40 million to a Turkish buyer. The government states the proceeds went to the Consolidated Fund and to debts owed to regional financial institutions, and Browne states flatly, "There is no missing money" — but as of April 2026 the government had not publicly identified which debts were settled or itemised the application of the proceeds (SuperyachtNews, April 2026; Antiguan Herald, 1 July 2026). The claims that the sale was steered and that commissions or a US$6 million kickback were paid — advanced by Guryev's daughter Yulia Guryeva-Motlokhov in U.S. court filings and by the financial-watchdog publisher OffshoreAlert in May 2026 — are ALLEGATIONs, denied in full by Browne ("Not a scintilla of evidence"), and no court has tested them on the merits; a Florida magistrate judge nonetheless allowed a fresh round of U.S. discovery aimed at the prime minister's son and the broker on 7 September 2026 (U.S. court reporting, September–October 2026). What is documented, and graded here, is narrower than the allegations: the largest single asset transaction of the tenure closed at roughly 60% of an earlier agreed price, and its proceeds have never been publicly accounted line by line. Browne's own response to the file has included a US$10 million defamation claim in Antigua against the opposing side's law firm, Boies Schiller Flexner, and its attorney Martin De Luca (SuperyachtNews, April 2026).
  • The citizenship programme that produced Choksi was graded by its customers' outcomes: the European Commission's eighth Visa Suspension Mechanism report (adopted 19 December 2025) records Antigua and Barbuda's CIP rejection rate at 1.7% for 2024 — the lowest of the five Eastern Caribbean programmes — and treats the schemes' due diligence as a standing concern (European Commission reporting via industry and Antiguan government statements, 2025–2026). Antigua's own account of the Choksi approval is that screening "produced no derogatory information" available at the time; Indian officials questioned whether inquiries ever reached the agencies holding older complaints — the dispute is recorded as contested (Citizenship by Investment Unit statements; Indian official statements, 2018). A programme that sells the state's own citizenship at a 98.3% approval rate is administering two laws in practice: a strict one for those screened out, and a near-automatic one for those who pay.
  • The seat and the household are not separated. His wife holds the housing portfolio — the ministry responsible for his flagship housing promise (Test 5) — and in 2026 the government's own Director-General of Communications publicly argued that, were Gaston Browne removed from the equation, "there is no other individual… who could be the next Prime Minister" than Maria Browne, a statement the Antigua Observer's reporting describes as upending the party's expected succession (Antigua Observer, 2026). Both Brownes won their seats at the ballot box, in 2023 and again in 2026; the graded fact is structural, not electoral: in a state this small, the family of the seat is inside the seat's own chain of accountability.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts rule against him and the rulings stand. In April 2022 the Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court issued an interim declaration that his own party had not selected its St. Peter candidate in accordance with the party's rules — in favour of his expelled rival Asot Michael — and in December 2022 the High Court nullified the ABLP executive's disciplinary measures against Michael outright (Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court and High Court decisions, 2022). In February 2024 the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council — Antigua's final court — held that two Barbudan litigants, John Mussington and Jacklyn Frank, had standing to challenge the development consent for the Paradise Found (Peace, Love and Happiness) resort project, against the position of Browne's government and its development authority; the substantive case was heard in 2026 with a decision still pending at grading (JCPC, February 2024). In June 2022 the same court decided the Barbuda communal-title question for his government ([2022] UKPC 25) — the referees have ruled both ways, which is what referees are for.
  • He has lost in his own courts as a litigant. A High Court judge ruled against Browne in a defamation suit brought by a former ambassador, Dr. Isaac Newton, over the prime minister's published comments, with damages to be assessed; at that point six further defamation claims against Browne by political opponents were pending (Antigua Observer, reporting the judgment). Other claims against him have been dismissed on fair-comment and truth defences (Antigua Observer, Vere Bird III case) — outcomes in both directions, delivered by judges he cannot remove.
  • The elections that keep returning him are certified by outsiders he invited. The Commonwealth Observer Group's final report on the 30 April 2026 election found the process "peaceful and transparent," polling procedures generally followed, and freedoms of association, assembly, expression and movement largely respected; the OAS Electoral Observation Mission's first report (1 May 2026) likewise commended a "transparent and orderly" election and ABEC's openness (Commonwealth Observer Group final report, 2026; OAS/EOM, May 2026). The central bank is the regional Eastern Caribbean Central Bank, outside his appointment power in any practical sense, and no interference with it appears in the record assembled here.
  • Critical media publish daily and at length — the Antigua Observer's coverage of his defamation losses, the Barbuda stand-offs, and the Alfa Nero allegations is itself part of the evidence base of this dossier.

Evidence against:

  • The prime minister is a serial defamation litigant against critics and the press, and says so as a threat. In January 2023, campaigning against Asot Michael, he sued Michael for defamation and told a rally that "ZDK, Observer and every single media organisation that slander Gaston Browne will have to pay" — naming media houses that carried his opponent's advertisements as defendants-in-waiting (Antigua Observer, January 2023). The pattern runs the whole tenure: suits and pre-action threats against opposition figures (Harold Lovell, 2020 — who sued Browne in return over remarks made on Browne's own weekly radio programme, the Browne and Browne Show), against an opposition MP who shared a photograph of a protest placard (Antigua Observer), against OffshoreAlert's publisher in May 2026, and the US$10 million claim against Boies Schiller Flexner (Test 1). The Commonwealth Observer Group flagged "polarisation of the media environment" and "polarised and personal attacks" in both its 2023 and 2026 reporting (Commonwealth Observer Group, 2026). A prime minister who answers coverage with writs is not closing newspapers; he is pricing criticism, in a country where the price of defending a libel suit is beyond most citizens — the two Barbudan land litigants, for scale, were left owing the government EC$141,000 in costs (Test 4).
  • The electoral referee's independence remains unarmoured, on the observers' own repeated findings. The Commonwealth's 2026 final report renewed the 2023 group's call for constitutional protections for the Electoral Commission's independence and for campaign-finance legislation — Antigua has no campaign-finance disclosure law — and urged that the Boundaries Commission be empowered to redraw constituencies that "have remained largely unchanged since 1984 despite significant demographic change" (Commonwealth Observer Group final report, 2026). Twelve years and four victories into his tenure, none of these three referee-strengthening reforms has been enacted; the map, the money rules, and the commission's unprotected status are the ones under which he keeps winning.
  • Parliament's check is thin by arithmetic and, in one documented episode, by the chair. With 15 of 17 elected seats plus government senators, his majority is constitutionally capable of amending almost anything; and during Asot Michael's final term, the Speaker expelled the independent MP from the House for three sittings amid his feud with the government — a sanction the by-election record describes as imposed by a "government-backed speaker," a characterisation the government side disputes (Wikipedia, 2025 St. Peter by-election, summarising the parliamentary record). The Barbuda Council — an elected body with constitutionally recognised authority under the Barbuda Local Government Act — has had its central function, land administration, legislated around by the Paradise Found (Project) Act 2015 and the Crown Lands Regulation Act 2018, and in 2026 by a centrally run land registry it formally rejects (Barbuda Council statement, May 2026). That is grip exercised by statute rather than by hand; the statute book is his majority's to write.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His most famous broken deadline was owned in public, with reasons, while it was breaking. On the 500-homes promise (Test 5), Browne told the country directly that the 500-days initiative had been abandoned because the first revenues had to go to inherited debts — the IMF, the Caribbean Development Bank, and Chinese EXIM arrears that underwrote the airport and port — and restated the commitment in revised form: "It was not a deceptive promise, it was a real promise and we will deliver the 500 homes before the end of the term" (Antigua Observer, June 2017). The explanation does not rescue the promise; it is recorded here because the adjustment was made openly rather than by denial.
  • The Cuba vote disclosure (October–November 2026) is an unusually candid account of a capitulation. Browne told his radio audience that his government had decided to support the annual UN resolution against the U.S. embargo of Cuba, received a call from the U.S. State Department warning that support could jeopardise the visa-restriction negotiations, and reversed the instruction — Antigua and Barbuda was ultimately recorded as absent from the 28 October 2026 vote. "We had decided to support Cuba on a resolution recently and changed our position because we had a call from the State Department advising us that they're making progress in the negotiations and if we supported Cuba, that it would undermine the negotiations, so we changed our position" (UPI reporting of Browne's radio remarks, 12 November 2026). A ruler who hides a foreign-pressure reversal does not narrate it himself on air; the wobble was answered in the open, even though the adjustment itself had been made in private, under pressure, and explained only afterwards.
  • On the Citizenship by Investment Programme, a decade of sovereignty-first defiance bent — openly — when the price was named. The European Commission's letter of 25 June 2026 requested phase-out by 1 June 2028 under the revised EU Visa Suspension Mechanism (Regulation (EU) 2025/2441, in force 30 December 2025), which makes operating an investor-citizenship scheme itself a ground for suspending visa-free access. Browne published the correspondence (7 July 2026), told the public the choice in plain terms — "we can continue our programs as we wish, but we would not continue to enjoy visa-free access into the Schengen area" — predicted on 24 October 2026 that suspension was "very likely," and by letter of 30 October 2026 offered closure by the end of 2030 in exchange for a longer transition and EU financial and technical support (Antigua Observer, October–November 2026; Office of the Prime Minister statement, July 2026). The EU had not accepted the 2030 terms at grading; the movement from defiance to a dated, public negotiating position is the adjustment graded here.
  • The water crisis was answered against his own cabinet. After commissioning a second Seven Seas reverse-osmosis plant, Browne announced in January 2026 that production could now meet demand, named distribution as the failure, and declared that the responsible minister, Melford Nicholas, and APUA's senior management "will be held accountable" — urging the public, in his phrase, to "blaze the minister" (Antigua Observer reporting of the prime minister's statement, January 2026). Whatever its theatricality, the failure was conceded by the seat, not denied by it.
  • An investor's pushback was answered against the investor: on 21 November 2026 Browne announced that the government had rejected Royal Caribbean's proposed EC$100 million Fort James development because the company wanted exclusive access to the beach, and would instead redevelop Fort James as a publicly accessible beach park (CMC, 22 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Barbuda is the standing counter-example: every pushback from the sister isle has been answered with escalation. After the Privy Council closed the communal-title claim in June 2022, the government proceeded with the development programme the claim had challenged. In January 2025, when Barbudans blockaded the Louis Hill housing development site with government trucks, riot-squad officers were deployed to the site and the Solicitor General wrote to the Barbuda Council chair warning that "any further resistance or action that may be taken to frustrate this development will not be tolerated" (Antigua Observer, January 2025). In May 2026 the elected Barbuda Council formally rejected the central government's land-registry and land-sale plan — "Barbuda's Lands are NOT for Sale" — and Cabinet's published notes nonetheless recorded preparations to launch the registry "on or about 31st August 2026," with the Council's challenge pending in court (Barbuda Council statement and Cabinet Notes, May 2026). At no point in the decade-long file is there a documented instance of the government pausing the Barbuda programme to negotiate the island's consent.
  • A lost seat was answered first with litigation. Kelvin "Shugy" Simon won St. Mary's South for the UPP in January 2023; the result was challenged by election petition, Simon resigned the seat in mid-2023 rather than fight the petition to judgment, and the ABLP won the October 2023 by-election. The sequence was lawful, and the by-election was the voters' own verdict; Simon's recorded characterisation — "he did not respect the will of the people and took me to court" — is printed as the loser's account, contested by the government's position that the petition raised genuine eligibility questions (Antigua Observer). The pattern is nonetheless the one this test grades: the first instrument reached for was the court, not the campaign.
  • Criticism itself is the pushback he answers hardest. The defamation docket (Test 2) is also a wobble record: journalists, publishers, opposition MPs, and a U.S. law firm have each been met with suits or public threats of suits, including the January 2023 rally warning to media houses carrying an opponent's advertisements. Where the street marched — some 2,000 people joined a UPP cost-of-living march in 2017 — his recorded response was dismissal: "an exercise in futility" (Antigua Observer, 2017). Adjustment, on this record, is something he does for creditors, courts, and superpowers; for domestic critics, the answer is the writ or the shrug.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Barbudans, the opposition's voters, the poor, and those who share the streets — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The wage floor moved, for everyone, on the dates announced. In March 2026 the government raised the national minimum wage from EC$9.00 to EC$11.50 per hour effective 1 April 2026 — described by Browne as one of the largest single increases ever applied — as a phased step toward a previously announced EC$13.00 target; an interim 5% salary increase for public servants took effect at the end of March 2026, a matching 5% increase for pensioners at the end of April 2026, and two rounds of sales-tax (ABST) relief were scheduled around the school year (CMC, 20 March 2026; Cabinet statements, March 2026). Retroactive pay owed for 2018–2023 was substantially discharged: EC$28.7 million disbursed by January 2026 across established staff, non-established workers, weekly-paid workers, pensioners and separated employees, with a target of completing all outstanding payments by the end of March 2026 (Cabinet Notes, 25 March 2026). The year-end Dollar Barrel and ABST-reduction programmes were continued for December 2026 (Cabinet Notes, 18 November 2026).
  • His government's signature international work counts people who cannot vote for him. Antigua and Barbuda co-founded the Commission of Small Island States on Climate Change and International Law, whose request produced the ITLOS advisory opinion of May 2024, and Browne's climate advocacy — carried to the COP31 leaders' segment in Antalya in November 2026 — is conducted explicitly on behalf of small-island populations with no seat at his table (Government of Antigua and Barbuda statements, 2024–2026). Domestically, the rejection of Royal Caribbean's exclusive-access beach club at Fort James (Test 3) was justified on the record as protecting public access to a public beach (CMC, November 2026).
  • Barbuda has received real investment on his watch: the island's new international airport opened in October 2024, ending its dependence on a grass-strip-era facility, and the Louis Hill project at the centre of the 2025 stand-off is a housing development — hurricane-resilient homes, a primary school and a cultural centre in the government's description, with US$1 million allocated by the Steve Morgan Foundation Fund for resilient homes in January 2025 (Antigua Observer, January 2025). The government's case on the land itself is stated at full strength in Test 4's against-shelf, where it belongs: that individual freehold is the precondition of Barbudan prosperity, and that plots are being offered to Barbudans at $1.

Evidence against:

  • Barbuda's communal land system — the institution by which some 1,800 Barbudans have held their island in common for centuries, codified in the Barbuda Land Act 2007's declaration that all land in Barbuda "shall be owned in common by the people of Barbuda" — has been dismantled by his government's statutes over the community's sustained objection. The Paradise Found (Project) Act 2015 nullified the Act's critical sections to clear a 99-year lease of 391 acres to Paradise Found LLC for a resort (US$5.2 million payable to the central government, per the Privy Council's own summary); the Crown Lands Regulation Act 2018 then supplied the framework under which, the Privy Council held in [2022] UKPC 25, the 2007 Act's ownership provisions had been overtaken. When Walker and Frank carried the constitutional claim to London, it was dismissed on 13 June 2022 as having no realistic prospect of success, and the two men — the island's MP and its Council chairman, funding the case privately — were left owing the government over EC$140,000 in legal costs (JCPC judgment, 13 June 2022; Antigua Observer). Browne's recorded comment on the ruling was that the communal-ownership case had always been a "frivolous argument" and an "ownership myth" "weaponised" by his opponents (Antigua Observer, June 2022).
  • The counting-out is continuing, not historical. The elected Barbuda Council — which the BPM retained in the 2025 Barbuda Council election, even as the ABLP was sweeping Antigua — formally rejected the land registry and land sales in May 2026 and states that the matter is before the courts; the government's answer is a registry launch date and the clearing of 216 acres for sale (Barbuda Council statement; Cabinet Notes, May 2026). The government's offer — that Barbudans may buy individual plots of the island, initially at $1 — is, to the community holding the other position, an instruction to purchase what is already theirs; Walker, re-elected as Barbuda's MP in April 2026 with 609 votes to the ABLP candidate's 398, states that Barbudans "are not going to subject themselves to purchasing land in Barbuda" and "will not play dead" (Antigua Observer). A documented allegation in the environmental litigation's commentary — that electricity connection was withheld for years from Barbudans who challenged the state — is recorded here as an ALLEGATION from an interested commentary, unverified against utility records (opinion commentary, November 2026); it is not needed for the grade, which rests on the statutes, the judgment, and the registry.
  • The opposition's voters are counted at a discount written into the map. The UPP's 37.30% of the April 2026 vote returned exactly one of seventeen seats — first-past-the-post arithmetic in part, but arithmetic performed on constituency boundaries "largely unchanged since 1984," which two consecutive Commonwealth observer groups have urged be redrawn on current census data and which his governments have not redrawn (Commonwealth Observer Group final report, 2026). Women are counted thinly: two of the seventeen MPs elected in 2026 are women, and the Commonwealth group again recommended measures to raise women's and young people's participation (Commonwealth Observer Group, 2026).
  • The poor are, in the most literal sense, uncounted: Antigua and Barbuda has published no official poverty rate in well over a decade, so the number of people the wage increases leave behind cannot be stated from the government's own statistics — an absence recorded here as an absence. What is documented is the lived distribution of the water failure (Test 5): through 2025 and 2026 residents across Antigua reported multi-day outages and erratic rationing, a burden that falls hardest on households without cisterns or storage — the prime minister himself conceded in January 2026 that distribution, not production, was failing, a decade into his tenure (Antigua Observer reporting and residents' published complaints, 2025–2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Election pledges and public statements vs. the record since June 2014, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The tax word — kept for twelve years. The ABLP's standing commitment that there is no personal income tax in Antigua and Barbuda has been maintained through four terms and was reaffirmed in the 2026 "Renaissance" agenda (Antiguan Herald, 2026). On the money promises of the most recent cycle he was exact: the minimum-wage increase to EC$11.50 was announced for 1 April 2026 and took effect 1 April 2026; the interim 5% public-service increase landed at the end of March 2026; the pensioners' 5% at the end of April 2026; EC$28.7 million of the 2018–2023 retroactive-pay arrears was disbursed by January 2026 against a completion target of end-March 2026 (CMC, March 2026; Cabinet Notes, March 2026).
  • The built word, where it was eventually kept: the V.C. Bird International Airport terminal opened in 2015; Barbuda's international airport opened in October 2024, years behind its first announced dates and over cost and environmental objections, but opened; the CHOGM hosting pledged for 2026 was delivered on 1–4 November 2026 (Government of Antigua and Barbuda; Commonwealth Secretariat, 2026).
  • The CIP word, restated as it changed: Browne never promised the programme would survive at any price; his 2026 statements named the price (Schengen access) and his counter-offer (closure by end-2030 with EU support) in public, in advance of any agreement (Test 3). The word changed; it changed in daylight, with the reason attached.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • 500 homes in 500 days — the flagship promise of 2014, unkept at twelve years. The pledge was the centrepiece of the campaign that brought him to office. By the third anniversary of his government, in June 2017 — past day 1,000 — 48 homes had been completed and about a dozen handed over; Browne publicly abandoned the timeline, attributing the abandonment to inherited debt, and substituted a promise of 500 homes by the end of the term (Antigua Observer, June 2017). That term, and two more, have ended. In June 2026 he was again promising scale — a $100 million housing bond, 300–400 homes a year rising to 500 a year, "no less than 5,000 homes" over ten years — against a waiting list of some 7,500 applications (Antiguan Herald, 22 June 2026). The promise was not quietly dropped; it was reissued, at the same number, a decade later, by the same man, with the housing portfolio held by his wife. Graded as the clearest broken word in the file, with the openness of the 2017 abandonment recorded in Test 3.
  • Water — the promise renewed every cycle, the failure conceded by the promiser. Successive Browne governments pledged to end Antigua's water crisis; production was genuinely expanded (Seven Seas reverse-osmosis plants entering service in 2025 and 2026, alongside APUA plant expansions), yet in January 2026 the prime minister was publicly blaming distribution and his own minister for households still going without, and residents' documented complaints of multi-day outages run through October 2026 (Antigua Observer, January 2026; residents' published complaints, 2025–2026). A promise whose own author is still apologising for its non-delivery in year twelve is recorded as unkept.
  • The Alfa Nero accounting — pledged to the public purse, never itemised to the public. The government stated the US$40 million proceeds would satisfy state obligations including debts to regional financial institutions; no public accounting identifying the debts settled had been produced by April 2026 (SuperyachtNews, April 2026). Browne's "there is no missing money" is a denial, printed as such; a denial is not an accounting, and the promise graded here was the accounting.
  • The open words, recorded so the record can grade them later: his May 2026 statement that there is "no guarantee" he will lead the ABLP into a fifth election, and his call for a second tier of leadership (Antiguan Herald, 7 May 2026); and the CIP closure offer of 30 October 2026 — end-2030, conditional on EU support — which Brussels had not accepted at grading (Antigua News Room reporting, October–November 2026). Neither can be graded now; both are dated, and the file will hold them.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Gaston Browne is the longest-serving ruler in this series' Caribbean file and, on the electoral evidence, the most successful: four consecutive victories, the last of them a 15–2 landslide that three observer missions certified as peaceful and transparent, delivered by a seat that has also kept its money word to the dollar and the date — the minimum wage raised as announced, the pension and public-service increases landed on schedule, EC$28.7 million of inherited back-pay discharged, no income tax across twelve years. The referees around him are alive: his courts have ruled against his party, his government, and the man himself, and the rulings stood; his final court gave Barbudan litigants standing against his flagship resort in 2024 two years after it gave him the land title in 2022. What keeps the card from the no-fails tier is where the seat's grip actually falls. It falls on critics, who are answered with a defamation docket that runs from opposition leaders to the publishers of foreign allegations, and on the referee-strengthening reforms — campaign-finance law, an armoured electoral commission, boundaries untouched since 1984 — that two observer cycles have requested and four of his majorities have not enacted. It falls on the money files: a US$40 million state asset sale whose proceeds have never been publicly itemised, defended by denial and by suing the other side's lawyers, and a citizenship programme that approved 98.3% of applicants, including, in November 2017, a man who fled India days before a bank-fraud scandal broke. And it falls, hardest and longest, on Barbuda: a community of some 1,800 people whose centuries-old common holding of their island was legislated away in 2015 and 2018, whose constitutional claim was struck out in London in 2022 with EC$141,000 of costs laid on their two representatives, whose protests were met with riot police in 2025, and whose elected council's formal rejection of the land registry in May 2026 was answered with a launch date. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the Alfa Nero proceeds are ever publicly accounted; whether the CIP closes on his 2030 terms, the EU's 2028 terms, or not at all; whether the boundary and campaign-finance reforms are enacted before the fifth election he says he may not contest; and whether Barbuda's land question is ever settled with Barbudans rather than merely over them.
