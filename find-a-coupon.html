The hard line: never pay to use coupons

Real coupons are always free. The moment a coupon costs money — a fee, a subscription, a "club" — it stopped being a saving and became a product: usually your own discount sold back to you. Anything that charges for coupon access is excluded from the lists below and named in the Avoid pile instead.

    How the free sites stay free

    Stores pay affiliate commissions — a percentage of the sale — to whoever sent them the buyer. Coupon sites, deal blogs, and cashback portals live on those commissions, on ads, or on both. Cashback portals split the commission with you. That's the whole business model.

    Why it matters: a free tool is paid by merchants, not by you — so its loyalty follows the money. When a site won't say how it makes money, that's a flag.

    No account, no install — just go look

    The lowest-friction doors. Open the site, find the deal or code, done.

    Slickdeals Free · no account

    Best for: finding out whether a price is actually good, and catching price mistakes and short-lived sales.

    A community deal board: real shoppers post deals, the community votes, editors promote the best to the front page. It's a deal board, not a code vault — many deals need no code at all. Makes money on affiliate links and ads.

    Catch: deals die fast. See it, check it, move.

    RetailMeNot Free · codes visible without an account

    Best for: the standard first stop for a promo code at checkout — the biggest promo-code database.

    Codes are partly user-submitted and untested, so check the "last used / success" signals on each code before you count on it.

    Catch: quantity over quality. Independent testing puts general aggregator code success around 30–40% — see Will the code even work? below. Have a second code ready.

    Coupons.com — printable coupons Free · no account to browse or print

    Best for: manufacturer coupons for groceries and household goods — print at home, use at almost any store.

    Selection is thinner than its old heyday — about 46 printables when Clark.com checked.

    Catch: before your first print from a device, it asks for a cell number and texts a verification code (anti-fraud, so print limits hold). Most coupons print only 1–2 times per person, and a failed print can still burn your limit — Coupons.com itself suggests saving the coupon as a PDF when the print dialog opens, so a printer jam doesn't cost you the coupon.

    CamelCamelCamel Free · alerts need a free account

    Best for: telling a real Amazon price drop from a fake "was $X, now $Y" markup game.

    Paste an Amazon link and see what the item actually cost over months. Charts need no account; email price-drop alerts need a free one. Amazon only. Funded by affiliate tags and ads.

    Catch: none for shoppers beyond that.

    The Krazy Coupon Lady · Hip2Save Free to read · no account

    Best for: groceries and drugstores (CVS / Walgreens deal scenarios).

    Coupon blogs with human deal editors who do the stacking math for you — stacking: which store, which digital coupon, which rebate app, final price.

    Catch: ad-heavy. They earn affiliate commissions and run clearly-labeled sponsored posts — The Krazy Coupon Lady says so on its own disclosure page, which is the honest way to do it. Their best final prices often require combining a store app plus a rebate app.

    Groupon Coupons Free codes section

    Best for: verified-ish promo-code listings for big brands.

    Catch: don't confuse the free codes section with the vouchers Groupon sells. Buying a voucher is a purchase with its own refund rules — a product, not a coupon.

    Brad's Deals Free to browse

    Best for: a short, hand-picked daily list instead of a database dump.

    Human editors pick a small number of deals daily. Lower volume, higher hit rate.

    Free account or free app — still $0, one sign-up

    A free account is fine. A fee is not. Everything here costs $0; what each one asks of you instead is printed in its catch.

    Your own store's loyalty program + its digital coupons Free to join

    The single highest-value move for groceries. Clip manufacturer + store coupons to your card in the app; they come off automatically at checkout.

    Kroger (and its banner stores: Fry's, King Soopers, Fred Meyer, Ralphs…) — hundreds of digital coupons weekly, member prices, fuel points.
    CVS ExtraCare — 2% back in ExtraBucks on most purchases, plus personalized register coupons. CVS policy lets one manufacturer coupon + one CVS coupon stack on the same item.
    Target Circle — free tier: 1% back in earnings, personalized offers, birthday discount. Target also sells a paid "Circle 360" tier — not needed for coupons; the offers live in the free tier.
    Also the same pattern: myWalgreens, Dollar General (myDG), Publix, Safeway / Albertsons.

    Catch (all of them): the trade is your purchase history — the coupons are personalized because the store tracks what you buy. Digital coupons also require the app/card, which shuts out shoppers without smartphones; paper weekly ads and in-store sale prices remain the no-account fallback.

    Rakuten Free account

    Best for: cashback on online orders — the biggest cashback portal (formerly Ebates), 3,500+ stores.

    Click through Rakuten (site, app, or its extension) before buying; the store pays Rakuten a commission and Rakuten shares it with you. Paid quarterly by check or PayPal once you're over ~$5. Rates typically 1–10%+, higher in promos.

    Catches: you must start at Rakuten — going direct, using a code from another site, or another extension interfering can void the cashback. Returns void it. The wait is real: quarterly payout, and stores take weeks to confirm. Some users report tracking failures and rare account freezes — keep your order confirmations until you're paid.

    TopCashback Free account

    Best for: often a better rate than Rakuten on the same store — check both.

    Its stated model is passing 100% of the retailer commission to members; it makes money from on-site ads and sponsored placements instead. No minimum payout (from $0.01) via bank transfer, PayPal, or gift cards — gift-card payout sometimes adds a small bonus.

    Catches: account required. Cashback sits "pending" until the retailer's return window closes (often 30–90 days). Tracking can occasionally fail, and using codes not listed on TopCashback can void it.

    Ibotta Free app

    Best for: grocery and household rebates — real cash back, $0.25–$5 per item is typical.

    Activate an offer in the app before you shop, buy the item, scan the receipt (or link your store loyalty card). Cash out at $20 via PayPal, Venmo/bank, or gift cards.

    Catches: forget to activate first and you earn nothing. Offers are brand-funded, so the app is quietly steering you toward specific brands — and account + receipt data is the product on their side.

    Fetch Rewards Free app

    Best for: a set-and-forget extra on receipts you're already getting.

    Snap any receipt (grocery, drugstore, restaurant, gas) and earn points; partner brands pay bonus points on specific products. It stacks on the same receipt as Ibotta.

    Catches, plainly: the base rate is tiny — a plain receipt earns ~25 points ≈ 2½¢ — and points redeem only as gift cards, never cash. It's a slow drip, not a rebate engine; your receipt data is what Fetch sells insight on.

    SmartSource + manufacturers' own sites Free

    Best for: printable coupons Coupons.com doesn't have — check both; inventories differ.

    SmartSource is the other big printable-coupon source. Brand sites (P&G, Unilever, General Mills…) also post their own printable/downloadable coupons and free-sample offers.

    Catch: print limits apply, like Coupons.com.

    CouponFollow / SimplyCodes Free

    Best for: codes chosen for verification, not volume.

    A code tool built around live code testing rather than raw volume — and the publisher of the most detailed public stats on code failure (below). Its checkout tool publicly commits to "standing down" — not overwriting another referrer's affiliate credit — which is the exact practice at the center of the Honey controversy.

    Catch: smaller database than RetailMeNot, and its success stats are self-published — interest noted in the numbers section below.

    Browser extensions — free, useful, read the catch first

    An extension is the highest-friction door: it sees every site you shop on. All of these are free. They are not equal.

    Capital One Shopping Free · no Capital One account or card needed

    What it does: auto-tests promo codes at checkout, compares prices across sellers, and pays "Shopping Rewards" on activated offers — despite the name, you do not need a Capital One account or card.

    Catches: the rewards are not cash — Capital One's own terms say they "are not equivalent to money and do not have a cash value," and they redeem only as eGift cards. Read that both ways, honestly: legally it's store-locked money, not cash you can aim anywhere — but if the gift card is for a store you'd spend at anyway, it works out to the same saving, face value where you'd already shop. If it helps save money, a gift card can be the equivalent of money. Redeeming can require linking a bank/card (any bank) to confirm eligibility, and rewards can take 30–90 days to post. Also on the record: in 2025, class-action suits accused Capital One Shopping and Microsoft Shopping of affiliate-attribution practices comparable to Honey's — allegations, no findings (see the Honey record below).

    Rakuten extension Free · same Rakuten account

    What it does: pops up when a store offers cashback so you don't forget to activate, and tests codes.

    Catch: same click-through discipline as Rakuten itself. An extension that can activate cashback can also be the thing that breaks another portal's tracking — pick one portal per purchase.

    PayPal Honey Listed with a warning — not a recommendation

    It still finds working codes for some shoppers and costs nothing. But it sits at the center of the biggest trust controversy in this whole landscape — allegations of silently taking affiliate credit from creators even when it found no discount, and of hiding better codes at partner merchants' request. PayPal disputes the framing, changed some behavior after Google rewrote the rules in March 2025, and as of late 2026 the lawsuits are ongoing with no finding of liability.

    If you install it, know the record and decide with eyes open — the full both-sides account is directly below.

    Will the code even work? The honest numbers

    Success rates depend entirely on who's counting and how. The studies disagree — so here is the spread, with each counter's interest labeled.

    Share of coupon codes that worked, by who counted

    Four counts, four different methods — read them as a range, not a single truth. Full study details under the chart.

    • ~32% worked — Coupono's analysis of 47,181 "active" codes listed across aggregator sites (May 2026 press release). Coupono sells a verification tool — it benefits from a scary number.
    • ~30% worked — CouponZania's live crowd-vote data (1,904 shopper attempts, July 2026). A small coupon site; small sample, real checkouts.
    • 76% worked — CouponDopa's controlled test of 1,000 codes across 11 countries (July 2026). Its key finding: most failures weren't expired codes — they were terms the shopper hadn't met (minimum spend was the #1 cause, then category exclusions and new-customer-only rules). Also a coupon platform with an interest, but the failure-cause breakdown matches every other source.
    • 75.2% worked — SimplyCodes' self-published figure across 87.2 million codes its own tool tested (2023–2026 data). Self-reported by a seller of a code tool — treat as the optimistic end.
    • 35% of code users hit a failure in the last 60 days — SimplyCodes' survey of 1,463 US shoppers (April 2026): the most common failure was an expired code (35%), then hidden restrictions (28%), then new-customer-only codes (23%).
    Read the terms before checkoutMinimum spend and exclusions kill more codes than expiration does. The fine print on the code listing — expiry, minimum spend, what it can't be used on — is where codes live or die.
    Expect to try two codesCodes fed directly by merchants to a site work far more often than scraped or user-dumped codes. Arrive with a backup.
    Don't let a chatbot invent codes for youCoupons.com's own test (August 2026 — self-interested, but method-described) found under 1 in 4 AI-suggested codes worked.
    It still pays offPer CouponFollow's household research, consistent coupon users save real money anyway — an average of ~$1,465/year per household is their estimate (advocacy-flavored, labeled as such).

    The Honey controversy — both sides, on the record

    Honey is a free browser extension (owned by PayPal since a ~$4B acquisition in 2020) that auto-tests promo codes at checkout and pays small "Honey Gold" points back. This is the fullest public account the research found — allegations labeled as allegations, verdicts labeled as verdicts.

    The allegations

    From MegaLag's investigation (Dec 2024; parts 2–3, Dec 2025), reported by The Verge, USA Today, Washington Post and others:

    • Affiliate credit-taking ("cookie stuffing" — replacing the tracking marker that credits a sale): when a shopper arrived via a creator's affiliate link, clicking Honey at checkout — even when Honey found no code and saved the shopper $0 — replaced the creator's tracking cookie with Honey's, diverting the commission to PayPal. In MegaLag's demonstration, a $35.60 commission was redirected and the shopper received $0.89 in points.
    • Hiding better codes: Honey's merchant partners could control which codes Honey showed, so partner stores' smaller codes were applied while better public codes were suppressed — the opposite of the advertised "we find every working code and apply the best one" (a claim the BBB had already investigated in 2020; Honey discontinued the ad).
    • (Dec 2025) Scraping and publishing private/employee-only codes, and building "stand-down evasion" code — logic that detected affiliate-network auditors and behaved compliantly only while being watched.

    PayPal's side, and what happened next

    PayPal, in its own words: "Honey follows industry rules and practices, including last-click attribution" (to The Verge). Merchants determine which coupons are offered through Honey (to USA Today). On January 12, 2026, PayPal acknowledged the stand-down-detection code existed and said it had been disabled.

    Facts, not allegations: Google rewrote Chrome Web Store policy in March 2025 to prohibit extensions claiming affiliate commissions without providing a discount or benefit; Honey changed its extension to stop claiming revenue when no discount was applied. Honey lost roughly 8 million of its ~20 million Chrome users by the end of 2025. Rakuten Advertising removed Honey from its affiliate network on Jan 12, 2026; Awin confirmed policy breaches and Impact.com also acted.

    The lawsuits: class actions filed Dec 29, 2024 (incl. Wendover Productions; GamersNexus later withdrew as a plaintiff). The first suit was dismissed in Nov 2025 for failing to identify a cognizable injury, with leave to amend; an amended complaint followed, and in June 2026 the judge denied PayPal's motion to dismiss the amended case. As of the research date there is no finding of liability and no settlement. The dispute is genuinely unresolved: the mechanics are largely documented; whether they broke contracts/law is what the court is deciding.

    Why it matters to a shopper, not just creators: your click at checkout decides who gets paid for the sale — and a tool that profits by being clicked last has an incentive to be clicked even when it saves you nothing, and (per allegation 2) to show you a partner's smaller code instead of the best public one. The general lesson applies to every extension above: free code tools are paid by merchants, not by you, and their loyalty follows the money.

    The Avoid pile — paid doors and traps

    Two kinds of trouble: things that charge you for coupons, and fake coupons that charge you for everything else. This is where Find a Coupon meets Spot It, Check It, Block It.

    Post-transaction "discount clubs"

    Shoppers Advantage, Great Fun, and kin: pop-ups after an online purchase that enroll you — sometimes via a "$1.99 shipping" or rebate click — into a club billing $12–$59.99/month for coupons and discounts.

    The US Senate Commerce Committee probed the operators (Affinion/Trilegiant, WebLoyalty, Vertrue) in 2009 after the BBB logged 1,800+ complaints about one subsidiary alone, many from people who didn't know they were enrolled until charges appeared.

    Fake brand discount clubs

    A newer costume on the same scheme: social ads for a famous brand's "members-only warehouse program" with a small joining fee, then a recurring monthly charge under an unrecognizable name, with cancellation made deliberately labyrinthine.

    Tells: not on the brand's real domain, a card demanded before you can see any catalog, countdown timers.

    Paid coupon books & "coupon savings" memberships

    Coupon books sold door-to-door or by subscription, and membership-fee grocery clubs pitched as coupon savings: not necessarily fraudulent — but they fail the rule. You are buying a product whose payoff depends on you redeeming enough of their chosen coupons before expiry. The same brands' coupons are free elsewhere.

    Paying for clipped coupons or newspaper inserts

    Marketplace listings, "coupon fairies," insert sellers: coupons themselves state they may not be sold or transferred; manufacturers treat bought clippings as void, and the trade in bulk clippings is exactly the channel counterfeit rings use (below).

    The legitimate paper world has also shrunk — digital coupons passed paper in US redemptions for the first time in 2024 (~53% digital) — so the free digital doors above are now the main pile anyway.

    The social-media freebie

    A polished post — "$100 gift card," "free [expensive product]," "90% off everything" — from a look-alike brand page. It wants a click, a share-to-unlock, a survey, or your card/personal details.

    Real brands' real offers live on the brand's own site/app and never require sharing, surveys, or fees. The FTC's rule: a real prize is free; anyone asking you to pay a "fee" or hand over card/bank/SSN details to claim one is scamming. Never buy from a seller who demands gift cards, wire, payment apps, or crypto — "only scammers will demand you pay in those ways" (FTC). US consumers reported $12.5 billion in fraud losses in 2024, the FTC's highest ever.

    Counterfeit coupons — a prosecuted federal crime

    Fake manufacturer coupons — usually high-value or "free product" coupons circulating as images in social-media coupon groups. The Coupon Information Corporation (the manufacturers' anti-fraud body, est. 1986) estimates $300–600 million/year in US coupon-fraud losses.

    The largest case: Lori Ann Talens designed ~13,000 counterfeit coupons and sold them via social media/encrypted apps (2017–2020) — $31.8 million in losses, 12 years in prison; her biggest customer got 41 months in June 2026. A fresh multi-defendant counterfeiting case (the invitation-only "Weekly Coupon Service") was charged in Sept 2026 after ~180 CIC undercover buys.

    Tells for shoppers: a coupon for a completely free high-value item; a coupon image with no unique barcode ("PDF coupons" re-shared endlessly); anyone selling coupons; discounts wildly out of line with the brand's real promotions. Legit printable coupons carry a unique tracking barcode and a print limit enforced per person.

    "Download to unlock" sites & no-name extensions

    The code is bait for a software install, a survey wall, or your email. Even "safety" extensions have form here — Web of Trust was pulled from every major browser store in 2016 after being caught selling users' browsing history.

    Stick to the named doors above; a legitimate coupon tool never needs your credit card.

    ✅ The 60-second check for any coupon

    1. Don't tap the link you were sent.
    2. Open the store's real site or app yourself and look for the offer there.
    3. Check the fine print — expiry, minimum spend, exclusions.
    4. If it's not on the retailer's own property, it isn't real.

    Report fakes at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
