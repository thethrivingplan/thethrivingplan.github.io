The Ruler's Test — Todd Blanche, Attorney General of the United States

Subject: Todd Blanche — Deputy Attorney General (confirmed March 5, 2025, 52–46); Acting Attorney General from April 2, 2026; confirmed as Attorney General August 8, 2026 (50–49) and sworn in August 10, 2026 (Reuters, Aug. 8, 2026; FLEOA/DOJ biography, Aug.–Oct. 2026). Before government, he was President Trump's personal criminal defense attorney in the New York hush-money trial and the federal prosecutions (Reuters, Apr. 2026).

The transition, factually: President Trump fired Attorney General Pam Bondi on April 2, 2026, after frustration over her handling of the Epstein files and the pace of prosecutions of his critics (Reuters, Apr. 2, 2026; NYT via House Oversight Democrats' letter, Apr. 17, 2026). Blanche, her deputy, became acting AG the same day, was nominated for the permanent post in June 2026, and was confirmed in August. Because Blanche ran the department's day-to-day operations as Deputy AG from March 2025, this dossier grades his record across both roles where his own hand is documented, and says so where an action predates his time as AG.

How this grading works: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it — court rulings, official records, reputable reporting, and the subject's own statements, each cited. Both shelves are shown. An indictment is an ALLEGATION; a dismissal is a ruling; a conviction or guilty plea is a verdict. They are kept distinct throughout. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — Gives by one law

Are prosecutions and investigations even-handed across allies and opponents?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for

  • The prosecution of former National Security Adviser John Bolton — a prominent Trump critic — rested on an investigation that began before this administration's second term and had career-prosecutor backing; Bolton pleaded guilty on June 26, 2026 to one count of unlawful retention of national-defense information, agreeing to a $2.25 million fine and forfeiture of his federal pension (Reuters, June 26, 2026; DOJ statement, June 26, 2026). A case against a critic that ends in a guilty plea is evidence the charging decision could stand on its facts.
  • The department under Blanche indicted former Cuban leader Raúl Castro, announced November 2026 — enforcement aimed at a foreign ruler, not a domestic opponent (Bloomberg Law, Nov. 6, 2026; Blanche statement on X).
  • Blanche's stated standard, repeated under oath and on television: the department will "prosecute without fear or any sort of favor," and at his July 15, 2026 confirmation hearing he pledged aggressive fraud enforcement regardless of size — "there is no fraud too small" (Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, July 15, 2026; NBC "Meet the Press," Aug. 16, 2026).

Evidence against

  • In the case of Kilmar Abrego Garcia, U.S. District Judge Waverly Crenshaw dismissed the human-smuggling indictment in May 2026, finding the government had failed to rebut a presumption of vindictive prosecution and calling the case an "abuse of prosecuting power." The judge's opinion named Blanche personally: his "now unrebutted public statements" tying the reopened investigation to Abrego Garcia's successful deportation lawsuit "taint[ed] the investigation with a vindictive motive," and the court found the decision to reopen a closed investigation was made to "implicate Abrego" after he sued (U.S. District Court, M.D. Tenn., May 2026 ruling, as reported by AP/WSJ, May 22, 2026). The underlying smuggling charges were ALLEGATIONS, never tried; the dismissal is a ruling about the prosecution's motive, and it stands as a court's finding, not a commentator's.
  • ProPublica reported June 8, 2026 that the Office of the Deputy Attorney General under Blanche shut down a career-initiated federal criminal investigation into potential Clean Water Act violations by coal companies owned by Sen. Jim Justice (R-WV), a close Trump ally; MS NOW's follow-up investigation (Nov. 19, 2026) reported that Blanche's deputies ordered prosecutors to withdraw subpoenas and close the inquiry in February 2026, shortly after the companies' lawyer contacted Blanche's office — the lawyer had worked alongside Blanche defending Trump in the New York case. The falsification allegations against the companies are unproven and no one was charged. DOJ disputes the account, saying there was "no case to be made here for a criminal investigation" and that the matter belonged in the civil process; in November it told MS NOW it "did not shutter any investigation" (ProPublica, June 8, 2026; MS NOW, Nov. 19, 2026). This is reported intervention, denied by the department — recorded here at exactly that strength.
  • The Wall Street Journal reported that Antitrust Division head Gail Slater wanted an extended review of the $1.6 billion Compass–Anywhere real-estate merger, that the companies' side appealed over her head to Blanche's office as Deputy AG, and that Blanche's office overruled her and cleared the deal; Slater was forced out weeks later, in February 2026 (WSJ, Jan. 9, 2026; Sen. Warren et al. letters to DOJ and the DOJ Inspector General, Feb. 19 and Apr. 15, 2026). Whether the clearance was corrupt is an ALLEGATION under congressional inquiry, not a finding; the overrule of the career antitrust chief by the deputy's office is the documented part.
  • In July 2025, as Deputy AG, Blanche personally conducted a two-day interview of convicted sex trafficker Ghislaine Maxwell — a task senators of both parties noted is virtually never performed by the department's No. 2 instead of line prosecutors — in which Maxwell praised Trump and denied seeing him engage in inappropriate behavior. Within a week, the Bureau of Prisons transferred Maxwell to a minimum-security camp, a placement congressional investigators state violated BOP policy for sex offenders (DOJ interview transcripts, July 24–25, 2025; NYT, Aug. 1, 2025; House Oversight Democrats' letter, Nov. 18, 2026). A quid pro quo is an ALLEGATION, unproven; the sequence — the interview, the praise, the transfer — is documented fact.
  • On November 8, 2026, days after the midterm elections, Blanche went on CNN and declined to rule out prosecuting Democratic lawmakers whose offense, as he described it, was threatening investigations and impeachment of administration officials — speech at the core of Congress's constitutional oversight role. He framed the threats as potentially criminal after the president publicly directed DOJ to investigate the lawmakers (CNN, Nov. 8–11, 2026).
  • Former FBI Director James Comey was re-indicted on April 28, 2026, during Blanche's acting tenure, on charges arising from a social-media photo — an ALLEGATION, unproven, with defense motions to dismiss for vindictive and selective prosecution pending at this grading (case-status record, Oct. 25, 2026).

Test 2 — Holds without gripping

Does the AG preserve the independence of career prosecutors, U.S. Attorneys, the FBI, and inspectors general, or purge and pressure them?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for

  • Turnover among Senate-confirmed political appointees is normal in any administration, and Blanche's public handling of the November 2026 departures was formally gracious: he announced the exits of the National Security Division head, the Miami U.S. Attorney, and the Civil Rights Division head with public thanks for their service (Blanche statements on X, Nov. 5–6, 2026).
  • The department's own watchdog structure still exists on paper: the April 15, 2026 Senate letter was addressed to a sitting Acting Inspector General, and senators directed their antitrust-corruption inquiry to that office rather than declaring it abolished (Warren/Klobuchar letter to DOJ OIG, Apr. 15, 2026).

Evidence against

  • The scale of the career exodus under Blanche's leadership of the department — first as the deputy running day-to-day operations, then as AG — is documented by the people who left: a July 2026 letter from more than 1,200 former DOJ employees (career prosecutors, FBI officials, and former U.S. Attorneys of both parties) opposing his confirmation put total DOJ departures at roughly 16,000 employees, including about 21 percent of the department's attorneys, and stated that career employees had been fired for "declining to initiate vindictive prosecutions," for "working on cases the president didn't like," and for "refusing to lie in court" (Justice Connection letter, July 7, 2026). The letter is an advocacy document by former officials; its headcount is their figure, and the pattern it describes is corroborated in its particulars by the court records and reporting below.
  • CREW's running count, cited in the Take Care Ledger (updated Nov. 22, 2026), recorded 608 tracked DOJ departures by May 14, 2026 — 406 protest resignations and 202 firings. The Public Integrity Section, which prosecutes elected officials, was reduced to two lawyers; the Tax Division was shut down entirely.
  • The Antitrust Division's Senate-confirmed head, Gail Slater, was forced out in February 2026 weeks after Blanche's office overruled her merger recommendation (WSJ, Jan. 9, 2026; Warren letter, Feb. 19, 2026) — a division chief removed after losing an internal fight to the deputy's office.
  • In November 2026, National Security Division head John Eisenberg was, per Bloomberg Law's reporting from senior administration officials, forced out by Blanche after disputes over media-leak investigations; Miami U.S. Attorney Jason Reding Quiñones resigned under pressure from department leadership, per CNN's sourcing, after his "grand conspiracy" investigation of Trump adversaries failed to produce indictments and after two assistant U.S. attorneys in his office were pushed out for reluctance to join it (Bloomberg Law, Nov. 6, 2026; CNN, Nov. 6, 2026).
  • A federal judge ruled on October 26, 2026 that the firing of a career prosecutor (Maurene Comey, fired in July 2025 under Blanche's deputy's office tenure) was unlawful; the department under Blanche is appealing rather than accepting the ruling (Take Care Ledger, updated Nov. 22, 2026).
  • The Civil Rights Division lost more than three-quarters of its staff between January 2025 and November 2026 — its own departing chief put the figure at roughly 300 of 400 lawyers gone in the first seven months (AP, Nov. 2026; Dhillon statement, Aug. 2025). A division does not lose three-quarters of its career lawyers by accident; see Test 4 for what the remaining division was directed to do.

Test 3 — Answers the wobble

When courts rule against the department, judges find misconduct or vindictiveness, or grand juries decline to indict, does the department accept and correct, or attack and re-file?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for

  • On the Epstein files, Blanche owned the failure in public and under oath: at his July 15, 2026 confirmation hearing he accepted responsibility for errors in the department's file release, told senators roughly one percent of the redactions required correction after release, and described the corrective measures taken (Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, July 15, 2026).
  • He also killed a proposal his own side had floated: at the same hearing, and again on NBC on August 16, 2026, Blanche confirmed that a proposed $1.8 billion compensation fund for people claiming to be victims of politicized prosecution "would not move forward" / "was dead" — a public correction of his own department's initiative (hearing record, July 15, 2026; NBC, Aug. 16, 2026).
  • The department under Blanche has fought its losses through the courts rather than around them: after the Abrego Garcia dismissal it filed a conventional appeal to the Sixth Circuit arguing legal error; no documented instance exists in this record of the department under his AG tenure simply defying a court order (Sixth Circuit filing, Nov. 2026, as reported).

Evidence against

  • The Abrego Garcia appeal is itself the counterweight: rather than accept a district court's finding that the prosecution was presumptively vindictive — a finding built substantially on Blanche's own public statements — the department asked the appeals court to revive the case, arguing the AG's statements showed a "normal assessment of the societal interest in punishment" (DOJ brief to the Sixth Circuit, Nov. 2026). Appealing is lawful; it is also, on this record, a refusal to absorb the correction the judge delivered.
  • In November 2026 the department, over Blanche's signature, filed a motion asking federal judges to recuse themselves from matters involving the Department of Homeland Security — an institutional attack on the judges hearing its cases rather than an acceptance of adverse rulings (DOJ filing, Nov. 2026, per the Standing Record compilation).
  • The voter-roll litigation campaign run from Blanche's department (see Test 4) was pressed through 27 consecutive district-court defeats without a single win, per Democracy Docket's count — persistence past the point where the courts' answer was unmistakable (Democracy Docket, Nov. 5, 2026).
  • In Chicago, in November 2026, a federal judge dismissed charges against six ICE-protest defendants (the "Broadview Six") after finding prosecutors had misled the grand jury and withheld exculpatory evidence, and took sanctions under consideration — a second courtroom finding of prosecutorial misconduct in Blanche's department within six months of the Abrego Garcia vindictiveness ruling (court ruling, Nov. 2026, as reported Nov. 18, 2026). The misconduct findings are rulings about the prosecutions, not allegations.
  • When the Senate itself pushed back at his confirmation hearing, Blanche's posture was combative rather than corrective — including losing his composure in exchanges with senators and flatly denying, under oath, that Congress had been forced to legislate Epstein-file disclosure because the department would not produce documents voluntarily (hearing record, July 15, 2026).

Test 4 — Counts everyone

Does the department's protection extend to everyone, or are some people's cases deprioritized by category?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for

  • The Civil Rights Division under Blanche's department did bring enforcement on behalf of groups this administration favors protecting: it sued UCLA and Harvard alleging they allowed antisemitism to flourish — protection of Jewish students — and pursued cases alleging discrimination against white and Asian students, alongside religious-liberty matters (NPR, Nov. 5, 2026; CNN, Nov. 5, 2026). Whatever one thinks of the priorities, these are real civil-rights enforcement actions on behalf of real categories of people.
  • The division mounted the largest election-monitoring operation in DOJ history, dispatching roughly 1,000 monitors and observers to all 50 states on Election Day 2026 (Democracy Docket, Nov. 5, 2026).
  • Blanche's public characterization of his departing civil-rights chief's tenure — that she "defended the civil liberties of every American and enforced the law without fear or favor" (Blanche on X, Nov. 5, 2026) — is his claim, recorded here as his claim; the two bullets above are the documented portion of that shelf.

Evidence against

  • The division's traditional protected categories were deprioritized as a matter of stated policy: under Blanche's department it dropped the Biden-era lawsuits accusing the Louisville and Minneapolis police departments of civil-rights violations and abandoned the court-supervised reform agreements in both cities, on the stated ground that the agreements "went too far" (AP, Nov. 2026; USA Today, Nov. 5, 2026).
  • The same division abandoned or withdrew from lawsuits defending voting access, redirected its resources toward voter-fraud allegations, and sued some 30 states and Washington, D.C. demanding unredacted voter records — then lost 27 consecutive times in district court, with judges criticizing the suits as efforts to access voters' personal data (CNN, Nov. 5, 2026; Democracy Docket, Nov. 5, 2026). The people whose protection was withdrawn (voters in the sued states, residents of the two cities) and the people whose data was demanded are categories, not individuals — deprioritization by category is the documented pattern.
  • The capacity to protect anyone was itself dismantled: more than 75 percent of the Civil Rights Division's lawyers — roughly 300 of 400 — were fired or quit during the tenure Blanche praised, leaving the division, per CNN's reporting, "faltering due to a lack of staffing," with inexperienced replacements unable to handle referred cases (AP, Nov. 2026; CNN, Nov. 5, 2026; Dhillon's own figure, Aug. 2025).
  • The NAACP's general counsel — herself a former head of the division — stated on the chief's resignation that the division had "walked away from major civil rights cases" and demanded the successor "enforce the law fairly, and protect our civil rights" (NAACP statement, Nov. 5, 2026). That is an interested party's characterization; it is included because the staffing numbers and the dropped cases above independently document the withdrawal it describes.

Test 5 — Keeps his word over time

The holder's stated commitments vs. the record in office.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for

  • At his July 15, 2026 AG confirmation hearing, Blanche testified that he would refuse any unlawful instruction from the president, and Republican senators highlighted his years as a federal prosecutor; nothing in the documented record assembled here shows him carrying out an order a court has held unlawful (Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, July 15, 2026).
  • One hearing commitment was kept in the open: he said the $1.8 billion compensation fund would not move forward, and it did not (hearing record, July 15, 2026; NBC, Aug. 16, 2026).
  • His self-description has been consistent in one respect: from his 2025 deputy-AG hearings onward he has said the president is entitled to his honest counsel and disagreement, and he repeated on Fox News Sunday (Aug. 16, 2026) that Trump "would expect me to tell him no" — a claim about private conduct that this record can neither confirm nor refute, recorded as his statement.

Evidence against

  • The commitment: at his March 2025 deputy-AG confirmation hearing, Blanche pledged to keep politics out of DOJ decisions, affirmed to Sen. Klobuchar that the Deputy Attorney General is not the president's personal lawyer, and testified on recusal: "I will follow the rules as told to me by the experts, career prosecutors in the department, if it comes to ever recusing" (Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, Feb.–Mar. 2025).
  • The record on recusal: senators opened a formal inquiry in 2026 into reports that Blanche was advised by DOJ ethics lawyers to recuse himself from cases involving Trump in his personal capacity — including Trump's lawsuits seeking personal payouts from the government — and participated anyway (Schiff/Durbin/Blumenthal inquiry letter, 2026). The disregard of the ethics advice is reported and under inquiry, not adjudicated; the gap between the March 2025 testimony and the reported conduct is the documented part.
  • The record on independence: on August 16, 2026, one week after taking the oath as AG, Blanche told NBC's "Meet the Press" that he would not pledge that the department acts independently of the White House — "No, I'm not going to pledge that, and no attorney general should ever pledge that" — and confirmed he would take the president's views into consideration in individual prosecution decisions: "Will I take the president of the United States' view on something into consideration? Yes, of course" (NBC, Aug. 16, 2026; CNN and USA Today, Aug. 16, 2026). ProPublica's Aaron Davis noted on NPR that no prior attorney general had said this on the record; the post-Watergate norm of prosecutorial independence from the White House was the thing being declined. A man cannot both pledge to keep politics out of prosecution decisions and announce that the president's views will factor into them; the second statement is the one he made as AG.
  • The record on his role: at his own AG confirmation hearing, asked whether he and the president are friends, Blanche answered in the present tense — "I'm his lawyer" — before correcting himself: "was his lawyer" (Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, July 15, 2026). A slip of the tongue is not a broken promise; it is included because it is the precise confusion his 2025 testimony promised the Senate would not occur, spoken by him, under oath, while seeking the office.
  • In September–November 2026 Blanche campaigned at rallies for a Republican Senate candidate and, after the midterms, used television appearances to warn Democratic lawmakers that their oversight threats might be prosecuted — conduct that drew a Hatch Act complaint and that sits on the far side of his pledge to keep politics out of the department's work (rally reporting, Sept. 16, 2026; CNN, Nov. 8, 2026).

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Todd Blanche came to the seat by the most documented route of any attorney general in the modern record: the president fired his predecessor for moving too slowly against the president's critics and for mishandling the Epstein files, and installed his own former defense lawyer, first as acting AG, then — by a single vote — as the confirmed holder. In the seat, the courts have done the grading where it counts: a federal judge dismissed his department's signature prosecution as presumptively vindictive, naming Blanche's own statements as the taint; another court found his prosecutors misled a grand jury; a third ruled a career prosecutor's firing unlawful. His department's documented interventions run in one direction — a criminal probe of a senator-ally's companies ordered closed over career prosecutors' work (reported, and denied by the department), an antitrust chief overruled and then forced out, a convicted trafficker interviewed by the deputy personally and transferred to a minimum-security camp within the week — while the Civil Rights Division lost three-quarters of its lawyers and its traditional docket, and the AG himself announced on national television that he would not pledge independence from the White House and that the president's views would factor into prosecutions. Against that record stand real counterweights: a guilty plea in the Bolton case, an owned and corrected failure on the Epstein redactions, a compensation fund publicly killed, and a department that still takes its losses to the appeals courts instead of defying them. They are enough to make the wobble grade mixed. They are not enough to move the rest.
