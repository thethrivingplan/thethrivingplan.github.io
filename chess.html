How the bot plays, honestly — tap to open

The bot is a real chess engine that runs entirely on this page: it generates every legal move, looks ahead, and scores positions by material and piece placement. The four levels differ in how deep it looks and how much randomness is mixed in — Learning looks one move ahead and often picks a weaker move on purpose, like a learner; Steady looks two ahead; Sharp three; Pro four, with an extra search over captures so it doesn't hang pieces to tactics it can see. The levels were tested against each other in self-play while this page was built: Pro checkmated Learning in every test game (in as few as 20 moves), Sharp beat Steady, and Steady beat Learning — the ladder landed in order. No level phones home or looks anything up — there are no opening books and no tablebases; it's all thought out on your device, move by move.

Strategies & their stories — tap to open

“What’s the best option?” deserves evidence, not assertion. On this page it is answered three ways, and all three are shown: the documented record — real strategies from real games, with their stories, below; the engine’s read — Analysis, with the candidate plans and what each costs; and your own record — the strategy record under Teaching, built from your finished games. Where the documented record gives a clean fact, it is printed as one; where chess history only supports “often” or “rarely”, that is the word used — no invented percentages.

The Ruy Lopez — the opening that never left

The story: named for Ruy López de Segura, a Spanish priest who analysed 3.Bb5 in his 1561 book, one of the first chess books ever printed. Four and a half centuries later it is still a main weapon in world championship matches. Where it worked: everywhere, for centuries — it asks Black a small, lasting question about the e5 pawn and never stops asking. Where it didn’t: in the 2000 World Championship, Vladimir Kramnik answered it with the Berlin Defence (3…Nf6) and Garry Kasparov — the greatest Ruy Lopez player of his era — could not win a single game against it, and lost the title. The lesson the record teaches: no opening is a refutation; every famous line has a famous answer, and the game is in the plans after the opening, not the opening itself.

The King’s Gambit — glory, then the auditors arrived

The story: the romantic era’s signature opening — White offers a pawn for open lines and a king hunt. Its monument is the Immortal Game, London 1851: Adolf Anderssen sacrificed his queen and both rooks and checkmated Lionel Kieseritzky anyway. Where it worked: the whole 19th century, and club chess ever since — against unprepared defenders it still produces miniatures. Where it didn’t: defence caught up. In 1961 Bobby Fischer published “A Bust to the King’s Gambit”, giving a concrete defensive line (3…d6), and the gambit all but vanished from elite play. The lesson: a strategy’s record is against the defences of its time — a weapon can be famous, beautiful, and retired, all three honestly.

The Sicilian — the fighting answer

The story: 1.e4 c5, recorded as early as the 1600s and long considered suspect, became the twentieth century’s great fighting defence — the mainstay of Fischer and Kasparov when they played Black to win. Where it worked: in master practice it is the most-played reply to 1.e4 and, among the major defences, the best-scoring for Black — because it creates an unbalanced position where the stronger player has room to outplay. Where it didn’t: that imbalance cuts both ways — it also loses more often than the quiet defences, and whole White systems (the Alapin, the Closed Sicilian) exist just to drain its counterplay. The lesson: the “best” option depends on the result you need — the Sicilian is the pick when a draw is not enough, and the wrong pick when it is.

The Opera Game — the development lesson

The story: Paris, 1858, during an opera performance: Paul Morphy, the American prodigy, faced the Duke of Brunswick and Count Isouard consulting together. Morphy developed every piece once, opened lines, sacrificed his queen, and checkmated on move 17 — while his opponents’ queen and bishops were still on their starting squares. Where it worked: it is still the game teachers show first, because the pattern repeats at every level: undeveloped pieces are pieces not playing. Where it didn’t: Morphy’s opponents broke every rule the game teaches — the same attack fails against a developed defender, which is the other half of the lesson. When the Coach here nags about loose pieces and slow development, this game is why.

More stories join this shelf as the page grows. The pattern to notice: every strategy above has a “where it didn’t” — a strategy with no failures on its record is a story nobody checked.

The rules in plain words — tap to open

The documented game: this is chess — the game as written down in the FIDE Laws of Chess, the rulebook used in every rated tournament in the world (current edition in force since 2023). Nothing here is an invented house version of it. The variants are documented games too: Chess960 is Bobby Fischer’s Fischer Random Chess, now played in FIDE events; Capablanca chess was designed in the 1920s by world champion José Raúl Capablanca; Crazyhouse is the documented descendant of bughouse chess. Where a variant’s rules differ from the standard game, the board says so while you play it.

Winning: checkmate — a king in check with no legal move out. Draws: stalemate (no legal move, not in check); the same position three times (either player may claim it — a Claim draw button appears); fifty moves with no pawn move and no capture (claimable the same way, and automatic at seventy-five); running out of pieces that could ever mate; an agreed draw; or a flag fall against a player who could never mate, which is scored a draw. Castling: king and rook unmoved, squares between them empty, king not in check and not passing through check. In Chess960 the king and rook end on the same squares as standard castling, wherever they started. In Capablanca the king steps three squares toward the rook. En passant: a pawn that has just double-stepped can be captured as if it had moved one square — on the very next move only. Promotion: a pawn reaching the far rank must promote — your choice of piece. Crazyhouse drops: a dropped pawn may not land on the first or last rank, and a pawn that promoted and is later captured goes back into the pocket as a pawn.
