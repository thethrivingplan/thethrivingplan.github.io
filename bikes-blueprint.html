WHY? → THE PROBLEM

The rider is the crumple zone

A car crash works because the car is designed to die in your place. Crumple zones fold, frames bend, airbags fire — the machine spends itself absorbing energy so the people inside don’t have to.

A motorcycle has none of that. There is no structure around the rider at all — no cage, no crumple zone, no shell. When the energy arrives, the rider’s body is the structure that takes it. The rider is the crumple zone.

That single fact is the whole problem this blueprint exists to end. Not rider error, not visibility, not road design — those matter, and other work addresses them. This blueprint addresses the physics: in a crash, a large amount of energy has to go somewhere, and today it goes into a person.

The stakes, documented DOCUMENTED

≈28× Per vehicle mile traveled, motorcyclists are roughly 28 times more likely to die in a crash than people in cars (NHTSA, recent reporting years).
~6,000 Motorcyclist deaths per year in the United States in recent years — the highest numbers since at least 1975 (NHTSA).
37% NHTSA’s estimate of how effective helmets are at preventing rider deaths — real protection, and nowhere near enough. The best documented gear still leaves most of the energy with nowhere to go.

Read those numbers the design’s way: this is not a problem of careless people. Careful, geared, experienced riders die too, because gear can only spread and soften energy — nothing on a bike today can absorb it at full power. That gap is the design target.

HOW? → THE ANSWER

Absorption, worn and mounted

The answer is the house absorption field — carried in two places at once:

  • Worn: the field on the rider, from the suit — the same personal field the rider carries everywhere, walking included. (Companion blueprint: The Super Suit.)
  • Mounted: the field on the bike, generated by the machine, covering the machine and meeting the rider’s field.

In a crash, the two fields take the energy the way a crumple zone does — by spending it themselves. The energy is absorbed by the fields, spread out and bled off, instead of being delivered into the rider’s body. The rider keeps riding gear underneath it all, because the fallback law of this whole shelf is: if the field ever fails, the bike must still be a bike and the gear still gear.

THE CRAWL SO FAR

The real world is already walking this way

The field is not a leap from nothing. It is the same idea the real world is already crawling toward — put something between the rider and the energy — carried to full power. Everything in this section already exists, is documented, and already saves lives at low power. DOCUMENTED

Helmets EXISTS TODAY

The first absorption device: a crushable foam liner that spends itself so the skull doesn’t. NHTSA estimates helmets prevent about 37% of rider deaths and roughly two-thirds of brain injuries. Proof of principle, at the smallest scale: absorption works.

Armored gear EXISTS TODAY

CE-rated armor in jackets, pants, and gloves; abrasion-resistant materials built to survive a slide. It spreads impact over a wider area and buys the skin time against the road. Real, documented, standard practice for serious riders.

Airbag vests & jackets EXISTS TODAY

The closest living relative of this blueprint. Systems like Helite’s tethered vests and the electronic Dainese D-Air and Alpinestars Tech-Air detect a crash and inflate around the torso, neck, and spine in milliseconds — an absorption layer that appears exactly when it is needed. They work, they are documented in real crashes, and they are still low power: a cushion, not a field.

Crumple thinking EXISTS TODAY

Cars proved the philosophy decades ago: don’t make the structure harder — make it spend the energy. Modern cars are survivable because they are designed to be destroyed in a crash. Bikes never got that revolution, because there is no room on a bike for a crumple zone. The field is how the crumple zone finally fits.

The pattern, stated plainly: helmet (absorb a little) → armor (spread it) → airbag (absorb more, on demand) → field (absorb it all). Each step is the same idea at more power. This blueprint is the next step on a documented road — not a departure from it.

THE DESIGN, PART BY PART

Systems

Worn field generator DESIGN

Lives in the suit. Covers the rider completely, travels with the person across every kind of transportation — walking included. Full design in The Super Suit blueprint.

Mounted field generator DESIGN

Lives on the bike. Covers the machine and its moving parts, and overlaps the rider’s field so the pair act as one system with no seam at the rider’s hands, feet, or seat.

Crash sensing DESIGN

The field must know a crash is happening before the energy arrives — the same problem airbag systems already solve with accelerometers and tethers, at milliseconds scale. The field’s version: always-on at riding strength, so there is nothing to trigger late. OPEN HOW — power cost of always-on is unengineered.

Energy bleed-off OPEN HOW

Absorbed energy has to go somewhere — heat, light, sound, stored, spread over time. The design requires the bleed-off to be harmless to the rider, the bike, and anyone nearby. The mechanism is not yet designed. It is on the shelf, at full size, below.

Slide & secondary-impact handling OPEN HOW

Most crashes are not one impact. There is the first hit, the slide, and whatever the slide meets. The design requires the field to stay up through all of it — but how it treats a long slide against asphalt is unengineered. See Open How #2.

Fallback structure DESIGN LAW

Non-negotiable: with the field dead, the bike is an ordinary, fully rideable motorcycle and the gear is ordinary, fully protective gear. The field is an addition to a working bike, never a replacement for one. No stage of the build order may trade this away.

ACT → BUILD ORDER

Prove it small. Nobody’s life is a test.

The build order is the safety system of this blueprint. Energy goes up only when the stage below it has passed, on rigs, with instruments — never with a person as the measuring device.

  1. Paper and math

    Publish the energy budget: crash energies by speed and mass (the calculator above is the public version), what the field must absorb, and for how long. Anyone may check the arithmetic. If the numbers say a stage is impossible, the stage waits — an unknown is not a no, and it is not a yes either.

  2. Bench and test rigs

    Field generators on sleds and drop rigs, absorbing measured impacts into instrumented dummies and load cells. No vehicle, no rider. The question is only: did the energy go where the design says it goes?

  3. Low-speed closed course

    Mounted field on a real bike, remote-operated or dummy-ridden, at parking-lot speeds on a closed course. Tip-overs, low-sides, and gentle impacts — the crashes that today still break bones — absorbed on camera and on instruments.

  4. Raised energy, still no riders

    Speed and mass step up in stages toward road and highway energy, each step gated on the last one passing. Dummies carry the sensors; the data carries the argument.

  5. Human riders — last, and geared

    Only after the rig stages pass at full highway energy does a person ride with the field — and they ride in full conventional gear, on a fully conventional bike underneath it, per the fallback law. The field earns trust the way helmets did: by documented results, in the open.

THE OPEN HOWS SHELF — PRINTED AT FULL SIZE

What nobody has engineered yet

House law: an unknown is not a no. It is also not a yes, and it is never dressed up as a built fact. These are the open hows of this blueprint, at the same size as everything else on this page. Each one is an invitation: if you can engineer it, the copyleft below means the improvement belongs to everyone.

Open How #1 — Capacity at highway crash energy OPEN HOW

At 70 mph, a rider and bike carry roughly 135,000 joules of kinetic energy, and a sudden stop delivers it in a fraction of a second. That number is real, it is large, and it is the whole ballgame: a field that absorbs city-street energy but not highway energy has not solved the problem this blueprint names. How much energy the field can hold, and whether that capacity scales to a full-size bike at full speed, is not yet engineered. It is the first open how, and the build order is built so it gets answered on rigs, not on riders.

Open How #2 — Sliding, road rash, and secondary impacts OPEN HOW

A crash is a sequence, not a moment: the first impact, then the slide — body and bike traveling across asphalt — then often a second impact with whatever the slide reaches. What does the field do during a slide? Does it hold the rider off the road surface, roll, skid on its own boundary? Does it stay at full strength for the curb, the guardrail, the second vehicle at the end of the slide? The design requires yes to all of it; the mechanism is not yet designed.

Open How #3 — Field failure fallback OPEN HOW

Fields will fail someday: power loss, damage, a crash bigger than capacity. The design law is already written — the bike must still be a bike and the gear still gear — but the engineering of the failure is open: Does the field fail soft, shedding strength gradually instead of vanishing mid-crash? Does it warn the rider before it is weak? Does a partial field make anything worse than no field? Until those are answered, conventional gear is not optional under this design — it is load-bearing.
