🤖 How the bots differ — and the proof Tap to open · Tap again to close

Four skill levels, in plain words. The levels are not labels stuck on the same player — each level reads the hand differently:

  • Learning — guesses its chances from a quick look (about 40 sample runouts), misreads them by a lot, calls too easily, and plays a plainly wrong move about 1 time in 5. It is learning; it leaks chips.
  • Steady — checks about 150 runouts, misreads a little, calls about right against the pot odds, and slips about 1 time in 14.
  • Sharp — checks about 600 runouts, reads them closely, raises for value, bluffs now and then, and slips about 1 time in 50.
  • Pro — checks about 2,500 runouts, barely misreads at all, prices every call against the pot, sizes raises to the pot, bluffs on a schedule, and almost never slips (about 1 time in 250).

The honesty check: don’t take the labels’ word for it. Tap the button and this page deals real hands — one bot of each level at the same table, same blinds, stacks reset every hand — and prints what each level actually won. If the levels didn’t separate, the numbers would say so.

🎓 The pro tools — starting hands & pot odds Tap to open · Tap again to close

Starting hands, in plain words

Before the flop, your two cards are all you have. Pros sort them into rough groups and play the top groups from anywhere, the middle groups more carefully, and the bottom group almost never:

  • The top: big pairs (aces, kings, queens, jacks) and ace-king. Raise with these — they win more than their share.
  • Strong: medium pairs (tens down to sevens), ace-queen, ace-jack, king-queen. Worth a raise in good position, a call in bad.
  • Playable: small pairs, ace with a smaller card of the same suit, and two cards in a row of the same suit (like 8♠ 7♠). Cheap to see a flop with; easy to fold when it misses.
  • The trap zone: two unrelated middling cards (like king-nine, or jack-seven of different suits). They make second-best hands — the kind that call and lose. Pros fold most of these, most of the time.
  • Position changes everything: the later you act, the more you know. The same playable hand is a raise on the button (acting last) and a fold from the first seat. Acting last is the single biggest edge in the game — you decide with everyone else’s choices already on the table.

More than one way to play — and what each costs

There is rarely one “correct” style. Pros choose between real alternatives, and each choice buys something and gives something up:

  • Tight vs loose: tight players enter few pots with strong hands — fewer disasters, fewer chances, and a predictable image others exploit. Loose players enter many pots — more chances to win big pots, more time spent holding the second-best hand. Most pros sit between, tighter early, looser when acting last.
  • Aggressive vs passive: betting and raising can win two ways (best hand, or everyone folds); calling can only win one way (best hand). That’s the case for aggression. Its cost: bigger swings, and expensive mistakes. Passive play keeps pots small and cheap — and lets other people set the price of your hand.
  • Value bet vs bluff: a value bet wants a call from a worse hand; a bluff wants a fold from a better one. The same bet size can be either — what matters is what the other player holds and how often they do each. A bet that is neither (called only by better hands, folding only worse ones) is the one real mistake in this family.
  • Fast-play vs slow-play: betting your strong hand right away protects it and builds the pot; checking it (trapping) invites bluffs and keeps weaker hands in — at the risk of free cards overtaking you. On draw-heavy boards pros lean fast; on dry boards, trapping costs less.

The coach beside your decisions follows the same rule: it shows the alternatives and their trade-offs, and names a single way only when the math genuinely leaves one option.

Pot odds — the price of a call

Every call is a purchase: you pay the call for a chance at the pot. The math pros use is one fraction — what you must pay ÷ (the pot + what you must pay) — and it tells you how often you need to win for the call to break even. If your hand wins more often than that, the price is right; if less, you’re paying too much for the chance.

A worked example will appear here from your own last decision — the first time you call or fold against a bet, this box does the math on that real moment: what it cost, what was in the pot, and how often you needed to win. Until then: a 20-chip call into a 80-chip pot needs 20 ÷ 100 = 1 win in 5 to break even.

A rough shortcut pros use for draws: with one card to come, each card that completes your hand (“out”) is worth about 2% — a flush draw has 9 outs, about 18%, roughly 1 time in 5.5. Compare that to the price and the decision mostly makes itself.

♠ Documented strategies — the stories and their records Tap to open · Tap again to close

Strategies here don’t arrive as bare tips. Each one below is a documented way of playing this documented game, carried with its story: who used it, where it worked, where it didn’t. Judge them by their records — that’s what they’re for.

1. Tight and patient — play few hands, play them hard

The story: Dan Harrington built a tournament career on it — won the World Series of Poker Main Event in 1995 and reached the final table again in 2003 and 2004, in far bigger fields — and wrote it down in Harrington on Hold’em (2004), still a standard text. Phil Hellmuth’s version (“only play premium hands”) carried him to a record 17 WSOP bracelets.

Where it worked: everywhere patience is possible — big fields, deep stacks, cash games. Most hands are losers; declining them is free.

Where it didn’t: Harrington himself documented the failure mode — in tournaments the rising blinds eat a waiting player alive. His answer (the “M” system: track how many rounds your stack survives) is a strategy for when to stop being tight. On this page’s tournament tables you can watch that exact pressure arrive on the blind schedule.

2. Relentless aggression — bet and raise, make them decide

The story: Doyle Brunson codified it in Super/System (1978), the first great strategy book by a champion, after winning the Main Event back-to-back in 1976 and 1977. Stu Ungar played it at its purest and won the Main Event three times — 1980, 1981, and 1997 — a record of wins at the table nobody has matched.

Where it worked: against players who fold too much — which, in their eras, was nearly everybody. Aggression wins pots no showdown ever sees.

Where it didn’t: against players who call everything. A bluff that never gets a fold is just a donation — the documented counter-adjustment is to stop bluffing callers and value-bet them instead. The bots on this page include loose callers (that’s the Learning level’s honest leak), so you can test both sides of this on play chips.

3. The math first — price every decision

The story: David Sklansky’s The Theory of Poker (1987) stated the “fundamental theorem”: you gain whenever opponents play differently than they would with perfect information — and its working tool is pot odds, the same math the coach beside your decisions uses.

Where it worked: as a floor under everything else, in every era, at every level. A call priced wrong is wrong no matter who makes it.

Where it didn’t: math alone doesn’t tell you what a specific player’s bet means, or when they’re bluffing — Sklansky’s own later work layers opponent-reading on top. Odds are the floor, not the ceiling.

4. The amateur’s run — a story about variance, honestly told

The story: in 2003 Chris Moneymaker, an accountant who won his seat through a small online satellite (reported at under a hundred dollars), won the Main Event and bluffed Sammy Farha off a famous hand on the way. Poker boomed worldwide on the story.

What it proves and doesn’t: it proves a short run can crown anyone — which is exactly why one tournament is not a track record. The strategies above earned their standing over thousands of documented hands and decades. Treat every story here, this one included, as evidence with a sample size attached.

5. The solver era — equilibrium, computed

The story: from about 2015, software “solvers” (PioSOLVER was the first widely used) computed game-theory-optimal play — balanced ranges, mixed bluffs — and the top of the game reorganised around their output.

Where it worked: elite heads-up and high-stakes play, where opponents are strong enough that being unexploitable is the whole game.

Where it didn’t pay: against beginners. If an opponent calls too much and folds too little, the documented professional view is that simple exploitative play — value-bet more, bluff less — earns more than equilibrium does. Knowing which game you’re in is itself the strategy.

So — what’s the best option?

Answered from the whole evidence base, not asserted:

  • Documented: the literature’s consensus baseline for a learner is tight-aggressive — few hands, played firmly, in position, with every call priced. It is the common core of stories 1–3 above.
  • Simulated, on this page: the bot honesty check deals that exact experiment — the loose, over-calling profile (Learning) bleeds chips to the tight, odds-priced profile (Pro) over hundreds of hands. Run it and read the numbers yourself.
  • Your own record: the strategy record below grades your version of the question — how many hands you play, how you call versus raise, what your showdowns say. The best option for the table in front of you is the documented baseline, adjusted by what your record shows you actually do.

One honest caveat the stories all agree on: “best” depends on the table. Against callers, bluff less and value-bet more; against folders, the reverse. Any page that names one way for every table is selling something. This one isn’t selling anything.

📖 The rules, in plain words Tap to open · Tap again to close

The documented game

This page plays Texas Hold’em by its documented rules — the most played poker game in the world, written down in Robert’s Rules of Poker (the rulebook card rooms settle arguments with) and in the Tournament Directors Association rules, and played at the World Series of Poker since the 1970s. The dealing order, the blinds, the betting rounds, the hand rankings, and the side-pot settlement here all follow that documented game. Where this page adds anything of its own — table limits, bot opponents, teaching — it says so, and it never changes the game underneath.

Texas Hold’em in one breath

Everyone gets two cards only they can see. Five shared cards come out in the middle — three (the flop), one (the turn), one (the river) — with a round of betting after each. Your hand is the best five cards out of your two plus the five shared. Last player standing, or best hand at the showdown, takes the pot.

Blinds and the button

The dealer button moves one seat each hand. The two players after it post forced bets — the small blind and the big blind — so there is always something to play for. Heads-up (two players), the button posts the small blind and acts first before the flop.

All-ins and side pots

If you bet your last chip, you can only win the part of the pot you could cover. The rest becomes a side pot for the players who covered it. This page builds the side pots exactly — each pot goes to the best hand among the players who paid into it, split evenly on a tie, with any odd chip going to the winner closest after the button.

The three betting structures

  • No-limit (standard): raise anything from the minimum raise up to your whole stack.
  • Pot-limit: the biggest raise makes the pot grow by at most its own size — the cap is the pot after your call.
  • Limit: every bet and raise is one fixed step — one big blind before the flop and on the flop, two big blinds on the turn and river — and a round allows at most four bets total (a bet and three raises).

Hand rankings — strongest first

  1. Royal flush — A K Q J 10, all one suit
  2. Straight flush — five in a row, one suit
  3. Four of a kind
  4. Full house — three of a kind plus a pair
  5. Flush — five of one suit, not in a row
  6. Straight — five in a row, mixed suits
  7. Three of a kind
  8. Two pair
  9. One pair
  10. High card — none of the above

Suits never break a tie — equal hands split the pot. An ace can be low for a straight (5 4 3 2 A, the “wheel”).
