Ember & Iron

A smithing game built on the real, documented craft of blacksmithing. Take an order, choose your steel, heat it and read its colour, hammer it to shape while it is hot, quench it, temper it, and hand it over. Your grade comes from how the work actually went — the heat you kept, the strikes you landed in the window, and the moment you chose to quench. Nothing here is dressed up: a cold strike is a cold strike.

Documented basis: smithing as the documented craft itself — the sequence, colours, and heat treatment on this page follow how blacksmiths have worked and taught the trade for centuries, written here in our own words.

Safety — read this at full size, because it is full size in real life

Real forges burn. Steel at forging heat looks almost like cold steel in bright light, and it will burn you to the bone before you feel it properly. Smiths treat every piece of metal in the shop as hot until they have proved otherwise.

Real quench oil catches fire. Plunging hot steel into oil can flare, splash burning oil, and set a bucket alight. Real smiths quench with a lid within reach, never lean over the bucket, and keep water far away from hot oil — water in hot oil erupts.

Real hammering throws scale and sparks that can blind. Eye protection is not optional at a real anvil, hot scale on skin burns, and a real shop needs ventilation, a fire extinguisher, and training from a person standing next to you.

The honest limit: this game teaches the ideas, the order of the work, and the judgement calls. It cannot teach your hands. If you take up real smithing, learn in person from an experienced smith, in a real shop, with real safety gear. No score on this page qualifies you to light a forge.

Learn the craft — the knowledge under the game

Reading heat by colour

For most of smithing history there was no thermometer on the forge. There still mostly isn't — the smith reads the steel's colour, and the colour is the temperature. Learn this table and you can play the whole game by eye, the way it is really done.

ColourAboutWhat it means for the work
Blackunder 500°CCold for forging. Hammering now does almost nothing and can crack the steel. Heat it.
Dull red~600°CFirst visible glow in a dark shop. Still too cold to move metal well.
Cherry red~750°CApproaching forging heat. Fine for gentle straightening; heavy work waits for orange.
Orange~900°CThe forging window. Metal moves under the hammer like stiff clay. Most shaping happens here.
Yellow~1050°CVery hot: metal moves fast and scale forms fast. Work quickly; forge-welding heat lives just above this.
Yellow-white~1200°CToo hot for shaping ordinary work. Steel starts to burn — it sparkles, crumbles, and that damage does not heal.

Why the sequence is the sequence

Why you don't hammer cold steel: cold steel barely moves, so your effort goes into stress instead of shape. Stressed cold steel cracks — sometimes inside, where you cannot see it until the piece fails in use.

What quenching does: heating steel past its critical point rearranges its crystals; cooling it suddenly — the quench — traps that arrangement in a hard, stressed state called martensite. Hard, and brittle as glass.

What tempering undoes: a gentle reheat lets some of that trapped stress relax. You trade a measured slice of hardness for toughness — the difference between a blade that chips and a blade that survives work.

Why reheats cost you: every heat grows scale — a crust of oxidised iron that flakes off and is gone. More heats, thinner piece. Metal wasted is counted in your record for exactly this reason.

Two honest strategies: work hot and fast spends the heat before it fades — fewer heats, less scale, but less time to check your shape. Slow and careful checks constantly — cleaner work, but every check is cooling, and cooling means another heat and more scale. Real smiths blend both: fast while roughing out, careful at the finish.
