Soccer — Skills & the Thinking Game

Touch, passing, shooting, dribbling, and the part that decides matches before the ball even arrives: seeing the game early. Every skill below comes with the mechanics, the reasons behind them, and an honest grading of what works, what really hasn't, and what is worth trying for yourself.

What you're playing for — no money, ever

Nothing on this page is bought, bet, or earned as coins. The stakes here are the real ones soccer already has: a teammate's run not wasted because you saw it in time, a personal best in the scanning trainer that is yours alone, bragging rights with the people you train with, and a first touch that stops bouncing away in matches. That is the prize, and it is enough.

1 · First Touch and Control

Tap to open or close

The idea: your first touch is not the end of receiving the ball — it is the first move of your next play. A good touch kills the ball's pace and puts it where your next action starts.

Receiving surfaces — what each one is for

  • Inside of the foot — the biggest, flattest surface. The default for ground passes: most control, most forgiving.
  • Outside of the foot — for taking the ball across your body or away from a defender on your inside, without breaking stride.
  • Sole — traps a ball dead under your foot. Useful in tight space and on fast surfaces; risky on wet grass where the ball can skid through.
  • Thigh — for balls dropping between knee and waist height. Let it drop onto the middle of the thigh and cushion downward.
  • Chest — for higher balls. Present a broad chest, lean back slightly, and drop the ball in front of you — or glance it sideways into space.

Cushioning — and why it works

A ball bounces off a stiff surface because the surface pushes back all at once. Cushioning is the opposite: as the ball arrives, withdraw the surface — pull the foot, thigh, or chest back a few inches at the speed of the ball. That stretches the stop over time, so the pace drains away instead of rebounding. It is the same reason you lower your hands when catching something fragile rather than holding them rigid: give with it, and it stays in your hands.

Touch direction — pick before the ball arrives

  • Away from pressure — when a defender is closing one side, take the touch to the other side. The touch itself beats the first challenge.
  • Into space — when you have room ahead, push the touch further out, a full stride or more, so you are already moving at speed.
  • Across your body — let the ball run past you and take it with the far foot when a defender has committed to your near side, or when you want to open your hips to play forward.
  • Dead, under your feet — when surrounded, kill it close and shield. A small safe touch beats a clever one that gets taken.

✓ What works

  • Checking your shoulder before the ball arrives, so the touch direction is already decided.
  • Cushioning by withdrawing the surface — the single biggest fix for balls bouncing away.
  • Receiving on the back foot (the foot furthest from the passer) when you want to turn and play forward.

✕ What really hasn't

  • A stiff, planted receiving foot — it is a wall, and the ball treats it like one.
  • Juggling records dressed up as match skill. Juggling builds soft feet and comfort, honestly — but it never asks you to decide a direction under pressure, so it does not by itself build a match first touch.
  • Taking every touch straight ahead, into the defender who was always there.

? What to try

  • Try-and-see: call your touch direction out loud ("away!", "across!") before the ball arrives in practice, until deciding early feels normal.
  • Try receiving some passes deliberately with your weaker foot in low-pressure drills — many players find the weak side catches up faster than expected.
Light tip

Give with the ball as it arrives — pull your foot back a touch — and aim the ball where you want to go next, not just down at your feet.

Coach — full teaching

Mechanics: get in line with the ball early, on the balls of your feet, knees soft. Present the surface before the ball arrives, then withdraw it on contact. Eyes on the ball at contact, head up immediately after.

The why: a touch that only stops the ball costs you a second touch to set up the next play — and that second is when pressure arrives. A directional first touch does two jobs in one contact.

Common faults: reaching for the ball with a straight leg (no give, ball rebounds); watching the ball all the way in and never looking up (touch goes wherever it goes); receiving square-on with both feet planted (nowhere to turn).

The fix: wall work with a called direction — before each return arrives, decide "left", "right", or "across" and take the touch that way. See the Drills section.

2 · Passing

Tap to open or close

The idea: a pass is a gift with a deadline — it must arrive before trouble does, and arrive kindly enough that your teammate's first touch is easy.

Inside-foot mechanics — and why they are accurate

Turn your foot out so the inside faces the target, lock the ankle firm, keep your knee slightly bent, plant foot beside the ball pointing at the target, and strike through the middle of the ball with a short, firm swing. The inside of the foot is its largest flat surface — a bigger flat area means small errors in contact change the ball's direction less. That is the whole secret of its accuracy: geometry, not magic.

Weight of pass

Too soft and a defender steps in; too hard and your teammate's touch bounces into trouble. The honest rule: firm enough to arrive before trouble, soft enough to control. On a fast surface or over distance, add pace. To a teammate with a defender on their back, add a little more — a slightly firmer pass is easier to protect than a dying one.

Play to the correct foot

Pass to the foot away from pressure, or the foot your teammate can play forward from. If their marker is on their left, the pass goes to their right foot. It costs you nothing and buys them a full second.

One touch or take a touch?

  • One-touch keeps the tempo and beats a press — the ball moves faster than any defender. The risk: a rushed return goes heavy or astray, and you never got a look at what changed.
  • Take a touch buys control and a fresh picture. The cost: pressure arrives, and the passing lane you saw may close.
  • The judgment: if you scanned before the ball came and the picture is clear, play one-touch. If you did not see it, take the touch — a safe touch beats a blind giveaway.

✓ What works

  • Locked ankle and contact through the middle of the ball for passes that roll true.
  • Passing to the foot away from pressure.
  • Scanning before receiving, so the one-touch decision is informed, not guessed.

✕ What really hasn't

  • A loose, floppy ankle — the foot bends on contact and the pass sprays.
  • Passing to whichever foot is nearest you, straight into your teammate's marker.
  • One-touch everything as a badge of honour — under no pressure, it just gives the ball away faster.

? What to try

  • Try-and-see: in rondos (keep-ball circles), aim every pass so your teammate can play forward without adjusting. Notice how often "correct foot" is the difference.
  • Try calling "man on" or "turn" with your pass — information is part of the pass, and many players find it sharpens their own decisions too.
Light tip

Lock the ankle, hit the middle of the ball, and aim at the foot your teammate can play forward from.

Coach — full teaching

Mechanics: plant foot level with the ball, toe at the target; striking foot turned out, ankle locked, knee over the ball slightly; follow through low and short toward the target.

The why: accuracy comes from a flat surface and centre contact. Spin comes from off-centre contact — fine when you want bend, a fault when you want a straight roller.

Common faults: leaning back so the pass lifts; striking with the toe or the heel of the inside surface; no follow-through, so the pass dies short.

The fix: pairs passing through a narrow gate, ten balls each foot, counting clean rolls. Then add a passive defender standing on one side so "correct foot" becomes a real choice.

3 · Shooting

Tap to open or close

The idea: power is a by-product of clean contact and leg speed. Placement beats keepers; wild power beats only the corner flag.

The instep drive — mechanics and the why

  • Plant foot beside the ball, pointing at the target — your hips and swing follow where it points.
  • Ankle locked. A firm ankle makes the leg and foot one stiff lever, so force transfers into the ball instead of being absorbed by a bending joint. A loose ankle is a cushion where you wanted a hammer.
  • Knee over the ball at contact. This keeps the shot down.
  • Strike through the middle of the ball with the instep (the laces area).

Why balls balloon: lean back and your foot arrives under the ball's centre. Contact slides underneath, the ball lifts like off a ramp, and it sails over. The fix is not "try harder" — it is knee over the ball and chest over the shot.

Placement vs power — the honest trade-off

Keepers cover the middle easily and the corners barely. A placed finish at about 80% pace into a corner beats a blasted shot at the keeper or over the bar. Power grows from clean contact and fast leg swing — not from throwing your upper body at it, which mostly wrecks your balance and your accuracy.

Volleys

Watch the ball all the way onto your foot, knee over it, and let the ball's own pace do much of the work — you are redirecting energy, not creating it all. Swinging at a volley as hard as you can is how balls end up in the car park.

Heading basics

Contact on the forehead (the flat, hard part — never the top of the head), neck firm, eyes open, and attack the ball — move to meet it instead of letting it hit a still head. Aim by turning your forehead at contact.

Heading safety — stated plainly, at full size

Neck and head strength work matters, and technique matters: a firm neck and forehead contact spread the force far better than a passive head. This page is not a medical verdict in either direction — here is the standing guidance as it stands:

  • US Soccer bars heading in matches and practice for players aged 10 and under, and limits heading in practice for ages 11–13.
  • The English FA phases heading out of youth training — no heading in training for the youngest age groups, with graduated limits as players get older.

If you coach young players, follow your own federation's current rules — they are the authority, not this page.

Contested: toe vs instep — tools, not religions

Both sides at the same size, because both are true — in different moments.

The case for the toe poke

Almost no backlift, so the shot leaves before a defender or keeper can set. In a tight space, stretching for a toe poke reaches balls an instep swing cannot. Futsal and tight-box finishing use it honestly.

The cost: a small contact area means less control and less accuracy. As a default finish from distance, it sprays.

The case for the instep / inside foot

The instep (laces) with a locked ankle gives power you can repeat; the inside foot gives the accuracy of that big flat surface for placed finishes.

The cost: both need a swing — a fraction more time and space than a toe poke. In the tightest moments, that fraction is the whole game.

The honest summary: toe poke = quick release in tight space, at the cost of control. Instep = power with a locked ankle. Inside foot = accuracy. Pick the tool the moment asks for.

✓ What works

  • Locked ankle, knee over the ball, plant foot at the target.
  • Aiming for corners; placed finishes at controlled pace.
  • Forehead contact and attacking the ball when heading.

✕ What really hasn't

  • Leaning back and swinging harder — the two faults behind most ballooned shots.
  • Toe-poking everything as a default, including from distance where control matters most.
  • Closing your eyes and letting the ball hit your head when heading.

? What to try

  • Try-and-see: finish a set of ten at 80% pace, all aimed at corners, and count on-target. Many players score more than their full-power set.
  • Try striking a few balls with almost no run-up to feel how much power is contact, not momentum.
Light tip

Knee over the ball, ankle locked, pick a corner. Clean contact first — pace follows.

Coach — full teaching

Mechanics: approach at a slight angle, plant foot level with the ball, striking knee over the ball at contact, head steady and eyes on the ball, follow through toward the target and land on the striking foot for driven shots.

The why: the shot's height is decided by where contact lands on the ball and your body lean at that instant — not by effort.

Common faults: plant foot behind the ball (shot lifts); loose ankle (pace dies); head up before contact (contact wanders).

The fix: film side-on at hip height (see Film Yourself) and check two frames: knee over ball? chest over shot? Fix those two and most ballooning stops.

4 · Dribbling and 1v1

Tap to open or close

The idea: you do not beat a defender with your feet alone. You beat them with a story your body tells — then you go the other way.

Close control

Little touches, one per step, ball within a stride at all times. If the ball is further away than that, a defender's toe can reach it and yours has to travel. Touch with the inside, outside, and sole — whichever keeps the ball close while you look up between touches.

Changes of direction

  • Inside cut — hook the ball back across your body with the inside of the foot. Sharp, protective, your body ends up between defender and ball.
  • Outside cut — flick it away with the outside of the foot. Faster out of the turn, keeps your run going.
  • Sole roll — roll the ball across with the sole. Changes the ball's line without changing yours — good for wrong-footing a defender who is watching the ball.

Body feints — and why they work

A defender cannot react to everything, so they read the biggest signal: your hips and shoulders. Drop a shoulder and shift your weight as if going left — the defender's weight answers — then push off and go right. The feint works because you are selling a direction with the parts of you the defender trusts, while the ball quietly goes elsewhere.

Shielding

When you cannot go past, keep what you have: body between defender and ball, ball on your far foot, knees bent, and your near arm out as a sensor — it feels where the defender is and which way they lean (within the laws: feeling, not holding or pushing off). Roll away from the pressure you feel and the space opens on the other side.

✓ What works

  • A touch every step, ball within a stride.
  • Feinting with hips and shoulders, then accelerating out of the move — the change of pace is what separates you.
  • Shielding with the far foot and rolling away from felt pressure.

✕ What really hasn't

  • Head-down dribbling into three defenders when a pass was on — the dribble is a tool, not a duty.
  • Step-overs at walking pace with no change of speed after them. The trick is not the separation; the burst is.
  • Big touches in tight space, gifting the ball to the nearest toe.

? What to try

  • Try-and-see: one feint, done at full commitment, instead of three small ones. Many players find one honest sell beats a dance.
  • Try practising exits both ways from the same feint, so the defender cannot learn your tell in a single game.
Light tip

Sell it with your shoulders, then burst. The move opens the door; the change of pace walks through it.

Coach — full teaching

Mechanics: approach the defender at an angle, not straight on, so both exits stay alive. Feint about a stride and a half away — close enough to force a reaction, far enough to use it.

The why: defenders commit their weight before they move their feet. Your feint is aimed at their weight, not their eyes.

Common faults: feinting too far away (no reaction forced) or too close (no room to exit); slowing down after beating the defender, letting them recover.

The fix: 1v1 to a small gate, attacker scores by dribbling through either side. The gate punishes one-sided tells honestly.

5 · The Thinking Game

Tap to open or close

The idea: the best players are not faster of foot. They are earlier of eye. And this whole section is a helping skill: you scan early so a teammate's run is not wasted, and you hold a support angle so the player on the ball is never alone. You are doing the thinking for somebody — that is the stake, and it is the real one.

Scanning — looking before the ball arrives

Scanning means turning your head to look around — at teammates, opponents, and space — in the seconds before the ball comes to you, so the picture is already in your head when it arrives.

The evidence, as it stands: observational research by Geir Jordet and colleagues, studying elite midfielders on match video, finds the best players scan more often in the seconds before receiving, and higher scan frequency is linked with better next actions — more forward passes completed, more turns away from pressure. It is a link shown by observation, not a guarantee: scanning does not make a pass succeed by itself. It gives the decision a head start.

Building the habit

  • Scan on a trigger: every time a teammate touches the ball, look. Their touch is your cue — it costs nothing and happens dozens of times a match.
  • Take a picture: nearest defender — where? Nearest teammate — where? Space — where? Three things, one glance.
  • Decide before it comes: turn, play back, or play forward — chosen while the ball travels, not after it lands.

Positioning and support angles

  • Open body shape: stand side-on to the passer, not square, so you can see the field and the ball at once.
  • On the half-turn: receive with your body already half-turned toward where you want to play, back foot ready. One touch then plays forward instead of two touches turning.
  • Make a passing lane: move until the passer can see a clear line to you — a step sideways out of a defender's shadow is often the whole skill.
  • The third-man idea, in plain words: A passes to B, but the real target is C, running free. B plays it one-touch into C's path. The defence watched A and B; C was the one they never marked.

Pressing — when to go, when to hold

Pressing means closing the player on the ball down fast to win it back high up the pitch. Holding means keeping your team's shape and protecting the middle, letting the other team have the ball in harmless places.

  • Press on triggers: a heavy touch by an opponent, a pass going backwards, a receiver facing their own goal. Pressing wins the ball near their goal — but it opens space behind you if they play through it.
  • Hold when outnumbered, when your team is stretched, or late in a game you are winning. Holding protects the middle — but concedes the ball and invites pressure to build.

Your energy is a resource — capacity is a skill

A team has a finite tank, and so do you. Pressing is capacity spending: every full-team press spends some of the tank, which is exactly why good teams press in bursts on triggers instead of chasing everything for 90 minutes — chase everything and there is nothing left when the game is decided.

The same law runs your own game. Top players spend much of a match walking and jogging to recover, and save their high-intensity runs for the moments that decide games. Empty the tank early and the consequences are real and plain: late in games, tired legs make heavy touches and tired minds make late decisions. Managing your energy across 90 minutes is a skill, taught like any other on this page.

✓ What works

  • Scanning on the trigger of a teammate's touch; deciding before the ball arrives.
  • Recovering on the far side of play; sprinting with purpose, not constantly.
  • Pressing in bursts on clear triggers, as a team, with cover behind.

✕ What really hasn't

  • Chasing every ball until you are empty by the hour mark — effort is not the same as effect.
  • Endless laps dressed up as football fitness. Matches are bursts and recoveries, not steady laps; train the pattern the game actually has.
  • Standing square and flat-footed, hiding behind a defender and calling it "being open".

? What to try

  • Try-and-see: count your own scans in one half of a practice game. Most players are surprised how low the number starts — and how fast the trigger habit raises it.
  • Try tracking your own late-game drop-off when you film yourself: compare your touch in minute 10 and minute 80, honestly.
Light tip

Every time a teammate touches the ball, look around. Decide before it comes to you — and spend your sprints like they matter, because they do.

Coach — full teaching

Mechanics: scans are quick head turns, not stares — a glance gathers the picture; your memory holds it. Body shape does the rest: side-on, half-turned, back foot ready.

The why: the ball's travel time is free thinking time. Players who use it play one touch with confidence; players who don't must stop, look, and lose the lane.

Common faults: scanning once at kickoff and never again; looking only at the ball side of the field; offering for the ball from inside a defender's shadow.

The fix: the scanning trainer below trains exactly this picture-taking habit, and rondos with a "call what you saw" rule build it with real people.

6 · Set Pieces, in Brief

Tap to open or close

Corners

  • Inswinger — the ball bends toward goal. Dangerous because any touch, attacker's or defender's, can turn it in, and it can go straight in. Harder for attackers to attack cleanly in a crowd.
  • Outswinger — bends away from goal. Easier for an attacker to run onto and head with power, and the keeper cannot come and claim it as easily — but it drifts away from goal, so the header must do more.
  • Near-post flick: a runner meets it at the near post and glances it across — the defence's positions are all set for the original flight, and the flick changes everything a half-second too late for them.
  • Blocking runs — screening a defender's path with your body is part of the game within the laws: holding, grabbing, and barging are fouls, and this page teaches the legal version only.

Free kicks near goal

The wall (defenders standing shoulder to shoulder) protects one side of the goal so the keeper can guard the other. What it gives up: it blocks the keeper's view on that side, and it stands still at the moment of the kick.

  • Over the wall, with dip — up and down fast. Needs height then drop; the trade-off is pace — dip costs speed.
  • Around the wall, with bend — into the keeper's side. Beats the wall entirely, but the keeper sees it the whole way.
  • Under it, when it jumps — low, along the ground, as the wall leaves the floor. Only works if they jump — read it, don't pre-decide it.

The taker's first job: beat the first line — then the keeper. A free kick that does not pass the wall was never a shot at all.

✓ What works

  • Choosing inswing or outswing for a reason, and telling your runners which.
  • Attacking the ball at corners — meeting it at its highest reachable point, not waiting for it.

✕ What really hasn't

  • Corners that never beat the first defender — the most common wasted set piece at every level.
  • Free kicks blasted straight at the wall at full power, hoping.

? What to try

  • Try-and-see: practise one corner routine until it is automatic rather than five routines loosely. Teams at every level score more from the one they truly own.
Light tip

First job on any set piece: beat the first line. Everything after that is a bonus the first job earns.

Coach — full teaching

Mechanics (free kick dip): strike slightly under the middle with the instep, follow through short and upward, and the ball rises then drops as its spin and pace fade. Bend comes from striking across the ball's side so it curves in flight.

Common fault: full run-up and full swing on a ball 20 yards out — the wall is ten yards away; touch beats thunder at that range.

7 · Drills — With Honest Transfer Notes

Tap to open or close

Every drill below says what it honestly builds and what it does not. A drill that builds one thing well is a good drill — pretending it builds everything is how training time gets wasted. Where a drill is a game, the stake is standing with the people you train with: keep-up counts and gate-game scores are bragging rights, plainly, and that is plenty.

Wall work

Setup: a solid wall, a ball, a marked target square if you can. Run it: pass against the wall, take the return with a called direction (left, right, across), repeat — both feet. Build up pace only while the touch stays clean.

Builds: first touch, both feet, passing accuracy against a return. Does not build: decisions against people — the wall never presses you, disguises a pass, or punishes a bad picture.

Cones

Setup: a line or slalom of cones, a yard or two apart. Run it: dribble through with a touch per step, exit with a burst; vary inside-only, outside-only, sole rolls.

Builds: close control and change of direction at your own pace. Does not build: timing against a moving defender — cones never commit, feint back, or recover.

Small-sided games (3v3, 4v4, rondos)

Setup: a small pitch, small goals or target lines, few players. Run it: play. Add rules sparingly — a two-touch limit, or a point for a third-man play — to shine a light on one habit at a time.

Builds: decisions, scanning, and technique under pressure, with far more touches than an 11-a-side game gives each player. Does not build: high numbers of clean repetitions of one mechanic — the game keeps interrupting, which is both its power and its limit.

Contested: small-sided games vs drills-only development

Both shelves at the same size. This argument runs through coaching, and the honest answer is about what each tool genuinely delivers.

The case for small-sided games

More touches per player, decisions under real pressure, scanning that matters because the picture keeps changing. Technique learned here arrives already attached to the judgment of when to use it.

What it cannot do: give a beginner fifty clean repetitions of one new mechanic in ten minutes. A broken technique can hide inside a busy game for months.

The case for isolated drills

Clean, dense repetition of one technique with no pressure — the fastest way to groove a new movement pattern, fix a fault, or rebuild confidence.

What it cannot do: teach when to use the technique, against whom, at what moment. Skill grooved in isolation still has to survive its first real defender.

What the evidence supports, in plain words: most strong development mixes both — drills to build the tool, small-sided games to build the judgment of using it. Anyone selling only one shelf is selling, not coaching.

A note on capacity — rest is part of training

Quality repetitions have a limit. Once tiredness makes your touches sloppy, more volume teaches sloppy touches — you practise whatever you repeat, including the tired version. Stop the drill while it is still clean, and rest. That is coaching guidance, not medical advice: listen to your body, and for anything beyond ordinary tiredness, a real medical voice is the authority.

✓ What works

  • Matching the drill to the job: wall for touch, cones for control, games for decisions.
  • Short, clean sets stopped before fatigue wrecks the technique.

✕ What really hasn't

  • One drill claimed to build everything — it is the training version of a miracle cure.
  • Pushing volume long past the point where every touch is heavy.

? What to try

  • Try-and-see: finish every cone session with two minutes of 1v1 against a person, so the day's control meets a defender the same day.
Light tip

Pick the drill by the job. Wall for touch, cones for control, small games for decisions — and stop while it's still clean.

Coach — full teaching

The why: learning science calls it transfer — practice transfers best when it resembles the moment you will use the skill. The closer the drill is to a match moment, the more judgment it builds and the fewer clean repetitions it gives. That trade-off is the whole design problem of training, and it never goes away — you manage it, you don't solve it.

8 · Film Yourself

Tap to open or close

Stated plainly: this page cannot see your technique. No page can. Filming yourself and a real coach's eye are the real checks — everything here is written to make those two checks work harder.

Shooting — side-on, hip height

Phone on a bag or tripod, side view at hip height. Look for: is your knee over the ball at contact? Is your chest over the shot or leaning back? Where is the plant foot pointing? Two frames tell you why the ball flew the way it did.

First touch — behind, ground level

Camera behind the receiver, low to the ground. Look for: which direction does the touch go — toward space or into pressure? Does the ball stay within a stride, or bounce a yard away? Did your head come up before the ball arrived?

Dribbling — head-on

Camera in front as you dribble toward it. Look for: do your hips and shoulders actually sell the feint, or only your feet move? Is the ball within a stride? Do you burst after the move, or coast?

How to use a clip

Pick one fault per clip. Fix that one thing for a week of practice, then film again. A list of ten faults fixes none of them. And when you can, show the clip to a real coach — their eye is trained in a way no checklist replaces.

Light tip

One fault per clip, one week per fault. Film, fix, film again.

Coach — full teaching

The why: feel lies. A shot that "felt powerful" can show a leaning-back frame and a loose ankle in two stills. Video is the cheapest honest mirror a player owns — and comparing minute 10 with minute 80 of yourself is also how you see your own energy drop-off, honestly, with your own eyes.

The Scanning Trainer

A scene flashes — you, about to receive, teammates, opponents, space. Take the picture. Then the scene hides and you say where the best option was. Found the open teammate and the attack lives; missed the picture and the move dies. That match moment is the stake — plus your own personal best and streak, kept in the record below.

Honest transfer note: this trains the picture-taking habit — looking early and holding the picture. It does not by itself build real match awareness; that takes real games against real people. It builds the habit those games reward.

Ramping is the default because matches speed up as you tire: across 10 rounds the window steps down (3s → 2s → 1.5s → 1s) and later scenes carry more players to read. A ramping score and a steady score are different achievements — the record labels each result with its mode and never mixes them.
