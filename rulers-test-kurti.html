The Ruler's Test — Albin Kurti, Prime Minister of Kosovo

Subject: Albin Kurti — Prime Minister of Kosovo since 22 March 2021 (the "Kurti II" government), with a first brief stint in February–June 2020 before that. After the 9 February 2025 election left his Vetëvendosje (LVV) party with 48 of 120 seats and no government could be formed, he governed in a caretaker capacity through a year of institutional deadlock. LVV won 57 seats in the snap election of 28 December 2025; his third cabinet was approved on 11 February 2026 by 66 votes to 49. That government fell amid a second deadlock, this time over the election of a president; LVV won 53 seats in a further snap election on 7 June 2026, and his fourth cabinet was formed on 13 September 2026 — its constitutionality challenged by the opposition Democratic Party of Kosovo (PDK) and upheld by the Constitutional Court on 23 September 2026. Justina Shiroka-Pula was elected President on 7 October 2026. He is graded here as the sitting prime minister, on the full record since March 2021. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution reached his own government's appointees, and the cases proceeded. Nagip Krasniqi, chief executive of the state energy corporation KEK — appointed in October 2021 under a board installed by Kurti's government — was arrested in April 2023 on suspicion of abuse of official position, and in December 2024 the Special Prosecution indicted him and two others, alleging roughly €37 million in damage from KEK contracts in 2021–2023 (Special Prosecution indictment, December 2024). The charges are ALLEGATIONs; no verdict has been reported. Martin Berishaj, Kurti's ambassador to Croatia and a figure from his own movement, was questioned by the Special Prosecution as a suspect in June 2025 in connection with alleged money laundering, tax evasion and false asset declarations arising from payments by the Slovenian energy trader GEN-I (Special Prosecution reporting, June 2025). Those matters are likewise ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated; but the investigative machinery was not stopped from touching his circle.
  • The headline corruption measure improved on his watch before slipping slightly: Kosovo's Transparency International CPI score rose from 39 (2021) to 41 (2022), held at 41 (2023), rose to 44 (2024, rank 73 of 180), then eased to 43 (2025, rank 76) (Transparency International, CPI 2021–2025).
  • His government implemented, in March 2024, the Constitutional Court's 2016 ruling awarding 24 hectares to the Serbian Orthodox Monastery of Deçan — a ruling deeply unpopular with his own electorate and which he personally called harmful and unfair — ordering the Cadastral Agency to register the land (government decision, 13 March 2024; Quint statement welcoming it, 14 March 2024). The law, in that instance, was applied where it cut against his side.
  • Prosecutions of officials from predecessor governments also proceeded under his tenure, and the European Commission's 2026 Kosovo Report records some progress on serious and organised crime (European Commission, 2026 Enlargement Package, 4 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • When the prosecution arrested his appointee Krasniqi, Kurti's public response was to attack the referee rather than the conduct: in the Assembly in April 2023 he said there was "a negative mood in the State Prosecution against our government" and claimed a political agenda "higher up" against his government, contrasting the prosecution's "zeal" in this case with its inaction on past abuses (Assembly session reporting, 20 April 2023). The past-abuses comparison may be accurate; the documented effect was the prime minister publicly framing the prosecution of his own circle as persecution.
  • Berishaj, by contrast, was protected in post for years: despite the GEN-I reporting dating from 2022 and his later questioning as a suspect, he remained ambassador to Croatia until a replacement was proposed only in November 2026 (Kosovo media reporting, November 2026). The contrast — a predecessor-era official class prosecuted, his own ambassador retained — is the one-law seam in his record.
  • The law was not applied evenly to his opponents' participation. In 2025 his party and the caretaker government supported efforts to deny certification to the Serb List, the largest Kosovo Serb party, for the local elections — an attempt the courts blocked (Crisis Group CrisisWatch, September 2025; RFE/RL, September 2025). The European Commission's 2026 report separately identifies "attempts to politicise the Central Election Commission" in the 2025–2026 cycle (European Commission, 2026 Kosovo Report).
  • In October 2026 the Assembly — with the governing majority's votes — passed amendments to the Law on the Specialist Chambers, the Hague-based court trying former Kosovo Liberation Army leaders including ex-President Hashim Thaçi, restricting its jurisdiction and procedures. The EU warned the amendments conflict with Kosovo's international commitments and could undermine the court's independence; President Shiroka-Pula returned the law on 22 October 2026 after EU criticism, and Commissioner Marta Kos confirmed on 5 November 2026 that the EU was in discussions with Kurti's government over the amendments (European Commission 2026 Report; RFE/RL reporting, October–November 2026). The amendments were initiated by PDK, not by Kurti's party — stated exactly — but they could not have passed without the governing majority, and the court they curb is the one trying his own side's wartime leadership. His stated position, given at the Paris Peace Forum on 10 November 2026, is that dissatisfaction with the court's judgments should be addressed through appeals.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the media regulator and public broadcaster, the electoral authority, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Constitutional Court ruled against his side repeatedly, and its rulings took effect. It annulled his government's 2024 Law on the Independent Media Commission (judgment reported April 2025) and the law was not re-enacted in the same form; it blocked the attempt to exclude the Serb List from elections; it froze government formation by interim measure in September 2025 and ruled parliament not constituted — and Kurti's party complied, going to new elections rather than governing around the ruling. The same court then upheld his fourth government's formation on 23 September 2026 against the PDK's challenge (Constitutional Court rulings, 2025–2026; OBCT analysis, 25 September 2026).
  • The Central Bank's autonomy is documented in the most contested file of his tenure: the February 2024 regulation making the euro the sole currency for cash transactions was an act of the Central Bank of Kosovo under Governor Ahmet Ismaili — appointed through the Assembly in 2023 — not a government decree, and Ismaili remains in post in November 2026, conducting an independent reform and SEPA agenda (CBK statements, 2024–2026).
  • No judge or prosecutor has been removed by his government; the courts that convicted and acquitted across the political spectrum continued to sit, and the Specialist Chambers in The Hague proceeded to judgment against Thaçi (sentenced 16 September 2026) without Kosovo's institutions obstructing the court's operation on their territory.

Evidence against:

  • His government wrote, and his majority passed, a media law designed to tighten political control over the regulator. The Law on the Independent Media Commission, adopted 11 July 2024, changed how IMC members are proposed and dismissed, extended regulation to online media, and was condemned by the Association of Journalists of Kosovo, the Ombudsperson, the EU and the Council of Europe; the Constitutional Court annulled it as incompatible with the Constitution, finding provisions that undermined the IMC's independence (Constitutional Court judgment, April 2025; AJK and MFRR reporting, 2024–2025). Kosovo fell 24 places in the RSF World Press Freedom Index in 2025, to 99th — the worst in the Western Balkans — recovering 15 places in 2026 (AJK/OBCT Shadow Report on Media Freedom in Kosovo, June 2026).
  • The public broadcaster RTK was gripped in practice: the MFRR and the EU's 2025 assessment record political interference culminating in the removal of six editors from TV programmes, an Ombudsperson investigation into censorship, and staff salaries paid almost a month late in August and again in November 2025; the boards of both RTK and the IMC were left dysfunctional for lack of quorum as parliament failed to elect members (EFJ/MFRR statement, 13 November 2025).
  • The prime minister's own rhetoric targeted the referees. Kurti has described the Constitutional Court as a "shadow opposition"; his 7 September 2025 comments criticising the Court were among the stated background to the United States' decision five days later (Atlantic Council, 18 September 2025; RFE/RL, September 2025). A March 2026 international media-freedom mission recorded that it urged the prime minister to condemn attacks on journalists and "received no commitment to do so," and the US State Department's human rights report records government officials, including the prime minister, engaging in smear campaigns against media (Council of Europe Platform mission reporting, March 2026; US State Department, 2024 Human Rights Report, Kosovo).
  • Governance by "acting" officials and dual-hatting concentrated control: the Kosovo Law Institute documented that after February 2025, members of the caretaker government simultaneously sat as MPs contrary to the constitutional incompatibility as clarified by the Supreme Court, refused to vacate, and publicly attacked the Court's judges — rendering, on KLI's analysis, their decisions unlawful — alongside extensive reliance on acting appointees across the administration (Kosovo Law Institute, "Non-implementation of Court Decisions," September 2025). KLI is a domestic rule-of-law NGO; its legal analysis is contested by the government, which maintains the caretaker arrangement was constitutional — a position the Constitutional Court partially vindicated in September 2026 by upholding the fourth government's formation while finding parliament had been constituted after the constitutional deadline.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, allies, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Three times in eighteen months the voters and the constitution denied him a majority, and three times he went back to the voters rather than around them: he accepted caretaker status after February 2025, accepted the Constitutional Court's interim measures freezing government formation, contested the December 2025 and June 2026 snap elections, and formed governments only when the Assembly's votes existed (election and Assembly records, 2025–2026).
  • He complied with the Deçan ruling (Test 1) — the single most unpopular compliance available to him — openly stating he considered it harmful and implementing it anyway, in March 2024.
  • On license plates, escalation ended in a reciprocal settlement: after the 2022 barricades and mass resignations of Serb officials, Serbia confirmed in late 2023 that cars with Kosovo plates could travel freely into Serbia, with Kosovo reciprocating days later — the outcome his government had sought, reached through the EU-facilitated track (dialogue reporting, late 2023).
  • After the October 2025 local elections in the north — which the Serb List contested and won — the transfer of local governance was, in the EU's words, "smooth and orderly," and the EU lifted all of its 2023 measures against Kosovo in early 2026 and resumed financial assistance (European Commission, 2026 Kosovo Report; EEAS statements, December 2025–January 2026). Whatever produced it, the adjustment Brussels had demanded for two years was delivered.
  • On the March 2026 agreement over implementation of the Law on Foreigners — regulating staff and students in Serbia-supported institutions — his government negotiated a practical arrangement inside the dialogue rather than enforcing unilaterally (European Commission, 2026 Kosovo Report).

Evidence against:

  • The north sequence of 2022–2025 is a documented pattern of doubling down against his own allies' explicit warnings. The EU and US asked that the ethnic-Albanian mayors elected in April 2023 — after a Serb boycott, on minimal turnout — not take up office in the municipal buildings; they were installed under special-police escort in May 2023, and the ensuing clashes injured NATO KFOR soldiers. The EU imposed measures on Kosovo in June 2023 — frozen funds, suspended high-level contact — the first such package against an EU aspirant (EU statements, June 2023; RFE/RL analysis, January 2026).
  • The dinar regulation of February 2024 was implemented without the transition the US and EU publicly requested; queues of Serb pensioners unable to withdraw salaries and pensions were documented within weeks, and the government's own deputy prime minister later framed Brussels' earlier caution as itself the mistake (CBK regulation, 1 February 2024; AFP and KoSSev reporting, February 2024; Sbunker analysis, 2024). Limited transitional measures were announced, but the sequencing was never conceded as an error.
  • The Banjska attack is stated exactly, because both sides build on it: on 24 September 2023 an armed Serb group attacked the Kosovo Police at Banjska, killing Sergeant Afrim Bunjaku; three attackers also died. Kosovo, the EU and the European Parliament classified it as a terrorist attack; Kosovo's government holds Serbia responsible for orchestrating it, which Serbia denies; the European Commission's 2026 report records that Serbia has taken no meaningful steps toward accountability for the attackers, whose leader remains in Serbia. Kurti's conditions for implementing the 2023 agreement — Serbia withdrawing its September 2023 letter rejecting parts of it, handing over the Banjska group's leader, and signing the agreement — have been refused by Belgrade and treated by the EU as preconditions the agreement does not contain (dialogue reporting, 2023–2026; European Commission, 2026 Kosovo Report).
  • The wobble from Washington was answered with minimisation. On 12 September 2025 the United States indefinitely suspended its planned Strategic Dialogue with Kosovo, citing "caretaker government actions that have increased tensions and instability" and naming Kurti's "recent actions and statements" (US Embassy statement, 12 September 2025). Kurti replied that relations were "not broken," that the suspension concerned a dialogue not yet begun, and that the differences were "operational and tactical" (government meeting remarks, 19 September 2025). Kosovo's foreign ministry signed a $600,000 lobbying contract with a US firm in November 2026 aimed at the frozen relationship (FARA filings, 13 November 2026).
  • The 2025 speaker deadlock showed the same instinct at home: his party insisted on a single candidate for Assembly Speaker through more than fifty failed votes across spring and summer 2025 before a compromise candidate was elected in August — months of institutional paralysis in which his side's share of responsibility is documented alongside the opposition's refusal to propose alternatives (Assembly records, April–August 2025).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — the Kosovo Serb community, Roma, Ashkali and Egyptian communities, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The October 2025 northern local elections were held with Serb participation, the Serb List won the northern municipalities, and the handover of local governance from the 2023 Albanian mayors to the elected Serb mayors was smooth and peaceful — the fact the EU itself credited for lifting its measures (European Commission, 2026 Kosovo Report). The community he is accused of counting out was, in the end, counted back in through the ballot box, under his government.
  • He implemented the Deçan Monastery ruling (March 2024), restoring a Serbian Orthodox institution's property rights against his own voters' preference.
  • His social policy is universalist in form: child allowances were introduced for all children, the minimum wage rose from €170 — unchanged since 2011 — to €350 in October 2024, €425 in January 2026 and €500 in July 2026, and basic pensions were raised (to €150 in the 2026 budget plans), with no ethnic test on any of it (government decisions, 2024–2026; Decent Work Balkans Kosovo Fact Sheet 2024). Roma, Ashkali and Egyptian communities retain their constitutionally reserved Assembly seats, and no measure of his government has removed a right those communities held.
  • His government offered concrete integration measures to Kosovo Serbs outside the north's parallel structures — employment pathways and verification of diplomas from the University of North Mitrovica — as his spokesman restated in September 2025 (government statement, September 2025).

Evidence against:

  • For most of his tenure the Serb community of the north was governed as a problem to be dismantled rather than a public to be counted. The 2023 mayors — elected on a turnout of roughly 3.5% after the Serb boycott — were installed by force of police escort and held office for over two years; the European Commission's consistent position was that the status of Serbia-supported structures should be resolved through the dialogue "rather than uncoordinated measures," with structured consultation with Kosovo Serb representatives — the consultation his government declined to hold before acting (European Commission reports, 2023–2026).
  • The dinar regulation's burden fell on named, ordinary people: Serb pensioners, teachers and health workers whose salaries and pensions arrived in dinars and who, from February 2024, faced documented queues, withdrawal limits and missed payments while the government disputed the framing rather than the fact (AFP, KoSSev, RTS reporting, February–April 2024). The government's sovereignty reasoning is stated at full strength here, because it is genuinely held and legally grounded: the constitution names the euro as Kosovo's currency, the parallel structures are instruments of a state that denies Kosovo's existence, and no other community in Europe is paid by a foreign government in cash. The test, however, counts who bore the cost of how it was done — and the cost was assigned, in full and at once, to the community least able to bear it.
  • The closures continued into his caretaker period: in September 2025 Kosovo police closed the Serbia-run health-insurance and pension-fund offices in North Mitrovica, drawing formal concern from UNMIK, the OSCE and the EU Office, days before the US suspended the Strategic Dialogue citing, among reported reasons, those closures and the attempt to disqualify the Serb List (UNMIK Media Observer, 15 September 2025; Crisis Group, September 2025).
  • The attempt to keep the Serb List off the ballot (Test 1) is a counting act, not only a legal one: it sought to remove the chosen vehicle of the largest minority from an election. The courts stopped it; the intent is the evidence.
  • The European Commission's 2026 report finds the environment in Serb-majority areas "restrictive, encouraging self-censorship among the public, media and civil society" (European Commission, 2026 Kosovo Report). Kosovo Serbs who engaged with Pristina outside the Serb List — such as the minister Nenad Rašić's party — were seated, but the community's own majority choice was treated throughout as Belgrade's instrument rather than as voters; his opponents' Albanian voters, by contrast, were governed without incident, and the fail recorded here is specific: it is the Serb community, above all in the north, that his accounting repeatedly treated as an exception. Poverty among Roma, Ashkali and Egyptian communities remains the deepest in the country on every published measure, and no programme of his government is documented to have closed that gap — an omission, recorded as such, not an act.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises and stated positions vs. the record since March 2021, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Minimum wage and family policy — kept, late and then in full. The 2021 platform promised to raise a minimum wage frozen at €170 since 2011. It rose to €350 in October 2024 (after a year lost to a Constitutional Court fight over decoupling it from war-veterans' benefits — a fight his government won), then to €425 on 1 January 2026 and €500 on 1 July 2026 — the figure he cites as delivered (Law No. 08/L-142, in force 23 August 2024; Government Decision No. 10/273; government statements, November 2026). Child allowances, promised in 2021, were introduced and raised by 50% in November 2025.
  • No coalition with the "old regime" — kept. He pledged in 2021 not to govern with PDK or LDK, and through four cabinets — including a year of deadlock in which such a coalition was the available exit — he never did, governing instead with minority-community parties and his LVV satellite lists (cabinet records, 2021–2026).
  • Visa liberalisation — delivered on his watch. Kosovo citizens gained EU visa-free travel on 1 January 2024, a goal his government pursued and met the conditions for, including the Deçan implementation above.
  • The economy's headline numbers moved as he said they would: official unemployment fell from 20.1% (2021) to 10.9% (2023) — driven substantially by emigration, as the labour-force analyses note — and his government reported growth of 5.4% in Q1 2026 and budget revenues up 8.3% (Kosovo Agency of Statistics via Decent Work Balkans, 2024; government statements, November 2026). The emigration caveat is his critics' strongest documented point and is printed with the claim.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Vetting — the central promise, unkept. "Jobs and justice" was the 2021 slogan, and vetting of judges and prosecutors was its flagship. The Venice Commission (Opinion CDL-AD(2022)011, June 2022) declined to endorse full vetting and recommended a partial model; the Constitutional Court found the proposed amendments constitutional; and still, by the Kosovo Law Institute's December 2024 assessment — titled "The Failure of the Justice Reform" — neither full nor partial vetting had been implemented, and no Anti-Corruption Strategy had been adopted in the entire mandate (Kosovo Law Institute, December 2024). The European Commission's 2026 report likewise records no progress in the judiciary and a decline in final decisions in high-level corruption cases.
  • The Association — accepted, then conditioned into suspension. In February–March 2023 Kurti accepted the EU's Agreement on the Path to Normalisation and its Ohrid annex, under which Kosovo is to initiate the process of self-management arrangements for the Serb community — the Association of Serb-majority Municipalities in its current form. He has since attached three preconditions (Test 3) that the EU states the agreement does not contain. The Commission's finding in November 2026 is flat: "no further progress toward establishing the Association," and the 2026 report repeats that Kosovo is expected to initiate the process (European Commission, 2026 Kosovo Report). His counter-position is documented and coherent — that Serbia's signature and recognition steps must move in parallel, and that the October 2023 EU draft statute risks a Bosnia-style entity, a fear an 81% majority of Kosovo's citizens shared in 2024 polling (CASA survey reporting, 2025). But the word given in Ohrid was to initiate, and by his own account he has not.
  • "Rule of law begins with us" — narrowed in office. The movement that promised to end governance by acting officials and party capture ran the state through 2025 with ministers doubling as MPs against the Supreme Court's stated position and an administration thick with acting appointees (Test 2; KLI, September 2025). The CPI gain (Test 1) is real; the institutional promise underneath it was not kept at the same standard.
  • Dialogue on his terms — overtaken. He promised in 2021 that dialogue with Serbia would no longer be the government's first priority and would proceed on principles of reciprocity. By 2025–2026 Kosovo had absorbed EU measures, a suspended US strategic dialogue, a missed June 2026 deadline putting over €40 million in Growth Plan funds at risk (European Commission, 2026), and a dialogue the Commission describes as producing "insufficient progress" on the very agreement he accepted — a record his defenders attribute to Belgrade's equal non-implementation, which the Commission also documents. Both halves are on the record; the promise, as stated, was that his method would protect Kosovo's position better than his predecessors'. On the documented measures above, it cost more than it protected.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Albin Kurti is the rare ruler in this series whose grip problem runs in an unusual direction: the institutions he is accused of bending — the Constitutional Court, the prosecution, the central bank — repeatedly ruled, arrested, and regulated against his own side, and are still standing; what he gripped was the tempo and the terrain, above all in the Serb-majority north, where for three years his government treated a community's parallel life as a sovereignty violation to be closed on his schedule rather than a public to be consulted on theirs. The costs are itemised by his own allies: EU measures without precedent, a suspended American strategic dialogue, a frozen Council of Europe bid, and €40 million now at risk — set against real deliveries his predecessors never made: a minimum wage nearly tripled, child allowances created, the Deçan ruling implemented, visa liberalisation won, and a corruption-perception score that rose before it stalled. The word he gave in Ohrid and the vetting he promised in 2021 remain the two largest unkept items, and both are unkept in the open, argued for rather than hidden. The count is where the record will not follow him: the Serb List is back in the northern town halls tonight because its voters put it there, but they were put back by an election his government spent two years making harder for them to win — and the test counts the years, not only the ending.
