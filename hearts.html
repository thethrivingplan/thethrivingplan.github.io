Hearts

The classic four-player card game, in full. You are not trying to win tricks — you are trying to lose the expensive ones. Every heart costs 1 point, the queen of spades costs 13, and the player with the lowest score when someone reaches the target wins. Play a complete game below against three bots, learn why each move works, and take the strategy with you to any real table.

Why → How → Play: the why is taught first in the Coach and in The Full Why, the how is the game itself, and playing is the act. Nothing here is paywalled or cut short.

Your opponents — honestly different

These three bots do not play the same way, and this page does not pretend otherwise. Their skill levels are stated plainly so you know what you beat — or what beat you.

West · Pip — Learning

Skill: beginner. Pip passes almost at random and usually plays the lowest card that follows suit. Pip rarely ducks under a trick on purpose and often takes the queen by accident. Beat Pip by playing normally — Pip is here so a first game feels winnable while you learn.

North · June — Casual

Skill: solid club player. June passes high spades and stray hearts, creates a void when she can, and ducks under tricks she does not want. June will dump the queen of spades on someone else given the chance, but does not plan three tricks ahead.

East · Vera — Sharp

Skill: strong. Vera counts spades, protects herself with low spades when she holds the queen, voids a suit deliberately, saves a low heart to avoid leading hearts late, and will try to shoot the moon when her hand is genuinely strong enough. Vera is the test: if you finish ahead of Vera over a full game, you are playing Hearts well.

The Full Why — how Hearts is actually won

Hearts looks like a trick-taking game and is really a point-avoidance game. One hand holds exactly 26 points (13 hearts × 1, plus 13 for Q♠). Every decision below is about where those 26 points land.

1 · Passing strategy

What to pass, and why those three cards

Why: the pass is the only moment you choose your hand. You are doing three jobs at once: getting rid of danger, shortening a suit so you can void it, and — quietly — not handing the player next to you a weapon.

The order of danger: (1) A♠ and K♠ when you hold few spades — if Q♠ falls while you play one of them, you take her. Pass them unless you hold four or more spades to hide behind. (2) Q♠ herself — pass her only if you hold three or fewer spades total; with four-plus spades, keep her, because A♠/K♠ are unlikely both to flush her out and you control when she falls. (3) High hearts (A♥, K♥, Q♥) — a high heart wins a heart trick and collects every heart in it. (4) Your shortest suit's highest cards — passing all your cards in a suit of two or three creates a void (see §2).

What never to pass: low hearts (2♥–5♥). A low heart is an exit card — it lets you duck under a heart lead later. Passing your low hearts away leaves you holding only heart winners. Also be careful passing left early in the game versus passing to the player who is winning: standard practice is to pass your worst danger away from the leader, and some players deliberately pass low cards to the leader to spoil a moon attempt (see §5).

Direction matters: passing left, you feed the player who plays after you; passing right, the player who plays before you — they see your cards' effect sooner. On the hold hand, the hand you were dealt is the hand you defend with, which is why hold hands produce the biggest scores.

2 · Voiding suits

An empty suit is a dumping ground

Why: when you cannot follow suit, you may play any card. That freedom is the most valuable thing in Hearts: it is how Q♠, A♥ and K♥ leave your hand without winning the trick they land in — someone else collects them.

How: if you are dealt two clubs, pass both away (or pass one and play the other early) and you are void in clubs. From then on, every club lead is a chance to shed your worst card. Diamonds are the classic void target because they carry no points themselves. This is also why the opening 2♣ lead matters: watch who plays what on the first tricks — a player who sloughs (discards off-suit) early has just announced a void, and announced what they are dumping.

The cost, honestly: voiding takes two or three of your pass cards, which may mean keeping A♠. A void in a suit you cannot also control can backfire late, when you are forced to lead and have only hearts and high cards left. Void one suit, keep an exit card in another.

3 · Ducking

Play under, not over

Why: only the highest card of the suit led takes the trick — and takes every point in it. If hearts or spades are led and you can play a card below the current high card, do: the points go to whoever ends highest, and that should not be you.

How: following suit, play your highest card that still loses (“ducking under”), not your lowest. Playing your lowest keeps your high cards for later, when there may be no one left to hide behind and you will be forced over the top. The exception is the first trick: no points may be played on the 2♣ trick at all, so it is the one safe place to shed a high club.

Spades are the special case: below Q♠, duck freely. If A♠ or K♠ has not yet been played and Q♠ is still out, playing a spade higher than Q♠ is safe only if Q♠ is already on the table — otherwise the player holding her will drop her on your A♠ or K♠ and the 13 points are yours.

4 · The queen of spades

Q♠ — the card the whole game orbits

Thirteen points in one card — half of all the points in the hand. Hearts is, in large part, four people negotiating who gets stuck with her.

If you hold her: count your spades in total, Q♠ included — the same count §1 uses. With three or fewer spades in total (Q♠ plus two or fewer others) you are exposed — try to pass her, or hope A♠/K♠ are led before spades are played three times. With four or more spades in total you are protected; keep her and drop her on someone else's A♠ or K♠ trick, or on an off-suit lead where she costs you nothing extra.

If you do not hold her: count spades. Every spade played without her appearing raises the danger that she is coming on the next spade trick. If you hold A♠ or K♠ and few spades, you are the likely victim — strong players will lead low spades (“smoking out the queen”) precisely to force A♠/K♠ out while she is still held. Holding the A♠/K♠ with plenty of low spades underneath is safety; holding them bare is a target painted on you.

Why she changes everything: without Q♠, Hearts would be a gentle game of small numbers. She creates the bluffing, the counting, and the schadenfreude — she is, as players have said for a century, the heart of Hearts.

5 · Shooting the moon

Taking all 26 points on purpose — risk priced honestly

What it is: take every heart and Q♠ in one hand and the hand inverts: either you subtract 26 from your own score, or you add 26 to everyone else's (this table uses add 26 to everyone else, the more common modern rule — it is stated here so there are no surprises). Some tables also count taking every trick; this table scores the classic points version: all 13 hearts plus Q♠.

The honest price: a failed moon is the worst ordinary outcome in the game. If you collect 25 of the 26 points and one opponent takes a single 1-point heart, you score 25 — nearly a quarter of a 100-point game in one hand. Attempts fail most often on exactly that: one low heart escaping to a player who is watching for it. Any opponent who suspects a moon needs to take only one point card to stop you, and good players keep a low heart back for exactly that job.

When the attempt is right: your hand can plausibly win almost every trick — long, high suits (for example six-plus hearts headed by A♥ K♥, or a long suit no one can beat once it is established), high cards in at least three suits, and ideally Q♠ already in your hand or spades controlled by A♠. Position helps: a moon is easier when the scores mean 26 added to the leader actually changes the game. When it is wrong: as a comeback gamble from a merely “pretty good” hand, or when a sharp opponent (Vera, at this table) is known to hold an unplayed low heart. The rule strong players use: decide by trick three. If you have taken points in every trick so far and still control the leads, press on; if a point has escaped, abandon the moon immediately and play to dump your remaining points on others.

Rules — the documented game, in full

Hearts is a public-domain folk game; the rules below are the standard documented form played here, with this table's choices named where variants exist.

Tap to open — Setup and the pass

Four players, one standard 52-card deck, no jokers. Each player is dealt 13 cards. Before play, each player selects three cards and passes them face-down, simultaneously: first hand to the left, second to the right, third across, fourth no pass (“hold”), then the cycle repeats. This table's Passing setting can fix a direction or remove passing. No one may look at received cards until they have passed their own away.

Tap to open — Trick play

The holder of 2♣ leads it to the first trick. Play proceeds clockwise; each player must follow suit if able. The highest card of the suit led wins the trick and leads the next. There is no trump suit. No point cards (hearts or Q♠) may be played on the first trick. Hearts may not be led until a heart has been discarded on another trick (“hearts are broken”) — or, in the common variant this table follows, until the leader has only hearts left. Q♠ may be played whenever spades are led or the player is void in the suit led.

Tap to open — Scoring and winning

After 13 tricks: each heart taken scores 1 point, Q♠ scores 13. Points are bad. Shooting the moon: a player who takes all 13 hearts and Q♠ adds 26 points to every other player's score (this table's rule). Scores accumulate hand to hand; when any player reaches the target score (100 by default, changeable in Settings) the game ends and the lowest score wins. Tied lowest scores share the win.

Tap to open — Variants this table does not use

Named so nothing is hidden: the jack of diamonds −10 (“Omnibus”) bonus, the “no points on the first trick” rule being dropped, passing two cards in two-player forms, and the three-player (17 cards, 2♦ removed) and five-player (10 cards, 2♦ and 2♣ removed) adaptations. The game above is the standard four-player form.
