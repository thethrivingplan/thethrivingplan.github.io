The greater-good accounting — up front

The asymmetry, stated first so the weighing is transparent: Ken Paxton has run a large executive agency — the Texas Office of the Attorney General — since January 5, 2015, with thousands of staff and litigation authority. James Talarico has been one of 150 Texas House members since 2019, usually in the minority party. Paxton's ledger is therefore inevitably larger in absolute dollars and actions, and Talarico's is mostly authored bills and votes. A reader who weights total dollars and actions through an office will weigh Paxton heavier on helps. A reader who weights adjudicated harms and findings will weigh Paxton heavier on harms as well — because he is the only candidate here with such findings. Talarico's ledger shows smaller, specific helps and no adjudicated harm or finding in the searches described below.

Helps — documented, quantified

Paxton — through the Attorney General's office

  • Over $3.3 billion in opioid settlements obtained for Texas from drug manufacturers, distributors, and pharmacies.
  • $1.4 billion Meta biometric-privacy settlement (July 2024) — described by the AG's office as the largest privacy settlement by a single state.
  • $1.375 billion Google privacy settlement, finalized October 31, 2025, plus earlier Google settlements of $700M and $8M cited by the office.
  • 18,291 fugitive arrests and 186 missing or endangered children recovered by the Fugitive Apprehension Unit; 373 human-trafficking arrests, 126 convictions — office-reported figures, not independently audited in the sources read.
  • 106 lawsuits against the Biden administration by January 20, 2025; the office claimed it had “won more than three out of every four” — that win rate is office-claimed.

Talarico — as one House member; bills require majorities

  • $25 cap on insulin copays per 30-day prescription for state-regulated plans (SB 827, in force Sept 1, 2021). Does not cover federal plans, the uninsured, or most federally regulated employer plans. His authorship of the enacted bill is disputed — see his record below.
  • Helped write and pass HB 3 (2019) — an $11.6 billion school-finance reform, the first major reform in 20 years, with teacher-pay money — as one member and author among many.
  • Other enacted laws in his official bio: the first Texas pre-K class-size cap, Javier Ambler's Law (banning law-enforcement contracts with reality-TV shows), a diploma path for incarcerated minors, Narcan in schools, a Canadian drug-import framework (it authorized seeking federal approval; it did not itself make drugs available).
  • About 16 of roughly 200 authored bills became law (secondary compilation count, flagged as such).

Harms — documented, quantified, with legal status

Talarico

  • No adjudicated harm, court finding, or ethics finding against him was found. Searches run October 27, 2026 covered ethics-complaint and corruption queries, the Texas Ethics Commission, campaign-finance coverage, and fact-check roundups.   • The one live allegation — the MAYA Consulting “pay-to-play” claim (September 2026) — is an allegation only, with no finding: no ethics complaint, investigation, or proceeding reported, and Fox News Digital's reporting noted it was “not aware of any evidence that Talarico has misused tax dollars.” Full piece below.
  • His No votes on abortion restrictions, gender-transition care for minors, border enforcement, and permitless carry are counted as harms by opponents and as rights-protection by supporters. They are values-dependent, not adjudicated — the full vote table is below so a reader can weigh them.

Paxton

  • $6.6 million whistleblower judgment against his office — final. A Travis County judge ruled April 4, 2025 that the AG's office violated the Texas Whistleblower Act. Paxton called it a “bogus judgement,” appealed, then dropped the appeal July 3, 2025. The judgment is against the office and borne by taxpayers; payment still required a legislative appropriation in the sources read.
  • ~$271,000 restitution paid by Paxton personally, plus community service, under a March 2024 pretrial-diversion deal in the 2015 securities-fraud case. No plea, no admission of guilt, no conviction; charges dismissed June 18, 2025.
  • Court found his office “abused its discretion” (July 2026): an appeals court overturned his injunction closing a midwife's clinics, because the office relied solely on an anonymous email and affidavits without direct evidence. The criminal charges in that case remain pending.
  • Texas v. Pennsylvania (2020) — his suit to invalidate 62 electoral votes in four other states was denied by the U.S. Supreme Court for lack of standing. No relief obtained.

How to read this record

Allegation ≠ charge ≠ finding ≠ conviction. Every legal item below carries its current status and date, and the four are never blurred: an allegation is something claimed, a charge is something filed, a finding is something a court or agency decided, and a conviction is a criminal verdict. Acquittals, dismissals, and declined prosecutions are printed at the same size as the accusations they answer.

Attribution is labeled. Paxton's numbers are marked office — produced by an agency of thousands under his leadership, often in multistate coalitions — or personal, where the act or the finding was his. Talarico's are marked as one legislator's: a bill he authored still needed majorities of both chambers and a governor's signature.

Claimed is claimed. Figures that come only from a campaign or from the AG's office itself are labeled campaign-claimed or office-reported. Polls are snapshots of the day they were taken, not outcomes. Anything the research could not verify is named as unverified, in the open, rather than quietly dropped or guessed at.

The scope rule — if he made it public, it is public. Public servants should be as transparent as possible, so this record includes everything about each man that he has made public or that is legally obtainable public record — including personal-life material that is genuinely public: his own statements, his public filings, the finances and health facts he himself discusses. Each such piece is labeled with how it is public. The boundary holds at the same size: nothing sealed, nothing leaked or hacked, nothing from private sources, and nothing about private third parties — family members who are not public figures — beyond what the public record itself contains. If they made it public, it is public; if it was taken, it is not used.

The race, as of October 27, 2026

The incumbent is four-term Republican Sen. John Cornyn, in the Senate since 2002. The nominees are Ken Paxton (R) and James Talarico (D), with Libertarian Ted Brown also on the general-election ballot. Election Day is November 3, 2026; early voting ran October 19–30. Democrats have not won a Texas Senate election since 1988.

How each man got the nomination

Democratic primary — March 3, 2026: Talarico won outright with 52.4% over Rep. Jasmine Crockett (~46.2%), with Ahmad Hassan also running. Crockett conceded March 4.

Republican primary and runoff: On March 3 no one won a majority; Cornyn narrowly led Paxton, with Wesley Hunt third. President Donald Trump endorsed Paxton on May 19, 2026, during runoff early voting, calling him a “true MAGA Warrior.” In the May 26 runoff, Paxton won 63.8% – 36.2%. Cornyn became the first incumbent Texas senator to lose renomination since Democrat Ralph Yarborough in 1970.

Sources: CNN, Mar 4, 2026 (Democratic primary); USA Today, May 26, 2026 (runoff); Wikipedia race page, updated Oct 2026.

James Talarico — the record

Democrat · State Rep. since 2019

Born in Round Rock; McNeil High School; B.A. in Government, UT Austin (2011); M.A. in Education Policy, Harvard (2015); Master of Divinity, Austin Presbyterian Theological Seminary (2025). He taught sixth-grade language arts on San Antonio's Westside and worked in education nonprofits before the Legislature. Type 1 diabetes was diagnosed in 2018, after an ICU stay during his first campaign; his first insulin prescription cost $684 — the experience behind his insulin work.

The path

Elected to the Texas House in 2018, District 52, defeating Republican Cynthia Flores in a Trump-carried district at 29 — the House's youngest member. Re-elected 2020; after redistricting he moved to District 50 (Austin / Travis County) and was re-elected in 2022 and 2024 (unopposed in the 2024 general). Committees: Public Education throughout; Juvenile Justice & Family Issues; Calendars; in the 89th Legislature (2025): Public Education, Trade / Workforce & Economic Development (vice-chair), House Administration, and vice-chair of the Academic & Career-Oriented Education subcommittee. Named to Texas Monthly's Top 10 Best Legislators lists (2019 / 87th-session accounts). He joined the education consulting firm MAYA in 2019 — see the allegation in the conduct ledger.

Sources: Texas House official biography; Encyclopaedia Britannica.

What he passed, and what it did

  • Insulin copay cap — SB 827 (2021), in force September 1, 2021: a $25 maximum copay per 30-day prescription for state-regulated health plans. His January 2026 campaign ad said it aids ~16% of Texans. It does not reach federal plans, the uninsured, or most federally regulated employer plans. Authorship is disputed: Sen. Lois Kolkhorst (R) authored SB 827, and the American Diabetes Association credits Kolkhorst and Rep. Eddie Lucio III; Talarico was House sponsor of the companion effort and joint author of HB 82 in other accounts, and says he “helped pass” it. Kolkhorst said publicly in January 2026, “you did not author the bill that passed in 2021.” Both claims are on the record.
  • HB 3 (2019): helped write and pass the $11.6B school-finance reform — the first major reform in 20 years, including teacher-pay money — as one member and author among many, per his official House biography.
  • Also enacted, per his official biography: the first Texas pre-K class-size cap; Javier Ambler's Law (HB 54, 2021 — bans law-enforcement agencies from contracting with reality-TV shows); HB 30, a diploma path for incarcerated minors; Narcan equipped in schools; a Canadian drug-import framework (HB 25, 2023 — authorizes seeking federal approval; it did not itself make cheaper drugs available); childcare property-tax relief; and a 2025 deepfake-removal law (HB 3133, joint author).
  • Scale: campaign and SmartVote summaries count roughly 200 authored bills with 16 becoming law (Smarter.Vote, updated Oct 26, 2026 — a secondary compilation, flagged as such). His campaign claimed in October 2026 that he “collaborated on sixty bipartisan measures” — campaign-claimed, not independently verified.
  • Honest gap: no independent outcome study was found in the sources read — no measured statewide insulin savings, no counted Narcan reversals — so his effects are quantified only by the cap's price and scope and the laws' reach, not by measured outcomes.

Sources: American Diabetes Association, June 15, 2021; El Paso Times, Jan 24, 2026; KPRC / Texas Tribune, Jan 27, 2026; Texas House biography; Smarter.Vote.

The quorum breaks

2021: Talarico joined House Democrats who left Texas for Washington, D.C., in July–August 2021 to block SB 1, the elections bill. He was among the earliest to return, writing that it was time to “begin the work of rebuilding relationships, negotiating policy, and reducing harm.” SB 1 passed in a special session and became law regardless.

2025: He was one of 51 Democrats who broke quorum in August 2025 over mid-decade congressional redistricting. Paxton sued to declare his seat and 12 others' vacant; the members returned before a ruling, and the Texas Supreme Court dismissed the case in May 2026 for lack of jurisdiction over a legislative dispute.

Sources: Wall Street Journal, Oct 2026; research summary of the 2025 litigation.

Ken Paxton — the record

Warren Kenneth Paxton Jr., born December 23, 1962, in Minot, North Dakota, in an Air Force family. Baylor: B.A. Psychology (1985), M.B.A. (1986), student-body president; J.D., University of Virginia (1991). He worked at Strasburger & Price (1991–95), as in-house counsel at J.C. His wife, state Sen. Angela Paxton, filed for divorce on July 10, 2025, “on biblical grounds,” alleging adultery — a filing and an allegation; Paxton generally denied the allegations in court; a divorce filing is a personal civil matter, not a criminal finding.

Lawsuits against the federal government

Paxton sued the Obama administration 27 times in under two years (a ~80% win rate is a campaign / biographical claim, not independently audited) and filed 106 lawsuits against the Biden administration by January 20, 2025 (the office's count). The office claimed at that date it had “won more than three out of every four” — office-claimed. Independently verified outcomes include: the 2021 block on the Biden administration's 100-day deportation pause; the 2022 Supreme Court stay of the OSHA vaccine-or-test rule (multistate); Texas v. Pennsylvania (2020), denied for lack of standing; and California v. Texas, where the Supreme Court held the plaintiffs lacked standing and the Affordable Care Act survived. Courts ruled DAPA / DACA expansions unlawful in part, while the program remained partially in place through litigation. Many COVID-mandate, Title 42, and EPA suits ended moot, stayed, or withdrawn when federal policy changed — and a moot dismissal is not a merits win, a distinction legal analyses stress and the office counts differently.

Sources: AG's office release, Jan 20, 2025; Reason, Oct 27, 2026; court outcomes as cited.

How the honesty level is computed — the workings, so anyone can recompute it. The verified percentage is the share of a claimant's checked claims rated True or Mostly true, with the count printed beside it in the same breath, and the full verdict distribution printed next to it so the single number never hides the shape.
