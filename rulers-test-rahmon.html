The Ruler's Test — Emomali Rahmon, President of Tajikistan

Subject: Emomali Rahmon — President of Tajikistan since 16 November 1994, and head of state in fact since November 1992, when he became Chairman of the Supreme Assembly during the civil war. Born 5 October 1952; he is 74. He holds the statutory title "Founder of Peace and National Unity — Leader of the Nation," which carries lifetime immunity from prosecution for him and his family. His current seven-year term, won in October 2020, runs to 2027. His eldest son, Rustam Emomali (38), is simultaneously Mayor of Dushanbe and Chairman of the Majlisi Milli (the upper house of parliament) — the office that, under the constitution, acts as head of state if the president dies or is incapacitated — and is widely documented as the groomed successor; in November 2026 he was received in Tashkent by President Mirziyoyev and awarded Uzbekistan's Order for Selfless Service. His daughter Ozoda Rahmon has been head of the Presidential Executive Office (chief of staff) since January 2016 and sits in the Majlisi Milli. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • A formal anti-corruption machinery exists and does move against officials below the ruling family's circle: the Agency for State Financial Control and Combating Corruption publishes arrests and prosecutions of state officials, and Tajik courts regularly convict officials for bribery and abuse of office (agency reporting; Transparency International country coverage). Even long-serving insiders have been removed from post — most visibly Mahmadsaid Ubaydulloev, mayor of Dushanbe since 1996, replaced in January 2017 (Freedom House, Nations in Transit 2018).
  • The 2025 border settlement with Kyrgyzstan was reciprocal in form: equal-size land exchanges, shared water management, and ratification by both parliaments — the same instrument applied to both states (Treaty of Khujand record, March 2025; UN News, 13 March 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The law's source exempts the source. The December 2015 "Leader of the Nation" law grants Rahmon lifelong immunity from prosecution, a lifetime veto over major state decisions, and — per the law's documented provisions — permanent immunity for his family as well (parliamentary law, December 2015; RFE/RL, May 2016). The May 2016 constitutional referendum then removed presidential term limits for Rahmon personally, by name of office — the amendment applies to the "Leader of the Nation," a title only he holds (IFES referendum FAQ, May 2016; referendum approved officially by 94.7%).
  • The state's commanding heights are staffed by the family, as documented fact, not allegation: his son Rustam heads the capital and the upper house (after earlier heading the Customs Service and the anti-corruption agency itself); his daughter Ozoda runs the presidential administration; her husband, Jamoliddin Nuraliyev, became first deputy chairman of the National Bank in 2015; another daughter, Zarina Rahmon, was appointed deputy head of Orienbank — the country's largest commercial bank, chaired by Rahmon's brother-in-law Hasan Asadullozoda — in January 2017, aged 23 (RFE/RL, January 2017; Freedom House, Nations in Transit 2018, which describes Tajikistan as a "nepotocracy" in which businesses under the presidential family's authority "avoid following regulations, or paying taxes and tariffs").
  • The one organized opposition the law ever had to accommodate was processed out of existence by the courts on the state's application. The Islamic Renaissance Party (IRPT) — legal from the 1997 peace accord until 2015, and until then the only legal Islamist party in Central Asia — lost its last two parliamentary seats in March 2015, was deregistered by the Justice Ministry in August 2015, and on 29 September 2015 was declared a terrorist organization by the Supreme Court at the Prosecutor General's request (Supreme Court ruling, 29 September 2015; Columbia Global Freedom of Expression case record). The government's stated rationale, at full strength: the party was linked to the armed attacks of 4 September 2015 led by Deputy Defence Minister Abduhalim Nazarzoda — who was killed in a security operation on 16 September 2015 — and, the authorities alleged, to Islamic State recruitment and a planned wider assault. The IRPT denied any link to Nazarzoda (noting that military personnel are legally barred from party membership) and denied the coup plot; its leader Muhiddin Kabiri, in exile, called the charges fabricated. Those links remain the state's ALLEGATION, never tested in an open court. What followed is documented: on 2 June 2016 the Supreme Court sentenced 14 senior IRPT members to long terms, two of them to life imprisonment, in a trial held inside the security services' detention centre which Amnesty International found "fell far short of fair trial rules" (Amnesty International, 7 June 2016). Interpol removed Kabiri from its wanted list in March 2018 (Interpol, 2 March 2018).
  • The outcome measure: Tajikistan scored 19 of 100 on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025, ranking 166th (Transparency International, CPI 2025). No member of the president's family or inner circle has been prosecuted for corruption in 32 years.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the media, the election commission, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The institutions retain formal structure and occasional independent motion: the National Bank has run a professional monetary policy that kept inflation within its target band through 2025 (Asian Development Bank, Asian Development Outlook, April 2026), and the state still invites OSCE/ODIHR missions in form — an invitation was extended for the March 2025 parliamentary elections (OSCE statements, February 2025). Periodic presidential amnesties have released some prisoners of the system, most recently in July 2026, when several imprisoned journalists and a blogger were freed under an amnesty law — though their convictions stand (IPHR, October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The electoral authority: In February 2025, ODIHR cancelled its observation mission for the 2 March 2025 parliamentary elections — the first Tajik election since 1995 with no international observers — because the authorities failed to accredit the observers already in Dushanbe or to give assurances they would be (ODIHR statement, 4 February 2025; EU and joint statements to the OSCE Permanent Council, 6 February 2025). Independent media were likewise denied accreditation; Asia-Plus reported the accreditation process was never publicly announced. The foreign minister's response is on the record: the commission's leadership was busy observing elections in Belarus, ODIHR had "violated the procedure and left," and "the doors for all OSCE structures remain open" (Foreign Minister Muhriddin, press conference, 11 February 2025). The ruling People's Democratic Party took 49 of 63 seats. In October 2026, ahead of the 2027 presidential election, parliament approved Zarif Alizoda — Rahmon's former legal aide — as chairman of the Central Commission for Elections and Referenda, replacing a chairman of ten years (reporting, October 2026).
  • The pattern ODIHR has documented for decades: the October 2020 presidential election (Rahmon re-elected with 90.92%) "took place in a tightly controlled environment… voters were not presented with genuine political alternatives" (ODIHR final report, 29 January 2021); the March 2020 parliamentary elections were held after "genuine opposition had been removed" (ODIHR, 2020). ODIHR recommendations — on media independence, pluralism, election-commission impartiality — "remain valid and unimplemented" across successive reports (European Council statement, October 2020; ODIHR reports 2013–2025).
  • The courts: Judges of the higher courts are appointed and dismissed through the president; the 2016 referendum abolished the Council of Justice, the body that had administered judicial appointments, folding its functions toward the executive (IFES, 2016). The Constitutional Court's documented role in the system's key moments has been to clear the executive's path — it determined the 2016 amendments legal before parliament voted (IFES, May 2016) — and politically charged trials are held closed, inside security-service detention facilities (Amnesty International, June 2016; HRW, December 2022). The lawyer who defended the accused paid the documented price: Buzurgmehr Yorov, defence lawyer for the IRPT defendants, was arrested in 2015 and sentenced to more than two decades in prison; the European Parliament's resolution of 18 January 2024 urges his release (European Parliament, 2024/2506(RSP)).
  • The media: In August 2026 the Dushanbe office of RFE/RL's Tajik service, Radio Ozodi — for years the country's principal independent broadcaster — ceased operations, after years of accreditation obstruction documented by the OSCE and press-freedom monitors (IPHR, October 2026). The Committee to Protect Journalists' 2026 reporting places Tajikistan among the ten countries with the most imprisoned journalists in the world; at least six journalists remain in prison, nine having received lengthy sentences since 2022, several in closed trials — among them investigative journalist Rukhshona Khakimova, sentenced to eight years for treason in February 2025 in a classified case reportedly linked to a public-opinion survey on China's influence (CPJ; Asia-Plus, November 2026). Tajikistan ranked 155th in the 2026 World Press Freedom Index (RSF). The remaining major independent outlet, Asia-Plus, announced in May 2022 that it would stop covering GBAO after an official warning from the Prosecutor General's office that its coverage "destabilises the situation" and an unofficial warning it would be shut down (IWPR, 2022; Asia-Plus statement, 17 May 2022). Over 80 civil-society organizations ceased operations in 2025 alone, seven of them liquidated by court decision (IPHR; HIVOS Enabling Environment Snapshot, 2026).
  • The central bank: Its first deputy chairman is the president's son-in-law (documented above, Test 1). No documented instance exists of the National Bank, the prosecution service, or any court ruling against a core interest of the presidency in 32 years.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the street, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His founding act was a negotiated compromise under pressure: the General Agreement on Peace and National Accord, signed in Moscow on 27 June 1997, ended the five-year civil war on power-sharing terms — including a quota of government posts for the United Tajik Opposition — and the peace has held for 29 years. He governed with former enemies in office under that accord into the 2000s (1997 General Agreement; UN records).
  • He has adjusted to external pressure with real concessions in the region: the 2018 rapprochement with Uzbekistan reversed decades of hostility, and — after border clashes in April–May 2021 and September 2022 killed more than 100 people — he negotiated rather than escalated to a settlement: on 13 March 2025 he and President Japarov signed the State Border Treaty in Bishkek delimiting the entire ~1,000 km Kyrgyz–Tajik border, with equal land exchanges and joint water management, followed on 31 March 2025 by the Khujand trilateral treaty with Uzbekistan. The UN Secretary-General "warmly welcomed" the signing and commended both leaders' "political will to bring the decades-long negotiation process to a successful conclusion"; both parliaments ratified it and border crossings reopened (UN News, 13 March 2025; Treaty of Khujand record, March 2025).
  • Periodic mass amnesties — a standing instrument of his rule — have freed large numbers of prisoners, and the July 2026 amnesty released several imprisoned journalists and reduced Ulfatkhonim Mamadshoeva's sentence by three years (IPHR, October 2026; Asia-Plus/CPJ reporting, 2026). His government also publicly protested Russia's treatment of Tajik migrants after March 2024 (Test 4).

Evidence against:

  • Domestically, every documented pushback in three decades has been answered with force or process, never with a public reversal. The GBAO sequence is the fullest record: after police killed local resident Gulbiddin Ziyobekov on 25 November 2021, protests in Khorog were met with lethal force (several dead, dozens injured — FIDH, December 2022); when protests resumed on 16 May 2022 demanding an investigation, the authorities launched an "anti-terrorist operation" on 18 May 2022. The government's official figure for those killed was 21; the UN Special Rapporteur on minority issues reported as many as 40 may have been killed, and unofficial sources reported more than double the official figure, with allegations of extrajudicial executions that have never been independently investigated (Amnesty International Report 2022/23; HRW, May 2024). More than 200 people were arrested; no security official has been held accountable (HRW, May 2024).
  • The citizens who organized to investigate the wobble were themselves imprisoned: "Commission 44," the group of Pamiri lawyers and activists formed to investigate the November 2021 killings, was prosecuted wholesale — its head, lawyer Faromuz Irgashov, sentenced to 30 years; Manuchehr Kholiqnazarov, head of the Pamir Lawyers Association, to 15–16 years; journalist Ulfatkhonim Mamadshoeva to 21 years — in closed trials the UN and HRW criticized (Supreme Court, 9 December 2022; FIDH, 19 December 2022; HRW, 13 December 2022). State television had broadcast Mamadshoeva's "confession" before trial (Tojikiston TV, 24 May 2022).
  • The electoral wobble is answered the same way: ODIHR's findings have been formally received and substantively unimplemented through five election cycles, and when accreditation became the question in 2025 the mission was left unaccredited until it withdrew (Tests 1–2). No election result, court judgment, or protest in 32 years has caused him to change a policy in public.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — GBAO and the Pamiris, the labor migrants, the poor, the devout — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The poor, in aggregate, have been counted in on the numbers: poverty measured at the international line fell from over 90% in 1999 to roughly 11–15% by 2023–2025, and GDP growth averaged 7.7% a year over 2000–2023 and 8.4% in 2025 (World Bank, Country Partnership Framework diagnostic, 2024; World Bank country page, 2026; ADB, April 2026). The 1997 peace itself was an act of counting in: former enemies received amnesty and office rather than extermination or exile.
  • The migrants were defended, in words on the record, when it cost something diplomatically: after the 22 March 2024 Crocus City Hall attack (whose accused gunmen were Tajik nationals; Islamic State–Khorasan claimed responsibility) triggered raids, mass detentions at Russian airports, and a sharp rise in deportations, Tajik officials publicly complained, and Prime Minister Rasulzoda told a joint meeting in October 2024, in unusually blunt language: "We cannot but be alarmed by the trend of widespread violation of the fundamental rights and freedoms of our citizens" (Reuters, December 2024). Rahmon raised migration directly with Putin in a 3 May 2024 call (Kremlin and Tajik readouts, May 2024).

Evidence against:

  • GBAO and the Pamiris are counted out, by force and then by law's silence. The Pamiris — a linguistically and religiously (Ismaili Shia) distinct minority in the Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Oblast — were the object of the May–June 2022 "anti-terrorist operation" (Test 3): tens killed on monitors' counts against the official 21, 200+ arrested, long sentences in closed trials. The crackdown extended to the community's civic skeleton: authorities shut down and confiscated the property of multiple organizations linked to the Aga Khan Development Network working in education, economic development, and religious instruction in the region (Amnesty International Report 2022/23). Prominent Pamiris from the diaspora were detained on return or, per Amnesty, abducted abroad before resurfacing in Tajik custody. Two years on, HRW records: no accountability, activists still imprisoned (HRW, 15 May 2024). The government's stated position — that the operation targeted armed criminal groups destabilizing the region — is recorded; no independent investigation has ever tested it.
  • The devout are counted as a risk category. The 2016 referendum constitutionalized the ban on faith-based parties; the IRPT's 40,000 members lost their political home (Test 1). A June 2024 law on "regulating traditions and rituals" banned the wearing of clothing "foreign to national culture," applied in practice to the hijab; since 2011, minors have been barred from mosques except on religious holidays under the parental-responsibility law (legislative record; US State Department religious-freedom reporting).
  • The migrants are counted as income, not as citizens with a floor. Remittances reached an estimated $9.2 billion in 2025 — about 46–52% of GDP, the highest share in the world (World Bank, 2026; ADB, April 2026) — meaning roughly a million Tajiks work abroad, overwhelmingly in Russia, because the domestic economy's employment-to-working-age ratio remains 36% (World Bank, 2026). Their protection abroad rests on diplomacy of the kind quoted above; the documented post-Crocus reality — thousands detained at airports, deportations up 50% in Russia in the first half of 2024 — proceeded regardless (Reuters, December 2024; Russian Interior Ministry figures).
  • The regional count is uneven: GBAO remains the poorest region and, since 2022, a closed one — independent media were instructed not to report from it, and Asia-Plus complied under threat of closure (IWPR, 2022).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises and stated commitments vs. the record since 1992/1994, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Peace — kept. The central commitment of his rule, formalized in the 1997 General Agreement, was that the civil war would not return. It has not: 29 years without renewed civil war, in a state that lost tens of thousands dead in 1992–97 (1997 General Agreement; UN records).
  • Poverty reduction — substantially kept. He promised development and rising living standards; the documented outcome is the fall in poverty from over 90% (1999) to roughly 11–15% (2023–2025), on sustained 7–8% growth (World Bank, 2024–2026). The honest caveat belongs to Test 4: the engine is remittances from citizens working abroad, not domestic employment.
  • Rogun — partially kept, decades late, still open. Energy independence through the Rogun dam has been his signature material promise since the 2000s. He personally relaunched construction on 29 October 2016, switched on the first turbine on 16 November 2018 and the second in September 2019 — both at reduced head — and construction continues with heavy budget allocations (about $457 million in the 2024 budget alone). Full completion, once promised for the early 2020s, is now officially projected around 2033; the remaining financing gap is estimated above $6 billion, and winter electricity rationing in rural areas persists (Rogun project records; Interfax, November 2023; International Rivers/ENS, 2024; World Bank project appraisal, 2024). The 2010 public share issue that raised ~$185 million toward the dam was documented to have involved pressure on students and public employees to buy (Eurasianet reporting, 2018).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or reversed):

  • The 1997 accord's political half — reversed. The peace he signed promised pluralism: the UTO's parties, including the IRPT, were legalized as the accord's core bargain, with a 30% opposition quota in government. By 2015 the IRPT was banned as terrorist, its leaders imprisoned for decades or life, and faith-based parties constitutionally prohibited (2016 referendum). The security half of the accord was kept; the pluralism half was dismantled by the state he heads (Tests 1–2).
  • Democratic commitments — restated, unmet. Tajikistan's OSCE commitments (Istanbul, 1999) to invite observers and follow up ODIHR recommendations were invoked by the EU, US, UK, and Norway in February 2025 precisely because they had not been honored in the accreditation failure; ODIHR's core recommendations stand unimplemented across 2013–2025 reports (OSCE Permanent Council statements, February–March 2025; ODIHR final reports).
  • The constitutional word itself — rewritten around the man. The constitution he swore to in 1994 limited the president to two terms; referendums in 1999, 2003, and 2016 successively lengthened terms, reset his count, and finally removed limits for him personally, while a statute gave him and his family lifetime immunity (referendum records, 1999/2003/2016; Leader of the Nation law, December 2015). A promise structure that is amended whenever it binds is recorded here as the record shows it: the letter is always kept, because the letter is always his to change.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Emomali Rahmon has held one seat for 32 years, longer than any other post-Soviet ruler now in office, and the record shows a system in which the law's source stands outside the law: a personal exemption from term limits written into the constitution, lifetime immunity extended to his family, his son positioned as constitutional successor, his daughter running his office, his son-in-law at the central bank. The referees do not hold: the election commission left OSCE observers unaccredited until they went home in 2025, the last independent broadcaster's office closed in 2026, and the journalists who covered the one region that rose up are in prison after closed trials. GBAO's Pamiris are the named exception in the count — tens killed in 2022 on monitors' figures, their civic institutions confiscated, no investigation since. Against that record stand real entries on the other shelf: he ended a civil war by compromise and has kept the peace for 29 years; he negotiated a full border settlement with Kyrgyzstan after a shooting war rather than doubling down; poverty has fallen from over 90% to near a tenth of the population; and he has delivered two turbines of the dam he promised. But the tests measure the seat, not the intentions, and on the evidence assembled here the seat gives by family more than by law, grips every institution that could check it, and counts the flock with a named exception in the mountains — while the word it keeps best is the one it can rewrite.
