The Universal Screw

One drive, every size. Every fastener in everything we design carries the same drive — the same shape in the head, from the smallest electronics screw to the largest structural bolt. Sizes scale with the load, the way they always have. The interface never changes — so one driver set opens all of it.

"It's like a universal charging cable. The USB-C. Finally." … "Depending on size — everything has its purpose — but it doesn't all need to be changing."

— THE IDEA AS GIVEN, NOV 21, 2026

01

What it is — the need, named

A fastener standard for everything in this house: one drive geometry, graded by size, named on every sheet.

One drive

The recess in the head is one shape at every size in the series. A driver that fits the furniture screw fits the structural bolt — bigger, driven harder, but the same shape, the same motion, the same tool family. There is no second drive anywhere in a design from this house.

Graded sizes

Size still does its job. A furniture screw and a structural bolt carry different loads, so the series runs in seven grades, U1 to U7, on standard ISO metric threads — the size is chosen by the load, exactly the way USB-C grades power inside one connector: 15 watts for a phone, 240 for a workstation, one plug.

Named on every sheet

Every blueprint in this house details its parts down to the screw — and the screw it names is this one, by grade. Structures and furniture first, then everything else: if a sheet fastens, it fastens with the Universal Screw, and it says which grade, and why that grade.

What it means

The bit box shrinks to one set. The junk-drawer hunt for the one weird driver ends. What it could mean for you: anything built on these blueprints — a chair, a shelter, a machine — opens with the tools already in your hand, and anything you build adds to the same set instead of starting a new one.

03

The size series — down to the screw, sourced

Every grade answers the same four questions, the way every part on every sheet in this house does: what it is · what material it needs · what making or fitting it requires · where and how you get it. Torque figures are typical published tightening ranges for property class 8.8 steel — the standard's reference class — printed as class ranges, not as tests of a screw that is not built yet. Sourcing is stated as the general real channel it is; no vendor is invented.

U1

Micro — M1.6–M2.5 · electronics, wearables, instruments

What it is: the smallest fasteners in the series — the screws inside devices, glasses, watches, small mechanisms. Material: A2 stainless steel as standard; brass where the fastening must stay non-magnetic or gentle on threads (instrument work). Stock & making: starts as fine cold-heading wire; made by specialist micro-fastener manufacturers — cold-headed and thread-rolled on micro machinery. Stated honestly: this grade is factory-made, not a home-shop part, and it is not made by hand at a bench. Fitting: the universal driver at U1 size, on a torque-limited precision driver — at this size the driver's clutch, not the wrist, decides the torque. Getting it: micro-fastener and electronics-supply distributors, by specification — the ordinary channel for watch- and instrument-class screws.

≈0.1–0.5 N·m class
U2

Small — M3–M4 · devices, panels, enclosures

What it is: the device-and-panel grade — enclosures, brackets, small assemblies, the screws that hold the things on your desk together. Material: A2 stainless, or zinc-plated carbon steel where cost rules and the weather stays out. Stock & making: cold-heading wire, cold-headed and thread-rolled — the standard mass-production route for small screws everywhere. Fitting: the universal driver, hand driver or clutch driver; nothing else is required. Getting it: any fastener distributor or hardware trade counter, off the shelf, in boxes — the most ordinary sourcing on this page.

≈1–3 N·m class
U3

Furniture — M5–M6 · furniture and fixtures

What it is: the furniture grade — chairs, tables, shelving, cabinets, fixtures; the first grade the Visual Law named, because furniture is where fifty drives annoy the most people. Material: zinc-plated or stainless steel for structure; brass where the screw is visible furniture hardware and the look is part of the job. Stock & making: wire and bar stock, cold-headed and thread-rolled by any fastener maker. Fitting: the universal driver — the one in the kitchen drawer is the point of the whole sheet. Getting it: hardware stores and fastener suppliers everywhere; furniture-grade screws are a commodity on every continent.

≈5–10 N·m class
U4

General — M8–M10 · machinery and vehicles

What it is: the working grade — machinery, frames, vehicle-class assemblies, anything that vibrates for a living. Material: property class 8.8 carbon steel as the reference; 10.9 where a design specifies it. Stock & making: medium-carbon steel wire and bar; cold- or warm-headed, threads rolled, then heat-treated to the property class — the class is a heat-treatment fact, not a shape. Fitting: the universal driver on a ratchet or impact driver rated for the torque. Getting it: industrial fastener distributors, by property class and finish, in quantity.

≈25–50 N·m class
U5

Structural — M12–M20 · structures

What it is: the structural grade — the bolts that hold buildings, frames, and heavy assemblies together; the other grade named first by the rider, and the one where "universal" is never allowed to mean "casual." Material: property class 8.8 or 10.9 steel, hot-dip galvanized where the structure lives outdoors. Stock & making: bar stock, hot-headed or forged, threads rolled or cut, heat-treated, and — stated at full size — structural bolting is a certified trade: bolts, nuts, and washers are made, tested, and sold as matched assemblies with batch traceability. Fitting: the universal driver on calibrated torque or tension-control tooling, fitted by people qualified for structural work. The universal drive changes the interface; it does not certify the fitter. Getting it: structural bolting suppliers, as certified assemblies — never loose from an unlabeled bin.

≈85–410 N·m class
U6

Heavy — M24–M30 · heavy frames, infrastructure class

What it is: the heavy grade — large frames, heavy plant, infrastructure-class joints. Material: 10.9 and structural grades as specified, galvanized or coated for exposure. Stock & making: bar stock, hot-forged and machined, heat-treated, batch-certified like U5. Fitting: the universal driver on calibrated or hydraulic torque tooling, under an engineer's tightening specification. Getting it: heavy-fastener manufacturers and structural suppliers, made or stocked to order — this grade is procured against a specification, not picked off a shelf.

≈700–1,100 N·m class
U7

Extreme — M36 and up · bridge, crane, press and foundation bolting

What it is: the extreme end — the largest joints in the series: bridge bearings and splices, crane rails and slew rings, press frames, and foundation anchor bolting, where a single joint carries loads no hand tooling moves. Material: property class 8.8 and 10.9 steel as specified, with high-strength structural grades and hot-dip galvanized or coated finishes where the joint lives outdoors or in concrete; alloy steels where the specification calls for them — the material is an engineer's specification, not a shelf choice. Stock & making: large bar stock, hot-forged, machined and thread-rolled or cut, heat-treated, and supplied as certified matched assemblies — bolt, nut and washers — with full batch traceability, like U5 and U6 at larger scale. Fitting: the universal driver on hydraulic torque or hydraulic tensioning tooling — the norm at this size — fitted under an engineer's tightening or tensioning specification by crews qualified for heavy structural work. The universal drive changes the interface; it does not change the certification, the calculation, or the crew. Getting it: certified structural and heavy bolting suppliers, procured to order against a specification — never loose, never unlabeled, never substituted off a shelf.

≈2,500 N·m + class

The driver set itself: one bit per grade, seven bits, one case — made from hardened tool steel (S2-class bit steel is the ordinary material), cold-formed or machined, bought wherever driver bits are sold. Seven bits replace the fifty-piece drawer.

04

Why one — the why chain

WHY NOT FIFTY

Every extra drive is a small toll booth

Ask why a toolbox needs fifty drivers and the chain runs: because the heads differ → because each maker picked a drive → because the drive was patented, licensed, withheld, or made proprietary → because controlling the interface was worth money or control. The bottom of the chain is rarely engineering. The record (§05) shows the best early drive losing North America over a licence refusal, and a weaker one spreading because it licensed to everyone.

A proprietary drive is leverage in miniature: whoever controls the interface decides who may open the thing, who may repair it, and what the opening costs. That is the shape this whole plan exists to remove — at the scale of a screw head.

WHY ONE WORKS

The interface is not the load

The objection "a watch screw and a bridge bolt are different jobs" is true — about size. The drive is not the size. USB-C proved the separation: one connector carries 15 watts and 240, because the interface and the capacity are different decisions. The screw already knows this trick — the thread underneath standardized (ISO metric) while the heads fragmented. This design finishes the job the thread started.

And the payoff compounds: one drive means one driver set, one habit, one teaching. A child who learns the furniture screw has learned the structural bolt's interface too — the skill scales with the size, free.

05

The research — how we got fifty

Fifty drives were not chosen. They accumulated — each one a real answer to a real problem of its decade, and several of them kept alive by patents, licences, and gates long after the problem changed. The four cases that decide this design:

1933–1936 · The cross

Phillips — built for the line, cam-out and all

John P. Thompson patented a cross-recess design in 1933; Henry F. Phillips bought it, refined it, and licensed it to everyone — General Motors ran it on the 1937 Cadillac, and by 1940 most American screw makers held licences. Its tapered walls self-center under a power driver, and they cam out at a torque limit — on 1930s lines with no clutch drivers, that slip was the torque control. In a modern hand it is why Phillips heads strip. Phillips' own company later designed the corrections (Pozidriv, 1966 — which, confusingly, does not interchange with Phillips).

Assembly Magazine — Figuring Out Fastener Drive Styles

1909 · The square

Robertson — the best drive lost to a licence

Peter L. Robertson's tapered square socket grips, self-centers, holds the screw one-handed, and resists cam-out — and his cold-forming breakthrough made it cheap to make. Henry Ford found it saved about two hours per Model T and asked for an exclusive US licence. Robertson refused; Ford walked; the square stayed Canadian — where it remains the near-universal drive a century later, proof at national scale that one drive can serve furniture, boats, and building. The drive that spread was not the best drive. It was the licensed one.

Wikipedia — Robertson screw · ThomasNet — History of the Robertson Screwdriver

1967 · The star

Torx — engineered against cam-out

Once clutch drivers existed, Camcar Textron designed the opposite of Phillips: the hexalobular drive (Torx, standardized as ISO 10664) puts contact near 90° to the turning force, so it transfers more torque than a same-size hex socket, does not cam out by design, and wears bits slowly. The auto industry converged on it. It is the strongest documented starting point for this sheet's Open How 01 — and even it sprouted non-interchangeable variants (Torx Plus, Paralobe, pin-in security versions).

Wikipedia — Torx

2014–2024 · The plug

USB-C — the interface held, the tiers grew

One connector, and capacity graded inside it: USB Power Delivery 3.1 (2021) raised the same plug from 100 W to 240 W (48 V, 5 A) for workstations, while a phone sips 15 W through the identical shape. Then the European Union made the experiment law — from 28 December 2024, new phones, tablets, cameras, headphones, consoles, and more sold in the EU must charge by USB-C (laptops follow in April 2026), on stated grounds of convenience and waste. A big enough standard becomes the default everywhere.

Texas Instruments — USB PD Extended Power Range · European Parliament — USB-C becomes the standard

The honest case against one drive — at full size Printed, not buried

  • Tamper resistance is sometimes the point. Security drives exist because a public fixture anyone's pocket tool can open will be opened — restrooms, transit hardware, utility boxes. A universal drive puts the universal driver in every pocket, the wrong ones included. This design's answer (access governed by the Earned Access layer, §06) is graded partial: complete inside this house's own builds, honestly incomplete for a park bench. Where a fastening must resist the public, it is specified as a security fastening — a named exception, not a quiet return to fifty drives.
  • The ends of the range pull opposite ways. An M2 head has almost no material around its recess; an M24 bolt must move hundreds of newton-metres. No existing drive family has proven one geometry optimal across that whole span — that is Open How 01, the design's central unknown, and this sheet does not pretend otherwise.
  • Torque limiting has to live somewhere. Phillips put it in the geometry because 1930s drivers had no clutches. A no-cam-out drive moves it entirely into the tool and the operator — reasonable with modern clutch drivers, a real responsibility with cheap ones.
  • A standard can freeze. Critics of the EU mandate argued a locked interface locks out its successor. The counter-record: USB-C kept evolving inside the connector (2.5 W → 240 W). Both stand; the Adaptation Clause (§06) is this design's standing answer.

Standing Laws of This Design

Three laws stand over every sheet in this house, this one included — printed here at a fastener's honest scale: short, because a screw is a small thing and padding a small thing helps no one.

LAW ONE

The Three Modes

Set down Nov 21, 2026 · CC BY-SA 4.0. GOLDEN = spread, never align. PLATINUM = align exactly, equal, 1:1 — where alignment is the function. DIAMOND = align locally, spread globally. Governor over all three: use it when it works — don't force it.

Graded from this sheet, this is the most Platinum design in the house: PLATINUM is the design itself — one interface, identical everywhere it appears, 1:1 across seven grades and every blueprint that names it. DIAMOND is the series as a whole: the drive aligned exactly at every joint, the grades spread across every load class from watch to building. GOLDEN is claimed only where it truly fits: nothing here spreads by the golden angle, and the sheet says so rather than dress arithmetic in a mode — the grade steps are sized by load tables, not by φ.

LAW TWO

The Adaptation Clause — Adapt and Overcome

"Learn your lessons and grow." If a better drive geometry is proven — at the small end, the big end, or across the span — it is fitted into the series and the sheet is updated; the interface changes once, everywhere, in the open, never piecemeal and never by stealth. Today's version is the floor, not the ceiling. This is the standing answer to the frozen-standard objection in §05.

LAW THREE

Earned Access — with the Resources Clause

Access to powerful things is earned, and earned access means access to the resources, not just a permission slip. Carried here at a fastener's weight, in both directions: the screw itself is deliberately not a gate — the driver is common because opening the things we build is the people's ordinary right, and this design refuses the tamper-drive trick of gating owners out of their own property. Where a design genuinely needs a gate — the suit, a structure's critical joints — the gate stands at the design's Earned Access layer: on who may build, fit, and open, decided openly. It does not hide inside the screw head. Graded honestly in §05: that answer is partial in the open world, and the residue is printed there, not smoothed over.

07

Open hows — labeled open

OPEN HOW 01 — THE OPEN HOW

One geometry that performs at every size

The exact drive geometry that (a) does not cam out destructively or split heads at micro sizes, (b) does not round, slip, or fail at structural torque, and (c) can be cold-formed or machined economically at every grade in the series. This sheet names the requirement and does not invent a shape and claim it works. The documented starting point is the hexalobular family (Torx / ISO 10664) — the best-recorded combination of torque, no cam-out, and bit life — but its record is proven per-size, not across this whole span, and its own variant family shows the failure mode to avoid. The geometry is settled by testing across the grades, in the open, or it is not settled at all.

OPEN HOW 02

Torque discipline at the small end

A no-cam-out drive hands torque control to the tool. At U1–U2, cheap drivers without clutches will overdrive fasteners until something gives. Whether the answer is tooling (clutch drivers specified on the sheets that use small grades), training, or a deliberate, limited slip property engineered only into the smallest grades — open. What is not open: pretending the problem away.

OPEN HOW 03

The thread underneath, in an inch world

The series is specified on ISO metric threads. The installed world — especially North American structures and machinery — still runs heavily on inch (Unified) threads. Whether the universal drive ships on both thread systems during a transition, or the sheets hold metric-only and let conversion be the builder's stated choice, is a real question with friction on both sides. Open.

08

Honest limits & safety

Read before building

  • This is a design on paper. It is not built, tested, or certified. No Universal Screw exists yet; nothing on this sheet is a test result. The torque figures are the standard published classes for the reference steel, not measurements of this fastener.
  • Structural grades are certified work. U5, U6 and U7 fastenings belong to qualified people working to an engineer's specification, with certified assemblies. A shared drive shape changes none of that — ever.
  • The tamper residue is real. In the open world, a universal driver lowers the effort of casual interference with public fastenings. §05 grades the design's answer partial and names the security-fastening exception. Builders of public fixtures should read that section as part of this sheet, not as fine print.
  • Micro grades are factory parts. U1 is specified and sourced, not hand-made. A sheet that needs U1 buys U1 through the channel §03 names.
  • Existing standards are not mocked here. Phillips, Robertson, and Torx each solved the problem of their decade; the research in §05 grades them fairly, including where they beat this design's current state — Robertson's century of Canadian service is a record this sheet has to earn, not a record it has.

What are we missing?

Every blueprint has a blind side. If a grade is mis-sized, a sourcing channel is wrong, or an Open How is missing from the shelf — say so. The sheet gets better in the open or not at all.
