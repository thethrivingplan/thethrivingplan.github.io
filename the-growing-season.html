How a day works

A day holds 8 work hours. Work spends your energy too — plant, water, and harvest until one runs out, then end the day and sleep. Game time is compressed: 1 game day ≈ 10 real growing days, and a season lasts 14 game days. Real days-to-harvest are printed on every crop.

Crops & companions — the knowledge, with the why

Companion planting is real knowledge and folk knowledge mixed together. Each pairing below is labelled: Documented where the mechanism is well supported, Contested where gardeners swear by it but trials are mixed. Nothing here is dressed up as more certain than it is.

PairWhy it can helpStanding
Lettuce + TomatoDifferent root depths (lettuce shallow, tomato deep) so they don't fight for the same water; lettuce grows in the tomato's shade during hot spells and is harvested before the tomato needs the space. The classic space-and-shade pairing.Documented
Corn + Beans (the Three Sisters, with squash)Beans are legumes: root bacteria fix nitrogen from the air into the soil that heavy-feeding corn needs. Corn gives bean vines a pole to climb. Squash leaves shade the soil, holding moisture and crowding out weeds.Documented
Tomato + BasilBasil's strong scent may confuse tomato pests like thrips and hornworm moths (pest confusion), and both want the same sun and water, so care is shared. Claims that basil makes tomatoes taste better or hugely boosts yield are not proven.Contested for yield/taste; scent effect plausible
Carrot + OnionOnion scent is said to confuse carrot root fly, which finds carrots by smell. Gardeners have used it for generations; controlled trials show only partial, inconsistent protection.Contested
Beans + almost anythingLegumes leave soil richer in nitrogen than they found it. Following or flanking a heavy feeder (corn, kale, tomato) with beans is the engine of rotation.Documented
Tomato + Potato — keep apartSame family (nightshades): they share diseases, above all late blight, which jumps between them fast in wet weather. Planting them together is planting an epidemic a short cut.Documented — a pairing to avoid
Onion family + Beans — keep apart?Allium root compounds are said to inhibit the bacteria legumes depend on. Evidence is thin and mostly from lab dishes, not fields — many gardeners grow them together without trouble.Contested — a pairing to watch

The deep why, in one line each: pest confusion — strong or mixed scents make it harder for a pest to find its one host plant by smell. Root depth — shallow and deep roots drink from different layers. Shade — a tall crop is a parasol for a cool-season crop. Nitrogen — legumes are the only common crops that add fertility instead of spending it. Shared family, shared disease — related crops catch each other's illnesses.

Soil & seasons

Soil health — it remembers

Every plot carries a soil score (shown on the plot). It responds to what you actually do:

  • Rest it: an empty plot left fallow slowly recovers (+1 a day, up to full health).
  • Rotate: planting a different plant family than last time protects the score.
  • Repeat the same family in the same plot and soil drops sharply — pests and diseases that love that family build up in it.
  • Beans give back: harvesting a legume leaves the plot richer than planting found it.
  • Tired soil (under 40) grows crops at half speed and halves the harvest. There is no fertiliser button that fixes it in a day — in real gardens, as here, soil is rebuilt by rest, rotation, and legumes over time.

Seasons — difficulty ramps

  • Spring — the kind season. Cool crops (lettuce, carrot, kale) thrive; frost is rare.
  • Summer — heat and pests arrive. Tomatoes, corn, and basil fly; lettuce bolts (goes bitter and to seed) in heatwaves.
  • Autumn — frost returns. Kale and carrots sweeten after a light frost; tender crops must come in before a hard one.
  • Winter — the resting season. Almost nothing grows; this is the soil's holiday and the planner's season. Crops planted in winter mostly wait, slowly, for spring.

Weather turns daily: sun, cloud, rain, heatwave, frost. Rain waters everything free; a heatwave drinks double; frost can kill tender crops outright.

Pests & weather — honest responses

When trouble arrives in the game, it arrives with these shelves — the same shelves shown here so you can read ahead. What works is supported by practice and trials. What really hasn't is the shelf most guides are too polite to print. What to try is plausible, lower-certainty, worth an experiment.

Aphids on the greens

What works

  • A hard jet of water knocks them off; repeat every few days.
  • Ladybirds and lacewings — leave them somewhere to live and they do the patrol.
  • Pinch out and bin the worst-infested tips.

What really hasn't

  • Broad-spectrum insecticide: kills the predators too, and aphids — breeding faster than their enemies — come back bigger.
  • One spray of soapy water, once, then declaring it useless or done.

What to try

  • Neem or insecticidal soap, in the evening, on the undersides of leaves.
  • A sacrificial nasturtium patch nearby to draw them off.

Drought & heatwave

What works

  • Deep, less-frequent watering that sends roots down, early in the morning.
  • Mulch — straw, leaves, grass clippings — to shade soil and hold moisture.
  • Shade cloth over cool-season crops.

What really hasn't

  • A light sprinkle every midday: it evaporates, keeps roots shallow, and wets leaves for fungus.
  • Watering wilted-looking beans at noon before checking — beans fold their leaves in heat even when moist.

What to try

  • Clay-pot (olla) buried irrigation for thirsty beds.
  • Planting the thirsty crop on the shady side of a tall one — companions again.

Late blight (tomato & potato)

What works

  • Space plants for airflow; water the soil, not the leaves.
  • Remove and bin (never compost) infected plants fast.
  • Keep tomato and potato apart — see the companions table.

What really hasn't

  • Saving seed or tubers from a blighted crop.
  • Composting blighted plants — home heaps don't get hot enough to kill it.

What to try

  • Blight-resistant varieties next season — the honest long game.
  • Copper sprays where permitted; evidence is preventive only, never a cure.

Slugs & snails

What works

  • Night patrol with a torch after rain — unglamorous, decisive.
  • Iron-phosphate pellets (the lower-harm kind), sparingly.
  • Encourage frogs, hedgehogs, and ground beetles.

What really hasn't

  • Beer traps alone in a big garden — they catch some and may attract more.
  • Salt — it damages the soil you're feeding.

What to try

  • Copper tape around prized pots.
  • A board or grapefruit half as a trap-and-collect shelter.
