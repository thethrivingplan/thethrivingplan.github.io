The Ruler's Test — Mamady Doumbouya, President of Guinea

Subject: Mamady Doumbouya — President of Guinea. (The name is spelled "Mamady" in the authoritative record — Reuters, the UN, ECOWAS and the Guinean electoral authority — not "Mamadi" as it appeared in the brief; the dossier uses the documented spelling.) The seat graded is the presidency, Guinea's executive seat, which he has held continuously since 5 September 2021 under two different titles. Doumbouya, a special-forces commander born in Kankan in 1980, led the unit that seized power on 5 September 2021, detaining President Alpha Condé, dissolving the government and the 2020 constitution, and ruling through the CNRD junta under a Charter of the Transition adopted later that month; he was sworn in as transitional president on 1 October 2021 and promoted himself through the ranks during his own transition, reaching general. The timing is stated plainly because the seat changed its legal nature mid-tenure: a new constitution was approved by referendum on 21 September 2025 (official results: 89.38% in favour, turnout reported at 86–88%, the figures contested by the opposition), promulgated on 26 September 2025; it removed the transition charter's bar on junta members standing for election, extended the presidential term from five to seven years (renewable once), and created a Senate one-third of whose members the president appoints. Doumbouya filed his candidacy on 3 November 2025, won the presidential election of 28 December 2025 with 86.72% of the vote (Direction Générale des Élections figure, announced 30 December 2025; runner-up Abdoulaye Yéro Baldé 6.59%), was confirmed by the Supreme Court on 4 January 2026, and was inaugurated as elected president on 17 January 2026 for a seven-year term. He is therefore graded on the whole continuous tenure — four years and four months as transitional ruler, then as elected president — because it is one holding of one seat by one man, and the second phase was obtained under rules the first phase wrote. Legislative and communal elections followed on 31 May 2026; the elected National Assembly was installed on 17 July 2026; senatorial elections are scheduled for 20 December 2026, after this grading. His prime minister, Amadou Oury Bah (Bah Oury), in office since February–March 2024 and reappointed on 26 January 2026, appears as context; the seat graded is Doumbouya's. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic of the Test, printed on its card: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Two standing rules of method apply throughout. The mandate rule: governing on a platform voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one — the Test grades the holding, never the platform; carrying out what a ruler ran on is FOR-shelf evidence on Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done. The no-good-choice clause: where the record shows every option that actually existed carried real harm, the dossier names the options and grades the choice as the best possible available in the moment; hindsight options do not count, but whether the ruler built the trap earlier, was honest inside it, and took the least-harm door open remain gradable.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machinery he built did convict the powerful of the previous order, in court, with sentences. The special court he created after the coup, the Court for the Repression of Economic and Financial Offences (CRIEF), convicted former prime minister Ibrahima Kassory Fofana in February 2025 — five years for embezzlement of some 15 billion Guinean francs from social and Covid-era programmes, illicit enrichment and corruption (reduced on appeal in July 2026 to three years and nine months, the embezzlement conviction overturned, the enrichment and money-laundering convictions upheld). Former defence minister Mohamed Diané was convicted in December 2024 (five years), and former National Assembly president Amadou Damaro Camara in December 2024 (four years, reduced on appeal to three and a half). In November 2022 the junta ordered prosecutors to pursue Condé himself and more than 180 former officials (contemporaneous reporting, February 2025; appeal reporting, July 2026).
  • The justice his transition delivered for the 28 September 2009 stadium massacre reached a former head of the military order itself: the trial opened on 28 September 2022, the thirteenth anniversary of the massacre, and on 31 July 2024 the Dixinn Court of First Instance convicted eight defendants of crimes against humanity — including former junta leader Moussa Dadis Camara, sentenced to 20 years on the basis of superior responsibility — while acquitting four, and ordered reparations to victims (trial reporting, July 2024; FIDH and partner reporting, 2025). In March 2025 his government announced the state would itself pay the court-ordered reparations, which range from 200 million to 1.5 billion Guinean francs per victim file, rather than leave them to insolvent convicts (government announcement, 26 March 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The rulebook itself was rewritten for one man. The Charter of the Transition — the founding law of his own regime — barred members of the CNRD, the transitional government and the transitional council from standing in the elections that would end the transition, and Doumbouya publicly pledged in 2021 that neither he nor any junta member would be a candidate. The 2025 constitution deleted the bar, lengthened the term he would serve, and added a residency requirement that excluded his two most formidable rivals — Condé, 87 and abroad, and Cellou Dalein Diallo, in exile — from the ballot altogether (International Crisis Group analysis, 2025; KAS country report, January 2026). A law that is amended at the source so the lawgiver may keep the seat is the definition of a second law for the insider.
  • The protest ban ran one way. The blanket ban on demonstrations imposed in May 2022 was enforced against opposition and civil-society gatherings — with lethal force, see Test 4 — while demonstrations supporting the president were allowed to proceed (Amnesty International, The State of the World's Human Rights: Guinea 2024). One law for the street, another for the supporters.
  • Accountability stopped at his own gate. CRIEF's docket is a docket of the previous regime: no member of the CNRD, no commander of the forces that killed protesters between 2021 and 2024, and no official connected to the documented disappearances (Test 4) has been prosecuted on the record assembled here. The law that reached a former prime minister for Covid funds has not reached a single member of the order that took power promising to end impunity.
  • The pardon power was spent on a fellow soldier. On 28 March 2025 — eight months after the Dixinn verdict, two days after the state assumed the reparations bill — Doumbouya pardoned Moussa Dadis Camara "for health reasons" by decree read on national television, freeing the man his own court had convicted of crimes against humanity (decree reporting, March 2025). Victims' organisations and international rights groups condemned the pardon as unmaking the trial's meaning; the appeal process for the remaining convicts had still not been heard a year after the verdict (FIDH and partners, July 2025). Whatever its stated ground, the sequence teaches the country that a conviction holds for civilians of the old order and bends for a former junta head.
  • A contested coda: in September 2026 Doumbouya authorised two convicted Condé-era officials — Kassory Fofana and Damaro Camara — to travel abroad for medical care, Fofana on a flight his lawyer said was chartered by the presidency, after years in which the courts had refused Fofana's requests for treatment abroad. Doumbouya addressed the nation to insist the moves were "neither pardons nor amnesties" and the convictions stand — an explanation recorded here in full. Critics answered that the exception was available to well-connected convicts while disappeared activists' families cannot obtain even information (reporting and presidential address, September 2026). The item is recorded as contested; its weight in this grade is small next to the charter rewrite and the pardon.

Why the grade lands here: The for-shelf is real — men who looted under Condé are in prison, and a massacre trial the country waited thirteen years for was held. But Test 1 grades the source, and at the source the law was applied downward at opponents and rewritten upward for the ruler: the eligibility rule changed for his candidacy, the protest ban bound only his critics, the pardon freed his fellow ex-junta leader, and his own forces stand outside the accountability he advertises. That is not one law unevenly enforced; it is two laws, sorted by side.

Helped: Victims of the 2009 massacre and their families, who received a trial, verdicts and a state commitment to pay reparations; Guineans who lost public funds under the Condé administration, whose cases were actually prosecuted. Hurt: The voters and members of the opposition parties, for whom the eligibility and protest rules were written differently; the 2009 victims again, in the pardon that freed the convicted author of their harm eight months after sentence; every Guinean whose claim on the law depends on not being connected to the seat.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Courts under his tenure have, at documented moments, decided against the executive's immediate interest. The Dixinn court acquitted four of the twelve massacre defendants even as it convicted eight. The appeals process cut both Condé-era sentences: Kassory Fofana's term reduced in July 2026 with one conviction overturned outright; Damaro Camara's reduced in May 2025. The Supreme Court heard runner-up Yéro Baldé's petition against the December 2025 results before confirming them on 4 January 2026 (Baldé subsequently withdrew his complaint, per his own camp's reporting) (appeal and validation reporting, 2025–2026).
  • The institutional build-out of 2026 was real, whatever its conditions: legislative and communal elections were held on 31 May 2026, an elected National Assembly of 147 members replaced the appointed transitional council on 17 July 2026, and elected communal councils replaced the appointed special delegations that had run the communes since 2024 (Amani Africa briefing, June 2026; Reuters, 5 June 2026).
  • Regional referees were re-admitted rather than defied: ECOWAS lifted all remaining sanctions on 28 January 2026 and the African Union lifted Guinea's suspension the same month, both citing the completed referendum and election (ECOWAS communiqué, 28 January 2026; AU Peace and Security Council, January 2026). Guinea took its seat back in the bodies that judge transitions — and in July 2026 Doumbouya addressed an ECOWAS summit in Freetown as a member in good standing.

Evidence against:

  • The media referee was gripped early and never released. Signals of the main independent broadcasters were jammed from late November 2023; their channels were pulled from satellite packages in December 2023 on "national security" grounds; and on 21–22 May 2024 the information minister revoked the licences of six outlets — FIM FM, Djoma FM, Djoma TV, Espace FM, Espace TV, Sweet FM — by ministerial decree, although the organic law reserves frequency withdrawal to the media regulator, the HAC, a procedural bypass RSF documented at the time (RSF and CPJ reporting, May 2024). Further outlets were suspended around the September 2025 referendum (OHCHR statement, September 2025). The pattern continued into his elected term: on 16–17 September 2026 the HAC banned France 24 nationwide after a presenter called him a "coup president" — the channel apologised, per AFP reporting, and the ban stood — and days earlier the Jeune Afrique correspondent's accreditation was withdrawn (CPJ, 18 September 2026).
  • The electoral referee is his own appointee. Elections are administered by the Direction Générale des Élections, a body created under his transition inside the executive's orbit; its head announced his 86.72% victory and the 80.95% turnout, a figure civic groups dispute (DGE announcements, December 2025; International IDEA country reporting, December 2025). The ECOWAS and AU observer missions called the vote peaceful and orderly while noting the exclusion of major opposition leaders; the UN's human rights chief described the pre-election space as severely restricted (OHCHR, 26 December 2025).
  • The party registrar is a ministry, and it dissolved the opposition. After an October 2024 evaluation suspended or dissolved dozens of parties, the authorities suspended the three main opposition parties — UFDG, RPG and PRP — on 22 August 2025, weeks before the referendum; an October 2025 lifting freed 51 smaller parties while the main ones stayed barred. Then, on 6 March 2026 — less than two months after his inauguration — the Ministry of Territorial Administration dissolved 40 parties by decree, including UFDG, RPG Arc-en-ciel and UFR, stripping their legal status, banning their names and symbols, and placing their assets under sequestration, for stated failures to meet legal obligations (decree reporting, March 2026; Amani Africa, June 2026). The May 2026 legislative elections were then held with no major opposition party allowed to participate, and his GMD coalition and allies took at least 100 of 147 seats (Reuters, 5 June 2026).
  • Local referees were replaced by appointees for two years. In his 31 December 2023 address Doumbouya announced that state appointees would take over the municipal councils elected in 2018; by decree in March 2024 the communal councils were dissolved and replaced by appointed special delegations, and military officers were appointed as governors, prefects and sub-prefects across the territory (decree reporting, March 2024; opposition coalition statement citing the Local Authorities Code, March 2024). Elected local government returned only in May 2026 — and the minister responsible has since reminded new mayors that appointed delegations "remain a legal option" if a council is judged to underperform (ministerial statements reported 2026).
  • The new constitution builds the grip in: one-third of the Senate is appointed by the president, and the Senate cannot be dissolved (Constitution of 2025; electoral-system reporting, 2026).

Why the grade lands here: A ruler who does not grip lets referees he did not choose run games he might lose. Every referee that touched Doumbouya's tenure was, by grading, inside his hand: the electoral administration announced his victory, the party registrar dissolved his opposition between his election and the legislative vote, the media regulator's powers were exercised by his minister and then against a foreign channel that mislabeled him, and local government ran on appointees until the field had been cleared. The courts' documented moments of independence — acquittals, sentence reductions — are real, and they are recorded; they concern the previous regime's men and a massacre of 2009, never the tenure of the man who appoints.

Helped: The GMD coalition and its candidates, who contested the May 2026 elections on a field cleared of the three largest parties; state and aligned media, which operated without the competition of the closed outlets. Hurt: The dissolved parties and their voters — the UFDG, RPG and UFR together represented the largest organised electorates in the country; the journalists and staff of the closed outlets and of France 24's Guinea operation; Guinean voters, whose 2025–2026 ballots were counted by an administration answerable to a candidate.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The ECOWAS wobble was negotiated, not defied. Facing suspension and sanctions after the coup, his government negotiated the October 2022 "dynamic compromise" — a 10-point, 24-month roadmap — rather than breaking with the bloc as Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger later did; Guinea stayed in ECOWAS's process throughout (ECOWAS communiqués, 2022–2026).
  • When the roadmap's deadline was missed (see Test 5), the government did not deny the miss into silence: it stated a revised sequence openly — constitutional referendum first, then elections completing the transition by the end of 2025 — and then met the revised date to the day: referendum 21 September 2025, presidential election 28 December 2025, inauguration 17 January 2026. ECOWAS's January 2026 communiqué records the bloc accepting that the authorities "fulfilled their commitment to restore constitutional order by the end of 2025." Here the no-good-choice clause is applied as designed: by late 2024 the options that actually existed were an election run without a constitution, without a completed electoral roll, and under a charter that barred the incumbent camp entirely — or a stated extension to finish the sequence. Each carried real harm; the extension, openly announced and then honoured on its new date, is graded as the best choice available in the moment on the timetable question, and the constraint is recorded, not held against him. What the clause does not cover — because it was a choice, not a constraint — is the use of the extension to remove his own ineligibility, which is graded where it belongs, in Tests 1 and 5. It is also recorded that the sequencing emergency was partly of his government's own making: the census and constitutional drafting ran on his timetable for three years.
  • Domestic pushback once produced a visible, if unexplained, adjustment: on 19 February 2024, a week before a general strike over living costs, media censorship and detained unionists, he dissolved his entire government by decree — ministers' accounts frozen, passports surrendered — and appointed Bah Oury to rebuild it (decree reporting, February 2024). The strike proceeded on 26 February; the government that followed was his answer, offered without a stated reason.
  • In the September 2026 medical-evacuation controversy (Test 1), he answered criticism in person and on the record, giving reasons — medical necessity, convictions intact — rather than ignoring it (presidential address, September 2026).
  • The December 2025 result was challenged through the referee and the referee answered: Baldé petitioned the Supreme Court, the court confirmed the results on 4 January 2026, and the transfer proceeded without violence (validation reporting, January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The central wobble of the tenure — the charter's bar and his own pledge — was answered by abolishing the rule that produced it. When the pushback was "you promised not to run," the response was not adjustment, nor an open argument that the promise should be revisited; it was a new constitution, drafted under his transition, that removed the bar, surrounded by the suspension of the parties most likely to campaign against it. The Test's standing question applies directly: if another option becomes available, does the ruler at least consider it? An option was available throughout — preside over the transition's end as referee, as the charter contemplated, and let others contest — and on the documented record it was never openly considered; government officials were informally campaigning for his candidacy months before he announced it, while he maintained silence (International Crisis Group, 2025).
  • The street's wobble was answered with a ban and with fire. Demonstrations have been banned outright since May 2022; those who defied the ban were dispersed with tear gas and live ammunition (Test 4 gives the tolls). No pushback from the street — over prices, electricity, the cost of living — produced a documented policy adjustment; the February–March 2024 protests in Kindia and Boffa were answered by security forces, and the investigations announced into the killings of children there have produced no published findings on the record assembled here.
  • The UN's wobble was refused twice, in writing. In September 2025 the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights urged the authorities to lift the bans on opposition parties and media outlets ahead of the December polls; on 26 December 2025 he called again for an end to pre-election intimidation and for impartial investigation of the disappearances. Neither call was answered with any lifting, investigation or public reasoning: the bans stood, the election was held under them, and in March 2026 the suspended parties were dissolved outright (OHCHR statements, September and December 2025).
  • The smallest wobble produced the purest double-down: when a France 24 presenter called him a "coup president" in September 2026 and the channel apologised, the response was a nationwide ban on the channel by all technical means and the withdrawal of its journalists' accreditations (CPJ, September 2026). A ruler secure in the adjustment would have banked the apology.
  • The Forécariah wobble is still open. On 24 August 2026 six unidentified men were found in a mass grave in the Sabakhouré sector of Allassoyah, Forécariah prefecture — bound and blindfolded, according to consistent reporting; the prosecutor opened an investigation, the armed forces confirmed the discovery, and the National Defence and Security Commission promised results through official channels while urging against speculation (AFP via Al Jazeera, 1 September 2026; Guinean reporting, August–September 2026). Twenty African civil-society organisations called for an independent investigation with international forensic support. By this grading — three months on — no identities, causes of death, or findings have been published. Who the six men were, and who put them there, is NOT ESTABLISHED on this record and is recorded as unknown, not attributed; what is gradable is the unanswered state of the file, in a country where the disappearance file (Test 4) has stood unanswered for over two years.

Why the grade lands here: MIXED was weighed — the ECOWAS negotiation, the openly restated and then honoured end-2025 date, and the personal answer to the evacuation critics are genuine adjustments, and they are on the shelf. The grade fails on the pattern beneath them: every wobble that touched his tenure — the charter, the street, the UN, a television label — was met by rewriting the rule, banning the gathering, ignoring the call, or banning the channel. He adjusts the timetable and the personnel. He does not adjust the holding.

Helped: Guinea's regional standing and its traders and travellers, who kept ECOWAS membership and saw sanctions fully lifted in January 2026; the transition's administrators, whose revised sequence was allowed to run. Hurt: The protesters and unions whose pushback was criminalised by the standing ban; the disappeared whose cases the UN raised and the state left unanswered; France 24's Guinean audience and staff; the six unidentified dead of Forécariah and their families, whose file has joined the unanswered ones.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's voters, the regions and mining communities, the protesters, and the disappeared — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The dead of 2009 were counted, by name and by file, under his tenure: the massacre trial heard survivors and civil parties over nearly two years, convicted eight, and set reparations the state then agreed to pay (see Test 1). For the families of at least 156 killed — the toll documented by the UN-mandated commission of inquiry, with over 100 women subjected to rape and other sexual violence — this was the first counting the Guinean state had ever performed.
  • The flagship economic act of the tenure was framed, and partly built, as a national inheritance rather than a concession: Simandou, the world's largest undeveloped high-grade iron-ore deposit, began operations on 11 November 2025 after decades of failed starts — a co-development in which the state holds 15% of the infrastructure company (Compagnie du TransGuinéen), the 650-kilometre railway is multi-use and transfers to that company, and first exports sailed in December 2025, reaching over 5 million tonnes in October 2026 alone (project partners' statements, November 2025; trade reporting, 2026). His "Simandou 2040" programme presents the revenues as a fifteen-year national development fund, and his public line — Guinea "will no longer be content to export its resources in their raw state" — counts the country's interior regions into the project's stated purpose.
  • The communal elections of 31 May 2026 restored an elected tier that counts locally: councils in 342 communes, elected for the first time since 2018, on lists required to alternate men and women with at least 30% women (electoral reporting, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The protesters were counted as targets. The counts are kept separate, as they come: Amnesty International documented at least 47 protesters killed between September 2021 and 15 March 2024, and reported at least 19 children allegedly killed by defence and security forces during protests since the coup (Amnesty International, Wounded Youth, May 2024, and April 2025 statement to the African Committee of Experts). The FNDC and Guinean rights organisations consulted by Human Rights Watch put deaths during protests since June 2022 at up to 59, including at least 5 children (HRW, December 2024). Human Rights Watch's own research documented at least nine killed in Conakry between January and September 2024 alone, among them a woman and four children aged 9 to 17. Named instances on the public record: a 17-year-old shot dead in Boffa prefecture during the February 2024 general strike, and two boys aged 8 and 14 shot dead in Kindia on 12 March 2024 during protests over a power cut — all allegedly by security forces, the word being Amnesty's, the investigations announced and unpublished (Amnesty International reporting, 2024).
  • At Nzérékoré on 1 December 2024, a crush at the final of a football tournament held in Doumbouya's honour killed, on the government's toll, 56 people; a collective of local human-rights organisations, counting from the hospital, cemeteries, families, mosques and churches, estimated 135 dead, most under 18, with over 50 missing (government statement, 2 December 2024; Nzérékoré rights collective statement, December 2024). The two counts are printed separately and not blended. The collective blamed excessive tear gas and the protection of officials over spectators; the government promised an investigation; no published findings appear on the record assembled here.
  • The disappeared are not counted at all — that is the point of the method used on them. Oumar Sylla, known as Foniké Menguè, national coordinator of the FNDC, and Mamadou Billo Bah, a leading figure of the same movement and of Tournons La Page Guinea, were arrested at Sylla's home in Conakry on the night of 9 July 2024 by armed men witnesses identified as members of the defence and security forces, on the eve of protests Sylla had called against media repression and the cost of living. A third activist arrested with them, Mohamed Cissé, was released the next day and testified they had been taken to the Loos archipelago and tortured — testimony recorded here as his account, attributed. The attorney-general asserted they were held in no official detention centre; the authorities have since neither confirmed detention nor produced the men. Two years on, their fate and whereabouts remain unknown, no investigation has produced a public finding, and their families mark anniversaries instead of receiving answers (OHCHR statement, 19 July 2024; Amnesty International, CODDH and WAHRDN joint statement, July 2026; Front Line Defenders joint statement, July 2026). They are not alone on the file: the UN Human Rights Office recorded at least 10 individuals whose whereabouts remained unknown after arrest by security forces as of September 2025 — including former mines-ministry secretary general Saadou Nimaga and journalist Habib Marouane Camara, abducted in December 2024 — and Tournons La Page's Guinea branch has documented at least 35 disappearance cases since September 2021; the counts differ by source and method and are printed as their sources give them (OHCHR, September 2025; TLP figure via Al Jazeera, September 2026). In the election season itself, four relatives of the artist and opposition figure Elie Kamano disappeared on 16 November 2025 and the photographer Sanassy Keita was abducted by armed, hooded men on 27 November 2025; both files were unresolved when the UN raised them on 26 December 2025.
  • The opposition's voters were counted out of the count. The parties dissolved in March 2026 (Test 2) were the vehicles of the largest blocs of Guinean voters; their exclusion from the May 2026 legislative elections means the National Assembly installed in July 2026 does not contain the representatives of a very large share of the electorate — a fact the AU's own briefing records as a standing concern about a de facto one-party state (Amani Africa, June 2026).
  • One contested file from inside his own camp: former army chief of staff Sadiba Koulibaly, demoted and arrested in June 2024, died in custody on 22 June 2024; the military prosecutor announced the death days later, the authorities attributed it to cardiac causes, and his family and supporters disputed the account. The dispute is recorded; no independent finding appears on this record (military prosecutor's announcement and reporting, June 2024).

Why the grade lands here: The for-shelf holds a real counting — of the 2009 dead, by a state that had refused them for thirteen years — and a national project whose stated design includes everyone. But the test asks who is in the accounting now, and the present tense of this tenure is a set of empty chairs: protesters in morgues counted by three different tallies the state has never answered, two named activists whose whereabouts the state will not state two years after its forces took them, six unidentified bodies in a prefectural mortuary, and an Assembly missing the country's largest voting blocs. A ruler counts everyone or he counts the convenient; the disappeared are the clearest counted-out in this entire series, because the state will not even say their names back.

Helped: The families of the 2009 victims, counted at last with verdicts and a reparations commitment; the mining regions and the national treasury, as Simandou's revenues begin; commune voters, whose local tier was restored. Hurt: The families of Foniké Menguè, Billo Bah and the other disappeared, who are owed a fate and have received silence; the families of the protesters killed since 2021 — at least 47 on Amnesty's count, up to 59 on the FNDC's — and of the Nzérékoré dead, 56 on the state's count, 135 on the local collective's; the voters of the dissolved parties; the families of the six men of Forécariah, who cannot yet even be told whether the dead are theirs.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Charter pledges, roadmap commitments and public statements vs. the record since 5 September 2021, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Justice for 28 September 2009 — promised, and delivered as a trial. The junta pledged to finally try the massacre; the trial opened on the massacre's thirteenth anniversary, 28 September 2022, and ran to verdicts on 31 July 2024, with the ICC Prosecutor's office calling it a landmark (see Tests 1 and 4). The delivery is recorded here; its partial undoing is recorded below, where it belongs.
  • Simandou — promised for decades by every Guinean government; delivered under his. He made the integrated mine-rail-port project a signature commitment, held the partners to co-development terms including the state's 15% infrastructure stake and the multi-use railway, and presided over the start of operations on 11 November 2025 and the first exports that December (project partners' statements, November 2025). Whatever else is graded in this dossier, this word was kept in steel and tonnage.
  • The revised completion date — promised late, kept exactly. Having missed the original deadline (below), the government committed to completing the transition by the end of 2025, and did: referendum 21 September 2025, election 28 December 2025, inauguration 17 January 2026, sanctions lifted by ECOWAS and the AU in January 2026 on the stated ground that the commitment had been fulfilled (ECOWAS communiqué, 28 January 2026).
  • The anti-corruption drive — announced, and visibly executed. The prosecutions and convictions listed under Test 1 happened at the promised scale against the promised targets — the previous administration's senior figures (CRIEF verdicts, December 2024 – February 2025, appeals through July 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or reversed):

  • The founding word — broken. The Charter of the Transition, adopted under his authority in September 2021, barred him and every member of his junta and transitional government from standing in the elections ending the transition, and he pledged publicly in 2021 that none of them would be candidates. On 3 November 2025 he filed his candidacy at the Supreme Court, under a constitution his transition had written removing the bar (candidacy reporting, November 2025; BBC and Crisis Group analyses, 2025). This is the single clearest word-keeping fact in the file: the promise was written, public, and his; the candidacy is a fact; the bridge between them is a rule change he controlled.
  • The ECOWAS date — missed by a year. Under the October 2022 dynamic compromise, the transition was to end within 24 months — elections and a return to civilian rule by 31 December 2024. That date passed under military rule, with the referendum not yet held; Human Rights Watch's December 2024 assessment is titled by the fact: the authorities "failed to keep their promises to restore civilian rule by December 2024." The eventual completion on the revised date is on the for-shelf; it does not erase the first date.
  • The 2009 word — kept in court, reversed by decree. The pardon of Moussa Dadis Camara on 28 March 2025, eight months into a 20-year sentence for crimes against humanity, reversed in practice the justice commitment the trial had fulfilled; the victims' reparations were assumed by the state two days earlier, which compensated the families while freeing the convicted man — both halves are recorded, and the second half is the one Test 5 grades (decree reporting, March 2025).
  • The media word — reversed within two months. In March 2024 the new prime minister met media representatives and the information minister condemned the jamming of stations and promised to resolve the crisis by dialogue; on 21 May 2024 the same ministry revoked six outlets' licences (RSF reporting, May 2024).
  • The investigation words — unkept by silence. Investigations were announced into the killings of protesters at Kindia and elsewhere and into the July 2024 arrests of Sylla and Bah; as of this grading, no findings have been published in either file, and the two activists' whereabouts remain unstated after more than two years (Amnesty International and partner statements, July 2026). A promise of investigation that produces neither a finding nor a fate is recorded as a word not kept, not as a word pending.
  • His predecessor's word, as context for the seat: the coup that seated Doumbouya was justified by Condé's broken word — a constitution rewritten in 2020 so the incumbent could run again past his limit. The justification was widely accepted at the time, including in the street. The record above shows the same seat, four years later, held by the same method: a constitution rewritten so the incumbent could run. The context is stated because it is the standard the seat's holder himself set in 2021.

Why the grade lands here: His kept words are substantial — a massacre tried, a megaproject delivered, a revised date met to the day — and none of them is the word the tenure was founded on. The charter pledge was not a campaign line; it was the constitutional instrument of the regime and the answer he gave, in advance, to the question every coup is asked. He broke it by the most documented route available: not by denying it, but by replacing the document. Test 5 grades the trajectory of the word over time, and the trajectory runs from "none of us will stand" to a seven-year term, renewable once.

Helped: The partners and beneficiaries of Simandou's delivery — the state treasury, the mining regions, the project's workforce; the 2009 victims' families, whose trial happened as promised (before the pardon cut across it); ECOWAS and Guinea's neighbours, whose revised timetable was honoured. Hurt: Every Guinean who accepted the transition on the charter's terms — the parties that organised under its rules, the voters who waited out the 24 months on the roadmap's promise, and the activists who invoked the charter's protections, two of whom have not been seen since July 2024.

The crossings (word × outcome), named as they occur on this record: 1. Kept his word AND it helped people: the 2009 trial, delivered as pledged, which gave victims' families verdicts and reparations after thirteen years; Simandou's delivery, which put a national asset into production on the stated terms. 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt people: the end-2025 completion pledge, honoured on schedule — an election and referendum conducted under a protest ban, party suspensions and unresolved disappearances, so that the keeping of the date is also the record of how the date was kept; the hurt is entered in full in Tests 2–4 and is not laundered by the punctuality. 3. Went against his word AND it helped people: not documented on this record. The government's own framing claims the extra year beyond the ECOWAS date made an orderly sequence possible; the benefits it names (a completed roll, a constitution, Simandou's launch) are recorded in the ledgers above, but no documented instance shows a broken word of his producing a good that the kept word would have prevented, and the dossier does not invent one. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt people: the candidacy against the charter pledge, which hurt the parties and voters who had organised under the charter's rules; the pardon of Dadis Camara, which broke the justice commitment the trial embodied and hurt the 2009 victims a second time; the unkept investigation promises, which hurt the families of the killed and the disappeared by the specific cruelty of an answer announced and never given.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Mamady Doumbouya's tenure divides into a delivery record and a holding record, and the Test grades the holding. The deliveries are genuine and they are on the shelves: a massacre thirteen years unjudged was tried to verdict under his authority; Simandou, promised by governments for a generation, shipped its first ore in December 2025 with the state holding a stake in the railway that carries it; a transition that had slipped its ECOWAS deadline was completed on its revised date, and ECOWAS and the African Union readmitted Guinea in January 2026 on that basis. One law: FAILS — because the eligibility rule was rewritten at the source for the ruler's own candidacy, the protest ban bound only his opponents, the pardon freed a fellow former junta leader eight months after a crimes-against-humanity sentence, and the accountability he built has never once reached his own camp; helped: the 2009 victims (trial and reparations) and the public purse (the Condé-era convictions); hurt: opposition voters, and the same 2009 victims at the pardon. Without gripping: FAILS — because the electoral administration, the party registrar, the media regulator's powers and, for two years, every town hall in the country sat inside his executive, and the first legislative elections of his republic were held after the three largest parties had been dissolved; helped: his GMD coalition; hurt: the dissolved parties' voters, the closed outlets' journalists and audiences. The wobble: FAILS — because although he negotiated with ECOWAS, restated his deadline openly and met it, every pushback that touched his tenure itself was answered by rule-rewriting, bans or silence: the charter, the street, the UN's two written calls, and a television channel's label; helped: Guinea's regional standing; hurt: protesters, the disappeared, and the six unidentified dead of Forécariah, whose investigation has produced nothing public in three months. Counts everyone: FAILS — because the present-tense accounting excludes the people the state will not name back: Foniké Menguè and Billo Bah, unseen since 9 July 2024, among at least 10 unresolved disappearances on the UN's count and at least 35 documented cases on Tournons La Page's; protesters killed in numbers the state has never answered — at least 47 on Amnesty's count to March 2024, up to 59 on the FNDC's — and the Nzérékoré dead, 56 on the government's toll and 135 on the local collective's, the two counts printed apart as they must be. His word: FAILS — because the founding pledge of the regime, written into its own charter, was that none of them would stand, and he stands now in a seven-year term under a constitution that deleted the sentence; the crossings are named in Test 5: he kept his word and it helped (the 2009 trial, Simandou), he kept his word and it hurt (the punctual end-2025 completion, run under bans), no broken-word-and-helped instance is documented, and he broke his word and it hurt (the candidacy, the pardon, the investigations announced and never answered). The open files that could move these grades are named so the record can move in the open: published findings in the Forécariah investigation; the fate of Oumar Sylla and Mamadou Billo Bah, stated by the state that took them; verdicts or releases in the disappearance files; and a Senate election in December 2026 run with the dissolved parties' successors free to organise. Until then, the card stands as graded: the second straight fail in the series, and the first by a ruler who had held an election.
