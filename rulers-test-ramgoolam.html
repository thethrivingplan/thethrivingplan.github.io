The Ruler's Test — Navin Ramgoolam, Prime Minister of Mauritius

Subject: Navinchandra (Navin) Ramgoolam — Prime Minister of Mauritius since 13 November 2024. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Mauritius is a parliamentary republic whose president is a ceremonial head of state, elected by the National Assembly, while executive power — the government, the budget, the senior appointments, and in Ramgoolam's case the defence, home affairs (and with it the police), finance, and Rodrigues portfolios, all of which he holds personally — sits with the prime minister. The President reigns; the Prime Minister rules — so the Test grades where the power sits. The president at his taking office, Prithvirajsing Roopun, was succeeded on 7 December 2024 by Dharam Gokhool; neither is graded. Ramgoolam, born 14 July 1947, is the son of Seewoosagur Ramgoolam, who led Mauritius to independence, and has led the Labour Party since 1991; he was prime minister before, from 1995 to 2000 and from 2005 to 2014, and spent the decade 2014–2024 in opposition, during which provisional charges brought against him in 2015 were struck out. He returned to office at the head of the Alliance du Changement (Labour, the MMM, Nouveaux Démocrates, and Rezistans ek Alternativ), which won the general election of 10 November 2024 with 62.6 per cent of the vote and all 60 elected seats on the island of Mauritius — the remaining two elected seats, for Rodrigues, went to the Organisation du Peuple Rodriguais, and eight further members sit under the Best Loser system — unseating Pravind Jugnauth's Alliance Lepep in a landslide driven by the cost of living and by the wiretapping scandal that broke over the outgoing government in October 2024. He is graded on two years in office. His predecessor Jugnauth (prime minister 2017–2024) appears where the record Ramgoolam inherited — or the treatment Jugnauth's people have received under him — bears directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic printed on the Test card governs the grading: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected — the Alliance du Changement manifesto of November 2024 — is not itself a failure and is never graded as one; the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Marks are lost only for how it is done.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The flagship investigation of his tenure was run, on its face, as process rather than purge. The Financial Crimes Commission's cases against the previous government's principals moved through magistrates' courts with bail granted, charge sheets laid by the Director of Public Prosecutions, and the accused's denials carried in the same coverage as the arrests: when the FCC arrested former prime minister Pravind Jugnauth on 15–16 February 2025, after searches that seized Rs 114 million (about USD 2.5 million) in cash and luxury watches, a magistrate released him on bail the same weekend over the FCC's objection, with conditions against witness interference — the referee ruling against the prosecuting body in the government's highest-profile case (Reuters, 16–17 February 2025; International IDEA democracy tracker, February 2025). Jugnauth is provisionally charged with money laundering; he denies the charge, and it remains an ALLEGATION until a court rules.
  • The same Mauritius Investment Corporation file that consumed the previous government's finance minister and central bank governor also reached into his own government's household. The scandal that forced out Ramgoolam's own appointed Bank of Mauritius governor, Rama Sithanen, in September 2025 arose from the Menlo Park/MIC file his government's investigators had opened — the inquiry was not steered away when its blast radius touched the family of the sitting governor (Wikipedia, 2025 in Mauritius, summarising Mauritian press; Reuters via TradingView, September 2025). The governor resigned; the file continued.
  • The audit machinery was pointed at the record, in the open. Within days of taking office Ramgoolam announced an audit of the public finances and questioned the accuracy of the previous administration's published data (Reuters, November 2024); the National Audit Office's report was tabled and discussed in the National Assembly, a governance step the EY budget analysis records as an improvement in the management of public funds (EY, Budget 2025/26 synopsis).

Evidence against:

  • Every senior figure arrested in two years belongs to one camp. The documented arrest list of the tenure is: the former prime minister (money laundering, provisional charge, February 2025); the former finance minister Renganaden Padayachy (arrest warrant January 2025; arrested 9 April 2025 on an embezzlement charge concerning a Rs 45 million MIC payment; rearrested 16 April 2025 in a second MIC fraud case; both matters ALLEGATIONs, denied by him, no verdict in either as of grading); the former central bank governor Harvesh Seegolam (arrested January 2025, charged with conspiracy to commit fraud; arrested again alongside Padayachy in April 2025 over an alleged Rs 300 million MIC diversion; ALLEGATIONs, denied); two businesspeople and a deputy mayor from the former government's orbit in the Jugnauth case (Reuters, February–April 2025; International IDEA, February 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). No figure of the governing Alliance appears in the documented record as facing the same instruments in the same period. A law that only ever lands on the people who lost the election is not being given by one law, whatever the merits of each file — and each file's merits remain untested: as of November 2026, nearly two years after the first arrests, no trial verdict in any of these cases appears in the record assembled here, so the provisional charge itself is the punishment the record can document — a charge the subject of this dossier knows from the inside, his own 2015 provisional charges having been struck out after years in the same posture.
  • A state institution answered a critic's speech with a police complaint. On 11 May 2026 the former finance minister Padayachy posted a video alleging the Bank of Mauritius had injected Rs 83 billion into the economy in 2025; the Bank rebutted the claim publicly on 12 May — the clean instrument — and then, on 16 May 2026, filed a criminal complaint against him at the Central Criminal Investigation Department; on 26 May 2026 Ramgoolam disclosed the complaint in parliament, called the allegation "false, unfounded and malicious," and counter-alleged that the previous government had printed Rs 181.5 billion through the MIC (The Meridian, May 2026). Both money-printing figures are ALLEGATIONs in a live dispute between the two camps; what is documented without dispute is the choice of instrument — a central bank resolving a factual dispute with a former minister by police process rather than by publishing data, with the prime minister amplifying the complaint from the floor of the Assembly.
  • The one mass exercise of state power against individuals fell on the previous government's hires, at the bottom of the ladder. In May 2025 the Local Government Service Commission — whose new five-member board had been appointed in November 2024, after the election — terminated the employment of the workers recruited shortly before the 2024 election, described by the Minister of Local Government, citing the State Law Office, as an "illegal and abusive" recruitment. The counts are kept separate because the sources give different ones: 1,463 workers, mostly refuse collectors, in the Mauritius Times legal analysis; up to 1,765 dismissal letters in the Japanese foreign-ministry newsletter's summary of Mauritian reporting; about 1,800 in contemporary social-media reporting of the minister's confirmation. The Supreme Court first produced two contradictory rulings — one bench finding procedural unfairness and a violation of natural justice, another holding the contracts terminable without notice by their own terms — and on 23 January 2026 upheld the terminations and discharged the stay that had frozen them (Mauritius Times, 2025–2026; Ministry of Labour public notice, 9 February 2026). No documented sanction fell on any official who designed or approved the original recruitment; the entire cost landed on refuse collectors and their households, who were then formally notified that the Workers' Rights Act transition benefit did not apply to them (Ministry of Labour notice, 9 February 2026).

Why the grade lands here: The process credits are real — independent magistrates bailed his government's biggest target over the prosecuting commission's objection, and the MIC file was allowed to burn his own governor. But Test 1 grades the pattern of the source, and the pattern is one-directional: two years of arrests, warrants, and mass terminations, every one of them landing on the defeated government's people, its hires, or its former officials, and none documented landing anywhere else — capped by a central bank filing a police complaint against a critic. That is the shape of a second law for the other side, administered through courts that still work. The courts working is credited on Test 2; it does not cure the map on Test 1.

Helped: The public record itself — Mauritian taxpayers and depositors, if the MIC disbursements were what investigators allege (ALLEGATION, untested), gained an audit, an investigation, and a tabled audit report. Hurt: Pravind Jugnauth, Renganaden Padayachy, and Harvesh Seegolam (each under unproven charges, each held in provisional-charge posture for periods running from months toward two years); the 1,463–1,765 dismissed local-government workers and their households, who lost livelihoods for the manner of their hiring and were excluded from the transition benefit; and every future critic of the central bank, who now knows a factual claim about it can be answered with a criminal complaint.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The referees have bitten him and his government, in public, and the bites stand. Magistrates granted bail to the former prime minister and the former finance minister against the FCC's position (Reuters, February and April 2025). A Supreme Court bench found procedural unfairness in his own service commission's mass terminations before a later ruling upheld them (Mauritius Times, 2025–2026). The Supreme Court is seized of a constitutional challenge to his signature pension reform — the case is being heard, not obstructed (The Meridian, May 2026). Parliament works him over weekly: at Prime Minister's Question Time on 17 November 2026 he faced 32 questions spanning the riots, drug trafficking, prison capacity, and the cost of living (Mauritius News, November 2026).
  • When his police arrested a journalist, his own government condemned it from the front bench. Narain Jasodanand, a journalist reporting on the Menlo Park/MIC/Bank of Mauritius scandal, was arrested on 15 September 2025 and his phone and computer seized, on a complaint by the central bank governor's son concerning his reporting; Deputy Prime Minister Paul Bérenger and Attorney General Gavin Glover publicly expressed surprise and condemned the arrest, and Bérenger demanded a full report from the Commissioner of Police (Mauritius Times, 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). Under the previous government, comparable arrests drew no such condemnation from the top — the contrast is documented in the same Mauritian commentary.
  • The surveillance referee was handed back. On 4 February 2025 Ramgoolam told parliament he had halted a mass surveillance system which, on the preliminary findings of a police and international-expert investigation, the previous government had operated without judicial oversight, capable of intercepting calls, internet traffic, and social media; he stated surveillance had stopped when he took office and the equipment had been deactivated (International IDEA, February 2025). The SIM-card re-registration requirement inherited from the same period was likewise revoked (prime minister's New Year message, 1 January 2025).
  • The central bank governorship, after its crisis (below), was given to a career technician: Priscilla Muthoora Thakoor, a longstanding IMF economist and the Bank's first woman governor, appointed 25 September 2025 for a three-year term, who has publicly defined her vision as cementing the Bank's reputation as "an independent and credible central bank" (Bank of Mauritius; Reuters via TradingView, September 2025; Ecofin Agency, September 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The seat is concentrated in one pair of hands to a degree the standard counts. Ramgoolam is simultaneously prime minister, minister of finance, minister of defence, minister of home affairs — the police answer to his portfolio — and minister for Rodrigues and Outer Islands (Prime Minister's Office, November 2026). His deputy resigned over exactly this concentration (Test 3), and two years in, the finance portfolio is still his.
  • The central bank's leadership turned over twice in a year under political pressure, and the pressure came from inside the governor's own household. Ramgoolam appointed Rama Sithanen governor on 16 November 2024. Second Deputy Governor Gérard Sanspeur resigned on 29 August 2025, accusing the governor's son of meddling in the running of the Bank — an ALLEGATION by the resigning deputy, denied in the governor's camp, recorded here as his stated reason. In September 2025 Ramgoolam publicly declared Sithanen's position "untenable" and asked him to step down; Sithanen resigned effective 28–30 September 2025 (Reuters via TradingView, September 2025; Africa Risk Consulting, September 2025; Bank of Mauritius Annual Report 2024-25). A governor removable at the prime minister's public word, after a year in which his deputy's resignation letter alleged family interference in the Bank, is not a referee holding its autonomy — whatever the governor's own failings, and they were substantial and documented in the same record.
  • The Bank then acted as a political combatant. Its criminal complaint of 16 May 2026 against the former finance minister over a critical video (Test 1) was filed under Governor Thakoor, the technician appointed to restore its independence — the institution answering speech with police process while its new governor preached credibility (The Meridian, May 2026). The complaint's existence was disclosed to the public not by the Bank but by the prime minister, in parliament, as a debating point.
  • The journalist's arrest happened at all. The September 2025 arrest of Jasodanand — devices seized, an ICT Act file, a complainant who was the sitting governor's son, and a subject-matter that was the governor's own scandal — is the kind of event Test 2 exists to record; that the deputy prime minister condemned it afterwards mitigates the government's posture, not the police's act, and the ICT Act under which such arrests are made remains in force, unrepealed, two years into a mandate won partly on ending repression (Mauritius Times, 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2025).

Why the grade lands here: Mauritius's referees demonstrably still rule against the government — bail against the FCC, a split Supreme Court, a live constitutional challenge to his own reform, a front bench that condemned its own police on the record. That is why this is not FAILS. But the grip evidence is structural, not episodic: five portfolios in one man, a central bank governor installed and then removed at his word within a year amid an alleged family-meddling scandal, and that same bank filing criminal complaints against critics. The referees survive; the seat's hand rests on two of them — finance and the Bank — heavily enough that their autonomy is a favour renewed daily rather than a fact. MIXED, on the failing edge.

Helped: Readers and viewers of an uncowed press — L'Express, Défi Média, Mauritius Times, and The Meridian publish investigations and open criticism of the prime minister, including of the Bank complaint, without documented retaliation against the outlets themselves; litigants who found Supreme Court benches willing to rule against the government's commission. Hurt: The Bank of Mauritius as an institution — two leadership crises and a police complaint against a critic in fourteen months; Narain Jasodanand, arrested with devices seized for reporting on the governor's scandal; Gérard Sanspeur, out of the deputy governorship after alleging interference; and the principle that a Mauritian central bank governor serves a term rather than a prime minister's patience.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The pension wobble was answered with machinery, not denial — twice. The June 2025 budget's raising of the Basic Retirement Pension eligibility age from 60 to 65 was announced without prior consultation and met, on 21 June 2025, by large peaceful union demonstrations in Rose Hill and Port Louis. The government responded by establishing a Technical Committee of Experts with a broad consultation mandate; when the June 2026 budget then added means-testing to the reform and the backlash renewed, the government froze the means-testing component after the committee's report — a documented retreat from its own measure, in the open (Mauritius Times, "The Pension Reform Paradox," 2026; Japanese foreign-ministry newsletter, June 2025). The age rise itself stands, phased from September 2026 over ten years under the Finance Act 2025; the adjustment was to the reform's design and pace, made through a committee whose existence concedes the original announcement was mishandled.
  • The Chagos wobble — the largest external pushback of the tenure — was answered openly and without rupture. The treaty he signed on 22 May 2025 required UK ratification; the implementing bill passed the Commons on 20 October 2025, was amended in the Lords in January 2026, and then died: on 13 April 2026 the UK government announced it would not proceed, because Washington would not clear the updated Exchange of Notes after President Trump publicly reversed his support in February 2026; the bill fell at prorogation on 29 April 2026 (UK Parliament record via GIS Mauritius and Diplomacy and Law review, 2026). Ramgoolam's answer was escalation in the open, in sequence: a phone call with the British prime minister on 21 September 2026, a meeting with the UK National Security Adviser in London on 2 October 2026, a formal statement to the National Assembly on 17 November 2026 demanding a clear ratification roadmap by December 2026 and warning of legal action, and a stated preference, on the record, for "ratification and entry into force of the Agreement through dialogue and diplomacy" (GIS Mauritius, 18 November 2026; prime minister's Assembly statement, 17 November 2026). He likewise answered the Maldives' February 2026 challenge to Mauritian sovereignty over Chagos in the open, objecting at the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting to the Maldives hosting CHOGM 2028 (Cabinet highlights, 20 November 2026). If another option became available — litigation alongside diplomacy — he has said publicly that he is considering it, which is what the Test asks.
  • The wobble inside his own alliance was absorbed without a purge. When Deputy Prime Minister Paul Bérenger resigned on 20 March 2026 — citing the unappointed finance ministry, unmet campaign pledges, downgrade and grey-listing risks, and differences over electoral reform — Ramgoolam let him go; sixteen of the MMM's eighteen elected members pledged continued loyalty to the government and stayed, Bérenger's own party moved beyond him (he resigned from the MMM on 13 April 2026 and founded a new party), and Arianne Navarre-Marie became deputy prime minister (Business Day, 20 March 2026; Mauritius Times, March–April 2026; Wikipedia, Paul Bérenger). No retaliation against Bérenger or his loyalists appears in the documented record.
  • The October 2026 riots were answered first with the referee, then with the curfew. Protests over water shortages and the cost of living began on 7 October 2026; on 9 October a police sergeant shot and gravely wounded a 27-year-old supermarket cashier at a roadblock in Camp Levieux as she travelled home from work — she survived, after seven hours of surgery, and the prime minister later reported her condition stable. The sergeant was arrested within days and provisionally charged, three other officers were suspended, and the Independent Police Complaints Commission opened its inquiry, issuing a public witness appeal on 24 October (The Field, October 2026; Associated Press, October 2026). The charges against the sergeant are ALLEGATIONs until a court rules. Only after a police station at Rose-Hill was burned on 12 October did Ramgoolam impose the overnight curfew — announced 13 October, extended in shortened hours, and lifted at 4:30 a.m. on 19 October (Al Jazeera via The Field, October 2026; Associated Press, October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The riot response also carried the doubling-down strain. In his televised address Ramgoolam warned those involved in violence of "very heavy consequences," and officials were reported considering prosecutions under the Prevention of Terrorism Act, restrictions on petrol sales, and lowering the age of detention — measures aimed at a crowd whose documented grievances were water and prices, and among whose arrested, on the Cabinet's own count of 94 arrests through 14 October (including 16 minors and one police officer), were children (Associated Press, October 2026; Cabinet reporting via AP; Particle News, October 2026). Arrest counts are kept separate by source and date: 94 through 14 October on the Cabinet/police count; at least 188 in total on Al Jazeera's count, which the Police Force confirmed in its reply to The Field. The accountability instrument for the shooting itself — the IPCC's public witness appeal — came two weeks after the event, a lag The Field's reporting names as the inquiry's weak first step.
  • The grievances under the riots were answered after the fire, not before. Reservoirs stood at 57.1 per cent of capacity on 8 October 2026, down from 87.4 per cent a year earlier, and September inflation had risen to 4.1 per cent (Statistics Mauritius via The Field, October 2026); the emergency water programme — Rs 920 million of urgent Central Water Authority projects and a €120 million AFD/EU water package — appears in the Cabinet record of 20 November 2026, six weeks after the riots began (Cabinet highlights, 20 November 2026).
  • Two of his deputy's stated wobbles were never answered at all. Bérenger's core complaints — that Ramgoolam would not appoint a stand-alone finance minister and that electoral reform had stalled — were met with silence rather than adjustment: at November 2026 the prime minister still holds the finance portfolio himself (Prime Minister's Office, November 2026), and no electoral reform bill appears in the documented record. Where the pushback touched the seat's own concentration, the answer was to continue exactly as before.
  • The pension reform's first move was the refusal to look: a change to the retirement age of every Mauritian, announced in a budget speech with no prior consultation, in a country where the pension at 60 is a load-bearing part of household planning. The consultation machinery came only after the street moved (Mauritius Times, 2026). Considering another option became available only once not considering it had become untenable — the sequence is credited as adjustment on the FOR shelf and recorded here in its order.

Why the grade lands here: Ramgoolam's instinct under pressure is to answer in the open — committees, statements to the Assembly, deadlines to London, a charged sergeant, a lifted curfew — and the record shows real adjustments: a frozen means-test, a diplomatic track maintained under American sabotage of his treaty, an alliance rupture absorbed without revenge. Against that stand the refusals where the wobble touched his own grip (the finance ministry, electoral reform), the riot week's reach for terrorism law and child detention, and a pattern of answering grievances after they ignite rather than before. He adjusts; he adjusts late, and not on the files that are about himself.

Helped: Pensioners and near-pensioners, who kept the universal (non-means-tested) pension when the government froze that component; the Mauritian negotiating position on Chagos, which kept its legal and diplomatic options open in sequence; MMM parliamentarians, who were allowed to choose the government over their own historic leader without punishment. Hurt: The 94–188 arrested in the riot week (counts kept separate above), among them 16 minors on the Cabinet's count, facing a process their prime minister publicly framed with terrorism law; households in the water-short districts, whose emergency programme arrived after the riots; and Paul Bérenger, whose stated reasons for resigning were never engaged on their merits.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Rodrigues and the outer islands, the communities the Best Loser system exists for, the Chagossians, the dismissed, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The outer islands were counted in the first month. When Cyclone Chido passed over Agalega in December 2024, Cabinet recorded immediate assistance — a Dornier flight with food and medicine the same morning — to one of the republic's smallest and most remote populations (Cabinet highlights, 13 December 2024). Ramgoolam holds the Rodrigues and Outer Islands portfolio himself, and Rodrigues' two seats sit with the OPR inside the governing arrangement; the pension payment calendar was adjusted so January 2027 pensions are paid early, on 31 December 2026 (Cabinet highlights, 20 November 2026).
  • The December 2024 relief package was aimed at the bottom of the count. The 14th-month bonus was paid to employees earning up to Rs 50,000 a month and to pensioners in December 2024–January 2025; a salary compensation of Rs 610 a month went to the same band from 1 January 2025; petrol and diesel fell Rs 5 a litre from 14 December 2024 (Cabinet highlights, 13 December 2024; Special Allowance Act 2024). On the government's own account, some 90 per cent of the workforce received the bonus (prime minister's New Year message, 1 January 2025).
  • The Chagos claim is made in everyone's name, including the displaced. The treaty of 22 May 2025 restores Mauritian sovereignty over the whole archipelago and expressly provides for a Mauritian resettlement programme on the islands other than Diego Garcia (GIS Mauritius, November 2026) — the Chagossians are counted in the claim as Mauritians with a right of return, after half a century in which no Mauritian government could deliver even the sovereignty.
  • The universal pension survived his own reform. The means-testing component that would have sorted pensioners into the counted and the discounted was frozen after the expert committee reported (Test 3); the Basic Retirement Pension remains universal, and its eligibility-age rise phases over ten years from September 2026 rather than landing at once (Finance Act 2025; Mauritius Times, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Chagossians were counted in the claim and discounted in the making of it. The deal was negotiated government-to-government; Chagossian groups who objected that they had not been consulted — and who oppose the 99-year Diego Garcia lease that excludes their return to the largest island — carried their challenge to the UK High Court, which rejected the claims as unarguable (CLAT Gurukul treaty explainer, November 2026, summarising the UK proceedings). Chagossian opinion is divided — other Chagossian voices support the sovereignty transfer — and the division is recorded here as contested; what is not contested is that the community the claim is made for had no seat at the table where its terms were set.
  • The dismissed were counted out entirely. The 1,463–1,765 local-government workers terminated in May 2025 (counts kept separate, Test 1) lost their jobs for the manner of their hiring — a sin of the recruiters, not the recruited — received no sanction-supported redress when the Supreme Court upheld the terminations in January 2026, and were formally told the transition unemployment benefit did not extend to them (Ministry of Labour notice, 9 February 2026). They are, in the main, refuse collectors: the poorest workers in the story, bearing the whole cost of a patronage transaction conducted over their heads.
  • The pension age rise is an intergenerational counting choice. The Basic Retirement Pension cost over Rs 55 billion in 2024-25, more than doubled since 2019, inside a public debt of Rs 642 billion, about 90 per cent of GDP (PwC budget analysis; IMF Article IV, July 2025). The correction chosen — work to 65, phased over a decade — places the adjustment on those now approaching 60 and on the young, who will draw at 65 what their parents drew at 60 while servicing the debt incurred paying it; a constitutional challenge argues the point in court (The Meridian, May 2026). The arithmetic that forced a choice is real (see the no-good-choice finding under Test 5); the choice of whose years would pay for it was still a choice.
  • The riot week's count sorted by district and by age. The unrest began in working-class districts — La Cavern, Camp Levieux, Rose-Hill — over water and prices; the state's answer counted 16 minors among its first 94 arrests and contemplated lowering the detention age (Cabinet reporting via Associated Press, October 2026). The woman at the centre of it, a part-time supermarket cashier travelling home from a late shift, was counted first as an obstacle at a roadblock; the state corrected that count within days by charging its own sergeant (Test 3), but the order of events is part of the record.

Why the grade lands here: Ramgoolam counts the whole map when he is distributing — Agalega in a cyclone, pensioners in December, Rodrigues in the arrangement, the Chagossians in the sovereignty claim. He counts selectively when he is correcting: the dismissed workers carried a patronage debt they did not incur, the Chagossians were absent from their own negotiation, and the pension correction was loaded onto the years of the young and the nearly-old. A ruler who counts everyone in the giving and some people out in the taking lands MIXED.

Helped: Pensioners (the December 2024 bonus, the early January 2027 payment, universality preserved); employees earning under Rs 50,000 (the bonus and compensation); the inhabitants of Agalega (same-day cyclone relief); Rodriguans, inside the governing arrangement. Hurt: The dismissed local-government workers and their households (livelihoods lost, benefit excluded); Chagossians seeking return to Diego Garcia itself, whose exclusion the treaty he signed makes permanent for at least 99 years; Mauritians now in their fifties, whose pension age moved after their planning was set; and the arrested minors of October 2026.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Manifesto pledges and public statements vs. the record since 13 November 2024, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Chagos — the word was given and the signature delivered. The Alliance's programme made completing decolonisation a headline commitment; on 22 May 2025 Ramgoolam signed the treaty by which the United Kingdom recognises Mauritian sovereignty over the entire Chagos Archipelago, with the Diego Garcia base secured under Mauritian sovereignty for 99 years and a resettlement programme provided for (GIS Mauritius, November 2026). That the treaty is not in force is London's and Washington's doing — the UK bill died in April 2026 for want of an American clearance (Test 3) — and his pursuit of ratification since, in the open and with a deadline, is the keeping continuing. The Future Fund announced in the 2025-26 budget, to hold Chagos receipts for future generations, was likewise established as promised (EY Budget 2025/26 synopsis).
  • The surveillance state — dismantled as pledged. The manifesto promised to dismantle the previous government's "spying system" and end repression. The mass surveillance system was halted and its existence disclosed to parliament on 4 February 2025; SIM re-registration was revoked; the halt was stated as effective from his taking office (International IDEA, February 2025; prime minister's New Year message, 1 January 2025).
  • The first hundred days' relief — paid. The 14th-month bonus (a promise he adopted from his rival's platform and gave his word to honour) was legislated in the Special Allowance Act 2024 and paid in December 2024 and January 2025, with pensioners included; fuel prices were cut from 14 December 2024; the Rs 10 billion price-stabilisation fund for essential goods was a manifesto measure carried into the government's programme (Cabinet highlights, 13 December 2024; EY tax alert, December 2024; Alliance du Changement manifesto, November 2024). The scaling of these payments is recorded on the against shelf; that they were paid, on time, is recorded here.
  • The fiscal word — kept at cost to himself. From his first month Ramgoolam stated the policy plainly: the public finances were in an abyss (his budget theme was "From Abyss to Prosperity"), and consolidation would precede generosity. The 2025-26 budget raised revenue by over 2 per cent of GDP and cut spending by over 1 per cent, on a path to reduce debt from 87 per cent of GDP in 2024 toward 75 per cent by 2030 — a programme the IMF's July 2025 Article IV describes as aligned with its own recommendations (IMF, July 2025). The figures he inherited are contested by the former government, which disputes the falsification charge laid against its data (Test 1); the IMF's independent arithmetic broadly corroborates the debt level he cited.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The pension promise ran one way; the pension reform ran the other. The manifesto's third headline measure guaranteed that old-age and other social pensions "will be gradually increased to a minimum of Rs 21,500 during our mandate" (Alliance du Changement manifesto, November 2024). Nothing in the manifesto announced raising the age at which the pension begins. In June 2025 his budget raised the eligibility age from 60 to 65 (Finance Act 2025), and the amount promise remains, two years in, a mandate-length undertaking whose end is not yet in sight. The unions' charge — that the age rise breaks the pension word — is contested by the government on fiscal-necessity grounds; the no-good-choice finding below records the constraint in full. But the constraint does not change what the manifesto said, and the manifesto is the word being graded.
  • The relief promises that have not materialised. Free public transport for all and free internet for every family were headline manifesto measures (manifesto measures 4 and the 25 key measures, November 2024); two years in, neither appears in the documented record as delivered, and in 2025 three citizens served a formal legal notice — a mise en demeure — on Ramgoolam and the Alliance leaders accusing them of "democracy fraud" and demanding the pledges be honoured, listing the fuel cut (Rs 20 a litre as the notice states the promise; the cut delivered was Rs 5), electricity tariffs, food prices, the pension at Rs 21,000, free transport, and free internet (The State of the Mind, 2025, reproducing the notice). The notice is an advocacy document by critics, and the government answers that delivery is phased across the mandate; the shelf records both, and the two years elapsed.
  • The finance minister who never arrived. Bérenger's resignation statement of 20 March 2026 records, from inside the government, that Ramgoolam had failed to appoint a stand-alone finance minister as undertaken, and that campaign pledges remained unimplemented (Business Day, 20 March 2026). Bérenger is an interested party; but the fact he cited is independently documented: the prime minister is still his own finance minister in November 2026 (Prime Minister's Office, November 2026). Electoral reform — promised in the manifesto (MEWC election summary, 2024) and Bérenger's first stated reason for leaving his own party — has likewise produced no bill in the documented record.
  • The budget booked the treaty's money before the treaty existed in law. The FY2025/26 deficit path was helped by booking Chagos receipts as income across 2025/26–2027/28 (EY Budget 2025/26 analysis, deficit bridge) — revenue from a treaty that required another parliament's ratification and has not entered into force. Planning on a signature is optimism; budgeting on it is a word given to the Assembly that events in London have not honoured.

The no-good-choice finding (the fiscal trap, graded under the clause): In December 2024 the options actually open were these. (a) Deliver the manifesto's relief package at full scale and speed — the Rs 10 billion stabilisation fund, free transport costed by CARE Ratings at some Rs 18.8 billion a year, free internet at some Rs 5.3 billion, pension rises — on a treasury whose 2024-25 deficit had come in at 9.8 per cent of GDP against 3.4 per cent budgeted, with debt at roughly 90 per cent of GDP: the foreseeable price was a ratings descent (the downgrade risk his own deputy later cited publicly), a weaker rupee, and inflation that would have taxed the poor first and the pensioners hardest. (b) Consolidate first — raise revenue, cut spending, reform the pension age — and phase or break the relief promises: the foreseeable price was the cost-of-living pain, the union revolt, and the "democracy fraud" charge now on the record. There was no third door that kept both the promises and the currency. He chose (b), with partial early relief (the December 2024 package) as the bridge. Under the clause, the choice itself is recorded as the best possible available in the moment and is not held against him. Two things remain gradable, and are graded above: the trap was inherited, but the promises were volunteered — the manifesto was written in opposition, when published debt figures already stood in the mid-to-high eighties per cent of GDP, so the scale of the giving promised was chosen with the broad shape of the trap visible; and inside the trap, the least-harm distribution of the correction (Test 4) was his to choose. The Chagos file carries a smaller trap of the same shape: a treaty leasing Diego Garcia for 99 years, or no treaty and the indefinite continuation of the British Indian Ocean Territory — he took the door that existed, and the lease's cost to the Chagossians is recorded on Test 4, not held against the choice itself.

The four crossings, named: (1) Kept his word and it helped — the surveillance dismantling and the December 2024 bonus: words given in opposition, kept in office, and the keeping put money and privacy in ordinary hands. (2) Kept his word and it still hurt — the Chagos treaty: he promised sovereignty by agreement and delivered the agreement; the agreement's 99-year lease of Diego Garcia, excluding Chagossian return to that island, hurts the very community the sovereignty was claimed for, and the keeping earns its credit here while the hurt stands in full on Test 4. (3) Went against his word and it helped — the 14th-month bonus cap: the promise he adopted was for payment across the board; he paid it only up to Rs 50,000 a month, breaking the promise's letter — and the break concentrated the entire benefit on the lower-paid half of the workforce rather than spreading it to those who needed it least. (4) Broke his word and it hurt — the pension age: the manifesto promised Mauritians a rising pension and said nothing of a later one; the age rise to 65 took five pension years from workers who had planned their lives on 60, and the hurt is recorded in full on Tests 3 and 4.

Why the grade lands here: Two years is long enough to see the shape. His largest word — Chagos — was kept in the only part that was his to keep, and is pursued still. His earliest words — the bonus, the fuel cut, the end of the spying system — were paid within weeks. Against them stand the mandate's centre of gravity: a pension promise whose direction his own budget reversed, free transport and internet undelivered, a finance ministry never surrendered, electoral reform unbegun, and a budget that spent a treaty's income before the treaty was law. The fiscal trap excuses the consolidation; it does not rewrite the manifesto. MIXED — a word kept where it was cheap or glorious, and deferred where it was structural.

Helped: Chagos diplomacy's beneficiaries — the Mauritian state's sovereignty claim, advanced further in two years than in the previous fifty; lower-paid workers and pensioners (December 2024); every Mauritian who was being listened to under the old system and is not, on the documented record, listened to now. Hurt: Workers approaching 60 (five pension years); commuters and households still waiting on free transport and internet; the MMM's voters, whose party's seat at the top table — and its electoral-reform condition — left government with Bérenger; and the Assembly's own budget arithmetic, which carries receipts from an unratified treaty.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Navin Ramgoolam returned to power at 77 with the largest mandate in Mauritian history — every seat on the main island — on a record that gave him every instrument and no excuses. Two years on, the dossier shows a ruler whose failures and virtues run through the same channel: concentration. On Test 1, the law he gives has landed, in every documented senior case, on the people who lost to him — a former prime minister, finance minister, and central bank governor all in provisional-charge posture with no verdicts, a central bank that answered a critic's video with a police complaint, and some fifteen hundred of the old government's hires dismissed at the bottom of the ladder while no designer of their recruitment was sanctioned; the magistrates who bailed his targets over his commission's objection are the reason the failure is of pattern rather than of total system. On Test 2, the referees still bite — courts split on his commission's dismissals, the constitutionality of his pension reform is before the Supreme Court, his deputy and attorney general condemned his own police for arresting a journalist — but the seat holds five portfolios, and the central bank governorship changed hands at his public word within a year of his appointing it, amid allegations of family meddling that cost a deputy governor his post. On Test 3, he answers wobbles openly and sometimes well — a pension means-test frozen after consultation, a Chagos diplomacy that escalated in sequence rather than rupturing when Washington killed his treaty's bill, an alliance rupture absorbed without a purge, a police sergeant charged within days of the shooting that ignited the worst riots since 1999 — and he answers late on the grievances (water at 57 per cent reservoir capacity, prices) that ignited them, reached for terrorism law in the riot week, and has never answered the wobble about his own concentration: the finance ministry is still his, and electoral reform is still unbegun. On Test 4, he counts everyone when distributing — Agalega relieved the morning of the cyclone, pensioners bonused in his first December — and counts selectively when correcting: the dismissed carried a debt they did not incur, the Chagossians were absent from their own negotiation, and the pension correction was loaded onto the young and the nearly-old. On Test 5, the word is genuinely two-sided: Chagos signed and still pursued, the surveillance state switched off, the first relief paid in weeks — against a pension manifesto his budget reversed in direction, free transport and internet undelivered into a "democracy fraud" notice, and a budget that booked an unratified treaty's income. The fiscal trap he inherited was real, and under the no-good-choice clause the consolidation itself is not held against him; what is graded is that the promises were volunteered with the trap in view, and that the correction's weight was placed where he chose to place it. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the arrested predecessors ever reach verdicts; whether the Chagos roadmap he demanded by December 2026 arrives, and what he does if it does not; whether the pension amount promise is honoured before the mandate ends; and whether the IPCC inquiry into the Camp Levieux shooting reports in the open.
