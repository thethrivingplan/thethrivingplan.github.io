The Ruler's Test — Assimi Goïta, President of the Transition of Mali

Subject: General Assimi Goïta — President of the Transition of Mali, and since May 2026 also Minister of Defence. The seat graded is the presidency, because in Mali the executive seat is the presidency and there is no other candidate: Goïta appoints the prime minister and the government, appoints the members of the transitional legislature (the National Transition Council, CNT) by decree, and commands the armed forces. He led the coup of 18 August 2020 that removed elected president Ibrahim Boubacar Keïta, served as vice-president of the first transition, then led the second coup of 24 May 2021 that removed the civilian transitional president Bah Ndaw and prime minister Moctar Ouane; the Constitutional Court named him transitional president and he was sworn in on 7 June 2021. He was promoted from colonel to general in October 2024. The premise in the original brief is verified, with one correction of label: since July 2025 the word "transitional" describes a title, not a timetable. On 3 July 2025 the CNT — a body whose members Goïta himself appointed — adopted a revision of the Transition Charter granting him a five-year presidential term renewable "as many times as necessary," without an election, until the country is "pacified"; he promulgated it the same month (Al Jazeera, 4 July 2025; electos.net country file, updated 16 November 2026). Two months earlier, on 13 May 2025, his decree had dissolved all 297 political parties. No national election has been held in Mali since before the 2020 coup, and none is scheduled. He is graded on the whole tenure, August 2020 – November 2026, with the weight on his presidency from June 2021. The prime minister since 21 November 2024 is General Abdoulaye Maïga, a soldier and former government spokesman, who replaced the civilian Choguel Kokalla Maïga after the latter publicly criticised the absence of a handover timetable. Defence Minister Sadio Camara, the junta's other senior figure, was killed in the coordinated jihadist–separatist offensive of 25–26 April 2026; Goïta took the defence portfolio himself in May 2026 (conflict-tracker.com country file, November 2026; African Arguments, 21 October 2026). The 2009/2012 background — the 2012 coup, the loss of the north, the French intervention of 2013, the 2015 Algiers Accord — enters only where Goïta's own record runs through it. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic (printed on the Test card, above the five tests): "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule: governing on the platform the voters elected is NOT inherently a failure and is never graded as one; the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Goïta was never elected and ran on no platform; the closest thing to a mandate this seat ever carried is the transition's own founding promise — a short, dated road back to elected civilian rule, made by the junta to Malians and to ECOWAS in 2020 and renewed at his swearing-in. That promise is therefore the word Test 5 grades, and marks are lost elsewhere only for HOW: a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not choose him (Test 4).

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The 2023 Mining Code (Law No. 2023-040, in force August 2023), which raised the state's stake and tightened fiscal terms across the gold sector, was applied to the whole sector and not only to the government's enemies: it was enforced against Barrick, the largest operator, and against Australia's Resolute Mining, whose executives were detained in November 2024 and released after the company agreed to pay some USD 160 million; the code's terms were the stated basis in both cases, and the November 2025 Barrick settlement confirms the code itself — not expulsion — was the government's objective, Barrick accepting the 2023 code's application and paying approximately USD 430 million (IISD Investment Treaty News, September 2026; Reuters via Africa Briefing, November–December 2025).
  • The 13 May 2025 dissolution decree was formally universal: it dissolved all 297 political parties, including parties that supported the junta, and government employees who belonged to parties kept their posts, barred only from acting for a party (Wikipedia, List of political parties in Mali, citing the decree; IDEA Global State of Democracy, May 2025). As a text, it did not exempt his own side.
  • The state's audit machinery has reached a man who was, until November 2024, the junta's own civilian face: former prime minister Choguel Kokalla Maïga was charged in August 2025, on an auditor-general's report, with embezzlement, forgery, and money laundering involving billions of CFA francs (AFP via multiple outlets, 19–20 August 2025). The charges are an ALLEGATION — Maïga denies them and no trial date has been set — but the file exists against an insider, which is recorded here because Test 1 asks whether insiders are ever touched.

Evidence against:

  • The criminal law's incidence tracks loyalty with a consistency the mining code's does not. The 2019 Cybercrime Law — folded into the Penal Code in December 2024 and enforced by a specialised National Cybercrime Unit — has been used to imprison critics for speech: radio commentator Mohamed Youssouf Bathily ("Ras Bath") and activist Rokia Doumbia ("Rose Vie Chère") were arrested in March 2023 after broadcasts about inflation, power cuts, and the death in custody of former prime minister Soumeylou Boubèye Maïga, held through years of overlapping warrants and serial remands despite initial acquittals, and on 17 August 2026 sentenced by the Bamako Court of Appeal's Criminal Chamber to ten years (three suspended) for "criminal association" and "undermining the credibility of the state" (IFEX/MFWA analysis, 4 September 2026; IPI, 2026). Journalist Chahana Takiou was sentenced on 3 August 2026 to twelve months (six suspended) for criticising that same use of the cybercrime law; newspaper editor Abdrahamane Keïta was detained in June 2026 over remarks on the security situation in the north (IPI, 2026).
  • The two former prime ministers the seat has produced are both in prison or fresh from sentencing, and both were critics first. Choguel Maïga was sacked on 20 November 2024 days after publicly calling for clarity on the transition's end, audited in January 2025, and arrested on 12 August 2025 — the embezzlement charges are an ALLEGATION he denies, and their timing, arriving only after he became the junta's most prominent internal critic, is the contested fact: his lawyers and rights groups describe a weaponised judiciary, the prosecution describes an audit (AFP, August 2025; IDEA, August 2025). Former prime minister Moussa Mara was arrested on 1 August 2025 after expressing solidarity with jailed critics, and on 27 October 2025 sentenced to two years (one mandatory) for "damaging the state's credibility" over a social-media post (IDEA country file, October 2025).
  • Against that enforcement record stands a documented zero on the other side: Amnesty International's October 2026 report on the Mauritanian border operations states it knows of no prosecutions since 2023 of any Malian soldier or Russian partner for the killings it documents — at least 34 civilian men deliberately killed in seven incidents during joint army–Wagner patrols in 2023–2025, torture in the Léré and Nampala camps with at least five deaths in custody (Amnesty International, AFR 37/1347/2026, October 2026). For Moura (Test 4), no public indictment, trial, or conviction appears in the record assembled here. One law prosecutes broadcasters within months; the other has not, in over three years, produced a single charge for documented mass killing. That asymmetry is the second law.

Why the grade lands: A mining code applied to friend and stranger alike, and a dissolution decree that on paper exempts nobody, are real one-law artefacts. But the test grades the law as lived, and as lived there are two: speech against the seat is a crime pursued to seven-year sentences, while killing by the seat's own forces and partners is, on the documented record, not a crime at all. The source itself is split, so the grade is FAILS.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the Malian treasury and, through it, the state's claim that gold wealth serves Malians (the code's higher take, the Barrick and Resolute payments); party members in government posts, who kept their jobs under the dissolution. Hurt — Ras Bath and Rokia Doumbia (seven years to serve for broadcasts); Chahana Takiou, Abdrahamane Keïta, Moussa Mara, and the wider corps of journalists and activists prosecuted under the cybercrime provisions; Choguel Kokalla Maïga, in custody since August 2025 on charges he denies; and every victim's family in the border operations and at Moura, for whom the one law has produced no case at all.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • One referee did act, once, at the start: it was the Constitutional Court that regularised his position in May–June 2021, and the 2023 constitution was put to a referendum on 18 June 2023 rather than simply decreed — officially approved by some 97% of votes cast, on an official turnout of about 38%, figures the opposition contested at the time (contemporaneous reporting, June 2023). The referendum happened; it is the last national vote of any kind held under this seat.
  • The CNT does sit, debate, and pass laws — including the 2022 electoral law and the 2025 charter revision — and the courts do process the state's cases through hearings and appeals rather than by pure decree: the Ras Bath case went through a trial and an appeal chamber, however contested its substance (IPI, 2026; IFEX, September 2026). The forms of institutional life continue.
  • No documented interference with the Central Bank of West African States or the CFA franc framework appears in the record: Mali remained inside UEMOA and the CFA throughout, including through the 2022 ECOWAS sanctions (UK Parliament written evidence, September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The legislature is his appointment. The CNT's members are named by presidential decree; it was this appointed body that on 3 July 2025 voted the charter revision granting its appointer a renewable five-year term — adopted, per the electos.net country file, "by the votes of all 131 members present" (electos.net, updated 16 November 2026). A parliament that owes its seats to the president and votes unanimously to extend him is not a referee; it is part of the grip.
  • The judiciary is described by Freedom House as "beholden to the executive, despite constitutional guarantees of judicial independence," and under the 2023 constitution the president has sole authority to appoint Supreme Court judges (African Human Rights Law Journal, 2026, citing Freedom House). Attacks and pressure on judicial officers have led some judges to vacate their positions (same source). The cybercrime prosecutions in Test 1 are what this arrangement produces in practice: overlapping warrants and serial remands used to keep acquitted critics in custody (IFEX, September 2026).
  • The media referee has been progressively switched off. Party activity was suspended in April 2024 and then abolished in May 2025; major international broadcasters (France 24 and RFI) have been suspended or banned from Malian territory since 2022, and domestic outlets face suspension for coverage the authorities dislike (Niger Report, September 2026; HRW reporting via IFEX, 2026). In February 2023 the junta expelled MINUSMA's human-rights director; in June 2023 it ordered the whole UN mission out by year's end (Africa Center for Strategic Studies, February 2024). In September 2025 Mali, with Burkina Faso and Niger, announced withdrawal from the International Criminal Court, and the January 2025 ECOWAS exit removed Malian citizens' access to the ECOWAS Court of Justice — the two external courts that could hear a Malian against his own state (IDEA country file, September 2025; US House Tom Lantos Human Rights Commission hearing record, 14 May 2026).
  • In May 2026, after Defence Minister Sadio Camara's killing, Goïta took the defence ministry himself, concentrating the presidency, the government he appoints, and the army's political leadership in one person (conflict-tracker.com, November 2026; African Arguments, October 2026).

Why the grade lands: Every referee the test names has been appointed by him, suspended by him, expelled by him, or withdrawn from by him: legislature by decree, judges under a beholden judiciary, media banned or prosecuted, the electoral process abolished along with the parties, and the external courts exited. What remains standing — the CFA framework — is the one referee headquartered outside his reach. FAILS.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the junta's senior officers, whose tenure no institution can now check; the AES partner regimes in Burkina Faso and Niger, whose identical grips the ICC and ECOWAS exits were coordinated with. Hurt — Malian citizens as a whole, who lost the ECOWAS Court, the ICC's jurisdiction going forward, a parliament they did not choose being the only one they have, and any lawful organised way to oppose; judges and journalists named in the record (Takiou, Keïta) who tested the referees' independence and found its limits.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • On the region's pushback, he eventually took the negotiated door that was open in the moment. ECOWAS's January 2022 sanctions — borders closed, transactions suspended, state assets frozen — were lifted in July 2022 after his government published the revised 24-month timetable (House of Commons Library briefing; Reuters, 2022). The adjustment was real, and Malians ate because of it; that the timetable was later abandoned belongs to Test 5.
  • When another option became available in the Barrick dispute, he considered it and took it. After eighteen months of escalation — employees detained, gold seized by helicopter, a court-imposed provisional administrator running Loulo-Gounkoto from June 2025 — the government settled on 24 November 2025: Barrick accepted the 2023 code and paid ~USD 430 million; the state returned operational control in December 2025, released the four detained employees, restituted some three tonnes of seized gold, and renewed the permit for ten years (IISD, September 2026; Reuters, November 2025). He had a harder option — outright nationalisation — and declined it for a negotiated one.
  • After the April 2026 catastrophe he adjusted operationally rather than rhetorically: with Kidal lost and his defence minister dead, the government accepted a negotiated Africa Corps withdrawal from the northern bases, repaired relations with Algeria (an Algerian prime-ministerial visit to Bamako in September 2026, a Goïta visit to Algiers in preparation), and retook Kidal on 6 October 2026 behind an air campaign, the FLA calling its exit a "strategic, controlled and temporary withdrawal" (Crisis Group CrisisWatch, October 2026; Strature risk file, November 2026). On the ECOWAS exit itself, the AES position stated in September 2026 — exit "irreversible," but a legally framed partnership wanted — is a decline with stated reasons, which under the method is not counted as refusing to look (The Boston Report, 4 October 2026).
  • In late November 2025 he made a rare public reference to preparing "transparent and peaceful elections" that would end the transition, inviting the government to create the conditions — without a timeline (AFP via Nam News Network, 28 November 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The central wobble of the tenure — the demand, from voters, parties, the street, and the region, for the promised elections — has been answered at every stage by tightening. February 2022 passed; March 2024 passed; in September 2023 the February 2024 elections were postponed for "technical reasons" and then indefinitely (Reuters, April 2024). When some 100 parties formed a coalition in April 2025 and hundreds of Malians held Bamako's first large pro-democracy protests since 2020 on 3–4 May 2025, the answer was the 7 May suspension decree, the arrests on 8 May of party leaders Abba Alhassane (CODEM) and El Bachir Thiam (Yéléma) — reported by Human Rights Watch as arrests after protest participation, with a youth leader, Abdoul Karim Traoré, reported abducted — and on 13 May the dissolution of every party in the country (IDEA, May 2025; European Parliament resolution B10-0291/2025; OHCHR experts' statement, May 2025). Pushback was not answered; the pushers were abolished.
  • The sanctions wobble of 2022 ended the same way at the strategic level: having negotiated relief by promising a timetable, the government treated ECOWAS itself as the problem, announcing withdrawal on 28 January 2024 (with Burkina Faso and Niger), effective 29 January 2025, and rejecting the bloc's transition window to July 2025 (UK Parliament written evidence, September 2026). Whatever the merits of the grievances against ECOWAS — sanctions that hurt ordinary Malians, a bloc seen as enforcing others' priorities — the response to being pushed was to remove the pusher from the board.
  • The security wobble has been answered with denial of its scale. The junta's founding claim was that it would secure Mali better than the civilians and the French had; by late 2026 the documented position is a government controlling mainly the Bamako–Ségou–Sikasso axis, a capital whose fuel has been blockaded by JNIM since September 2025 — deliveries at a fifth of normal volume in October 2025, schools closed for two weeks, prices spiking (IDEA country file, September–October 2025; CES Intelligence risk assessment, November 2026) — and no public reckoning with the gap between the claim and the map. The response to the blockade was escorts and Russian supply pledges, which is management, not adjustment of the strategy that produced the exposure.

The no-good-choice clause, applied: April–October 2026 in the north is recorded under the clause. When the joint JNIM–FLA offensive of 25–26 April 2026 took Kidal and killed the defence minister, the options actually open were: fight to hold the northern bases in place, with a garrison already being overrun and heavy civilian cost the documented pattern of such stands; or accept a negotiated withdrawal, cede the north's symbolic capital a second time, and rebuild for a return. Both carried real harm; hindsight options — a larger Russian force, a different alliance — were not on the table in that week. The seat took the second door, then retook Kidal in October. That choice is recorded as the best possible available in the moment and is not held against him. What remains gradable, and is graded in Tests 4 and 5, is the trap's origin: the exposure of April 2026 was built by earlier choices — MINUSMA expelled before any replacement existed, the Algiers Accord torn up in January 2024, the northern armed movements converted from signatories into enemies — and by the honesty question, on which the record shows no public accounting for how the north was lost.

Why the grade lands: He can adjust — Barrick, the 2022 sanctions relief, the 2026 northern recovery all show it — but every adjustment is tactical, in service of the same fixed point: the seat itself is never the thing adjusted. On the one wobble that concerned his own tenure, the answer across six years escalated from postponement to abolition of the people asking. FAILS.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Barrick's four detained employees and the Loulo-Gounkoto workforce (released and back at work under the settlement); Malians who ate and traded under the 2022 sanctions relief; Bamako residents whose fuel convoys got through under escort. Hurt — Abba Alhassane, El Bachir Thiam, and Abdoul Karim Traoré (arrested or disappeared after the May 2025 protests); the 297 dissolved parties' members and the voters they represented; Malian households through the blockade years, whose shortages the strategy's exposure deepened; ECOWAS's remaining members, who lost three states and 76 million people from the bloc.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Tuareg north, the Fulani centre, the protesters, the displaced, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The seat's founding story was itself a counting story, and part of it was honoured. The 2020 coup followed mass protests against a government Malians across regions judged corrupt and unable to secure the country; the junta's promise to re-found the state drew genuine crowds, and the 2023 constitutional referendum — whatever its contested turnout — was an attempt to put the new order to a count rather than merely proclaim it (contemporaneous reporting, 2020–2023).
  • Territorial reunification was pursued as a counting claim: on 14 November 2023 the army retook Kidal, the northern capital no Malian government had held since 2012, and Goïta announced it as Mali made whole (Wikipedia, Kidal offensive; Africa Center for Strategic Studies, February 2024). The October 2026 re-entry repeated the claim after the April loss (CrisisWatch, October 2026).
  • The mining settlement and code are framed, with some factual basis, as counting Malians into wealth previously counted out to foreign shareholders: Barrick itself states more than 70% of Loulo-Gounkoto's economic benefits accrued to the Malian state even before the settlement, and the 2023 code raised that share further (IISD, September 2026). The AES project likewise counts its citizens into a larger unit — confederal passport, joint force, a confederal bank inaugurated in Bamako on 23 December 2025 with 500 billion CFA francs of capital (UK Parliament written evidence, September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Moura, 27–31 March 2022. In a five-day operation at Moura, Mopti region, Malian troops and foreign military personnel — identified by witnesses as Russian-speaking fighters of the Wagner Group, present in Mali from late 2021 at the government's invitation — detained hundreds of men from the market and their homes and executed them in groups. The counts are kept separate, as the record requires: the Malian government announced 203 killed, described as terrorists, and 51 captured (Malian defence ministry, 1 April 2022). Human Rights Watch documented over 300 civilians killed, most of them ethnic Fulani, calling it the worst single atrocity of Mali's decade-long conflict (HRW, 5 April 2022). The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights' fact-finding report, based on 157 interviews, found "strong indications" that more than 500 people were killed, the vast majority summarily executed, and documented 58 women and girls subjected to sexual violence; it concluded the acts could amount to war crimes (OHCHR, May 2023). The government rejects the massacre account and describes a counterterrorism operation; that dispute is recorded, and so is the fact that no prosecution followed (Test 1).
  • The pattern did not end with Wagner's rebranding. Amnesty's October 2026 report documents, along the Mauritanian border, at least 34 civilian men deliberately killed in seven incidents during joint army–Wagner patrols in 2023–2025 — shot, throats cut, or burned, the victims Fulani, Arab, and Tuareg pastoralists suspected for their ethnicity, their location, or for owning motorbikes or walkie-talkies — plus two army drone strikes in which no military target was found: Ejdeir market, 16 March 2025, 16 killed; Tangata, November 2025, 7 killed; and torture in the Léré and Nampala camps, mostly by Wagner members with the army's knowledge, with at least five deaths in custody (Amnesty International, October 2026). Human Rights Watch counted 47 drone strikes by the army and Africa Corps between April and August 2026 killing 94 civilians (HRW, reported November 2026). An analyst estimate puts civilian deaths in joint Malian–Russian operations between MINUSMA's December 2023 exit and June 2025 at nearly 1,500 — roughly four times the toll attributed to JNIM in the same window; it is an estimate, attributed as such (Good Authority, 2026).
  • The north was counted out politically before it was fought over. The junta took Kidal in November 2023 two weeks after MINUSMA's forced exit from the region, in breach of the 2015 Algiers Accord's framework, and on 25 January 2024 terminated the Accord "with immediate effect" — ending the only standing agreement under which the Tuareg-majority north had consented to be counted inside Mali (Africa Center for Strategic Studies, February 2024). The former signatories regrouped as the Azawad Liberation Front in November 2024, formally reviving the independence demand the Accord had retired. A population that had signed its way in was counted back out by decree.
  • The protesters and the poor are counted as threats and as ballast respectively. The May 2025 protesters were answered with arrests and the abolition of their parties (Test 3). Under the JNIM fuel blockade from September 2025, it was ordinary Malians — transport workers, market traders, schoolchildren out of school for two weeks in October–November 2025, hospital patients facing a 15% drop in consultations at Bamako's Point G hospital (Médecins Sans Frontières) — who absorbed the cost of a siege aimed at the seat (IDEA, 2025; The Panafrican Press, 2026). The seat's survival strategy and the population's hardship are, on this record, the same policy viewed from two ends.

Why the grade lands: A ruler counts everyone when the Fulani herder, the Tuareg northerner, and the Bamako protester appear in his accounting as Malians. On the documented record they appear as suspects, separatists, and criminals — killed by his forces and partners in numbers kept in three separate sourced counts above, stripped of their peace accord, their parties, and their courts. The reunification claim is real but it is a claim about territory; the people on the territory were not counted. FAILS.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Bamako's and the south's populations in one narrow sense: the state's coercive weight fell elsewhere, and the capital was held through April 2026; mining-region communities promised a larger state share of gold revenue. Hurt — the dead of Moura (203 by the government's count of "terrorists"; over 300 civilians by HRW's count; more than 500 by the UN's count — three counts, never blended) and their families; Fulani, Arab, and Tuareg pastoralists along the Mauritanian border (34 named-incident dead in Amnesty's count, five dead in custody); the 94 civilians HRW attributes to the April–August 2026 drone strikes; the Tuareg north, whose signed route into the Malian state was cancelled; and the displaced and blockaded poor of 2025–2026, who paid for the seat's strategy in fuel, food prices, closed schools, and missed hospital care.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

The transition's promises and his public statements vs. the record since 2020, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The constitutional referendum — held. The June 2022 timetable promised a referendum in March 2023; it was held on 18 June 2023 and the constitution promulgated on 22 July 2023 — three months late against a schedule everything else missed by years, and the one dated promise of the transition that was substantially kept (House of Commons Library briefing; electos.net, 2026).
  • Kidal — taken, as pledged. The junta promised to restore the state's authority over the whole territory; on 14 November 2023 the army entered Kidal for the first time since 2012, and after losing it in April 2026 the government retook it on 6 October 2026 (Wikipedia, Kidal offensive; CrisisWatch, October 2026). The word was kept twice, at the price graded in Test 4.
  • The sovereignty programme — executed as announced. He said French forces and MINUSMA would go, that Mali would leave ECOWAS's tutelage and build the AES with Burkina Faso and Niger, and that foreign miners would pay under a Malian code. Each happened: Barkhane completed its withdrawal in 2022, MINUSMA left by 31 December 2023, the ECOWAS exit took effect 29 January 2025, the AES confederation treaty was signed at Niamey on 6 July 2024 and its institutions — joint force (banner presented by Goïta at Bamako, 20 December 2025), confederal bank (23 December 2025), confederal parliament (installed at Niamey, August 2026) — were built on schedule-ish, and Barrick settled under the 2023 code in November 2025 (Wikipedia, Alliance of Sahel States; IISD, 2026). Whatever these choices cost (Tests 2–4), they were his stated intentions, carried out.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The founding word — the transition itself. September 2020: the junta agreed an 18-month transition, elections on 27 February 2022 (House of Commons Library briefing). At his swearing-in on 7 June 2021 Goïta committed himself personally to "the organisation of credible, fair and transparent elections that are held as scheduled" (same source). February 2022 passed without elections; ECOWAS sanctioned; in June 2022 he promised a new 24-month clock from 26 March 2022, ending March 2024, presidential election February 2024. In September 2023 that election was postponed for "technical reasons"; in March 2024 the deadline lapsed in silence (Reuters, 1 April 2024). In April 2024 party activity was suspended; in May 2025 parties were abolished; in July 2025 the appointed CNT granted him five renewable years. Each promise was replaced by a new instrument extending the same tenure, and the final instrument removes the need for promises altogether: the term renews "as many times as necessary," until "pacification" — a condition the seat itself gets to declare.
  • The 2021 assurance to the region. After the second coup he assured ECOWAS the 2022 timetable stood; ECOWAS and the African Union suspended Mali anyway, and the timetable did not stand (Wikipedia, Next Malian parliamentary election). The EU sanctioned his officials, including his prime minister, in February 2022 specifically for obstructing the agreed transition (EU Council, February 2022).
  • The November 2025 election remark — unconverted. His late-November 2025 invitation to the government to prepare elections (Test 3) produced, in the year since, no electoral calendar, no restored parties, no electoral commission activity in the record assembled here. A word about a word is recorded as such.

Why the grade lands: The test does not grade whether his programme was wise; the sovereignty programme was announced and executed, and those keepings are on the FOR shelf in full. It grades the seat's word — and the seat's first and defining word, sworn in public in June 2021, was that elections would be held as scheduled and power handed back. That word has been broken at every dated instance — 2022, 2024, and finally by abolishing the mechanism of keeping it. A ruler who keeps every promise except the one that limits his own tenure has told you which promises bind him. FAILS.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Malians who wanted the French and UN presence ended and the mining terms rewritten, and got exactly what was announced; the AES partner regimes, whose shared project his reliability inside the alliance underwrote. Hurt — every Malian voter, who was promised a say in February 2022 and again in February 2024 and has now been told the question is postponed until "pacification"; the ECOWAS negotiators and Malian parties who organised their conduct around timetables that were not honoured; and the transition's own civilian figures — Bah Ndaw and Moctar Ouane, removed in 2021; Choguel Maïga, sacked and jailed — who lent the promise their names.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Assimi Goïta holds Mali's executive seat in November 2026 exactly as the record said he would: the premise is verified, and the only correction is that "transitional president" is now a courtesy title for a presidency the appointed legislature made renewable without elections in July 2025. Test 1 fails because the law as lived is two laws — the mining code genuinely fell on everyone, but speech against the seat is prosecuted to seven-year sentences (Ras Bath, Rokia Doumbia, Moussa Mara, Chahana Takiou) while documented mass killing by the seat's own forces and Russian partners has produced, on Amnesty's October 2026 count, no prosecutions at all since 2023. Test 2 fails because every referee was appointed, suspended, expelled, or exited: a CNT he names voting unanimously to extend him, a judiciary Freedom House calls beholden, broadcasters banned, MINUSMA expelled, and the ECOWAS Court and ICC withdrawn from — with the defence ministry added to his own person in May 2026. Test 3 fails because his real adjustments — the 2022 sanctions relief, the 2025 Barrick settlement, the 2026 northern recovery — are all tactical moves around an unadjustable centre: when the street, the parties, and the region pushed on his tenure, he postponed, then arrested, then abolished the pushers; the April 2026 northern collapse is recorded under the no-good-choice clause for the choice itself, with the trap's origin — MINUSMA expelled, the Algiers Accord torn up — graded where it belongs, in Tests 4 and 5. Test 4 fails on the bodies, kept in their separate counts: Moura (government: 203 "terrorists"; HRW: over 300 civilians; UN: strong indications of more than 500, mostly summarily executed, plus 58 women and girls subjected to sexual violence), Amnesty's 34 border killings and five custody deaths, HRW's 94 civilians in the April–August 2026 drone strikes — Fulani, Arab, and Tuareg Malians counted as suspects, and a Tuareg north whose signed peace was cancelled by decree. The two axes, crossed. Word-keeping and helped/hurt were graded independently, and three of the four crossings occur in this record. Kept his word and it helped: the sovereignty programme's benign face — the referendum held in June 2023, the mining code enforced and settled, gold revenue redirected toward the state. Kept his word and it still hurt people: the same programme's other face — MINUSMA expelled and the French gone exactly as announced, and the documented sequel was a security vacuum in the centre and north, the Algiers framework's collapse, and the exposure that April 2026 cashed in; the keeping earns its Test 5 credit and the hurt stands in the ledger in full.
