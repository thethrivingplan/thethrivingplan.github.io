How much of the world's work could AI already run?

Ask it plainly: if nobody needed the paycheck — if money stopped holding people to jobs — how much of the work being done today could AI already just do, so the people doing it could be free to do something else? This page is the honest answer, from the studies that actually measured it. Not a forecast, not a scare headline — what today's AI can technically already do, counted carefully. And because freed hours are only half the story, the page ends where the hours land: what the freed time is for — personal relationships, business relationships, and all that — cross-referenced from every section it touches, so everything here is one stop, easy to find.

Lane 1 AI touches some of the work

The world~25–40%≈885M–1,416M jobs
Explain this step

What this lane counts: jobs where AI can already do at least ~10% of the tasks. In the main study's scoring, a task counts as “exposed” if an AI could cut the time to do it at least in half, at equal quality.

It does not mean the job can be done by AI. It means the job contains some tasks AI can speed up. Exposure is not displacement — in the US, Eloundou et al. also found 15% of all worker tasks could be done significantly faster by an LLM alone, rising to 47–56% of tasks once LLM-powered software is included. Goldman Sachs lands in this same lane with different wording: roughly two-thirds of US occupations are exposed “to some degree.”

United States: ~80% of workers — ≈130.5 million of 163.2 million employed (BLS, Sept 2026 base). The world: two measured studies bracket the lane — the ILO's occupation-level GenAI index at ~25% (≈885M jobs) and the IMF's broader AI-exposure index at ~40% (≈1,416M jobs).

Sources: Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock, “GPTs are GPTs,” Science 2024 · ILO–NASK, WP 140, May 2025 · IMF, SDN/2024/001, Jan 2024. Full citations in Sources below.

Lane 2 AI could already do most of the job
United States19%stricter count: 7% · ≈31.0M workers
The world~7–8%≈265M jobs
Explain this step

What this lane counts: jobs where AI could already do half or more of the tasks — today's models plus the available tooling and software built on top of them.

It does not mean no human is needed. The remaining tasks still need a person — the job shrinks, it doesn't vanish. One sharp caveat inside the US 19%: on human-rater scores for an LLM alone — no added software — only 3% of US workers have more than half their tasks exposed.

United States: 19% ≈ 31.0M workers (Eloundou et al., with tooling); the stricter count is 7% ≈ 11.4M (Goldman Sachs, 2023). The honest US range is 7–19% — the spread is different task scoring and different treatment of tooling. The world: ~7–8% ≈ 265M jobs, derived from the ILO's published task-score gradients (gradients 3+4: men 5.5%, women 10.4%, employment-weighted) — a reading of the ILO's own components, so confidence is medium, not high.

Sources: Eloundou et al., Science 2024 · Goldman Sachs, Briggs & Kodnani, Mar 2023 · ILO–NASK, WP 140, May 2025. Full citations in Sources below.

Lane 3 AI could already run the whole job
United StatesUnder 5%<8.2M jobs
The worldUnder ~3%<117M jobs
Explain this step

What this lane counts: jobs AI could run completely, end to end, with no human — every core task. This is the lane that answers the question as asked: work people would not need to be doing, because AI can already do it.

Honest flag: no study measures this lane directly for AI — for any country. Both figures are upper bounds built from the nearest measurements, labeled as such everywhere on this page.

United States: under 5% — fewer than ~8.2 million of 163.2 million jobs. The bound comes from McKinsey (2017) and covers all demonstrated automation technology, not AI alone; the true AI-only figure is likely lower — a low single-digit share, at most about 1 job in 20. The world: under ~3% — fewer than ~117 million of ~3.54 billion jobs. 3.3% is merely the world's highest-exposure category, and even those jobs average 0.7 of 1.0 on task-automation scores — ~30% of their tasks still need a human (ILO).

Sources: McKinsey Global Institute, A Future That Works, 2017 · ILO–NASK, WP 140, May 2025. Full citations in Sources below.

Lane 1 in detail

Eloundou et al. also found 15% of all US worker tasks could be done significantly faster at the same quality by an LLM alone — rising to 47–56% of tasks once LLM-powered software is included. Those are shares of tasks, not jobs.

Goldman Sachs lands in the same lane with different wording: roughly two-thirds of US occupations are exposed “to some degree.”

Lane 2 in detail

The honest Lane 2 range is 7–19% (≈11–31 million jobs). Why the spread? Different task scoring and different treatment of tooling.

One sharp caveat inside the 19%: on human-rater scores for an LLM alone — no added software — only 3% of US workers have more than half their tasks exposed.

Lane 3 in detail

McKinsey's “less than 5% of occupations can be automated entirely” dates from 2017 — before modern AI — and covers all demonstrated technology (robotics, ML, everything). Later task-based studies have not overturned its order of magnitude.

At a looser threshold — jobs where ≥70% of tasks are automatable — OECD task-based work finds ~9% (Arntz, Gregory & Zierahn, 2016) to 14% of jobs at high risk (Nedelkoska & Quintini, 2018). Even a looser question stays small.

Hours are not jobs

McKinsey Global Institute (Agents, Robots, and Us, Nov 2025): currently demonstrated technology could, in theory, automate activities accounting for 57% of US work hours — 44 points from AI agents (nonphysical work), 13 from robots (physical work).

US work hourstechnically automatable today
57%of hours — not jobs

This number is real and is not a job count. Automatable hours are spread thinly across nearly all jobs rather than concentrated in whole jobs — which is exactly why 57% of hours coexists with under 5% of fully runnable jobs. McKinsey is explicit: this is current technical capability, not a forecast of job losses, and adoption “may take decades.” Its 2023 scenario of ~29.5% of US hours automated by 2030 is a modeled adoption path — never cite it as today's capability.

AI and robots, counted together: Anthropic's robot study (Sep 30, 2026 — given its own section below) finds LLMs and robots combined expose ~80–81% of US job tasks, weighted by working time, to one or the other. Read that denominator carefully: it is a share of tasks by hours — the same family as the 57% above (44 points AI agents, 13 robots) — and it is not Lane 1's ~80%, which counts workers (Eloundou et al.). Two similar-looking numbers counting two different things; this page keeps them separate and never merges them. And exposure still is not adoption: priced against the people doing the work, robots are cost-competitive for just 0.3% of job tasks today.

Where AI could already do most of the work

  • Office & administrative support — the single most exposed family in every study. Data entry keyers show 67% observed AI coverage in real usage data; bookkeeping, payroll, and general office clerks alongside.
  • Customer service & contact centers — representatives (~2.86M US jobs) rank near the top of observed AI coverage.
  • Writing, editing, translation — writers, technical writers, proofreaders, editors, interpreters and translators top applicability rankings.
  • Basic coding & IT support — computer programmers show the highest observed coverage Anthropic measured (75%) — though so far that has meant heavy augmentation, with programmers employed to direct and verify.
  • Legal & financial support — paralegals, legal assistants, financial and market research analysts, bank tellers, credit and loan officers.

Where it can't — and why

  • Skilled trades & repair — electricians, plumbers, mechanics, HVAC: physical work in unpredictable environments.
  • Food & personal services — cooks, dishwashers, bartenders, hairdressers, childcare workers: in-person, physical, trust-based.
  • Hands-on healthcare — nurses, care assistants, dentists, surgeons: physical care plus legal accountability a machine cannot hold.
  • Protective & emergency work, construction, agriculture — outdoor, unpredictable, physical.

The structural reason, common to every source: a job is a bundle of tasks. Automating 60–90% of the bundle still leaves a human doing the rest — which is why Lane 3 is so much smaller than Lane 2. In real usage data, 30% of US workers have zero observed AI coverage at all (Anthropic names cooks, motorcycle mechanics, lifeguards, bartenders, dishwashers).

Self-driving vehicles: a robot whose driver is software

The first bullet of “Where the capability is real,” below, expanded — because one machine on this page is both halves of it at once. A self-driving vehicle is a robot: sensors for eyes, actuators for hands, a body that moves through the physical world. And the thing doing the driving inside it is a computer — the software this page counts everywhere else. Call it a car, a robot, or a computer; here they are one family, counted the same way as everything else on the page — what it can do, and what it costs. Its workplace is also the hardest one the robot study counts: the open road sits in the unstructured, open-environment tier — ~1% of all tasks — which is why driving is at once the most visible robot work there is and some of the hardest to hand over.

The ladder everyone quotes — SAE's Levels 0–5, in plain words:

  • Levels 0–2 — the human is still the driver. Level 0: no automation. Level 1: the car assists with steering or speed, not both. Level 2: it steers and holds speed at once — but the human must supervise the whole time and remains the driver. Nearly everything marketed as “self-driving” lives here, including Tesla's Full Self-Driving (Supervised).
  • Level 3 — conditional. In defined conditions, the system truly drives and the human may look away — but must take back over when the system asks. Approved in only a few places: Mercedes-Benz's Drive Pilot in Nevada and California; Honda in Japan.
  • Level 4 — the system is the driver, inside a set area. Within its defined territory and conditions, no human fallback is needed or expected: if something exceeds the system, the system itself brings the vehicle to a safe stop. This is the level carrying paying passengers today.
  • Level 5 — anywhere, any conditions. It does not exist, and nobody sells it; a claim otherwise is marketing, not a level.

What is actually running: Waymo carries paying passengers with nobody in the driver's seat in several US cities — Level 4 as a going commercial service. Tesla's robotaxi service launched in Austin in June 2025, labeled by Tesla as Level 4; market reporting put its driving at roughly 200,000 miles a week by early 2026 (market-reported). Both are real — and both are fenced to set cities and set conditions, which is precisely what Level 4 means.

The honesty that travels with it: “driverless” is not yet “no humans.” These fleets employ people you never see — remote supervisors (teleoperators) who watch the fleet, answer when a car asks for help, and guide it through the odd situation. Those are people's wages inside the robot's price, and they belong in the 0.3% story told in “Why almost none of it is running yet,” just below: for taxis and freight alike, the machine has to beat not just the driver's wage but its own support crew's. It is also why no lane moves: driver occupations can top the exposure list — Lane 1's question — while the job itself stays out of Lane 3. Capability inside a fence is not a job handed over.

Sources: SAE International, J3016 (the levels) · Waymo (the driverless service) · Tesla robotaxi reporting, Austin (the launch and the scale — market-reported) · Anthropic's robot study above (the exposure tiers and the 0.3%). Full citations in Sources below.

Where the capability is real

  • Vehicle operators — nine of the ten most robot-exposed occupations are drivers; autonomous taxis already run paying passengers on city roads, the study's most exposed setting of all. Those taxis get their own treatment in self-driving vehicles, just above.
  • Warehouses & logistics — mobile robots moving stock, unloading, and sorting in buildings laid out to be machine-readable.
  • Fabrication & construction support — AI-guided welding cells, trench excavators that dig straight lines without a driver.
  • Packers & packagers — the clearest case where the robot is already slightly cheaper than the person: the thin leading edge of the 0.3%.
  • At the other end: nurses and mechanics — dexterity, judgment in unstructured places, and human contact keep them near zero exposure.

Why almost none of it is running yet

The bottleneck is not capability — it is price. Robot prices have fallen at roughly 3% a year, quality-adjusted, since the 1990s. At that pace, the study estimates it takes ~40 years for robots to become cost-competitive for even 10% of job tasks.

Even the authors' fast scenario — costs falling up to four times faster, robots learning new tasks twice as fast — does not make robots cheaper than people for half of today's physical work until around 2050. Capability isn't the bottleneck; economics is.

Counted together with AI, the combined reach is stated once, where it belongs — in “Hours are not jobs” in The United States above — with its denominator (tasks by working time) kept separate from Lane 1's 80% of workers. And what does all this change in the three lanes? Nothing. The robot study measures hours and tasks, not whole jobs — and its 0.3% cost figure is the strongest evidence yet for why Lane 3 stays under 5%: a robot that can do the work but costs more than the person doing it has not freed anyone yet. What robots add is reach: the physical half of the world's work that software alone could never touch is now counted — and most of it turns out to be a matter of price and time, not possibility. There is also a flip side, in the very next section: robots you wear — suits that keep the human in the job and take the load instead of taking the task.

Source: Anthropic, Legate-Yang & Massenkoff, “What work can robots do?” (Sep 30, 2026) — primary — anthropic.com. US only — no comparable world robot study exists yet, so the world and region lanes above remain AI-measured.

Wearable robots: the suit takes the load

Exoskeletons — worn, not replacing · the human keeps the job, the machine carries the weightExplain

The flip side of the section above. A robot in a warehouse takes the task; a wearable robot — an exoskeleton — is worn by the person doing the task. The human keeps the job, the judgment, and the hands; the suit takes the load off the back, the shoulders, and the spine. They already exist, they are already deployed, and they are augmentation made of metal: they do not push a job toward Lane 3 — they move physical work toward the pattern the rest of this page keeps finding, a person doing the work with a machine carrying part of it. And what a worker saves in the body comes back after the shift — see What the freed time is for below.

On the job: powered suits

  • German Bionic Cray X / Apogee — powered back support for lifting and carrying, giving roughly 30 kg (66 lb) of lifting assistance per lift — the kind of help that turns a two-person lift, or a back injury waiting to happen, into a routine task.
  • Sarcos Guardian XO — a full-body powered suit that lets the wearer lift and move loads of around 200 lb while feeling a fraction of the weight — built for logistics, depots, and heavy handling where a forklift or a fixed robot cannot go.
  • Where they work: warehousing and logistics (lifting, parcel handling), automotive and aerospace assembly, and construction — anywhere the work is heavy, repeated, and done by a person who has to be there.

On the job: passive frames

  • EksoVest — a spring-loaded upper-body vest for overhead assembly work, deployed at Ford and BMW. Ford documented roughly 40% fewer above-shoulder injury claims after deployment (employer/vendor-reported).
  • Ottobock Paexo — a family of passive supports (shoulder, back, thumb) for overhead and lifting work, worn like a backpack, no battery at all.
  • SuitX BackX — a passive back frame; its makers report spinal-force reductions up to ~60% (maker-reported).
  • Passive suits trade power for simplicity: no charging, lighter, cheaper — and for overhead and back work, often enough.

Why employers actually buy them

The driver is not replacing anyone — it is injury prevention. Musculoskeletal disorders — backs, shoulders, strains from lifting and overhead work — are over a third of all work injuries. A suit that keeps a back intact keeps an experienced worker working: longer careers, fewer lost days, and heavy jobs opened to people the work used to exclude — older workers, smaller frames, anyone whose body was the only barrier. Reported payback windows run roughly 8–28 months, passive logistics suits at the fast end (market-reported).

It files under relationships too: a worker whose body is not destroyed by the job has a self left over for everyone else in their life.

What holds them back

  • Cost — powered suits run to thousands of dollars each; passive frames are far cheaper, which is why they spread first.
  • Battery — a powered suit runs roughly 6–8 hours on a charge: a shift, barely, with swapping and charging to manage.
  • Comfort and fit — the deciding factor in practice. A suit that chafes, overheats, or fits one body type only gets left on the hook, however strong it is.

The same suits, in medicine: standing and walking again

Outside the workplace, the same technology is cleared medical equipment — suits that let people with spinal-cord injury and other conditions stand and walk:

  • ReWalk — the first exoskeleton to win FDA clearance for personal use, in 2014; its current generation, ReWalk 7, was cleared in 2025, with insurance recognition expanding.
  • EksoNR — a rehabilitation-clinic suit cleared for stroke, spinal-cord injury, acquired brain injury, and multiple sclerosis — re-teaching walking, step by step, with a therapist beside the patient.
  • Wandercraft Atalante X — a self-balancing suit: the wearer does not have to balance it with crutches, so hands stay free while walking is relearned.
  • CYBERDYNE HAL — reads the wearer's own faint nerve signals and assists the movement they are trying to make — the suit follows intention, not a program.

This is the furthest point from Lane 3 on the whole page: a machine whose entire job is to give a person more of their own capability back.

So where do suits sit in the three lanes? Nowhere — deliberately. They change no lane figure: a job done in a suit is still done by the person. What they change is the cost of the job to the body doing it — and that cost is paid in the hours and the self that are left afterwards, which is exactly what the next section is about.

What the freed time is for: relationships

Qualitative — no lane figures · personal relationships · business relationships · and all thatExplain

This layer is qualitative, and it says so at the door. The three lanes above count hours machines could already carry — robots, wearable suits, and software. This section counts nothing; it says what those hours are for. The direction is well supported by time-use research; the size of the effect varies by household and job, so no number is printed here as one. It sits beside the measured lanes, clearly distinguished — the one-stop-shop rule of this page is that everything is in here, cross-referenced, and each kind of claim is labeled as what it is.

Personal relationships

Hours are the raw material of every relationship — there is no other material. Long and irregular work hours are a standing strain on marriages, parenting, friendship, and the care of elders. Freed time returns as presence:

  • Evenings present instead of exhausted — a partner who is actually there, not recovering in the same room.
  • Kids actually raised by their people — parenting is time, spent in person, and no efficiency substitutes for it.
  • Friends kept instead of lost to scheduling — most friendships do not end; they get postponed to death. Time is what keeping one costs.
  • Care of elders, given by family — presence is a gift, and also a time expenditure; freed hours are how it gets paid.

Cross-reference — the unicorn lesson: this page's companion examination, “Unicorns Do Not Exist” — Claim Examined, found that in relationships “unicorn” names the partner who checks all the boxes — a creature hunted precisely because it does not exist. The lesson travels, and it is named here as a door between the two pages: the “unicorn partner who checks all the boxes” is partly a time problem wearing a fantasy's clothes — people are more themselves, and more available, when they are not spent. Part of what looks like a shortage of perfect partners is a shortage of unspent people.

Business relationships

Work relationships under time pressure collapse into transactions — coverage, handoffs, throughput. Freed from drudgery (Lane 1 and Lane 2 work, above), the same hours return as the parts of work that were always relationships:

  • Trust built face to face — the slow kind, which is the only kind that holds.
  • Customers known instead of processed — a name, a history, a person, not a ticket.
  • Mentoring — teaching is the other half of every craft, and teaching takes time nobody under pressure has.
  • Craft done properly — and small owners getting their evenings, and their judgment, back.

The exoskeletons file here as well as with the robots: a worker whose body isn't destroyed by the job has a self left over for everyone else — at work and at home.

And all that — the wider ripple

  • Sleep and health — the first thing time pressure eats, and the first thing freed time restores.
  • Community — neighbours with time can organize: a community land fund, a tenants' purchase, a team, a choir all run on somebody's free hours, not just money.
  • The arts, and learning for its own sake — every song, book, and skill in the world was made out of someone's unrequired time.
  • Civic life — meetings attended, juries served, votes cast by people who had the evening to think.
  • Rest itself, unapologized for — time that is for nothing is not wasted time; it is the ground the rest grows in.

Cross-reference — closing the loop: the three lanes at the top of this page are the supply side — hours that could be handed over. The robots carry the physical share; the wearable suits hand the load back to the body gently instead of taking the job. This section is the demand side — what the hours are worth when they come back. Neither half means much without the other, which is why they live on the same page, every reference a labeled door.

The reality check: what AI is actually doing so far

Observed use today: only ~4% of occupations use AI across 75% or more of their tasks.Explain

Capability is one thing; observed use is another. Anthropic's Economic Index measures real AI usage across millions of conversations — and the gap between theory and practice is wide.

Observed use today

  • 49% of jobs have seen AI used for at least 25% of their tasks (pooled Anthropic reports, Jan 2026) — but only about 4% of occupations use AI across 75% or more of their tasks.
  • Usage splits 57% augmentation (AI helping a person) vs 43% automation (AI doing the task).
  • Computer & Mathematical occupations: 94% theoretical exposure vs 33% observed coverage. Highest observed: computer programmers 75%, data entry keyers 67%.
  • 30% of workers have zero observed coverage — cooks, motorcycle mechanics, lifeguards, bartenders, dishwashers, dressing-room attendants.

And in the labor market itself

Anthropic's Labor Market Impacts of AI (March 2026) finds no systematic increase in unemployment among highly exposed workers since late 2022 — with suggestive evidence of slower hiring of young workers in exposed occupations.

That's the honest state of play: the capability counted on this page is real, measured, and mostly not yet how the work is actually being done. The question this page answers is what could already be run — not what has been handed over.

Three famous numbers — and what they actually say

47% · 300M · +78M · 40% — what each headline number actually means.Explain

You'll meet these in headlines and videos. Each is real. None of them means what it usually gets quoted as meaning.

47%Frey & Osborne, Oxford, 2013

“47% of US jobs are at high risk of automation.” This scored whole occupations with a classifier — the method the later literature was written to correct. It also described automation “over some unspecified number of years, maybe a decade or two,” with pre-modern-AI technology. Score the tasks inside the same occupations instead, and the figure collapses to ~9% at a ≥70%-of-tasks threshold — because most “automatable” occupations contain many tasks that aren't. The 47%-vs-9% gap is methodology, not disagreement about facts.

300MGoldman Sachs, 2023

“AI could expose 300 million full-time jobs.” “Exposed” is Lane 1 language — jobs containing some automatable tasks, extrapolated globally from US and European task data (the bank says so). Goldman's own split of the US workforce: 63% in jobs where less than half the workload could be automated, 30% in physical/outdoor jobs unaffected, ~7% where half or more could be. index Verified via reproductions of the bank's summary and contemporaneous reporting; the primary Goldman PDF was not retrieved.

+78MWEF Future of Jobs, 2025

“170 million jobs created, 92 million displaced by 2030 — net +78 million.” This is what 1,000+ employers across 55 economies expect, across all macro trends at once — green transition, demographics, geoeconomics, and technology — not an AI-capability measurement, and not US-specific. It answers “what do employers think will happen,” a different question from every lane on this page.

40%IMF, January 2024

“Almost 40% of global employment is exposed to AI.” True — as Lane 1. The IMF's own split matters: of the exposed jobs in advanced economies (~60% exposed there), roughly half may be negatively affected and the rest could benefit from productivity gains through AI integration. Exposure is overlap between a job's tasks and tasks AI can perform — it is not displacement, and the IMF published no US-specific figure.

Read every number with these attached

Nine warnings the studies themselves attach to every figure on this page.Explain

These are not fine print added for caution's sake — they are the studies' own warnings, stated in nearly identical words by the IMF, ILO, OECD, Eloundou et al., and McKinsey.

  1. Exposure ≠ automation ≠ people losing work. Every Lane 1 number is an upper bound on affected tasks, not a count of jobs anyone could hand over.
  2. Technically feasible ≠ deployed. These figures count capability only — not cost, employer willingness, regulation, or trust. This page's question brackets all of that on purpose; job-loss forecasts are a different document.
  3. Thresholds move the headline. ≥10% of tasks → 80% (US). ≥50% of tasks → 19% or 7%. 100% of core tasks → under 5%. Any single percentage quoted without its threshold is meaningless.
  4. Tasks are not weighted equally. A job whose automatable tasks take 10% of the workweek can score like one whose automatable tasks take 60%. Real-world impact shrinks further once you weight by time and success rate.
  5. Method explains the huge spreads. Occupation-based scoring (Frey & Osborne) vs task-based scoring (OECD, ILO) differ by roughly 5× on the same economy. Most task studies are also built on US occupational data; the ILO's 2025 index is the one major exception, built on a global occupation standard — the ILO itself calls the US-crosswalk assumption “tenuous” elsewhere.
  6. Formal-sector bias grows as income falls. In regions with 60–87% informal work, all three lanes describe the formal enclave and overstate whole-economy shares.
  7. Capability estimates assume infrastructure. Electricity, broadband, devices, and digital skills are preconditions baked silently into “technical capability.” Latin America's study is the one place this is priced: ~17 million jobs' worth of blocked benefit.
  8. Exposure falls harder on women. In the ILO's global index, 4.7% of women's employment sits in the highest-exposure category vs 2.4% of men's (high-income: 9.6% vs 3.5%) — because clerical and administrative work, the most exposed category everywhere, is female-majority in most economies.
  9. Vintage matters — and the central gap is a finding. The Lane 3 anchor (McKinsey) is from 2017, before modern AI; the exposure literature (2023–25) postdates it but doesn't measure full runnability. As of late 2026, no study directly measures “AI runs the entire job, no human” for any country. When that study exists, this page's Lane 3 gets replaced — not patched.
