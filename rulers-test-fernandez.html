The Ruler's Test — Laura Fernández Delgado, President of Costa Rica

Subject: Laura Virginia Fernández Delgado — President of Costa Rica since 8 May 2026. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: Costa Rica's 1949 constitution vests executive power in the president as head of state and head of government, and the presidency directs the budget, the public force, and the legislative agenda of the governing party. The holder named in the original brief, Rodrigo Chaves Robles, no longer holds the seat: barred from consecutive re-election, he completed his term (8 May 2022 – 8 May 2026) and handed the sash to Fernández, his former Minister of National Planning and then Minister of the Presidency, who won the 1 February 2026 election outright in the first round with 48.3 per cent of the valid vote against Álvaro Ramos of the National Liberation Party (PLN), at 33.4 per cent (Supreme Electoral Tribunal figures via Directorio Legislativo post-election report, February 2026; CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). Her Sovereign People's Party (PPSO) took 31 of 57 seats in the Legislative Assembly — the first time since 1990 that one party has won both the presidency and a legislative majority (Atlantic Council, May 2026). She is the country's fiftieth president and its second woman in the office, and she is graded here on roughly six and a half months in office; the file's thinness is stated where it matters. The arrangement that defines the seat, however, cannot be graded as a simple succession: on taking office Fernández appointed Chaves himself as Minister of the Presidency and Minister of Finance — the government's two coordinating portfolios. The University of Costa Rica's CIEP polling (fieldwork 25–28 August 2026) found 64 per cent of Costa Ricans rating her management positively, and 84 per cent perceiving that Chaves influences her government's main decisions (64.4 per cent "much influence"), a perception CIEP itself labels "dual presidentialism" (CIEP-UCR survey via Delfino, September 2026). Fernández has insisted publicly that she holds the final word in policy disagreements between them (Inter-American Dialogue, Latin America Advisor, May 2026). This dossier grades Fernández as the seat-holder; Chaves's completed tenure appears as context where it bears directly on a test, and his acts since 8 May 2026 are treated as what they are — acts of her government, by her appointee, in her cabinet. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. No grade is naked: every grade is earned inside its own section, with the receipts — the evidence and its sources — on the same page, and each test closes with why the grade lands where it lands and a ledger of who her actions on that test helped and who they hurt. The mandate rule: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one. The Test grades the holding, never the platform: carrying out what she ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Marks are lost only for how it is done — a second law for her own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect her (Test 4). Test 3 also grades whether, when another option became available, she at least considered it openly: considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it is. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are separate axes and are graded and printed independently — a promise can be faithfully kept and still hurt people, and a word can be gone against in a way that helped people; the Overall Summary names the crossings where they occur. Where the record shows a situation in which there really was no good choice — or none that appeared to be at the time — the dossier says so, names the options that actually existed and the harm each carried, and grades it as the best possible choice available given the situation in the moment; that is not held against her. What remains gradable in such a case is whether she built the trap herself earlier, whether she was honest about it while in it, and whether she took the least-harm door that was actually open. The ethic: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution service her movement has campaigned against for four years operates, on the documented record, against her own camp's center. As of July 2026, the Attorney General's Office (Fiscalía) counted 42 open causes against Rodrigo Chaves — 41 under investigation and one with a formal accusation — a count reported by the Fiscalía's own leadership in the context of Attorney General Carlo Díaz's public proposal to narrow official immunity (Al Pulso, November 2026). An open cause is not guilt, and this dossier does not treat it as such; what it evidences is that the file exists, grows, and is maintained by an institution her government does not control.
  • The law reached the summit of the old judicial establishment while her movement held the executive. Under the May 2025 constitutional reform allowing extradition of nationals for drug trafficking and terrorism — enacted under Chaves and executed across the handover — a San José appeals tribunal on 3 February 2026 confirmed the extradition to the United States of Celso Manuel Gamboa Sánchez, a former Minister of Public Security, former intelligence director, and former Supreme Court magistrate, together with Edwin López Vega; both were handed to US authorities on 20 March 2026, the first extradition of Costa Rican nationals for drug trafficking in the country's history (DiarioYa, 3 February 2026; Trivisión, March 2026). The US charges against Gamboa are indictments — ALLEGATIONs he has not been convicted of — and the tribunal itself policed the law's limits, revoking a third man's extradition because his alleged conduct predated the reform (DiarioYa, February 2026). The point for this test is the direction of travel: the machinery moved against a former magistrate, not only against the powerless.
  • The electoral referee applied its rules to the most powerful people in the country, including to Fernández herself. The Supreme Electoral Tribunal (TSE) ran the February 2026 election, delivered her credentials, and its results were accepted by the losing side — Ramos conceded on election night (Directorio Legislativo, February 2026; TicosLand, March 2026). During the campaign, on 25 February 2026, the same tribunal upheld an electoral protection action against candidate Fernández after a voter, Mia Fink Uleth, was blocked from Fernández's campaign Instagram account; the tribunal held that unjustifiably restricting a voter's access to campaign information infringed the right to an informed vote, and granted the action for costs and damages (TSE ruling via CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The referee ruled against the incoming president, in her own election, and the ruling stood.
  • The application of the law to Chaves personally was set in motion by every referee in sequence, under the previous administration and continuing into hers: the TSE referred the 2022 campaign's parallel-financing file to prosecutors in June 2022; the Attorney General accused Chaves and his Culture Minister Jorge Rodríguez Vives of concusión (extortionate abuse of office) in the "BCIE case" on 7 April 2025; the Supreme Court's full bench voted 15–7 on 1 July 2025 to ask the Assembly to lift his immunity (case file 25-000019-0033-PE, via Wikipedia's case record and International IDEA's democracy tracker, 2025). Whatever blocked enforcement (below), the application was not selective in his favor at the prosecutorial or judicial level.

Evidence against:

  • At the source of this government, one man is procedurally unreachable, and the architecture that keeps him so is maintained by her appointment and her party's votes. The BCIE accusation — that a US$405,800 communications contract, financed by the Central American Bank for Economic Integration and awarded to producer Christian Bulgarelli's firm, was steered so that US$32,000 would pass to Chaves's former image adviser Federico Cruz, "Choreco," toward a house purchase — is an ALLEGATION, denied by Chaves, never tested at trial (Fiscalía accusation via case record, 2025). It has never been tested because the Assembly voted 34–21 on 22 September 2025 to lift his presidential immunity, four short of the 38 the constitution requires, and voted 35–21 on 16 December 2025 against lifting it again, that time on the TSE's request over 15 cases of alleged political belligerence (partisan campaigning by a president, which the constitution forbids) (AM Costa Rica, September 2025; International IDEA, December 2025). The TSE-referred campaign-finance file is reported ready to ground a further immunity request (La Revista, 2026). On 8 May 2026 Chaves's presidential immunity expired — and Fernández appointed him to two ministries, restoring the same procedural shield, because ministers are also members of the Supreme Powers under Costa Rican law. Attorney General Díaz has proposed limiting the immunity to duty-related offenses precisely because, as the record stands, a formal accusation against the government's second-most-powerful figure cannot proceed while he sits in her cabinet, and her party's 31 seats make the 38-vote threshold arithmetically unreachable (Al Pulso, November 2026). Test 1 grades whether one law reaches everyone; at the center of this seat, it does not reach — not because a court acquitted him, but because the calendar and the appointment book are arranged so that no court is asked.
  • The one documented exception to her party's stated line on judicial renewal ran in favor of the magistrates who had shielded Chaves. Through 2026 the executive repeatedly and publicly asked the Assembly to renew none of the Supreme Court magistrates whose terms expired; PPSO voted against re-election in three of the five cases. The two exceptions were Jorge Olaso and Roxana Chacón — both of whom had voted, in the full court's 15–7 decision of July 2025, against asking the Assembly to lift Chaves's immunity in the BCIE case (Visible, November 2026). Olaso's re-election was recommended unanimously by the Appointments Committee on 27 October 2026 after PPSO's four members reversed the party's position, and the plenary re-elected him unanimously (50 deputies present) on 11–12 November 2026, for eight more years to 2034 (CRHoy, October 2026; Prensa Mercosur and Infórmate, November 2026). Frente Amplio deputy José María Villalta stated on the record that the only element explaining the change of criterion was Olaso's immunity vote; PPSO deputy José Miguel Villalobos denied this, saying each magistrate was assessed individually and that agreements with other factions had proved difficult (Prensa Mercosur, 12 November 2026). The quid pro quo is an ALLEGATION, printed here with its denial; the votes and the sequence are documented fact, and they are the only two exceptions the party made.
  • Under Chaves, the response to the prosecutor who accused him was pressure on the prosecutor's tenure, not submission to the process: from early in his term he demanded Attorney General Díaz's resignation, pressed the Supreme Court to remove him, and in March 2025 led a government-organized march to the Fiscalía's offices demanding that resignation (La Revista, 2026; the march and its demands were widely reported at the time). The accusation against his own minister (Rodríguez Vives) proceeded in parallel — the one-law machinery worked — but the president's answer to being accused was to campaign against the accuser's job. Fernández inherited this posture and, as Test 2 records, has continued it in her own register.

Why the grade lands here: The application of the law in Costa Rica is visibly even-handed — the same prosecutor's office holds 42 files on her finance minister, the electoral tribunal ruled against her personally mid-campaign, and a former magistrate was extradited under her movement's own reform. The enforcement of the law is not: the single formal accusation against the man who shares her government's direction is suspended by an immunity her appointment renewed and her party's seats protect. One law, applied to all; one man, unreachable by it. That is MIXED, and the unreachable man is not at the periphery of the seat — he is at its center.

Helped: The public in cartel-hit regions, and the principle of accountability at the top, by the extradition machinery that took a former magistrate and security minister out of the country to face a US court (charges unproven). Voters generally, by an electoral tribunal that certified a clean transfer and ruled against the winner when the winner broke its rules. Hurt: The complainants and the public interest in the BCIE file, whose formal accusation — right or wrong — cannot be tested while its subject holds ministerial office; and the credibility of the magistrate-renewal process, where the governing party's only two exceptions coincided exactly with the two votes that had protected its leader (the coincidence documented, the motive contested).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The prosecutor her movement tried for years to dislodge was renewed by his own institution, unanimously, on her watch — and the renewal stands. On 19 October 2026 the Supreme Court's full bench re-elected Carlo Díaz as Attorney General for a second four-year term, to October 2030, by 21 votes of the 21 magistrates present, where 12 were needed (La República, November 2026; Delfino, 2026). The executive had no lawful instrument to prevent it and did not obstruct the process; the man who holds the 42 files on her finance minister will hold his office for the whole of her term.
  • The electoral referee's autonomy is intact in fact. The TSE administered the 2026 election without documented executive interference, ruled against Fernández during the campaign (Test 1), and remains, with the judiciary and the public universities, among the most trusted institutions in the country in CIEP's August 2026 polling — above the presidency itself (CIEP-UCR via Radiotica analysis, September 2026).
  • The central bank's autonomy shows no documented breach. Fernández ratified Róger Madrigal López as president of the Central Bank of Costa Rica in her cabinet announcements (Bloomberg Línea, May 2026), and the record assembled here contains no instance of the executive directing or punishing a monetary-policy decision. The Assembly's ratification of two government-nominated board members (former finance minister Rudolf Lucke and economist Max Soto, seven-year terms, July 2026) drew PLN objections over a Constitutional Chamber ruling that had found Lucke, as minister, in violation of the right to public education for withholding transfers; PPSO answered that no personal sanction against him was documented (Legislative Assembly records via its official channels, June–July 2026). Board appointments by the political branches are the constitutional design; they are recorded here as contested, not as capture.
  • The courts rule against her government openly and repeatedly, and most rulings are being processed through compliance channels: the Constitutional Chamber annulled the SUTEL frequency auctions inherited from Chaves (27 February 2026), ordered the Health Ministry to unblock a citizen from its official page (21 April 2026), and on 20 November 2026 struck down her budget's judicial-funding conditions (Test 3) — with the Assembly's constitutional-consultation committee tasked to implement the corrections before the 29 November vote (Al Pulso, November 2026). A unanimous Assembly also repealed the 1902 Press Law's prison penalties for journalists (Law 10.853, 3 February 2026, in force 27 March 2026), welcomed by the Inter American Press Association — enacted weeks before she took office, recorded as the system she inherited operating in the press's favor (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Her government has declined, for more than three months, to comply with two Constitutional Chamber rulings ordering it to seat the trade unions' elected representative on the board of the Costa Rican Social Security Fund (CCSS) — and answered the rulings by rewriting the rules. The union movement's assembly elected Rocío Alfaro Molina as its board representative on 9 June 2026; the Governing Council rejected her designation; the Constitutional Chamber annulled the rejection on 13 August 2026 (resolution 2026-031380), ordered the necessary steps to appoint her within eight days on 24 September 2026 (resolution 2026-036620), and reaffirmed the order on 6 October 2026 (resolution 2026-038199) (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026; University of Costa Rica University Council statement, 15 October 2026; SURCOS, November 2026). The government instead seated Roger Rivera Mora — who was not elected by the sector — as representative pro tempore, and on 9 November 2026 published executive decree 45985-MP, signed by Vice President Francisco Gamboa and by Chaves as Minister of the Presidency, rewriting the sectoral-election regulation mid-dispute: new file requirements for the electing assemblies and a mechanism extending prior representatives when a seat is unfilled (SURCOS, November 2026; Al Pulso, November 2026). On 13 November 2026 the CCSS board was again convened with Rivera in the union seat; the union movement characterizes the government's course as direct disobedience of two constitutional rulings, the University of Costa Rica's University Council formally requested compliance on 15 October, and the National Medical Union warned against converting the board's composition into a political contest (SURCOS, 13 November 2026; UCR Council, October 2026; Al Pulso, November 2026). The government's stated position — that it may verify a designee's qualifications — was considered and rejected by the Chamber, which held that verification is objective and formal and does not authorize substituting the government's assessment for the sector's choice (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). As of grading, Alfaro has not been sworn in. This is not rhetoric against a referee; it is a constitutional order unexecuted for a full quarter, with the rules amended to route around it.
  • The Constitutional Chamber itself was left short-handed for most of her tenure by her majority's handling of its substitute magistrates. The mandates of the Chamber's substitute (suplente) magistrates expired in December 2025; the Assembly failed to appoint successors, and by 14 July 2026 the Chamber reported 123 constitutional proceedings — covering healthcare, employment rights, social protection, and access to public information — unresolved because it could not assemble the necessary panels (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026, citing the Chamber). On 30 July 2026 the Chamber invalidated the legislature's return of the Supreme Court's candidate list, ordered the appointment process to resume, and provisionally extended the former substitutes' mandates itself. On 29 September 2026, ruling-party legislators voted to return the list again; on 5 October the Supreme Court produced a fresh list of 24 candidates for 12 substitute positions; the appointments remained unresolved at the end of October (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The failure began under the previous, opposition-led Assembly; from 1 May 2026 the majority that owns the delay is hers.
  • A ruling of the Constitutional Chamber was answered with a criminal complaint against the judges who issued it. On 31 July 2026, Fernández publicly accused four named Constitutional Chamber justices — Fernando Cruz Castro, Paul Rueda Leal, Ingrid Hess Herrera, and Jorge Araya García — of participating in what she called a judicial coup against the Legislative Assembly, for the ruling extending the substitutes' mandates. On 3 August 2026, ruling-party legislators, accompanied by Fernández, filed a criminal complaint alleging prevarication (knowingly unjust adjudication) against the four (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026, citing Teletica and La Nación). On 17 August 2026 the UN Special Rapporteur on the independence of judges and lawyers, Margaret Satterthwaite, expressed concern at reported attacks and pressures on judicial officials, noting that statements by senior public officials might contribute to a hostile environment; on 26 October 2026 the Costa Rican Association of the Judiciary (ACOJUD), CEJIL, and the Due Process of Law Foundation presented the pattern — public attacks on judges, appointment delays, financial pressure — to the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights; the Costa Rican state rejected the allegations (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). On 21 September 2026 ACOJUD informed the Supreme Court that three judges were on medical leave because of threats and pressures, attributing some of the pressures to the executive branch (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). Criminal judge William Serrano Baby disclosed on 15 October 2026 that he and his family had received death threats while he handled a high-profile extradition proceeding; he confirmed protection measures and stated the threats had not influenced his decisions (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The threats themselves are attributed to private and criminal actors, not to the executive; what is attributed to the executive is the public vilification that the UN Rapporteur and the judges' association identify as part of the environment.
  • The budget was used as an instrument of pressure on the judiciary, until the Constitutional Chamber struck the mechanism down. The executive's 2027 budget project (file 25.754, presented 1 September 2026) contained execution norms 9–12 conditioning ₡29,843.8 million — 5.6 per cent of the judiciary's budget — on requirements including audited financial statements, Comptroller certifications, and legislative-committee approval, alongside a direct reduction of ₡10,500 million; the project passed its first debate 31–23 on 5 November 2026 on PPSO votes (Corporación GC budget analysis, November 2026; NAI CR, November 2026). Fernández had publicly backed significant cuts to the judicial branch and accused the judiciary of infiltration by organized crime (Reuters interview, 4 September 2026). On 20 November 2026 the Constitutional Chamber, unanimously, declared the conditioning norms unconstitutional — they had been introduced without the constitutionally required consultation of the judiciary (sentences 45630 and 45631, via Al Pulso, November 2026). In the same Reuters interview, Fernández supported a legislative proposal to transfer the appointment of the Attorney General from the Supreme Court to the Legislative Assembly — the chamber her party controls; the proposal is not enacted at grading, and is recorded as an announced direction, not an accomplished change.
  • The pattern predates her inauguration by weeks and involves her chief minister in his prior office: on 29 January 2026 then-President Chaves publicly questioned the Constitutional Chamber justices' impartiality ahead of the frequencies ruling, warning they would keep hearing from him for years; on 22 April 2026 he publicly insulted Supreme Court President Orlando Aguirre Gómez as either a criminal or a fool, after Aguirre responded to the physical assault on Judge Kattia Carballo Chaves — a judge whose prisoner-rights rulings Chaves and his officials had publicly criticized, by name, through 2024–2025, and who was beaten outside her home on 17 April 2026 by a neighbor who had allegedly threatened her two days earlier; the suspect was arrested and held in pre-trial detention on attempted-homicide allegations, and the International Association of Judges condemned the threats and called for an independent investigation and protection (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The assault was a private individual's alleged crime; the documented executive contribution to the file is the prior public singling-out of the judge. On 29 April 2026 the judicial workers' union SITRAJUD filed a criminal complaint against Chaves over his repeated statements against Aguirre and other judicial authorities (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). On 5 August 2026, now as Fernández's minister, Chaves publicly disparaged the citizens preparing to march in defense of judicial independence as naïve, masochistic, or politically motivated (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026).

Why the grade lands here: The referees still stand — the prosecutor was unanimously renewed over her movement's objection, the electoral tribunal is untouched, the central bank is unpressured on this record, and the Constitutional Chamber rules against her government and is, in the budget file, being complied with through the Assembly's own machinery. That is the strongest for-shelf, and it is real. But the test asks whether independence is punished and control centralized, and on the judicial branch — the referee this government fights — the grip has not merely been attempted; in documented places it has succeeded for months at a time: a constitutional court's panels left unstaffable for most of her tenure by her majority's inaction and repetition, four justices criminally denounced by her legislators at her side for the content of a ruling, a social-security board seat held by an unelected substitute in defiance of two constitutional rulings while the election rules were rewritten by decree, and a budget mechanism conditioning the judiciary's money on legislative permission until the court annulled it. A holder can survive the referees and still fail this test by how she treats them; the treatment, on this record, is the point of the exercise. FAILS.

Helped: Her government's legislative program and its freedom of maneuver — a short-handed Constitutional Chamber reviews less, a CCSS board without the unions' chosen voice decides without that dissent, and a judiciary negotiating for its funds is a quieter counterweight. Hurt: Rocío Alfaro Molina, elected by the union movement on 9 June 2026 and still unseated in late November despite three rulings in her favor; the litigants behind the 123 constitutional proceedings stalled by July 2026 — healthcare, labor, social-protection, and information cases waiting on panels that could not sit; the four named justices subjected to a criminal complaint for a ruling; judges generally, three of whom were on medical leave by September over threats and pressures their association ties partly to the executive; and every citizen whose check on this government runs through a court the government is actively weakening.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down? And when another option became available, did she at least consider it openly?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • After months of confrontation, she opened a door to the judiciary in public and put concessions on the table. In a bilateral meeting with Supreme Court President Patricia Vargas (reported ~10 November 2026), Fernández agreed to restore structured institutional dialogue between the executive and the judiciary, invited Supreme Court magistrates to join the national Fuerza Élite security working group, and committed to a technical review of the restrictive budget-execution rules in the 2027 budget that the judiciary had warned could jeopardize its constitutional functions (TicosLand, November 2026). The meeting preceded a planned expanded summit of the supreme powers on 20 November. It is a late adjustment, and it is documented as hers.
  • The hardest option on her own menu was considered and declined, openly, with reasons. During the campaign Fernández had spoken of a state of exception to bring crime down quickly (Inter-American Dialogue expert commentary, February 2026). In office, with the Bukele model regionally available and her own prison modeled on it, she told Reuters on 4 September 2026 that she is not considering requesting a state of emergency or a suspension of constitutional guarantees, noting that homicides are declining (Reuters, 4 September 2026). Under this test's standard, an option considered and declined with stated reasons is not against-evidence; it is the clause working. In the same interview she welcomed the possibility of US ground operations against transnational crime while stating that nothing concrete was in preparation and that any such step would require legislative approval — a second hard option routed, on her own words, through the Assembly rather than around it (Reuters, 4 September 2026).
  • The street was answered, once, in the register the test asks for. After the 6 August 2026 nationwide demonstrations in defense of judicial independence — thousands marching in San José and at least eight other cities, with judicial employees, students, opposition legislators, former presidents, and Attorney General Díaz among the participants — Fernández on 7 August acknowledged the protest as an expression of democratic freedom, while defending her electoral mandate and maintaining her criticism of the Constitutional Chamber (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The acknowledgment is partial — the criticism stood — but the march itself proceeded peacefully and was not dispersed, and its legitimacy was conceded by the president it targeted.
  • The November budget rulings are, at grading, being answered through compliance machinery rather than defiance: after the Constitutional Chamber's 20 November 2026 sentences annulling the FEES cut and the judicial-funding conditions, Assembly President Yara Jiménez referred the texts to the Constitutional Consultations Committee with a deadline to eliminate the annulled provisions before the second debate, convened for Sunday 29 November 2026; if the corrections are not made, the norms are automatically excluded (Al Pulso, November 2026). PPSO floor leader Nogui Acosta stated his fraction would apply no changes until it received the rulings' full text (Al Pulso, November 2026). The final vote falls after this grading; the record here is a process moving toward compliance, recorded as in-motion, not as completed adjustment.

Evidence against:

  • The Constitutional Chamber's clearest order of her tenure was answered with escalation, twice. When the Chamber ruled on 30 July 2026 that the substitute-magistrate appointment process must resume, Fernández's response the next day was to accuse four of its justices of a judicial coup, and three days later her legislators filed a criminal complaint against them with her at their side (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). When another option — simply letting the Assembly appoint from the Court's list — remained available, her legislators voted on 29 September 2026 to return the list again (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The option was not considered and declined with reasons; it was refused, and the refusal was repeated after a court order.
  • The CCSS file shows the same motion in administrative form: two rulings (13 August and 24 September 2026) and a reaffirmation (6 October) ordering Alfaro's appointment were available doors; the government walked through a different one — an unelected substitute seated, then a decree of 5 November 2026 rewriting the election regulation so the seat could remain unfilled (SURCOS, November 2026; Al Pulso, November 2026). Whatever its legal theory, as an answer to pushback it is doubling down by rule-change, executed while the University of Costa Rica's governing council was formally requesting compliance (UCR University Council, 15 October 2026).
  • The first pushback of her presidency was answered with silence toward the press and, later, a court finding against the police account. After officers detained two university students at her 8 May 2026 inauguration for displaying a Palestinian flag, the Ministry of Public Security refused to answer La Nación's questions about the legal basis for the flag restriction, the officers involved, or the governing instructions (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026, citing La Nación). On 8 September 2026 the Constitutional Chamber declared the detention arbitrary and disproportionate, violating the students' liberty, equality, and expression rights, and found on video evidence that neither student had been violent or disruptive — contrary to the police reports — and that the restriction had been applied selectively, since other attendees displayed foreign flags, including Israeli flags, without intervention (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). No documented acknowledgment or correction by the government appears in the record assembled here.
  • Police answers to later pushback followed the same pattern. On 14 September 2026, during Independence Day celebrations in Cartago attended by Fernández, police restricted access to the central square, searched entrants, and removed journalists and protesters; the teachers' union APSE and a human-rights activist filed constitutional actions, the Ombudsperson's Office requested the operation's legal basis on 15 September, and by 18 September the Constitutional Chamber had received six challenges (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). La Nación reported on 29 September that an internal police operational plan had instructed eight intelligence officers to monitor groups critical of the government and identify potential agitators; the Ministry of Public Security maintained the monitoring was preventive security, not political surveillance — the characterization is contested between the newspaper's reporting and the ministry (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026, citing La Nación). On 26 May 2026, police detained UNA sociology student Elián Xavier Jiménez Campos at a protest against the electricity bill outside the Assembly; he was released after nearly 48 hours; student federations alleged pursuit and beating and questioned proportionality, while the ministry alleged he had damaged Assembly premises and injured an officer — the facts are contested between the parties (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026).
  • Chaves's context is the template she inherited and has not disowned: when the Constitutional Chamber struck four articles of his first Ley Jaguar bill on 29 July 2024 (resolution 2024-021375) and found further unconstitutional provisions in the second version that October, he adjusted the route — re-filing reduced versions — while publicly blaming the Comptroller General for the consequences and omitting the court's role; a September 2025 fact-check of his claim that Comptroller Marta Acosta had blocked the Limón marina by capricious interpretation found the claim false, the marina model having been declared unconstitutional by the Chamber itself (Doble Check, Radios UCR, September 2025). Route adjusted; ruling never accepted on its merits.

Why the grade lands here: Her two genuine adjustments are substantial — the November opening to the judiciary, with the budget rules she had defended put up for technical review, and the considered, reasoned refusal of the state-of-exception door her own campaign had left open. Against them stand the two files where pushback was answered by escalation rather than adjustment: a court order on judicial appointments met with a coup accusation and a criminal complaint, and a court order on a board seat met with a substitute and a rewritten rulebook. The wobble is answered where the counterparty is a president of another power speaking her language of security; it is refused where the counterparty is a ruling she dislikes. MIXED.

Helped: The judiciary as an institution, and the public that needs it functional, by the November dialogue restoration and the budget-rules review — if the thaw holds past the 29 November vote. Every Costa Rican whose guarantees were not suspended, by the state of exception considered and declined. Hurt: Aren Vindas Álvarez and Yoilyn Bermúdez Morales, the two students detained at the inauguration for a flag others were free to fly, whose account the Constitutional Chamber vindicated in September without a documented word of correction from the government; protesters and journalists screened at Cartago on 14 September; and the four justices for whom a ruling became a criminal file.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the minorities, the opposition's voters, the poor, the prisoners, and those who share the streets — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The violence that fell hardest on the poor is, on the official count, receding under the combined Chaves–Fernández security line she now directs. Homicides fell 14 per cent in the first half of 2026 against the same period of 2025 (judicial data via Reuters, September 2026); the early-2026 reductions were recorded in all seven provinces, including the historically hardest-hit coastal provinces of Limón (20 to 16) and Puntarenas (19 to 15) in the first six weeks (OIJ via TicosLand, February 2026). The Judicial Investigation Department (OIJ) recorded 707 homicides through 3 November 2026, projecting roughly 850 for the year against 873 in 2025 (OIJ via TicosLand, November 2026). The decline is modest against the record 907 of 2023, but its geography matters for this test: the count that improved first is the count in the places where the dying was concentrated.
  • The multidimensional count moved to its best reading in the series. INEC's ENAHO 2026 (released 27 October 2026) put multidimensional poverty at 8.9 per cent of households, down from 9.9 per cent in 2025 and the lowest in the survey's history, with rural multidimensional poverty falling 2.1 points (INEC ENAHO 2026 via El País CR and La Prensa, October 2026). The survey's fieldwork (July 2026) straddles the handover; the reading belongs to the country she now governs and to the trend she inherited and has not reversed.
  • Her mandate itself was assembled from the counted-out. Fernández won the coastal and rural cantons most heavily — the cantons with the lowest schooling and human-development indices and the highest homicide counts — and Chaves's election-night message to her, as reported, named the source of the victory: "Limón, Puntarenas, Guanacaste and the poor neighborhoods of San José gave you their vote" (El Mundo CR, 2 February 2026, via El Socialista Centroamericano analysis, 2026). The opposition's voters, for their part, retain functioning doors: their legislators' constitutional consultations stopped her budget's centerpiece provisions (Test 3), their marches fill the capital's squares un-dispersed, and Nicaraguan exiles commemorated the 2018 uprising publicly in San José on 19 April 2026, displaying photographs of the dead and demanding justice, without documented interference (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The income count deepened even as the headline rate held. ENAHO 2026 recorded 15.4 per cent of households in income poverty (298,713 households; 919,313 people, 16.9 per cent of the population), statistically unchanged from 15.2 per cent in 2025 — but average real household income fell 3.8 per cent, the poverty gap widened from 5.1 to 5.4 per cent, and poverty severity rose from 2.6 to 2.9 per cent: the poor are, on the official measure, poorer. Extreme poverty held at 3.8 per cent of households (73,830 households; 242,993 people). Rural poverty stood at 20.7 per cent against 13.5 per cent urban, and the Brunca region's rate jumped 3.5 points to 27.3 per cent, the country's highest (INEC ENAHO 2026, October 2026).
  • Her first budget distributed its cuts downward before the Constitutional Chamber intervened. The 2027 budget as approved in first debate (5 November 2026) reduced allocations not only to the judiciary but to the CCSS, to Cen-Cinai (the child-nutrition and care centers), and to the state housing bank BANHVI, and — by a motion of PPSO deputies in committee — cut ₡8,048 million from the FEES higher-education fund, money whose repayment to the public universities had been agreed between the executive and the universities in prior years (NAI CR, November 2026; Al Pulso, November 2026). Thousands of students, university staff, and union members had marched on 20 October 2026 against the proposed education cuts; on 26 October, UNA students protesting the same cuts reported being surrounded by more than 60 officers including riot units, with an officer warning that force would be used unless the road cleared in ten minutes — the Public Force's Heredia director stated police negotiated and the students withdrew voluntarily (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The FEES cut was annulled by the Constitutional Chamber on 20 November 2026 (Test 3); that it required annulment is the record for this test.
  • Indigenous land defenders remain outside the count of the protected. In February and March 2026 — the final months of Chaves's presidency, recorded as the inherited baseline — suspected arson attacks struck the recovered lands of Brörán defender Pablo Sibar Sibar in Térraba, who holds Inter-American Commission precautionary measures dating to 2015 and who reported an inadequate institutional response; the University of Costa Rica documented four fires on recovered Indigenous lands between January and March 2026 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). On 18 June 2026, under Fernández, members of the Maleku community reclaiming land at Viento Fresco de Guatuso were attacked by individuals they identified as non-Indigenous occupiers; a Maleku woman leader suffered a head wound; community members questioned the police response, alleging officers failed to prevent a second confrontation despite being present and that an officer pushed an elderly Indigenous man (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). No documented protective-policy change by her government appears in the record assembled here.
  • A minority viewpoint was counted out at her own inauguration, on the Constitutional Chamber's findings: the two students detained for a Palestinian flag while other foreign flags flew untouched (Test 3) — the Chamber held that hostile reactions from other spectators could not justify restricting peaceful expression and that the police duty ran the other way (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). Women survivors of sexual violence were counted down in her majority's Assembly: on 21 October 2026 the Women's Committee approved a substitute text of the rape-survivor care bill (23.633) removing emergency contraception from the expressly guaranteed minimum services, drawing a protest of hundreds outside the Assembly on 23 October (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The action is the legislature's, in a legislature her party leads; her personal position is not documented in the record assembled here, and the item is recorded at the weight the evidence carries.
  • The incarcerated are counted as a security input in the model she chose to import. The CACCO mega-prison (Test 5) replicates El Salvador's CECOT design — donated plans, mass high-containment capacity — a model whose Salvadoran original is documented by international monitors as a site of systematic due-process and conditions abuses under a state of exception. Costa Rica has not adopted the exception, and its courts remain open to prisoners' claims (it was a prisoner's-conditions judge, Carballo, whose rulings began the judiciary file); the import is of the architecture and the doctrine, not — on this record — of the exception regime. The distinction is recorded because it is the honest one, in both directions.

Why the grade lands here: Nobody in this file is counted out wholesale — the election that seated her was powered by the poorest cantons, the homicide decline is arriving first where the killing was worst, and multidimensional poverty sits at a record low. But the accounting has a consistent tilt: when her government's choices distribute costs, the documented costs land on university students, child-nutrition centers, social-security users, Indigenous communities awaiting protection that does not arrive, and two students whose flag was the wrong one — while the income of the average poor household fell and poverty deepened beneath a stable headline rate. Counted in the mandate; counted out in several of the distributions. MIXED.

Helped: Residents of Limón, Puntarenas, and the poor urban districts where homicide — the most unequal tax of all — is measurably falling; households lifted out of multidimensional poverty to a series-low 8.9 per cent; the coastal and rural electorate whose votes were courted, won, and are being answered with the security policy they chose. Hurt: The 919,313 people in income poverty whose poverty deepened on the official measures (gap 5.4 per cent, severity 2.9 per cent); Brunca's households, where poverty jumped to 27.3 per cent; public-university students and the Cen-Cinai and CCSS clienteles written into the first-debate budget at lower numbers until a court intervened; Maleku and Brörán land defenders, attacked and burned out under a protection regime that has not changed; and women survivors of rape, whose guaranteed-care bill lost its emergency-contraception provision in her majority's committee.

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 8 May 2026, with dates; her predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The mega-prison — promised for the first months, inaugurated in them. Fernández campaigned on a maximum-security prison modeled on El Salvador's CECOT and was expected to deliver it early (Inter-American Dialogue, February 2026). On 14 October 2026 she inaugurated the Centro de Alta Contención y Crimen Organizado (CACCO) at the La Reforma complex in Alajuela — capacity 5,100 inmates, cost approximately US$33–35 million, built on architectural plans donated by El Salvador — and used the ceremony to announce the designation of six Mexican cartels and three Colombian armed groups as terrorist organizations, without specifying the designations' legal formalization (AP via Conven, October 2026; InSight Crime, October 2026). The seam is shared credit: construction was ceremonially launched in January 2026, under Chaves, who had toured CECOT in December 2025. The promise was hers; the head start was his; the opening happened on her schedule.
  • The security machinery — built as announced. She chairs the Fuerza Élite task force, launched in May 2026, meeting weekly with the security and justice ministries to review homicide trends and deploy by crime heat maps (Latina Republic, May 2026). On 15 June 2026 she presented the promised six-bill organized-crime package — repeat offenders, criminal-organization membership, policing, and the penitentiary system among its subjects (Presidency via CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The bills' enactment is not documented in the record assembled here; the machinery and the package exist as pledged.
  • No new taxes and fiscal discipline — kept at grading. Fernández ruled out new taxes and committed to fighting evasion instead (pre-inauguration statements via Substack transition analysis, 2026), and her 2027 budget totals ₡12.4 trillion, roughly 3.2 per cent below the 2026 budget (NAI CR, November 2026). The consolidation she inherited held through her first budget cycle: central-government debt closed 2025 at 60.4 per cent of GDP, the fiscal deficit at 3.4 per cent of GDP, with the IMF's 2026 Article IV consultation judging the debt path sustainable while projecting a rise to 61.4 per cent in 2027 (IMF Country Report 26/116, 2026).
  • The mandate itself — governed as elected. Per the mandate rule in this dossier's Method, this belongs on the for-shelf: she ran on an iron fist against organized crime, continuity with Chaves's fiscal and institutional line, and alignment with the United States, and the first six months delivered exactly that platform — the prison, the task force, the terrorist designations, the openness to deeper US security cooperation (Reuters, September 2026), and Chaves himself in the two coordinating ministries. CIEP's polling records a public that knows what it elected: 84 per cent perceive the continuity in the person of her predecessor (CIEP-UCR, August 2026). Whether the platform's content helps or hurts is graded on the other axes and in the ledgers, not here; the keeping is documented.

Evidence against (promises not kept, stalled, or receding):

  • The homicide promise — direction kept, scale not. The campaign's central pledge was to break the record violence with an iron fist. The record at grading: a 14 per cent first-half decline and a projected ~850 homicides for 2026 against 873 in 2025 (OIJ via TicosLand, November 2026; Reuters, September 2026) — a real improvement she cites, accurately, as her reason for not needing a state of exception, and a figure that remains within roughly three per cent of the worst years in the country's history. The emergency she promised to end is, by her own November framing, ongoing. Not broken; nowhere near kept at the promised scale.
  • The Banco de Costa Rica sale — announced on day two, unpassed and short of the votes. On 3 February 2026, the day after her victory, Fernández announced she would present a bill to sell the Banco de Costa Rica and its subsidiary BICSA, earmarking the proceeds for the CCSS pension regime (IVM) and urging speed before the bank "breaks" (Semanario Universidad, February 2026). The sale requires 38 votes she does not have; an August 2026 Universidad Nacional poll put public support at 32 per cent, the lowest of her three flagship proposals; at grading the bill remains in legislative debate, blocked by the PLN–Frente Amplio alignment (UNA poll analysis, August 2026). Critics note the bank is profitable — ₡38,494 million in earnings in the last reported year — which contests her stated premise, though not her right to propose the sale (SURCOS, 2026).
  • The electricity opening — her flagship structural bill sank in her own Assembly. The harmonization bill (23.414) passed its first debate on 26 May 2026 and failed in November 2026 when a ruling-party deputy voted against her own bancada — headlined by Delfino's newsletter as the government voting against itself (Delfino Molomix, November 2026). A simple-majority bill, lost with a simple majority in hand.
  • The state of exception — the harder word, walked back. In campaign debates Fernández spoke of a state of exception to bring crime down quickly (Inter-American Dialogue, February 2026). On 4 September 2026 she stated she is not considering requesting one (Reuters). The walk-back is recorded here because Test 5 records the word; its consequences are recorded on the other axis in the Overall Summary, because they run in her favor: the non-event protected every Costa Rican's guarantees. Both entries stand, unmerged.
  • The "third republic" — the refounding word exists as pressure, not as enacted law. Fernández campaigned on a refounding that would include judicial-branch reform (Inter-American Dialogue, February 2026). At grading, its concrete instruments are the unenacted proposal to move the Attorney General's appointment to the Assembly, the budget conditions the Constitutional Chamber annulled, and the substitute-appointment blockade (Tests 2–3) — direction without delivery, recorded as such.
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: Chaves's completed record is the ground hers grows from, and it is two-sided in the same shape. Delivered: the extradition of nationals — constitutional reform in force 28 May 2025, first extraditions executed 20 March 2026 (Test 1); and fiscal consolidation in direction — the debt ratio fell 6.6 points between end-2021 and end-2025. Not delivered: the Ley Jaguar referendum, the flagship promise of his middle term — the Constitutional Chamber struck four articles of the first bill on 29 July 2024 and found further unconstitutional provisions in the second version that October, the TSE archived the signature-collection process in December 2024, and no referendum was ever held (TSE resolutions 5513-E9-2024 and 9068-E9-2024; Sala Constitucional resolutions 2024-021375 and 2024-28774). Overstated: his July 2026 claim, as finance minister, of an "unprecedented" debt reduction — Doble Check (Radios UCR, 16 July 2026) rated the superlative false: the ratio rose again in 2025 (58.9 to 60.4 per cent), the colón balance grew from ₡27.27 trillion (2021) to ₡31.30 trillion (2025), and the Arias Sánchez administration's 10.8-point reduction exceeds his 6.6. And the security promise on which his movement was built set its records before it bent: 907 homicides in 2023, the highest in the country's history, with the decline beginning only in his final quarter (InSight Crime country profile, 2026; OIJ via TicosLand, 2026).

Why the grade lands here: The visible, dated promises of her first year are landing — the prison opened on schedule, the task force sits weekly, the tax pledge holds, the budget shrank. The structural promises are not: the bank sale lacks its 38 votes, the electricity bill died by her own side's hand, the refounding has produced pressure rather than law, and the central promise — an end to the killing emergency — is being kept at three per cent of its scale. A six-month file with the flagship delivered and the architecture stalled is MIXED, with the homicide trajectory the open file that will move it.

Helped: The voters who chose the platform and are receiving it — the prison built, the designations announced, the fiscal line held; communities where the homicide decline is arriving, who were promised relief and are getting its first installment. Hurt: The public universities and the pension debate, promised a bank sale whose proceeds were pledged to the IVM and whose arithmetic has not survived contact with the Assembly; electricity consumers and producers awaiting an opening that passed one debate and sank in the second; and anyone who banked on the campaign's hardest word — rapid safety — which at current pace is a decade's errand, not a term's.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED.

Laura Fernández Delgado holds Costa Rica's presidency the way no one has held it in a generation — elected outright in the first round, with the first single-party Assembly majority since 1990, governing at 64 per cent approval — and she holds it in tandem: the predecessor she served sits in her cabinet with the presidency's and the treasury's portfolios, and 84 per cent of the country perceives his hand in her decisions. Graded on her six and a half months, with his completed tenure as the ground, the record is this. On one law (MIXED): the application of the law is strikingly even — her own prosecutor-by-inheritance holds 42 files on her finance minister, the electoral tribunal ruled against her mid-campaign, and a former Supreme Court magistrate was extradited under her movement's reform — while the enforcement stops at her government's center, where a formal accusation against Chaves waits, suspended, behind an immunity her appointment renewed and her party's 31 seats make permanent; helped: the public, by referees that still bite upward; hurt: the BCIE file's complainants and the credibility of a magistrate-renewal process whose only two exceptions were the two judges who had voted to shield him (motive alleged and denied). On gripping (FAILS): the referees stand — Díaz unanimously renewed, the TSE untouched, the central bank unpressured — and still the grade fails, because on the judicial branch the grip has succeeded in documented stretches: the Constitutional Chamber short-handed for most of her tenure with 123 proceedings stalled, four justices criminally denounced for a ruling with the president at the filers' side, the judiciary's funds conditioned on legislative permission until the court annulled the mechanism, and the CCSS's union seat held by an unelected substitute for a full quarter in defiance of two constitutional rulings while the election rules were rewritten by decree; helped: her program's freedom of maneuver; hurt: Rocío Alfaro Molina, still unseated; the stalled litigants; and every citizen whose check on power runs through those courts. On the wobble (MIXED): she adjusted where adjustment was offered as dialogue — the November thaw with Court President Vargas, the budget rules put up for review, the state of exception considered and declined with reasons — and doubled down where pushback arrived as a ruling she disliked, answering a court order with a coup accusation and a criminal complaint, and a seating order with a substitute and a new rulebook; helped: the institutions, if the thaw holds; hurt: the two inauguration students vindicated without acknowledgment, and the justices for whom judging became a criminal file. On counting (MIXED): the mandate came from the counted-out coasts and is being answered where they live — homicides falling first in Limón and Puntarenas, multidimensional poverty at a record-low 8.9 per cent — while the distributions tilt the other way: income poverty deepening beneath a stable rate (919,313 people; gap 5.4 per cent), Brunca at 27.3 per cent, a first-debate budget that cut universities, child nutrition, housing, and social security until a court struck the education and judiciary items, Indigenous defenders attacked without a changed protection regime, and a rape-survivor bill stripped of emergency contraception in her majority's committee; helped: the violent provinces' residents and the multidimensionally less poor; hurt: the deepening poor, the universities, and the unprotected. On her word (MIXED): the dated, visible promises are landing — CACCO inaugurated 14 October 2026, the Fuerza Élite sitting weekly, no new taxes, a smaller budget — and the structural ones are not: the BCR sale short of its 38 votes, the electricity bill sunk by her own bancada, the refounding unlegislated, and the killing emergency reduced by roughly three per cent against its promised end.

The two axes, stated plainly, because they do not launder each other. Kept her word and it helped: the security build-out as elected — the prison, the task force, the designations — delivered to the communities that voted for relief and are receiving its first installment in falling homicide counts. Kept her word and it still hurt people: the continuity platform itself, faithfully executed, includes the institutional offensive — the judicial budget pressure, the appointment blockade, the CCSS substitution — whose hurts are recorded in full in Tests 2 through 4; the keeping earns its Test 5 credit and does not reduce those ledgers by one line. Went against her word and it helped people: the state of exception, floated in the campaign as the fast door, was declined in office with stated reasons — a break with the harder word that protected every Costa Rican's constitutional guarantees, recorded here as help, and on Test 5 as a word not kept, both in full. Broke his word and it hurt people belongs to the context that seated her: Chaves's Ley Jaguar referendum, promised as the people's bypass, was never held — its articles struck down in 2024 — while the campaign against the Comptroller who had flagged its flaws continued on a premise the Constitutional Chamber had already ruled false.

One file in this record is graded under the no-good-choice clause, and it is the one she inherited rather than made: the cartel war. At her inauguration the options actually open were a suspension of guarantees on the Salvadoran model (a real and lasting harm to the constitutional order, available and popular), ordinary-law enforcement at the existing pace (a real and continuing harm in lives, roughly 870 a year), or the middle she chose — capacity built inside the constitution: the prison, the task force, the extraditions her predecessor's reform enabled, and no exception. She did not build the trap; she has been publicly honest that the emergency continues; and she took the least-harm door that was actually open. That choice is recorded as the best available given the situation in the moment, and it is not held against her. What remains gradable — and is graded above — is everything around that choice: the referees she pressures while fighting the cartels, the board seat she will not fill as ordered, and the distributions her budgets choose.

The open files that will move these grades are named so the record can grade them later: the 29 November 2026 budget vote and whether the annulled provisions stay annulled; whether Rocío Alfaro Molina is seated or the CCSS decree becomes the standing method; whether the substitute magistrates are appointed and the 123-case backlog clears; whether the criminal complaint against the four justices proceeds or is withdrawn; whether the Attorney-General appointment bill advances; and whether the homicide curve bends at the promised scale or settles at its current three per cent.
