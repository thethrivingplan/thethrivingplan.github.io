The Ruler's Test — Laʻauli Leuatea Schmidt, Prime Minister of Samoa

Subject: Laʻaulialemalietoa Leuatea Polataivao Fosi Schmidt ("Laʻauli Leuatea Schmidt") — Prime Minister of Samoa since 16 September 2025. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Samoa's Head of State, the O le Ao o le Malo — Tuimalealiʻifano Vaʻaletoʻa Sualauvi II, in office since 21 July 2017 and re-elected by the Legislative Assembly in 2022 for a second five-year term — is a ceremonial head of state who appoints the prime minister on the Assembly's majority and acts on ministerial advice, while executive power — the government, the budget, and the ministries — sits with the prime minister. Schmidt also holds the portfolios of Foreign Affairs and Trade and of Police and Prisons himself, alongside the premiership (Australian DFAT heads-of-government listing, 2026; Embassy of Japan, Apia, 30 September 2025). Premise correction, stated exactly: the brief's expectation of "recent political turbulence" is borne out, and the seat has changed hands. Fiamē Naomi Mataʻafa — prime minister from 24 May 2021, seated after the 2021 constitutional crisis — was expelled from the FAST party in January 2025 by a party process chaired by Schmidt (see below), led a minority government, survived two no-confidence motions (25 February and 6 March 2025), and called a snap election after parliament voted down her government's budget on 27 May 2025. At the election of 29 August 2025, Schmidt's FAST won 30 of 51 seats (36,708 votes, 40.86%), the HRPP won 14, Mataʻafa's new Samoa Uniting Party (SUP) won 3, and independents won 4; one independent then joined FAST, giving it 31. Schmidt was sworn in as Samoa's eighth prime minister on 16 September 2025, when the 18th Parliament first sat (Samoa Observer, 16 September 2025; Xinhua, 16 September 2025; Embassy of Japan, 30 September 2025). He is therefore graded on his own record: the road to the seat from January 2025, and roughly fourteen months in office to November 2026. Mataʻafa's completed tenure (24 May 2021 – 16 September 2025) is not graded here; she appears as context where the record Schmidt inherited, made, or campaigned against bears directly on a test. Schmidt, born 14 April 1966, is FAST's founder and chairman, a former Speaker and agriculture minister, and the MP for Gagaʻifomauga No. 3 on Savaiʻi — only the third prime minister from Savaiʻi (Samoa Observer, 16 September 2025). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic, printed verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure; the Test grades the holding, never the platform. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The law reached the very top of his own government, and he did not stop it. On 24 March 2026 the Electoral Court — Chief Justice Satiu Simativa Perese and Justice Leiataualesa Daryl Clarke presiding — found his Deputy Prime Minister, Toelupe Poumulinuku Onesemo, guilty of the corrupt practice of bribery (a third party acting for the candidate gave 150 tālā to two voters the night before the 2025 election) and, under Section 116 of the Electoral Act 2019, declared his election void (Samoa Global News via PACNEWS, 24 March 2026; Commonwealth Union, March 2026). Toelupe resigned from Cabinet on 30 March 2026, stating he did so "to protect the integrity of the government," while pursuing constitutional challenges to the ruling; the deputy premiership has stood vacant since (PINA, 31 March 2026; State Media Monitor 2026). The affidavits did not directly implicate Toelupe himself; the court held the candidate responsible for his agent's act — one law, applied upward, against the government's own number two.
  • The law reached Schmidt himself, before he held the seat, and ran its course while he held it. Charged on 3 January 2025 with ten criminal offences — including conspiring to pervert the course of justice, conspiracy to fabricate evidence, harassment by electronic means, and false statements harming reputations, arising from a smear campaign connected to the unresolved 2021 Vaitele hit-and-run death of a student — he pleaded not guilty, stood trial in the District Court alongside five co-accused, and on 27–29 March 2026 was acquitted of all charges by Senior District Court Judge Talasa Atoa-Saaga (RNZ Pacific, January 2025; Samoa Observer reporting via Nepituno, 29 March 2026). The same verdict convicted two co-accused: Samuelu Sua was found guilty of eight counts of defamation and Sivai Kepi of two counts of perverting the course of justice (Samoa Observer via Nepituno, 29 March 2026). A court that acquits the prime minister and convicts his associates in the same case is applying something other than favoritism. In September 2026 a further co-accused from the wider affair, Lise-Marie Schmidt, pleaded guilty to charges of misleading police (Samoa Global News reporting, September 2026).
  • The 2025 change of hands itself ran by the book after January's party rupture: two no-confidence motions were moved against Mataʻafa and lost on the floor (25 February and 6 March 2025); the budget defeat of 27 May 2025 (34–16, two abstentions) was answered not by decree but by dissolution and a general election, which the Electoral Commission ran and whose result — a FAST majority under new leadership — every party seated on 16 September 2025 (2025 election reporting, RNZ Pacific and Samoa Observer; Coconet, May 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The road to the seat ran through a process in which the law inside his own party cut one way. When Mataʻafa dismissed Schmidt from Cabinet on 10 January 2025 after he refused to resign over the criminal charges, Schmidt — as FAST chairman — presided over the party process that on 15 January 2025 expelled Mataʻafa, her deputy, and three other cabinet ministers from the party, and on 16 January elected himself leader. Mataʻafa and the expelled ministers denounced the expulsion as illegal and maintained they were still FAST members; the dispute was never resolved by a court before the election overtook it (RNZ Pacific and Samoa Observer, January 2025; Australian Institute of International Affairs analysis, 2025). The man who would not stand down when charged used the party rulebook to remove the woman who charged him — the expulsion is recorded as contested, with the disputants named; what is not contested is that the instrument was his.
  • In office, the investigative machinery of parliament has been aimed at one side only. In March 2026 Schmidt personally moved the motion establishing a special parliamentary committee to investigate the three most senior opposition figures — Opposition Leader Tuilaʻepa Saʻilele Malielegaoi, HRPP deputy leader Fonotoe Pierre Lauofo, and HRPP secretary Lealaʻilepule Rimoni Aiafi — on allegations of treason and defamation, over their roles in the 2021 constitutional crisis and more recent statements criticising the government (Talamua, 13 March 2026; Samoa Observer reporting, March 2026). Treason and defamation, as of grading, are ALLEGATIONS before that committee; no equivalent inquiry into any government figure appears in the record assembled here.
  • State money was conditioned on an opponent's removal in the rugby file: through early 2026 Schmidt withheld his signature from an Australian-funded regional rugby agreement while Tuilaʻepa remained chairman of Lakapi Samoa, stating the government's position would not change while the governance issue stood; Tuilaʻepa stepped down as chairman in March 2026 (Samoa Observer, 5 March 2026; RNZ Pacific reporting via Pacific Juice, March 2026). Whatever the merits of the governance complaint, the condition named a person, not a standard — and the person was his chief political opponent.

Why this grade: The strongest one-law evidence in this dossier is real: an Electoral Court voided his own deputy's seat, a District Court tried the sitting prime minister and acquitted him while convicting his associates, and nobody in the documented record stopped either process. That is the law running upward, and it earns the For shelf. But the same record shows the source being worked asymmetrically where Schmidt holds the instrument himself — a party expulsion he chaired against the prime minister who sacked him, a treason-and-defamation inquiry aimed only at the opposition's top three, and funding released only when his opponent vacated a chair. One law from the courts; a second, personal standard from the chair he sits in. That split is exactly what MIXED is for.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: voters in Falealili No. 1, whose election was re-run under law rather than left bought; the courts' standing, publicly vindicated at the government's expense; FAST's rank and file, who got the leadership question settled at a general election rather than in a locked room.
  • Hurt: Toelupe Poumulinuku Onesemo (named — the documented court finding establishes the wrongdoing the names rule requires: guilty of the corrupt practice of bribery, seat voided, resigned); Mataʻafa and the five expelled ministers, removed from their party by a process they dispute as illegal; the three HRPP leaders now under a treason inquiry that is, as of grading, allegation only.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the sporting and civic bodies keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts' autonomy is not a claim in this dossier; it is demonstrated against him. The Electoral Court voided his deputy prime minister's seat (24 March 2026); the District Court put the sitting prime minister himself on trial and acquitted him on the merits while convicting two co-accused (March 2026); the Electoral Commission ran the August 2025 snap election that his predecessor called and his party won. No documented act of interference with any of these appears in the record assembled here.
  • The 2021 referees still stand. The constitutional crisis of 2021 — in which the Head of State's proclamation purporting to prevent the Legislative Assembly from meeting was overturned, FAST was locked out of parliament and sworn in in a tent on 24 May 2021, and the Court of Appeal ruled on 23 July 2021 that Mataʻafa had been prime minister since 24 May — was resolved by courts against the then-incumbent, and Schmidt's own 2025 accession ran through the same machinery without challenge to its independence (2021 crisis record; Court of Appeal ruling, 23 July 2021).

Evidence against:

  • The media referee was punished, by name, within nine weeks of his taking office. On 17 November 2025, on his return from eight weeks of medical treatment in New Zealand, Schmidt announced the "temporary" suspension of the Samoa Observer — the country's only daily newspaper — from his press conferences, and directed that cabinet ministers would not respond to the paper's requests for information either (Prime Minister's press statement, 17 November 2025, published by the Ministry of the Prime Minister and Cabinet). The stated grounds were inaccurate and unfair reporting, including coverage of his medical absence; days earlier, Observer editor Shalveen Chand had alleged he was assaulted by the prime minister's security officers while seeking to confirm Schmidt's return, and had filed a police complaint — the assault is an ALLEGATION, contested, and no finding appears in the record assembled here (IPI statement, 24 November 2025; Pacific Freedom Forum via IFEX, 21 November 2025). The Observer wrote on 19 November 2025 offering corrections and clarification on the five points cited; it received no response (Global Voices / Advox, December 2025). The ban was still the operative fact in the 2026 cycle reporting (State Media Monitor 2026).
  • The measured consequence followed: Samoa fell 15 places in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index, to 59th of 180 (score 64.53, from 44th and 69.28 in 2025) — the largest fall in the Pacific and the second-largest globally — with RSF-linked reporting naming the Observer ban as a major factor (RSF 2026 Index; University of the South Pacific news, May 2026). PINA issued a statement of "deep concern" calling the ban "a serious threat to media freedom, public access to information, and democratic accountability" (PINA, November 2025). Schmidt rejected the criticism, saying the government held "documented evidence of unprofessional reporting and breach of media ethical standards" (Samoa Observer reporting, November–December 2025).
  • The prosecution-adjacent portfolios are held by the man the police investigated. Schmidt is his own Minister of Police and Prisons (DFAT listing, 2026) — the portfolio he retained on taking office in September 2025, while his own criminal case was still before the courts (it concluded with his acquittal in March 2026). No documented act of direction of his own case appears in the record; the structural fact stands on its own: for the first six months of his premiership, the minister responsible for police was a defendant in a police-brought prosecution alleging conspiracy to pervert the course of justice.
  • Parliament's autonomy narrowed around its critics. The March 2026 treason-and-defamation committee (Test 1) put the opposition's three senior figures under investigation partly for "statements criticising the government"; in October 2026 HRPP deputy leader Lauofo was suspended from parliament for 24 hours after criticising that process in an online interview — Lauofo disputes the suspension and has had his lawyer write to the Speaker contesting its correctness (Samoa Observer, 27 October 2026). And in sport, on 29 July 2026 the government withdrew all financial support and recognition from Lakapi Samoa, the internationally recognised rugby governing body, citing "unresolved internal issues and division," while a rival body, the Samoa Rugby Football Union, launched two days later (Rugby365 / AFP reporting, July 2026; Island City Media Group, 2026).

Why this grade: Test 2 is where this dossier's two shelves pull hardest apart, and the grade follows the weight of documented acts by the ruler's own hand. The courts demonstrably kept their autonomy — but nothing in the record shows Schmidt protecting that autonomy; it shows the courts exercising it over him, and him submitting because he had to. Where his own discretion ran, the pattern is grip: the country's only daily newspaper excluded from all ministerial access for its coverage of him; the police portfolio kept in his own hands while he was a criminal defendant; a parliamentary inquiry opened into critics for criticising; an opposition MP suspended after criticising the inquiry; a sporting body's recognition withdrawn. Independence was punished in the media, in the chamber, and in sport. That is a FAILS, with the courts' intact independence recorded on the For shelf as the limit of the failure, not as a mitigation he earned.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: no group is documented as helped by the gripping itself. The courts and the Electoral Commission helped Samoa generally by functioning independently — credited here as institutions, not as his act.
  • Hurt: the Samoa Observer's journalists and its readers, cut off from ministerial information; Samoan voters generally, whose press-freedom environment measurably worsened (RSF 2026); Lauofo and the HRPP's parliamentary voice; Lakapi Samoa, its players and staff, placed under a recognition withdrawal that put international participation at risk until October 2026.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, the ruler at least considered it openly; considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence — refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The rugby wobble was answered with negotiation, not force. After the 29 July 2026 withdrawal of recognition from Lakapi Samoa triggered warnings that Samoa could face exclusion from international rugby, and a public plea from Manu Samoa's captain and head coach for a swift resolution, Schmidt met World Rugby Chair Brett Robinson in Apia, and on 30 October 2026 all parties — World Rugby, the government, Lakapi Samoa, and the national Olympic committee — announced an agreed process: an independent, Samoan-led review group whose membership both sides agree, a World Rugby compliance review, Lakapi Samoa's self-suspension from the World Rugby Council during the review, and high-performance programmes transferred to the Olympic body's stewardship so national teams keep playing, with the 2027 World Cup campaign unaffected (World Rugby statement, 30 October 2026). Faced with a governing body he distrusted and a ban risk he could not accept, he took the negotiated door that was actually open.
  • Court pushback was accepted in the open, twice. He stood his own trial to verdict rather than obstructing it, and when the Electoral Court voided his deputy's seat, the government accepted the resignation and the by-election path rather than defying the court (March 2026 record, Test 1).
  • The inherited energy wobble was owned as a priority. Samoa's early-2025 energy crisis — blackouts and compensation for affected households and businesses — predates his government; on returning from medical treatment he named the power disruptions among his government's top priorities to fix, and the 2026/27 budget carries a $100 million energy-crisis response, including $44.2 million for renewable mix (Budget Address 2026/27; Samoa Observer reporting, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The press-freedom wobble was doubled down on, in full view. PINA, the Pacific Freedom Forum, IPI, and RSF all called publicly for the Observer ban to be lifted; the paper itself offered corrections and dialogue. The government's answer was a press-secretary statement insisting "this is not a ban on media freedom," citing the paper's conduct, and promising a formal review "in due course" — and the ban stood through the 2026 index cycle, costing Samoa 15 places (Pacific Freedom Forum / IPI / RSF, November 2025; PM's office statement via Samoa Observer, December 2025). If another option became available — the Observer's own offer of correction and clarification was exactly that — the documented record shows it was not answered, let alone considered openly.
  • The parliamentary wobble was answered by widening the instrument: when the opposition's senior figures criticised the government, the response was the March 2026 treason-and-defamation committee; when Lauofo criticised the committee's process in an interview, the response was his suspension (October 2026). Each pushback was met by reaching for the same handle again.
  • His own absence was a wobble handled by grievance rather than account. Eight of his first nine weeks in office were spent in New Zealand on medical treatment; the public learned the shape of that absence substantially through the Observer coverage he then banned the paper over, and his first press conference back was given to announcing the ban (PM's press statement, 17 November 2025; IPI, 24 November 2025). Opposition claims about dishonesty regarding his health and travel, made in HRPP letters of November 2026, are ALLEGATIONS by an interested party and are recorded as such, not as findings.

Why this grade: Two wobbles, two opposite answers. On rugby — where the counterparty was World Rugby and the cost of refusal was national exclusion — he adjusted openly and signed a compromise that gave him a review and gave the sport its future. On the press and in parliament — where the counterparty was domestic critics with no sanction to threaten — he denied, defended, and reached for the handle again. The no-good-choice clause does not apply here: in the Observer file a plainly open door existed (answer the paper's letter, use the media council and correction channels the watchdogs named), and it was not taken. The split between the international answer and the domestic answer is the record, and it grades MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: Samoa's rugby players, coaches, and supporters, whose international future was secured by the 30 October 2026 agreement; electricity consumers, named as a stated priority with funded response measures.
  • Hurt: the Samoa Observer and its readers, for whom the wobble produced a standing exclusion rather than an adjustment; Lauofo, suspended after criticising the process; public confidence in the government's account of the prime minister's own eight-week absence.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's voters, the other island, the non-matai, the press's readers, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The signature money is distributed by geography, not by loyalty. The District Development Programme — FAST's flagship, raised from $1 million to $2 million per constituency — is allocated to all 51 districts alike, government and opposition seats included, with district offices for all 51 districts scheduled to open from 12 January 2026 (Budget Address 2026/27; PM's New Year address, 1 January 2026). An opposition district's families draw from the same fund as a FAST district's.
  • The household measures are universal on their face. In February 2026 the government paid a $150 tālā per-citizen back-to-school assistance through the district programme (Budget Address 2026/27). The 2026/27 budget raises the pension for citizens aged 70+ from $300 to $500 (some 7,285 beneficiaries), creates a Child Wellbeing Benefit ($20 million, including a $200 baby bonus and $100 monthly for ages 0–35 months), increases NGO funding, doubles shelter financing to $2 million, and lifts medical consumables funding from $14 million to $21.5 million plus $12 million for overseas referrals (Government of Samoa budget presentation, October 2026; Budget Address 2026/27).
  • Savaiʻi — the big island long counted second to Upolu — holds the premiership itself for only the third time, and Schmidt was re-elected in his own Savaiʻi constituency in a landslide (Samoa Observer, 16 September 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The opposition's voters are counted in the money and counted out in the chamber. The HRPP won 36.78% of the popular vote in August 2025; its three most senior MPs — the men its voters sent to lead the opposition — are under a treason-and-defamation investigation moved by the prime minister himself, and its deputy leader was suspended for criticising that investigation (Tests 1–2). A third of the electorate's leadership is treated as a security question.
  • The rugby decision put a whole community's game at risk for a governance dispute. The July 2026 recognition withdrawal was answered by the national captain and coach pleading publicly for resolution, and by reporting that Samoa risked years of exclusion; the October agreement repaired the risk, but for three months the players — who hold no seat in the dispute — carried it (Island City Media Group, 2026; World Rugby, 30 October 2026).
  • The counted-out of longer standing remain counted out, unaddressed in his record: only matai (titled chiefs) may stand for the Legislative Assembly, so untitled Samoans — the majority of adults — can vote but cannot be voted for. That rule predates Schmidt by decades and is not his act; it is recorded here as the standing structure his government has not, in the record assembled here, moved on — an absence, not an act. Likewise, no documented step on press access for the Observer's readership — disproportionately the independent-press audience — appears beyond the ban itself.

Why this grade: On the distributive shelf this is the most inclusive record in the dossier: the district fund and the household benefits genuinely run to all 51 districts and all qualifying citizens, whoever they voted for, and under the mandate rule that delivery is credited, not discounted. But counting is not only money. The same government that pays every district alike investigates the opposition's entire senior leadership and suspends its deputy for speech. The HRPP's voters are counted at the pay window and discounted in the house they elected those leaders to. Both facts are documented; the grade is MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: families in all 51 districts (district funds, back-to-school assistance); pensioners aged 70+; mothers and children under three (Child Wellbeing Benefit); patients needing overseas referral; Savaiʻi, holding the premiership.
  • Hurt: HRPP voters (36.78% of the 2025 electorate), whose senior representatives face a treason inquiry and suspension; rugby players and supporters, who carried three months of exclusion risk; untitled (non-matai) Samoans, whose exclusion from candidacy continues unaddressed.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 16 September 2025, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes: a kept word that hurt people earns its Test 5 credit here and its hurt in the ledger in full; a broken word that helped people is recorded the same way in reverse.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The district fund — kept, at the promised figure. FAST's 2025 manifesto promised to double the constituency development grant from $1 million to $2 million tālā. The 2026/27 budget sets the District Development Programme at $2 million per constituency, $102 million in total across the 51 districts (FAST manifesto, launched 12 July 2025, via PINA; Budget Address 2026/27). Kept word, and it helped people — the first crossing: the money reaches opposition districts on the same terms as government ones (Test 4).
  • The back-to-school payment — delivered. The $150 tālā per-citizen back-to-school assistance was implemented in February 2026 through the district programme (Budget Address 2026/27).
  • Public-service pay — moved as promised. The FAST campaign committed to a civil-service pay rise; Cabinet approved it alongside the minimum-wage process, announced by Schmidt personally (Samoa Observer reporting via Fiji Global News, February 2026).
  • The $6 minimum wage — in motion, not yet kept. FAST promised a minimum wage of ST$6 per hour (from $4.84, itself reached in July 2025 under the previous government). Cabinet has approved consultations and a review with private-sector input; as of the February 2026 reporting the wage had not yet been raised (Samoa Observer via Fiji Global News, February 2026). Recorded as a promise in process — neither kept nor broken at grading.
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: Mataʻafa's completed tenure (2021–2025) carried FAST's original 2021 promises into government through a constitutional crisis, delivered the first $1-million-per-district programme from which Schmidt's doubling proceeds, and ended by the constitutional route — she advised dissolution after losing her budget vote rather than clinging to office, and conceded the seat on the election result. The seat Schmidt holds changed hands twice in five years by court ruling and by ballot, never by force; that continuity is the ground his own word stands on.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or not documented):

  • The tax promises — not documented as delivered. FAST's 2025 platform promised VAGST exemptions on essential goods (rice, sugar, baby formula among the items named in campaign reporting) and family cash top-ups for households earning under $20,000. In the record assembled here, neither measure appears as enacted in the 2025/26 budget (approved 28 October 2025, WST 1.24 billion) or the 2026/27 budget documents; they are recorded as not documented, not as disproven (FAST manifesto via PINA, August 2025; Budget Addresses 2025/26 and 2026/27).
  • The big-ticket platform — announced scale, no documented delivery. The manifesto's $1.5 billion carbon-credit market, a national stock exchange, and a $300 million injection into Samoa Airways appear in the record as campaign figures only; no implementing step is documented by November 2026 (FAST manifesto via PINA, August 2025). Fourteen months is early for projects of that size; they are recorded so the record can grade them later.
  • The "temporary" ban — a word with no date kept. The Observer suspension was announced on 17 November 2025 as "temporary," with a formal review promised "in due course." A year on, no lifting and no completed review is documented in the record assembled here (PM's statement, 17 November 2025; State Media Monitor 2026). This is the fourth crossing's neighbour: the word was soft, but it was a word — and the soft word has hurt people (Test 2's ledger) precisely by never expiring.
  • The charges he promised against his predecessor-but-one. In letters of 1 and 8 November 2026, Tuilaʻepa states that courts are still awaiting the corruption and defamation charges Schmidt promised against him from his premiership, and that he welcomes them to clear his name. Tuilaʻepa is an interested party and his characterisations are ALLEGATION-level; the narrow factual point — that promised charges have not been filed — is recorded here as his unrebutted public statement, not as a finding (HRPP letters, November 2026).

Why this grade: The delivered core is real and it is the part of the platform ordinary households feel: the doubled district fund at the exact promised figure, the back-to-school payment paid, the pay rise moved, the pension increase budgeted. Against that, the tax relief that headlined the cost-of-living pitch is not documented as enacted, the platform's largest numbers remain campaign numbers, and the one promise made to a critic — that the newspaper's exclusion was temporary — has run a year without expiring. The two axes cross in both directions in this file: the district-fund promise was kept and helped everyone alike (crossing one); the treason-inquiry commitment to his own base, pursued as promised to them, is recorded in Tests 1–2's ledgers as hurting the opposition's voters (crossing two — the keeping earns no discount on the hurt). Nothing in the record supports crossing three (a broken word that helped) or crossing four (a broken word that hurt) as documented fact; both are recorded as not documented rather than invented.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: district communities and school families (kept promises, delivered); civil servants (pay rise in motion); pensioners (budgeted increase).
  • Hurt: households still waiting on the promised essentials tax relief amid cost-of-living pressure — hurt by delay, on the record as not-yet-delivered rather than refused; the Samoa Observer, held under a "temporary" exclusion with no documented end.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Laʻauli Leuatea Schmidt came to the premiership by the most contested road in the series' recent files — charged with criminal offences in January 2025, sacked from cabinet for refusing to resign, chairing the party process that expelled the prime minister who sacked him, then winning the argument the only way that settles it, at a general election on 29 August 2025 — and the dossier grades the fourteen months since 16 September 2025 on that record. One law (MIXED): the courts ran one law upward through his own government — his deputy's seat voided for bribery, himself tried and acquitted while his co-accused were convicted — but where the instrument was his own hand, it cut one way: a party expulsion he chaired, a treason inquiry aimed only at the opposition's top three, rugby money conditioned on his opponent's chair; helped: Falealili No. 1's voters and the courts' standing; hurt: his deputy, the expelled FAST ministers, and the three HRPP leaders under inquiry. Without gripping (FAILS): the country's only daily newspaper banned from all ministerial access within nine weeks and still banned a year later, a 15-place fall in the RSF index, the police portfolio held by a sitting criminal defendant for six months, critics investigated and suspended in the chamber — against which stands only the courts' independence, which the record shows him submitting to, not protecting; hurt: the Observer, its readers, and Samoa's measured press freedom. The wobble (MIXED): he negotiated openly when World Rugby pushed back and signed the 30 October 2026 agreement that saved Samoa's international rugby, and accepted adverse court rulings — but doubled down on every domestic critic, refusing even to answer the Observer's offer of correction; helped: the rugby community; hurt: the paper, Lauofo, and public trust in his account of his own absence. Counts everyone (MIXED): the money genuinely counts everyone — $2 million to each of the 51 districts, universal child, pension, and school payments — while the politics counts the opposition's 36.78% of voters out of the chamber's protection; helped: every district's families, pensioners, and young children; hurt: HRPP voters, rugby players during the standoff, and the untitled majority still barred from candidacy. His word (MIXED): the household core of the platform was delivered at the promised figures, the $6 wage is in process, the essentials tax relief is not documented as enacted, the platform's biggest numbers remain campaign numbers, and the "temporary" press ban has run a year; helped: districts, school families, civil servants; hurt: households waiting on tax relief and a newspaper held under an unexpired "temporary." The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the Observer ban is ever lifted, whether the treason committee's allegations are ever proven or withdrawn, whether the $6 wage and the VAGST exemptions are enacted, and whether the February 2027 proceedings reported against him proceed and how they end.
