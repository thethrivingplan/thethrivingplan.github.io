The Seven Maybes

In 2024, a search of roughly five million stars kept exactly seven — each an ordinary-looking red dwarf with more infrared heat than the star alone could explain, fitted as a partial Dyson sphere: a swarm of collectors turning starlight into waste heat. This page follows the goal you set after the video: figuring out which one of those seven is the one — and it starts by being precise about what “the one” means.

The honest position, up front

As of 23 November 2026, none of the seven still stands as a Dyson-sphere candidate in any positive sense. That is not the same as “all seven are formally disproved.” Every candidate that has been looked at with an instrument sharp enough to separate a background source ~1–5″ from the star has turned out to be a background galaxy. No follow-up has found on-star excess, youth, or anything at the star itself that requires a Dyson sphere, an extreme debris disk, or new astrophysics. Where spectra exist, the stars are ordinary main-sequence M dwarfs.

So “which one is the one” cannot honestly be answered as a winner. It can be answered as a map: where the independent lines of evidence meet on each star, where they don’t, and which single test would settle each one. That map is this page.

What it is: a candidate only exists where three surveys meet

Suazo et al. (2024), Project Hephaistos II, MNRAS 531:695, ran a pipeline over ~5 million objects within 300 pc. The claim was explicitly candidates deserving further analysis, not detections: seven M dwarfs “for which astrophysical phenomena cannot easily account for the observed infrared excess.” The paper itself estimated ~2 contaminated sources were expected in its sample, and flagged candidate G’s offset as “suspicious.”

Foundation

Gaia DR3

Fixes where the stars actually are — positions, distances, proper motions. Every later test works by propagating a Gaia position to another instrument’s epoch and asking whether the heat sits on that spot.

Middle

2MASS

The near-infrared bridge between Gaia’s visible light and WISE’s heat. A real sphere’s star should look like one continuous object across this band; a background galaxy starts to pull away here.

Burn

WISE

The heat eye. A Dyson sphere is not found by its light but by its waste heat in the mid-infrared (W3/W4 bands). All seven candidates live or die in these two bands — and WISE’s beam is wide, 6.5–12″, which is exactly where the trouble hides.

How five million became seven

~5,000,000 objects→ ~320,000 with W3/W4 detections→ CNN rejects WISE confusion & nebulosity→ Variability, astrometry, extended-source cuts→ 368 visual-inspection survivors→ 7 candidates

The fit for each survivor is a star plus a partial-sphere blackbody: γ (gamma) is the covering fraction — the share of the star’s light the sphere intercepts, LDS/L★ — and TDS is the waste-heat temperature. Across the seven, the fits ran TDS ≈ 100–275 K and γ ≈ 0.03–0.16. Low variability and clean astrometry in the original analysis argued against youth or binarity, and ordinary warm debris disks were argued implausible: typical disks are colder (10–100 K) with fractional luminosity f < 0.01, and extreme disks (f > 0.01) had never been seen around M dwarfs.

The hub: pick a letter, see where the evidence meets

“The one” here is a system number, not a ranking. A, B, C … G are seats in one system — and a candidate only ever existed at the meeting point of Gaia, 2MASS, and WISE together. No single survey could name one; remove any piece and there is no candidate at all. Tap a letter to open its full evidence file. The centre of the diagram is that meeting point — the weave itself.

What it means: the convergence map

Each line of evidence below is an independent piece. Where the pieces agree, a candidate is settled. Where a piece is silent, that silence has a reason — usually a resolution floor. The pattern in one sentence: every diagnostic that can see a contaminant has found one; the candidates differ only in whether the sharp test has been pointed at them yet.

Line 1 · Archival radio cross-match

Catches G, B, and weakly A — silent for C, D, E, F

Radio surveys (RACS, VLASS, FIRST, NVSS, TGSS) find counterparts near G in many surveys, B at multiple frequencies, and A in one survey. C, D, E, and F have no counterpart — but radio only catches the radio-loud minority of hot DOGs and AGN, so a non-detection is nearly uninformative.

Line 2 · High-resolution radio interferometry (VLBI)

Definitive for G — never applied to A or B

The one time it was used (e-MERLIN + EVN on G, June 2024), it was decisive: three compact components, brightness temperature >10⁸ K, nothing at the star. A and B are the obvious untested targets.

Line 3 · WISE centroid astrometry vs. Gaia

Converges for B, C, and (in retrospect) G — failed for D

For B and C, the W3/W4 centroids sit on the interloper, not the star. But D passed this test cleanly — smallest offsets of any candidate — and JWST still found a galaxy ~1″ away. A ~1″ contaminant inside a 6.5–12″ WISE beam can shift centroids by less than 1″ and pass. A “no offset” result is not evidence of no contaminant.

Line 4 · Deep optical / near-IR imaging

Finds G (~5″), C (3.59″), B — misses D and E entirely

Archival imaging (DESI Legacy, VHS, VMC, UKIDSS) resolves interlopers at ≳3″. The JWST galaxies behind D and E sit at ~1″ — invisible or unrecognised in these data. For F, imaging actively disfavours the usual geometry: the nearest galaxy-like source is >5″ away, judged too distant to affect the mid-IR photometry.

Line 5 · Optical spectroscopy

Kills the young-star branch — cannot separate sphere from galaxy

Every tested star (A and D among the seven, in Hephaistos III) is a normal main-sequence M dwarf with no Hα emission — not young, not accreting. That rules against a young-disk explanation, but a normal star is expected in both the Dyson and the background-galaxy scenarios. B, C, E, F, and G were not spectroscopy targets in that paper.

Line 6 · JWST / MIRI imaging + spectroscopy

Definitive for D and E — never applied to A, B, C, or F

Both times JWST looked (Cycle 4 GO 7199, 2025), it found a galaxy below the resolution floor of every archival test: D’s at z ≈ 0.9, consistent with a hot DOG; E’s at z ≈ 0.4, a dusty starburst. That is the strongest inductive reason to expect the remaining four to resolve the same way — and it is still an induction, not a measurement, for those four.

Line 7 · Population statistics

Explains the ensemble — classifies none of them

Blain (2024) and the corrected Ren et al. counts agree that a handful of chance Gaia–WISE hot DOG alignments is expected in a 5-million-star search (~1.4 expected within 6″ of any of the 5 million stars; ~5.6 within 12″, the W4 beam). Statistics explain why seven false positives are unsurprising; they say nothing about any individual star.

What would settle each one

For every unresolved candidate, the settling dataset is the same in kind: spatially resolved mid-IR photometry or spectroscopy that separates star from field at ≲1″, followed by a re-fit of the deblended stellar SED. If the deblended star is photospheric, the candidate is closed. Only a deblended, on-star, blackbody-like excess would be a surviving candidate — and no candidate has yet produced one.

How the seven were checked, in order

2024 · Peer-reviewed

Suazo et al. — the seven are published as candidates

MNRAS 531:695. Candidates deserving further analysis, with background-galaxy alignment already discussed as a risk.

2024 · Peer-reviewed

Ren, Garrett & Siemion — radio counterparts near A, B, G

Research Notes of the AAS 8:145. Cross-matches against VLASS, RACS, FIRST, NVSS, TGSS propose dust-obscured galaxies contaminating W3/W4. (A 2026 erratum corrects the hot DOG surface-density conversion; the authors maintain the contamination conclusion.)

2024 · Preprint

Blain — the population can account for the count

Statistical only: at ~0.1 hot DOGs per square degree and a 5–6″ matching radius, a handful of chance alignments is expected in a 5-million-star search. No Dyson spheres needed to explain the number seven.

2025 · Peer-reviewed

Ren, Garrett & Siemion — G resolved as a radio-loud AGN

MNRAS Letters 538:L56–L61. e-MERLIN and EVN resolve G’s source into three compact components with brightness temperature >10⁸ K. No radio emission at the M dwarf’s position — also the only SETI-type radio statement in this literature: no evidence of broadband radio leakage from that system.

July 2026 · Preprint, submitted to MNRAS — not yet peer-reviewed

Korn et al. (Hephaistos III) — the stars themselves are ordinary

Re-derived parameters for A–G show no irregularities vs. the main-sequence M-dwarf population. New optical spectra for A and D (among the seven): normal M dwarfs, no Hα, not pre-main-sequence. The authors state they “still find no clear explanation” from photometry and spectroscopy alone, and nominate JWST and/or ALMA as the instruments that could decide.

July 2026 · Preprint, submitted to MNRAS — not yet peer-reviewed

Zackrisson et al. (Hephaistos IV) — JWST resolves D and E

MIRI imaging and MRS spectroscopy from Cycle 4 GO 7199 (observed 28 July and 9 September 2025), plus SPHEREx spectra: both excesses are background galaxies ~1″ away, with no mid-IR excess associated with either star itself.

2026 · Accepted by MNRAS

Ren et al. — archival diagnostics for nine candidates

Two independent methods (epoch-propagated WISE centroids vs. control samples; deep archival imaging) across A–F plus three extra low-S/N objects, with G excluded as already solved. Verdicts: significant contamination for B and C; suggestive for A; marginal for E and F; no obvious contamination for D — a verdict JWST then superseded for D and E, which is the cautionary tale of this whole story.

2026 · Hypothesis only

Mullan — could M-dwarf flares add infrared emission?

Research Notes of the AAS 10:162 proposes optically thin free–free flare continuum as a complication for all seven. No new observations, and no per-candidate test exists; the JWST resolution of D, E, and G into separate galaxies makes a flare origin unnecessary for those three. Treat as an untested generic alternative, not evidence about any individual candidate.

Open questions — marked plainly

  • F’s fitted parameters disagree between sources. Suazo et al. Table 5 gives TDS = 137 ± 16 K, γ = 0.03 ± 0.008. Korn et al. (Hephaistos III) Table 1, purporting to reproduce Suazo, lists 176 ± 21 K and γ = 0.167 ± 0.032 — essentially D’s values. This page uses Suazo’s as authoritative; the Korn reprint appears to be a transcription error. Do not quote F’s parameters from Hephaistos III without checking.
  • Hephaistos III and IV are preprints. Both are July 2026 arXiv papers submitted to MNRAS, not yet peer-reviewed as of this writing. The JWST result is direct imaging/spectroscopy whose interpretation is straightforward, but its formal status is a preprint. Ren et al. 2026 (archival diagnostics) is stated as accepted by MNRAS.
  • An uncorroborated challenge exists. An October 2026 PhilArchive preprint (Pemberton, with AI contributors disclosed) claims a ~16-year coordinate-epoch inconsistency in Ren et al. 2026’s printed separations. No author response was found. It does not affect the radio positions, the B/C imaging interlopers, or the VLBI/JWST resolutions — and the standings on this page do not rest on Ren et al.’s significance values alone. Flagged here, not relied on.
  • Absence means “not found,” not “never observed.” No published narrowband radio-SETI (e.g. Breakthrough Listen), ALMA, JWST, or VLBI observations of A, B, C, or F were found in the indexed literature as of 23 Nov 2026. Proprietary-period data would not appear in such a search.
  • Flux bookkeeping loose ends remain for D and E — MIRI synthetic photometry vs. WISE differs by ~4σ in W4 for E and ~2σ in W3 for both. The cause is unclear (aperture / extended-source / background-subtraction effects suspected). This affects flux bookkeeping, not the spatial resolution of the galaxies, and cannot restore a Dyson interpretation.
  • No per-candidate spectral subtype is asserted. The literature characterises all seven as M dwarfs with the Teff values shown; no source read assigns a definitive subtype (M2 vs. M4, etc.) to each, so none is printed here.

The one is the hub — not the winner

The “one” does not mean better than the others. It is not a ranking, and it is not a prize one of the seven wins. The one is what all seven can access — or what can access all seven on its own. When a single hub exists that every piece connects through, in either direction, that is the one — because every piece is needed for it to work. Take one piece away and the hub holds nothing; the hub alone, without the pieces, is just an empty meeting point.

Applied honestly to this case, that principle gives a clear answer — and it is not a star. The seven candidates are seven separate stars, hundreds of light-years apart (142.9 pc to 274.7 pc from us, in different parts of the sky). They share no physical hub. No collector swarm links them; nothing physical passes between J1245−2652 and J0513−2511. Picking one of them as “the one” would be inventing a connection the evidence does not carry.

What does touch all seven today, in both directions, is the weave that found them. Gaia, 2MASS, and WISE together reach every one of the seven — and every one of the seven only exists as a candidate through that weave. It works in the other direction too: the seven, read together, are what revealed the weave’s own limit — a ~1″ background galaxy hides inside a 6.5–12″ beam, below every blunt test, and only the sharp instruments see it. And for most of them, the seven also share the same failure mode behind them: a distant, dust-obscured galaxy whose heat was mistaken, for a while, for a sphere. That shared pattern is a fact about our instruments and our sky, printed here as exactly that — not as a hidden link between the stars.

So the search for “the one” lands where it lands: the hub of this system was never one of the seven seats. It is the meeting point itself — three surveys, seven maybes, and the follow-up instruments that joined the weave afterward — and it only works because every piece is in it.

What could this mean for me?

It could mean that when something in your own work looks like a single standout — one signal, one candidate, one “one” — it is worth asking first whether it is a seat or a hub: whether it stands alone, or whether it only exists where several independent pieces meet. In this story, everything durable came from the meeting points: a candidate existed only where three surveys agreed, and it fell only where a sharper instrument joined them.

It could also mean the reverse is worth sitting with: if the pieces don’t actually connect — if they are hundreds of light-years apart, sharing nothing but the instrument that noticed them — then naming a winner could be forcing a pattern the evidence hasn’t offered. Letting it land as seven maybes, each with its own file and its own settling test, is not giving up on the question. It is the answer the record currently holds.

That is a possibility to weigh, not a verdict about you. The deciding stays with the reader.
