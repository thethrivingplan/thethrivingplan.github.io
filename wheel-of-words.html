How to play

  1. Spin. Tap Spin. Money wedge: you may guess a consonant. Bankrupt: your round score drops to zero and your turn ends. Lose a Turn: your turn ends, score kept.
  2. Guess a consonant. Every time it appears, you earn the wedge value again — an 800 wedge with the letter showing 3 times pays 2,400. A miss ends your turn.
  3. Buy a vowel. A E I O U cost a flat 250 (or your variant price) from your round score, any time on your turn, no spin needed. A vowel that isn't there still costs the price — and ends your turn.
  4. Solve. Type the whole phrase. Right: you bank your round score and the round ends. Wrong: your turn ends.
  5. Rounds & bonus. After the set number of rounds, the highest total goes to the bonus round: the gift letters are revealed free, you pick 3 more consonants and 1 vowel, then solve against the clock for a 5,000 bonus.

Strategy — more than one way to play, with the trade-offs printed

1. Letter frequency — spin for the common letters first

In ordinary English phrases, the most common consonants are roughly T, N, S, R, L, H, D, C, M, P… and the most common letter of all is E. Guessing common letters early finds more letters per spin. Trade-off: common letters are also what everyone else guesses — in a bot game, a letter you leave may be taken on the next turn, and its earnings go to them.

2. Why the bonus gift set is R S T L N E

Those six are gifted because, across big counts of English text, they are among the very most frequent letters — R, S, T, L, N are top consonants and E is the top vowel. Revealing them free typically uncovers a third or more of a phrase. Trade-off of any fixed gift: it helps most on ordinary phrases and least on phrases built from rare letters — which is exactly why your own bonus picks should target what the board shape suggests, not just the next-most-common letters.

3. Vowel-buying judgment — buy when the vowel unlocks more than its price

A vowel costs 250 flat. Buy when knowing it plausibly unlocks a solve or several consonant spins — early, with a board full of blanks and several words that could hinge on one vowel, 250 is cheap information. Trade-off: late in a round, if you can already nearly read the phrase, 250 buys nothing; and a vowel that misses still costs 250 and your turn. Rule of thumb the Coach uses: buy when at least 3 hidden letters could turn on that vowel, or when one specific word is blocking your solve.

4. Spin-vs-solve judgment — solve when the risk outweighs the letters left

Every spin carries about a 2-in-9 chance of Bankrupt or Lose a Turn (4 of 18 wedges), and Bankrupt wipes the round score you've built. The Coach's math: expected gain from one more spin ≈ (average money wedge ≈ 589) × (letters your best remaining consonant would likely hit) × 7/9, weighed against an 11% chance of losing your whole round score. Trade-off: solving early banks a smaller score safely; spinning on grows the score but risks it all — the bigger your round score and the fewer letters left, the stronger the case to solve. If you can read the phrase, solving is almost always right: an unsolved board pays nothing if someone else solves first.

5. Endgame math

Only the solver banks a round score — everyone else's round score evaporates. So a round score is not money until you solve. Near the end of a round, count what's left: if the hidden letters are worth less in expectation than the Bankrupt risk to your current score, solve. In the final round, totals matter, not round scores: if you're behind, spin aggressively (you need the solve and the points); if you're ahead and it's your solve to take, take it and close the game.

The bots — what each level really does, printed plain

LevelHow it actually plays (this is the code's logic, not a story)
LearningPicks a random consonant from a small common set (T N S R L D M C P H) it hasn't tried, and on about 1 turn in 5 buys the most common missing vowel instead — no judgment about price or board either way. Never solves unless the board is fully revealed. It is beatable on purpose — a first opponent.
SteadyGuesses consonants in fixed frequency order (T N S R L H D C M P F B G Y W K V X Z Q J), buys the most frequent missing vowel on about a third of its turns when it can afford it, and solves only when every letter is revealed. No board reading.
SharpFrequency order, plus board reading: it checks the phrase bank for phrases that still fit the revealed pattern and untried letters, prefers letters that appear in the most fitting phrases, and solves when exactly one bank phrase fits.
ProEverything Sharp does, plus solve judgment: it solves when the fitting-phrase count is one or when its round score is large and few letters remain (Bankrupt-risk rule from the Strategy section), and it buys a vowel only when that vowel appears in at least half the fitting phrases.

How this was checked: the four levels are four separate letter-choosing functions in this page's code, and the descriptions above were written from those functions line by line. Sharp and Pro genuinely search the same phrase bank you play from, filtered by the visible board — they never see the answer directly; if no bank phrase fits (it can't happen in normal play, since the answer is in the bank), they fall back to frequency order. Bots spin the same wheel with the same Bankrupt odds as you.
