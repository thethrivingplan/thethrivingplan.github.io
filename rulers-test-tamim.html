The Ruler's Test — Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani, Emir of Qatar

Subject: Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani — Emir of Qatar since 25 June 2013, when his father, Emir Hamad bin Khalifa Al Thani, voluntarily relinquished power, a move the Congressional Research Service noted departed from the Gulf pattern of rulers remaining in power for life (CRS, Qatar: Governance, Security, and U.S. Policy, updated 7 April 2021). The seat graded is the emirate, and the reason is stated plainly: under Qatar's constitution the Emir holds executive authority, has the final say on legislation after consulting the Shura Council, appoints the prime minister and approves the cabinet, and is commander-in-chief (Lloyds Bank / Bank of Scotland political framework, updated November 2026; Oxford Business Group, Qatar 2026). The Prime Minister, Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani — in office since 7 March 2023 and simultaneously Minister of Foreign Affairs — is head of government in title, but his authority is entirely derivative: the Emir appoints, dismisses, and accepts the resignation of the prime minister and all ministers, and the prime minister acts as supervisor and operational manager implementing the Emir's vision (analyst consensus summarized in the prime-ministerial record; CRS, 2021). The prime minister therefore appears in this dossier as the Emir's chief operator — above all as the face of Qatar's mediation diplomacy — not as the seat graded. Tamim's mandate is hereditary succession, not election: there is no platform the voters elected, so the mandate rule is applied in its adapted form — the Test grades the holding, and his word is measured against the constitution he swore to, his own public pledges, and the state's announced programs (see Test 5). He is graded on thirteen years in office, with particular weight on 2025–2026: the Israeli strike on Doha of 9 September 2025, the October 2025 Gaza ceasefire Qatar co-mediated, the February–March 2026 Iran war in which Qatar itself was struck, and Qatar's continuing mediation through November 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The Ethic: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The law reached the top of his own finance establishment. Ali Sherif al-Emadi, finance minister from 2013 and chairman of Qatar Airways, was arrested in May 2021, removed as minister, and on 10 January 2024 a criminal court sentenced him to 20 years in prison for laundering more than USD 5.6 billion, bribery, abuse of position and power, and damage to public funds, with fines exceeding QAR 61 billion (~USD 16.7 billion); a co-defendant from the ruling family itself, Sheikh Nawaf bin Jassim bin Jabor Al Thani, was sentenced in the same case to six years for misuse of public funds. Both could appeal (Reuters court-document reporting, January 2024; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025). Prosecutions of figures at this level were, as contemporaneous reporting noted, previously unheard of in Qatar.
  • The headline labor reforms were written as universal rules, not citizen-only rules. In 2020 Qatar removed the No-Objection Certificate requirement for changing employers and abolished exit permits for most workers, and in 2021 it introduced what the ILO framework describes as the Gulf's first non-discriminatory minimum wage — QAR 1,000 per month base, applying to workers of all nationalities (ILO and Qatari reform record via HRW, World Report 2025; reform summaries, 2024–2025). The Wage Protection System requires salaries to be paid electronically, with fines for violations.
  • The 2024 constitutional amendments removed one formal second-class marker: the requirement of "original" Qatari nationality for Shura Council membership and ministerial office was lifted, so naturalized citizens became eligible for appointment — presented by the state, in its November 2025 follow-up submission to the UN Human Rights Committee, as resolving differences and strengthening equal citizenship (State of Qatar submission, CCPR/C/QAT/FCO/1, received 4 November 2025; International IDEA, November 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The legal order itself is tiered at the source — that is the finding, not an enforcement footnote. Freedom House's summary is structural: most of the population consists of noncitizens with no political rights and few civil liberties, while citizens are among the wealthiest people in the world (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025). Human Rights Watch puts migrant workers at over 91 percent of the population (HRW, World Report 2025 and World Report 2026); a separate 2026 relocation profile puts expatriates at ~88 percent of a ~3.17 million population — the figures are printed separately, as sourced, and on either count the large majority of people living under the law are outside its full protection.
  • The tiering is codified in the labor system. HRW's 2026 report states that migrant workers "continue to face widespread abuse under the country's restrictive kafala system," that reforms have had "limited impact," and that workers still face wage theft, unexplained deaths, and dangerous conditions; "absconding" — leaving an employer without permission — remains a crime, passport confiscation and recruitment-fee abuses remain largely unpunished, and in 2024 the Shura Council itself proposed re-imposing an employer-permission requirement for workers leaving the country (HRW, World Report 2025; HRW, World Report 2026). Domestic workers — primarily women — remain excluded from the labor law altogether and face harsh conditions including verbal, physical, and sexual assault with weak protections (Amnesty International, Qatar 2025).
  • Citizenship itself has two classes in law and practice. Under the 2021 Shura electoral law ratified by the Emir (Law No. 6 of 2021), only citizens whose grandfathers were Qatari and born in Qatar could vote, and only those of "original" nationality could stand — a rule Human Rights Watch characterized as discriminatory citizenship and arbitrary disenfranchisement, falling hardest on the Al Murrah tribe, many of whose members were classed "naturalized" despite generations in Qatar (HRW, 2021; CIVICUS Monitor / Gulf Centre for Human Rights, August 2021). The 2024 fix removed the distinction only for appointed office, in the same act that abolished the elections in which the distinction had bitten (see Test 2).
  • Other groups live under different rules by identity. Women face a male-guardianship system embedded in law: permission of a male guardian to marry, restrictions on travel, work in many government jobs, scholarships, and some reproductive care; no specific domestic-violence legislation (HRW, World Report 2026). Consensual sex outside marriage, including same-sex relations, is criminalized with up to seven years in prison, with flogging or death possible for Muslims under the zina provisions (HRW, World Report 2026). Amnesty records discrimination in law and practice against religious minorities, including the Baha'i community (Amnesty International, Qatar 2025).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the two-law problem is not favoritism in applying one law — it is the law as written. A finance minister and a ruling-family sheikh were prosecuted, and the minimum wage is formally non-discriminatory, and those facts are real. But the same legal order assigns political rights, welfare, mobility, family rights, and criminal exposure by category — citizen or migrant, "original" or naturalized, man or woman, Muslim or not — before any official exercises discretion. One law for all does not survive a statute book that sorts the population first.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the public treasury and ordinary litigants in the al-Emadi case, where the state's own money was pursued against insiders; migrant workers who have used job-mobility and exit-permit reforms where employers comply. Hurt — migrant workers (over 91 percent of the population on HRW's count) still under kafala controls, wage theft, and the crime of "absconding"; domestic workers excluded from the labor law; Al Murrah and other "naturalized" citizens disenfranchised in 2021; women under guardianship rules; LGBT people under criminalization; Baha'i residents facing identity-based discrimination.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Some institutions have acted with visible independence inside the system. The criminal court convicted a sitting-system finance minister and a ruling-family member in January 2024 (Test 1) — a prosecution the executive permitted to run to a heavy verdict. The Central Bank of Qatar operates the currency peg and monetary policy through a professional governor-led structure, and no documented case appears in the record assembled here of the Emir overruling a court verdict, a central-bank rate decision, or a prosecution outcome in a named case.
  • One elected body survives: the Central Municipal Council, first elected in 1999, was not touched by the 2024 amendments, and its elections continue, with the next due after the current term ends in 2027 (electoral-framework record, 2025).
  • Al Jazeera, headquartered in Doha and state-funded, broadcasts reporting critical of many governments and of regional rivals, and Qatar-based and international outlets reported the 2025 strike, the labor record, and the referendum critically and openly (Al Jazeera reporting, September 2025; HRW and Amnesty country reporting, 2025–2026).

Evidence against:

  • The grip is constitutional, and Freedom House states it in one sentence: "Qatar's hereditary emir holds all executive and legislative authority and ultimately controls the judiciary." Its scores match: independent judiciary 1 of 4, due process 1 of 4, free and independent media 1 of 4, electoral process for the head of government 0 of 4 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025). Political parties are not permitted (Freedom House, 2025; HRW, 2026).
  • The one electoral check that existed was removed by the seat itself. The 2003 constitution provided for 30 of 45 Shura Council members to be elected; the election was held once, on 2 October 2021. On 15 October 2024 the Emir proposed abolishing the elections, saying the Council "is not a representative parliament in a democratic system" and its powers would be unaffected by appointment; the Council approved the amendments unanimously on 28 October 2024, and a referendum on 5 November 2024 approved them with 90.6 percent of valid votes on 84 percent turnout (Ministry of Interior figures via International IDEA and the State of Qatar's UN submission, 2025). On 9 October 2025 the Emir appointed 49 members to the now fully appointed Council (Shura appointment record, October 2025). The referendum was a real vote with a lopsided result, and it is recorded as such; it is also a vote, proposed by the Emir and organized by his Interior Ministry, to end voting — after which every referee in the legislative branch holds his seat by the Emir's decree.
  • The media's autonomy stops at the palace door. Criticism of the Emir in the media is illegal under the press law (Article 46), and the penal code criminalizes criticism of the Emir, insulting the flag, blasphemy, and inciting overthrow of the regime; the cybercrime law punishes spreading "false news" as authorities perceive it (press-law and penal-code record via HRW, World Report 2025 and World Report 2026). Journalists practice self-censorship particularly regarding the government and ruling family (media record summarized in censorship reporting, 2025). Al Jazeera's critical range, on the documented record, does not extend to its funder's domestic rule.
  • Independence has been punished in named cases. The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found in July 2024 that Abdullah Ibhais, a former media manager for the 2022 World Cup organizing committee who had raised concerns over migrant workers' treatment, was arbitrarily detained and denied a fair trial (UN Working Group finding via Freedom House, 2025). Amnesty's 2025 reporting likewise lists arbitrary detentions of critics (Amnesty International, Qatar 2025). Peaceful protest against the 2021 electoral law was met with arrests — seven people arrested for social-media posts said to fuel "racial and tribal strife" in August 2021, among a wider set of detentions of Al Murrah protesters (security reporting, August 2021; CIVICUS Monitor, 2021); an advocacy-center account reports three activists, including a lawyer from the tribe, sentenced to life imprisonment in May 2022 for protesting the law — a severe claim carried here as reported by the European Centre for Democracy and Human Rights (2024), not independently confirmed by a court record in the material assembled here.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the question is whether the referees keep their autonomy, and in Qatar the referees' autonomy is not merely infringed case by case — it is not constituted. The Emir appoints the legislature (wholly, since 2024), appoints and dismisses the government, ultimately controls the judiciary on Freedom House's assessment, and is personally placed beyond criticism by law. A court convicting a minister shows the system can move against an insider; it does not show a referee the seat cannot replace, overrule at the source, or silence. The 2024 referendum is the clearest single exhibit: the one elected institution was abolished through a process the seat proposed, timed, and administered.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the Emir and the ruling house, whose control of every branch is now formally complete; officials and media whose work aligns with the state, who operate in a stable, predictable system. Hurt — Qatari citizens, who lost the only legislative vote they ever held (2021) after one use; journalists and critics exposed to penal and cybercrime provisions, including the arbitrarily detained whistleblower named above; Al Murrah protesters arrested in 2021; migrant workers and all residents, who have no independent referee to appeal to against the executive that also employs, sponsors, or deports them.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The blockade (2017–2021) was answered by adaptation, not capitulation or denial. When Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, and Egypt severed relations in June 2017 and issued 13 demands — including closing Al Jazeera and curtailing ties with Iran — Qatar refused the demands, rerouted trade and supply through Iran and Türkiye, deepened the Turkish security relationship, and kept its airline and LNG exports running; on 5 January 2021 the Emir signed the Al-Ula Declaration restoring relations, with none of the 13 demands fulfilled and Qatar dropping its international legal cases as its side of the settlement (CRS, 2021; PISM Bulletin, February 2021; University of Navarra analysis, 2021).
  • Labor pushback produced real, if incomplete, adjustment. Under sustained World Cup scrutiny, Qatar entered an ILO partnership and enacted the 2018–2021 reforms — exit permits abolished, job change without employer consent, a non-discriminatory minimum wage, a wage-protection system, and labor-dispute committees (ILO/HRW reform record, Test 1). The adjustment was openly legislated, not denied; its enforcement gap is graded in Tests 1 and 4, but the wobble itself was answered with law.
  • The 9 September 2025 strike on Doha — the Test's central 2025 case — was answered openly, diplomatically, and without military retaliation. Israel struck a residential building where Hamas's negotiating delegation was meeting, killing six people including a Qatari Internal Security Force (Lekhwiya) member and five Hamas members, and wounding 18 (Emir's UN General Assembly address, 23 September 2025; Indonesian parliamentary research summary, September 2025). Tamim condemned a "flagrant violation of sovereignty," branded the act state terrorism through his prime minister, chaired an emergency Arab-Islamic summit in Doha on 15 September 2025, took the case to the UN General Assembly and to the International Civil Aviation Organization (letter, 20 September 2025), and suspended Qatar's mediation role as leverage while his prime minister went to Washington (QNA, September 2025; Arab-Islamic summit communiqué, 15 September 2025; Cambridge/AJIL account, 2025). Within three weeks the adjustment had produced documented results: on 29 September 2025 President Trump signed Executive Order 14353, "Assuring the Security of the State of Qatar," pledging that an armed attack on Qatar would be regarded as a threat to the peace and security of the United States; the same day, Prime Minister Netanyahu, in a call arranged by Trump, apologized to Qatar's prime minister and pledged Israel would not violate Qatar's sovereignty again (White House, Executive Order 14353, 29 September 2025; CSIS analysis, 2025; AJIL, 2025). Qatar then resumed mediation and co-secured the October 2025 Gaza ceasefire (Test 5). The no-good-choice clause is applied here by its mechanics: the options actually open were military retaliation against a US-armed state (risking war and the US alliance), acquiescence (ratifying a strike on his capital), or the diplomatic-legal track with mediation suspended as the one lever Doha held. Each of the first two carried real harm; he took the least-harm door actually open, stated the grievance honestly and in public while in it, and the trap's origin — hosting Hamas's political office at US encouragement while mediating — was a posture built over years with Washington's knowledge, not a trap of that month's making. The episode is graded as the best possible choice available in the moment, and is not held against him.
  • The 2026 wobble was answered the same way, at higher cost. After US-Israeli strikes on Iran began on 28 February 2026, Iran struck Qatari territory repeatedly — Al Udeid Air Base, and on 18–19 March 2026 Ras Laffan, Qatar's principal LNG export hub, damaging two of its 14 liquefaction trains and reducing export capacity by approximately 17 percent, with repairs estimated at three to five years and some USD 20 billion a year in lost revenue on QatarEnergy's CEO's estimate; the Strait of Hormuz was effectively closed to normal traffic, through which virtually all Qatari LNG must pass (Middle East Institute analysis, November 2026; Orbis Iran–Qatar record, checked 18 November 2026). Qatar did not declare war, kept its embassy channel to Tehran open, kept mediating between Washington and Tehran — Qatari delegations in Tehran five times in a single week, some meetings lasting 18 to 20 hours, per the prime minister's Oxford address of 7 November 2026 — and hedged openly: a mutual defense agreement with Türkiye signed in Istanbul on 13 November 2026, and the October 2026 Badr Memorandum with Türkiye, Pakistan, and Indonesia on defense-industrial cooperation (Qatar Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 7 November 2026; MEI, November 2026). When another option became available — a Turkish mutual-defense door alongside, not instead of, the US guarantee — he considered it and took it in the open. His defense minister stated the lessons publicly: Qatar was "surprised" by the Ras Laffan attack, had since held "a lot of discussion with Iran," and was rebuilding air and counter-drone defenses (Global Security Forum remarks, Doha, reported November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The street's pushback at home was answered with arrests, not adjustment. The Al Murrah protests of August 2021 against the electoral law were met with detentions and social-media arrests (Test 2), and the grievance — second-class citizenship — was answered three years later not by restoring equal voting rights but by abolishing the vote for everyone (2024 referendum record). Equality was achieved by leveling down, through a process the seat controlled.
  • The Shura wobble of 2024 shows the pattern from the other side: when the one elected institution produced tribal friction — the Emir's stated reason was that the election "had affected relations within families and tribes" (Emir's address, 15 October 2024) — the response was to remove the institution rather than repair its franchise, and to state that the Council's status "will not be affected whether its members are chosen by election or appointment." That is a ruler declining to adjust the law that caused the pushback, and adjusting away the pushers' channel instead.
  • On labor, adjustment stopped where enforcement would have bitten: HRW documents persistent wage theft, unpunished passport confiscation, and uninvestigated worker deaths years after the reforms, and the Shura Council's 2024 proposal to restore employer exit-permission points the adjustment in reverse (HRW, World Report 2025 and 2026). The wobble was answered on paper; the follow-through wobble — the monitors' findings — has not produced a documented second round of enforcement reform.
  • Even in the strongest external cases, the adjustment's limit is visible: the US guarantee Qatar won in September 2025 did not prevent the March 2026 destruction at Ras Laffan, a distinction the MEI analysis draws between deterring further aggression and shielding critical infrastructure from the first strike (MEI, November 2026). The wobble was answered; Qatar's exposure — one gas field, one strait, one US base that draws fire — was not mastered, and no documented reconsideration of the Al Udeid hosting posture itself appears in the record assembled here.

Why: The grade lands on MIXED because the record splits cleanly by direction. Pushed by foreign partners and events — blockade, strike, war — Tamim adjusts openly, states his position in public, uses law and mediation rather than force, and when if another option becomes available, as the Turkish defense door did in 2026, he considers it openly and takes it. Pushed by his own street and his own citizens, the same ruler arrests, levels down, or removes the channel. Test 3 grades both directions, and they point opposite ways.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Qatari residents and the wider region by the refusal to retaliate militarily in September 2025 and through 2026; hostages, prisoners, and Gaza's population by the resumed mediation that produced the October 2025 ceasefire (Test 5); Qatari trade and consumers by the blockade-era rerouting. Hurt — Al Murrah protesters and critics whose domestic pushback was met with detention; migrant workers whose documented grievances after 2022 have not produced an enforcement round; Qatari citizens whose electoral channel was removed as the answer to its own friction; Qatari energy workers and LNG customers exposed at Ras Laffan in March 2026, where the harm was inflicted by Iran's strikes — recorded here as the wobble's cost, not as the Emir's act.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Sunni, Kurdish, Turkmen, Christian, and Yazidi communities, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Among citizens, the count is generous and broadly even. Citizens receive free healthcare and free education from kindergarten through university, subsidized or free utilities for a primary residence, land and housing support, public-sector employment as the norm, and no personal income tax — a rentier welfare system that official and academic accounts describe as applying to citizens as a class (Qatari ambassadorial statement; Ben Brik, Social Policy and Society, 2026). Roughly 80 percent of Qatari college graduates are women on the older diplomatic record, and women's university participation is high (Washington Diplomat record).
  • The 2024 amendments formally leveled one citizen distinction: naturalized citizens became eligible for Shura appointment and ministerial office, ending the "original nationality" bar for those posts (Test 1; State of Qatar UN submission, 2025).
  • The labor reforms, where enforced, count the migrant majority in at least the wage floor: a minimum wage applying to all nationalities, a wage-protection system, dispute committees that issued 1,379 decisions in the fourth quarter of 2024 alone on the reform record, and a Workers' Support and Insurance Fund (reform summaries, 2024–2025; HRW, World Report 2025).
  • Abroad, his mediation counts people outside his polity: Qatar's diplomacy is credited by his own UN account with helping secure the release of 148 hostages across the Gaza mediation, and the October 2025 ceasefire exchange freed the remaining 20 living hostages and nearly 2,000 Palestinian prisoners and detainees (Emir's UNGA address, 23 September 2025; ceasefire reporting, 13 October 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The foundational count excludes most of the counted. Migrant workers are over 91 percent of the population on HRW's figure (~88 percent expatriates on the separate 2026 profile figure — printed separately, as sourced) and around 95 percent of the labor force, yet they hold no political rights, no path to citizenship — naturalizations are "almost non-existent" on the ILO-cited record — and welfare eligibility is tiered explicitly by citizenship and ancestry: citizens at the top, a small permanent-resident tier, temporary workers at the bottom (HRW, 2025–2026; Ben Brik, 2026). The majority of the people who build, clean, drive, and care in Qatar are counted as labor and counted out as people with a stake.
  • The World Cup's dead remain uncounted in the way that matters. HRW and Amnesty both record that Qatar has failed to prevent, investigate, or compensate the deaths of thousands of migrant workers — "thousands of unexplained deaths" in HRW's formulation — and that neither Qatar nor FIFA has compensated the abused workers who built the 2022 tournament (HRW, World Report 2025 and 2026; Amnesty International, Qatar 2025). The exact death toll is contested — Qatari officials have given far lower work-related figures than the widest press estimates — and the dispute is recorded here with its disputants named; what is not disputed by either monitor is the absence of systematic investigation and compensation.
  • Women are counted at half weight in the law's own terms: guardianship permission to marry, travel, and work; no primary guardianship of their children; inability to pass nationality to children or spouses on equal terms; testimony rules in Sharia-based family courts that can value a woman's testimony at half a man's; and no specific domestic-violence law (HRW, World Report 2026; Amnesty, Qatar 2025).
  • LGBT people are counted as criminals: consensual same-sex relations punishable by up to seven years, arbitrary arrest under broad morality laws, and provisional detention without charge for up to six months under the Law on Protection of Community — with documented harsh treatment in detention including, in HRW's account, beatings and forced conversion therapy for transgender women (HRW, World Report 2026). A British-Mexican resident, Manuel Guerrero Aviña, was arrested in February 2024 after agreeing to meet someone through a dating app (HRW, World Report 2025).
  • Smaller communities are counted out by identity or by silence: Baha'i residents face discrimination based solely on religious identity, with a Baha'i leader imprisoned and later acquitted on Amnesty's account (HRW, 2026; Amnesty, 2025); domestic workers are excluded from the labor law (Amnesty, 2025); Filipino workers who protested in 2025 were arrested on Amnesty's reporting (Amnesty, Qatar 2025); and the Al Murrah's citizenship grievance (Tests 1–3) remains a live exclusion inside the citizenry itself.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because counting everyone is the test, and Qatar's accounting has a designed perimeter. Inside it — the citizen minority — provision is lavish and comparatively even. Outside it stands roughly nine-tenths of the population, counted for their work and not for their stake, their deaths after the World Cup still uninvestigated as a class, and alongside them women, LGBT people, domestic workers, and the Baha'i, each discounted by a named law or practice. Mediation that frees other nations' hostages is real and credited; it does not change who is counted at home.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Qatari citizens, who receive one of the world's most generous per-capita welfare settlements; naturalized citizens, formally leveled for appointed office in 2024; migrant workers reached by the wage floor and dispute committees; hostages, prisoners, and their families freed through Qatar's mediation. Hurt — the migrant majority (no political rights, no citizenship path, kafala exposure, uninvestigated deaths, no World Cup compensation); domestic workers outside the labor law; women under guardianship and unequal nationality and testimony rules; LGBT residents under criminalization and morality detention; Baha'i residents; Al Murrah citizens classed as second-tier; protesting workers arrested in 2025.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 25 June 2013, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Mediation as the stated doctrine — kept, under fire, at cost. Qatar's declared approach, restated by the Emir at the UN on 23 September 2025 — "siding with values and diplomacy rather than weapons," adhering "to diplomacy when adversaries find it easy to use weapons" — matches the record: mediation with Egypt and the United States that his UN address credits with securing the release of 148 hostages before September 2025; resumption after the Doha strike; the October 2025 ceasefire whose first phase freed the remaining 20 living hostages and nearly 2,000 Palestinian prisoners and detainees on 13 October 2025, with Qatar a signatory of the Sharm El-Sheikh declaration the same day (QNA, 23 September 2025; ceasefire reporting, October 2025; UN Security Council Resolution 2803 framework record, November 2026). In 2026 the same word was kept toward Iran: the prime minister's Oxford address of 7 November 2026 records Qatari delegations in Tehran five times in one week and a first round of negotiations that produced a memorandum of understanding helping avert wider escalation, and on 22 October 2026 Tamim and President Macron jointly called in Doha for full implementation of the Gaza ceasefire, Israeli withdrawal under the agreed arrangements, and unhindered aid (Qatar MFA, 7 November 2026; Qatar–France joint statement, 22 October 2026). As of mid-November 2026 Qatari mediators were still meeting Iranian officials (17 November 2026) with no deal yet reached — the effort documented, the outcome open (Iran–Qatar record, checked 18 November 2026).
  • The sovereignty red lines of 2017 — kept. The 13 demands were refused in 2017 and were still refused at Al-Ula on 5 January 2021: Al Jazeera was not closed, the Turkish relationship was not severed, ties with Iran were not cut (CRS, 2021; PISM, 2021). The word given then — that Qatar would not trade its foreign policy for relief — was the word kept.
  • Labor reform legislation — enacted as announced. The reforms pledged under the ILO partnership were passed on the announced lines in 2018–2021: exit permits gone, job mobility without employer consent, the non-discriminatory minimum wage, wage protection (ILO/HRW record). The keeping is of the legislative word; the enforcement shortfall is graded where it belongs, in Tests 1 and 4, and is not double-counted here.
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: his father Hamad's voluntary handover of power on 25 June 2013 — itself a kept commitment to an orderly succession, unprecedented in the modern Gulf (CRS, 2021) — is the act that seated Tamim, and the Qatar National Vision 2030 framework inherited from that reign remains the state's stated program, now in its Third National Development Strategy phase (Oxford Business Group, Qatar 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The elected Shura Council — the constitution's word, postponed for a generation, then withdrawn. The 2003 constitution, approved under his father and carried into Tamim's reign, provided for election of 30 of 45 Council members. The election was postponed in 2013 (the year Tamim took power), again to at least 2019, held once on 2 October 2021, and abolished by the November 2024 amendments (electoral record, 2013–2024). Crossing, named plainly: this is a word broken by process — each postponement was lawful in form — and the breaking hurt Qatari citizens' one channel of legislative choice (crossing 4 in part: broken word, and the hurt recorded in Test 2's ledger).
  • Equal citizenship — promised in 2021, delivered by subtraction in 2024. After the Al Murrah protests, Tamim pledged movement toward equal citizenship; the 2024 amendments did remove the "original nationality" bar — for appointed office, in the same instrument that ended the elections where the inequality had operated (Tests 1–2). The letter was partly kept; the substance — equal citizens voting — was not. This is the dossier's clearest crossing-2 shape in reverse order: a word eventually kept in form, in a way that still hurt the people it was promised to.
  • Worker protection as a continuing pledge — the enforcement word recedes. Qatar's standing commitment, repeated through the ILO partnership and its UN Human Rights Council membership (re-elected for 2025–2027), is to uphold labor standards; HRW's 2026 finding of "limited impact," persistent wage theft, and uninvestigated deaths, plus the Shura Council's 2024 proposal to restore employer exit-permission, record a pledge whose implementation is, on the monitors' evidence, stalling or reversing (HRW, World Report 2026).
  • Not documented: a crossing-3 case — a documented instance of Tamim breaking a public word in a way that demonstrably helped people — does not appear in the record assembled here, and none is invented. Crossing 1 (word kept and it helped) is documented in the mediation file above; crossing 2 (word kept and it still hurt) appears in the sovereignty file in a limited form — the refusal of the 13 demands kept his word and his state's independence, and the blockade years it prolonged imposed real costs on Qatari residents, importers, and separated Gulf families, costs recorded here in the ledger rather than netted against the keeping.

Why: The grade lands on MIXED because the two halves of the record are both long and both documented. Outward, his word is unusually reliable: the mediation doctrine, the 2017 red lines, and the diplomatic posture of 2025–2026 were stated in advance and then done, including when doing them cost Qatar a bombed capital district and a damaged LNG hub.
