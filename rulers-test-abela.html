The Ruler's Test — Robert Abela, Prime Minister of Malta

Subject: Robert Abela — Prime Minister of Malta since 13 January 2020, when he succeeded Joseph Muscat as Labour Party leader (elected 12 January 2020, 9,342 votes to Chris Fearne's 6,798) and Muscat resigned in the aftermath of the assassination of journalist Daphne Caruana Galizia. He led Labour to victory in the general election of 26 March 2022 (55.11% of the vote, a majority of 39,474 votes) and again, in an early election he called in April 2026, on 30 May 2026 (51.77%, a majority of 21,721 votes) — a fourth consecutive Labour term, unprecedented in Maltese politics. He was sworn in again on 1 June 2026 and reached 2,500 days in office on 17 November 2026, passing Muscat as Labour's second-longest-serving prime minister. He is graded here as the sitting holder of the seat. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest prosecution in Maltese history proceeded against his own party's former leadership, under his government, and he did not stop it. The magisterial inquiry into the Vitals/Steward hospitals concession — a 2015 deal struck under Muscat, annulled by the civil courts in February 2023 as fraudulent and confirmed annulled on appeal in October 2023, the appeal court finding evidence of collusion — was concluded by Magistrate Gabriella Vella on 24 April 2024 and sent to the Attorney General. In May 2024 the Attorney General charged former prime minister Joseph Muscat, his former chief of staff Keith Schembri, and former minister Konrad Mizzi with, among other offences, bribery, money laundering, fraud, trading in influence, and association in a criminal organisation; Muscat's former deputy Chris Fearne and the sitting Central Bank governor Edward Scicluna were also charged. All are ALLEGATIONs; the accused pleaded not guilty on 28 May 2024, no verdict has been reached, and Muscat maintains the process is a political vendetta (Associated Press, 28 May 2024; court reporting, 2024–2026). That a sitting prime minister's own Attorney General prosecuted his predecessor and mentor is the strongest one-law fact in this file.
  • The ethics machinery reached his own cabinet and he eventually enforced it. Standards Commissioner Joseph Azzopardi found that Tourism Minister Clayton Bartolo and Gozo Minister Clint Camilleri had breached the ministerial code of ethics over a consultancy contract given to Bartolo's partner (later wife), who was paid a consultant's salary for some 13 months for work the Commissioner found she did not do. After a second matter emerged — an ALLEGATION, flagged by the Financial Intelligence Analysis Unit, that she had received a €50,000 kickback connected to a Malta Tourism Authority deal — Abela forced Bartolo's resignation on 26 November 2024 and expelled him from the Labour parliamentary group; the parliamentary Standards Committee ordered Bartolo to apologise to the House and refund €16,400, the excess salary paid (Standards Commissioner report and Standards Committee ruling, November 2024; Malta Today, 27 November 2024).
  • The 2020 reforms formally removed the prime minister's hand from prosecutions of serious crime: responsibility for prosecuting serious offences was transferred from the executive police to the Attorney General, and the Attorney General and State Advocate were given security of tenure, removable only by a two-thirds parliamentary resolution (constitutional and institutional reforms adopted unanimously, 29 July 2020; European Commission, 2020 Rule of Law Report, Malta chapter).

Evidence against:

  • The same ethics finding produced two different consequences. Camilleri, found by the same Standards Commissioner report to have breached the same code over the same contract, kept his ministry: the Standards Committee merely admonished him, with the Speaker's casting vote rejecting opposition proposals that he apologise, refund the money his ministry paid, and be recommended for removal (Malta Today, 27 November 2024). One law for the expendable minister; another for the one who stayed.
  • Abela publicly pre-judged a live case in a defendant's favour. In 2025 he reinstated Ronald Mizzi — a permanent secretary who had resigned after a magistrates' court found sufficient grounds to indict him in the hospitals case — as a permanent secretary serving in the Office of the Prime Minister, declaring him effectively innocent while his criminal case was still in progress. The Nationalist Party called the reinstatement "an unacceptable interference in an ongoing judicial process," and the Malta Independent's editorial board wrote that the prime minister had "unilaterally absolved" a man still facing proceedings (The Malta Independent editorial and PN statements, 2025). Mizzi's charges remain ALLEGATIONs, undecided.
  • While the hospitals prosecution was being prepared, Abela repeatedly cast doubt on the process that produced it — but only once it reached his own party's former leadership. He questioned the inquiry's timing ("after four and a half years, it concluded precisely with the opening for the candidacy for the European Parliament elections"), questioned whether the accused had been given a fair hearing, and said he was "not in a position to say whether the presumption of innocence was safeguarded during the inquiry" (press conference, 6 May 2024; Associated Press, May 2024). No comparable public scepticism from him toward inquiries or prosecutions touching the opposition is on the record.
  • His government narrowed the very channel through which the hospitals case had reached a magistrate. The inquiry that produced the Muscat charges existed because the NGO Repubblika could request a magisterial inquiry directly. By Act VIII of 2025 (enacted 11 April 2025, passed 37–30), citizens and civil society must now report to the police first, supply court-admissible evidence, and wait six months for police inaction before they may petition for an inquiry; legal scholars, including former chief justices, and the civil-society organisations that used the old channel describe the reform as shielding officials from exactly this kind of scrutiny, and Repubblika announced a constitutional challenge (International IDEA democracy tracker, April 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). The government's stated position — that the reform adds timeframes, victims' rights, and safeguards against abusive requests — is recorded; the documented effect is a higher gate in front of corruption complaints against the powerful.
  • The outcome measures remain weak. Malta scored 49 out of 100 in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index, among only six EU states below 50, and the European Commission's 2026 Rule of Law Report records that, despite more resources and new investigations, "a robust track record of final judgments" in high-level corruption cases "remains to be achieved" (European Commission, 2026 Rule of Law Report, Malta chapter, 17 July 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the prosecution, the media, the electoral authority, the Ombudsman, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The signature reforms of his first year genuinely removed appointment powers from the prime minister's hands. Under the July 2020 constitutional package, judges and magistrates are appointed by the President of the Republic from three candidates recommended by an enlarged, majority-judicial Judicial Appointments Committee; the Chief Justice requires a two-thirds resolution of Parliament (Chief Justice Mark Chetcuti was appointed by parliamentary unanimity in April 2020); the removal and discipline of judges was taken out of Parliament's hands and given to judicial peers; the President is elected by two-thirds; and the Police Commissioner is appointed through a public-call process rather than by prime-ministerial choice (reforms adopted 29 July 2020; Venice Commission Opinion No. 986/2020 and the Maltese authorities' implementation report; European Commission, 2020 Rule of Law Report).
  • The courts rule against his government and are obeyed. The civil courts annulled his party's flagship hospitals concession as fraudulent (February 2023, confirmed October 2023) and the hospitals were returned to the state. In April 2025 a court found that asylum seekers held on chartered boats outside Malta's territorial waters in 2020 had been unlawfully detained and awarded compensation to nine of the plaintiffs (court reporting, April 2025). Courts have also ruled against the prosecution's targets where the record required it — Muscat's human-rights challenge to the hospitals inquiry was thrown out (February 2026) — which is the same independence cutting the other way.
  • The murder trial of the alleged mastermind of the Caruana Galizia assassination ran its course without documented interference: after a 55-day trial, a jury acquitted businessman Yorgen Fenech on 2 September 2026 by 8–1 on charges of complicity and criminal association (the charges were ALLEGATIONs which Fenech denied; five other men stand convicted for their roles in the bombing, two of them sentenced to life in 2025). The government's response was a statement urging "prudence in public comments that could do more harm than good," noting that two investigations continue and that the prosecution may appeal; Abela declined to comment on the verdict itself (Reuters, 3 September 2026; ICIJ, September 2026).
  • Watchdogs operate in public and unpunished: the Ombudsman publishes pointed interventions (including a June 2026 opinion piece pressing the administration on fair treatment of third-country nationals), the Auditor General's three reports on the hospitals concession were published in full, and the Standards Commissioner investigated the prime minister's own ministers (Ombudsman, June 2026; NAO reports, 2020–2023).

Evidence against:

  • The prime minister personally attacked a sitting magistrate conducting an inquiry into his own party's former leadership. On 28 April 2024, at a campaign launch, Abela warned the judiciary not to engage in "political terrorism" — words directed, as Malta Today's editorial of 1 May 2024 put it, "squarely at the magistrate who has been leading the inquiry into the now defunct hospitals contract"; he repeated the charge as the inquiry concluded and passed to the Attorney General, and at his 6 May 2024 press conference questioned whether the inquiry had safeguarded the presumption of innocence. The Daphne Caruana Galizia Foundation's documented timeline records the pattern: the prime minister who took office saying "let the institutions work" shifted, once the institutions reached Muscat, to attacking "the Establishment" and the inquiring magistrate herself; he was criticised for it by the opposition and by the judiciary (Daphne Caruana Galizia Foundation timeline, May 2024; Malta Today editorial, 1 May 2024; Wikipedia summary of the judiciary's response, 2024). No magistrate was removed — Magistrate Vella could not be — but the head of government used his platform to de-legitimise a named judicial officer mid-process, in a case touching his own party.
  • The Chief Justice file has been left unfinished for six years. The Venice Commission's core 2018 concern was the concentration of appointment power; the 2020 reform subjected the Chief Justice to a two-thirds parliamentary vote but kept the judiciary itself out of the selection. The European Commission's 2026 Rule of Law Report records that "reforms to involve the judiciary in the process are still pending" and registers no progress on the recommendation (European Commission, 2026 Rule of Law Report, Malta chapter). The European Parliament's LIBE delegation, visiting on 16–17 November 2026, likewise stressed that "further efforts are needed to strengthen the independence of the judiciary, particularly through a reform of the appointment procedure for the Chief Justice" (European Parliament press release, 17 November 2026).
  • The statutory grip on the accountability machinery tightened in 2025: the magisterial-inquiries law (Test 1) routes citizen complaints through the executive police and the Attorney General before any magistrate may act, a design legal scholars describe as subordinating an independent judicial evidence-gathering tool to executive gatekeepers (Act VIII of 2025; University Students' Council legal analysis, 2025).
  • Media autonomy remains formally unprotected in the ways that matter. The Committee of Media Experts Abela appointed in 2022 was given a narrower remit than the public inquiry had envisaged — advising on legislation already drafted rather than shaping it — and its process was characterised by the Media Freedom Rapid Response consortium as opaque; the promised white paper and journalist-protection legislation had still not been enacted years later, and the Commission's 2026 report records that "legislative reforms remain pending to improve the protection of media freedom and the working environment of journalists," with protection against domestic SLAPPs and access to official documents still flagged by the European Parliament's November 2026 delegation as serious concerns (MFRR, 2023; European Commission, 2026 Rule of Law Report; European Parliament, 17 November 2026). Some ground was conceded: legislation on media-ownership transparency, state advertising, and safeguards for the public broadcaster's chief executive was adopted, which the Commission credited as "some progress" — governance rules for public service media that are not yet fully implemented.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Parliament, the EU, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He reversed himself, completely, on the Jean Paul Sofia public inquiry. Sofia, a 20-year-old worker, died on 3 December 2022 when a building under construction at the Corradino Industrial Estate collapsed. Abela refused the family's demand for a public inquiry for seven months, insisting the magisterial inquiry sufficed; on 12 July 2023 his parliamentary group voted against the opposition's motion for one, in a sitting the dead man's parents watched from the gallery. Within days — after public outrage that crossed party lines and with a protest being organised outside his office — Abela recanted and appointed a public inquiry with wide terms of reference, chaired by the Ombudsman, Joseph Zammit McKeon, with the Auditor General as a member; the board heard 69 witnesses and its report followed in 2024 (Malta Today, July 2023 and February 2024). The adjustment was real: the thing demanded was conceded in full, not in part.
  • He complied with the European Court of Justice on citizenship. Malta defended its investor-citizenship scheme through every stage of the Commission's infringement procedure from 2020; on 29 April 2025 the Court ruled in Commission v Malta (C-181/23) that granting nationality in exchange for predetermined payments infringes EU law. Within three months, Parliament enacted Act XXI of 2025 (24 July 2025), terminating the investment route and replacing it with a citizenship-by-merit framework with no fixed financial contribution (Court of Justice judgment, 29 April 2025; Act XXI of 2025).
  • On the FATF grey list, the adjustment was executed even though the initial reaction was grievance. Malta was grey-listed in June 2021 — the first EU member state so listed — and Abela called the decision unfair; his government nonetheless completed the FATF action plan, and Malta was removed on 17 June 2022, twelve months after listing, with the FATF recording "significant progress" on beneficial-ownership detection and tax-based money-laundering enforcement (FATF plenary outcome, 17 June 2022).
  • He submitted himself to the voters early and accepted the narrower verdict: having called the May 2026 election a year ahead of schedule, he accepted a reduced majority (21,721 votes, down from 39,474) and pledged in his victory statement to be "prime minister for all citizens of Malta" (Reuters, April–May 2026; election results, 30 May 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Sofia reversal came only at the point of maximum political cost, and without the admission that completes an adjustment: the reason publicly given — that the inquiring magistrate had extended her inquiry by another 30 days — was, as Malta Today's editorial noted, no material change from the months in which he had refused; "instead of using the magistrate's umpteenth extension as the reason to justify his volte face, Abela should have simply shown humbleness in front of Sofia's grieving parents and accepted that they were right all along" (Malta Today editorial, July 2023). The inquiry was conceded; the error was never owned.
  • On the Daphne Caruana Galizia public inquiry, five years of pushback have produced refusal, not adjustment. The inquiry reported in July 2021 with 28 recommendations; in September 2026 his government refused to debate an opposition motion calling for their full implementation by the end of October, and on 6 October 2026 Abela again refused the recommended anti-mafia legislation, arguing it would stigmatise Malta — as, per contemporaneous reporting, most of the 28 recommendations remained unimplemented (The Malta Post, October 2026; MFRR assessment, 2023, recording one of 28 fully implemented at the two-year mark).
  • On the magisterial-inquiries law, protest produced no pause: the bill was presented in January 2025 without stakeholder consultation, more than 3,000 people protested in Valletta on 16 February 2025 urging the government to stop and consult, constituted bodies and legal scholars opposed it — and it was pushed through on the government's majority and signed into law on 11 April 2025 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025; International IDEA, February–April 2025).
  • On citizenship, the adjustment came only under legal compulsion and after every lesser signal was resisted: Cyprus repealed its scheme in 2020 under the same Commission pressure; Malta re-issued its own scheme in a new form in November 2020, defended it through a reasoned opinion in 2022, and surrendered it only when the Court of Justice ruled against it in April 2025 (Court of Justice, C-181/23, case history).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — migrants, foreign workers, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The resident population's material floor was held through successive shocks. His governments kept utility and fuel prices frozen by state subsidy through the post-2022 European energy-price surge, and the employment count is broad: unemployment stood at about 3.5% in mid-2026, the IMF records real GDP growth averaging close to 7% a year over the decade to 2024, and foreign workers accounted for roughly three-quarters of employment growth since 2013 (IMF Malta assessments, 2026; NSO labour statistics, 2026).
  • Poverty did not widen on his watch, though it did not close either: the at-risk-of-poverty-or-social-exclusion rate was 19.7% in EU-SILC 2024 and 19.4% in EU-SILC 2025, and the Gini coefficient of income inequality fell 2.1 points to 29.2 (NSO, EU-SILC 2024 and 2025).
  • The 2025 Labour Migration Policy at least writes foreign workers into the accounting: it proposes two-year renewable permits in place of annual ones, a grace period for unemployed third-country workers extended from 10 days toward 60, pay-through-bank requirements, and bans on further recruitment for employers found guilty of abuse (Malta Labour Migration Policy, consultation document, January 2025; Eurofound factsheet MT-2025-2/3804).

Evidence against:

  • People in Malta's search-and-rescue zone were counted out at the cost of their lives, in an operation the government admitted coordinating. On 15 April 2020 — the "Easter Monday pushback" — 51 people, including seven women and three children, who had been picked up in Malta's SAR region by the fishing vessel Dar Al Salaam 1, contracted by the Maltese government, were returned to Tripoli and handed to the Libyan authorities; five were dead when the vessel reached Libya and seven more were reported missing at sea. The Maltese authorities confirmed in an official statement that they had coordinated the operation. A former operative of the Office of the Prime Minister, Neville Gafà, admitted under oath in related criminal proceedings that he had coordinated the pushback on the instructions of the Office of the Prime Minister (Amnesty International, Waves of Impunity, September 2020; ECRE Weekly Bulletin, 30 April 2020). The government's position — that Libya cooperation is necessary and that Malta cannot absorb arrivals — is recorded; it does not answer the documented facts.
  • The survivors' attempt to have those facts judged was defeated by the state's own procedural plea, without the merits ever being heard. Fifty-two survivors and relatives sued the Prime Minister, the Home Affairs Minister, and the Armed Forces commander in the constitutional jurisdiction. In 2026 the First Hall (Mr Justice Lawrence Mintoff) dismissed the case because the lawyer's proxy documents had not been authenticated through Maltese consular channels as the procedural code requires — a plea raised by the state defendants. The judge recorded that "the allegations made by the applicants are truly serious and therefore merited the greatest of attention," but held the court had no jurisdiction to proceed (judgment reported by The Malta Independent, 2026). The pushback itself has therefore never been adjudicated on its merits in Malta.
  • Rescued people were held outside the law's reach as a matter of policy: in spring 2020, some 425 people were detained for up to 38 days on government-chartered tourist boats outside territorial waters, without detention orders, without being told of their right to seek asylum, and without access to lawyers; when 32 of them sued, a court in April 2025 found the detention of the nine who testified unlawful and awarded €20,000 in compensation (ECRE, 2020; court reporting, April 2025).
  • Three rescued teenagers have spent their youth inside a terrorism prosecution. The El Hiblu 3 — aged 15, 16, and 19 when arrested in March 2019 after the rescued people aboard the El Hiblu resisted return to Libya and the ship came to Malta instead — were charged with offences including acts of terrorism, carrying life imprisonment; seven years on, in March 2026, the proceedings were still unresolved, and Amnesty International records that key witnesses were never summoned and no evidence of violence produced (Amnesty International, March 2026). The charges were brought before Abela took office; the decision to persist with them, year after year, was taken under his Attorneys General.
  • The workers his economy runs on are counted as labour, not yet as people with secure standing. Non-Maltese residents were 29.4% of the population at the end of 2024 — 168,938 people, of whom 129,406 were third-country nationals — and the Ombudsman documented in June 2026 their structural vulnerability: residence tied to a single employer, fear of retaliation deterring complaints, opaque and delayed permit decisions (NSO population figures, 2024; Ombudsman, June 2026). Budget 2027 then moved to raise the cost of employing third-country nationals and restrict some of their access to publicly funded services, including changes touching free public transport and childcare (Budget 2027 reporting, November 2026).
  • The domestic poor remain a stubborn fifth of the country amid record wealth: 95,157 people were at risk of poverty in EU-SILC 2025 (16.9%), the rate for those over 65 stood at 30.3% on the AROPE measure, and the Central Bank of Malta has noted that Malta's poverty outcomes are poor relative to its GDP; moderate or severe food insecurity rose from 4.3% in 2019 to 9% in 2024 (NSO, EU-SILC 2025; Central Bank of Malta, as reported 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises and stated positions vs. the record since January 2020, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The 2020 reform package — delivered on schedule. Abela took office in January 2020 promising institutional reform; by 29 July 2020 the constitutional and institutional package described in Test 2 had been adopted unanimously — judicial appointments removed from prime-ministerial discretion, the Chief Justice and President put behind two-thirds votes, the Attorney General's dual role split, prosecutions of serious crime moved to the Attorney General (Parliamentary record, July 2020; European Commission, 2020 Rule of Law Report).
  • The grey list — the pledge was "never again," and the exit was delivered. On Malta's removal from the FATF grey list on 17 June 2022, Abela said the country had completed the action plan in "record time" and stated: "I will be clear, never again should our country end up on a list like this." Malta has not returned to the list (FATF, June 2022; prime-ministerial press conference, 17 June 2022).
  • Price stability through the energy crisis — kept. Successive budgets held utility and fuel prices at their pre-crisis levels by subsidy, as pledged through the 2022 election and after; Malta's inflation outcome (2.4% HICP in December 2025) reflects that policy (Budgets 2022–2026; Eurostat HICP).
  • Citizenship compliance — kept, once ruled. Having lost in Luxembourg, he legislated the scheme's termination within three months rather than defying the judgment (Act XXI of 2025, 24 July 2025).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "Lock, stock and barrel" — the claim outruns the record. Contesting the Labour leadership in 2020, Abela reportedly dismissed adopting the Venice Commission's proposals wholesale, saying Malta should "pick and choose." By June 2025, addressing the Council of Europe's Parliamentary Assembly, he claimed: "When it comes to the absolute majority of the reforms, we took the report and implemented it lock, stock and barrel," and that Malta is "an example of best practice when it comes to reforms." Amphora Media's fact-check of that address (September 2025) sets the claim against the record: items including the judiciary's role in appointing the Chief Justice, erga omnes effect for Constitutional Court judgments striking down laws, and tightened conflict rules for MPs remain undone — the first of them confirmed as "still pending" by the European Commission's 2026 report, which found no progress on three of its seven recommendations to Malta (Amphora Media, FATTI, September 2025; European Commission, 2026 Rule of Law Report).
  • The Daphne inquiry's recommendations — accepted in principle, unmet in fact. His government received the public inquiry's 28 recommendations in July 2021 and committed to the reform course they set; at the two-year mark one had been fully implemented, and at five years most remained unimplemented, with the government in September 2026 refusing even to debate a motion for their completion (MFRR, 2023; The Malta Post, October 2026).
  • Media reform — promised, tabled, and stalled. Abela announced in October 2023 that the Media Experts Committee's report would lead to a white paper and new media laws; the committee's remit had already been narrowed from the inquiry's design, and by the Commission's 2026 assessment the journalist-protection legislation was still pending (MFRR, 2023; European Commission, 2026 Rule of Law Report).
  • "Let the institutions work" — the standard he set, reversed when it bound his own side. The phrase was his repeated public position from 2020 through the hospitals concession's annulment ("'Let the institutions work', Abela says on Steward and Joseph Muscat" — his response to the third Auditor General's report on the deal). In April–May 2024, when the institution in question was a magistrate concluding an inquiry into his predecessor, the same prime minister warned the judiciary against "political terrorism" and questioned whether the inquiry had respected the presumption of innocence (Daphne Caruana Galizia Foundation timeline, May 2024). The long arc is factual: he inherited the seat from a man forced out by a murdered journalist's revelations, built his early authority on deference to institutions, and kept that deference intact until the institutions' work reached the man he succeeded.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Robert Abela's Malta presents the tests with a genuine paradox, and the record supports both halves of it. The same government that removed judicial appointments from the prime minister's gift, split the Attorney General's dual role, and exited the FATF grey list in a year also produced a prime minister who warned a sitting magistrate against "political terrorism" while she investigated his predecessor, kept one minister and expelled another on the same ethics finding, reinstated an indicted permanent secretary into his own office, and in 2025 gated the citizen's route to a magisterial inquiry — the very route that had produced the hospitals prosecution — behind the police and the Attorney General. That prosecution is itself the strongest counterweight in this file: under Abela, a former prime minister of his own party stands charged with bribery and money laundering, and the courts that annulled his party's fraudulent hospitals deal and acquitted the alleged mastermind of a journalist's murder did so without documented interference. But the count is where the record breaks. A government-contracted boat returned 51 people to Tripoli in April 2020 and twelve of them did not survive it; the state confirmed it coordinated the operation, defeated the survivors' case on a consular-authentication technicality without the merits being heard, held 425 rescued people on pleasure boats outside its own law, and has kept three rescued teenagers under terrorism charges for seven years — while nearly a third of the people who live and work in Malta hold a status its own Ombudsman describes as structurally vulnerable. On the evidence assembled here, the seat holds its institutions better than its predecessor did and says so more loudly than the record allows; it adjusts when the cost of refusal peaks; and it counts everyone who can vote for it — and not everyone who cannot.
