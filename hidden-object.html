    Tap to open — Three ways to search, and what each one costs

    1. Grid sweep

    Divide the picture into nine boxes in your mind — three across, three down — and search each box completely before moving to the next. In Coach mode you can lay the grid right over the picture.

    Trade-off: the most thorough way there is, and the calmest — nothing gets skipped. The cost is speed: on an easy scene it feels slow, because it is.

    2. Edge-first

    Search the borders first: the picture's edges and corners, then the edges of the big things in it — the rim of the bench, the frame of the window, the line of the fence. Hiders tuck objects along edges because the eye slides past them there.

    Trade-off: fast finds when objects hug edges, which is often. The cost: anything hidden mid-picture waits until last, so finish with one honest pass over the middles.

    3. Shape, not colour

    Pick one object from your list, hold its outline in your mind, and look only for that outline. Hidden objects borrow the colours around them, so colour is the camouflage — shape is the tell.

    Trade-off: it beats camouflage better than any other way. The cost: it takes practice, it tires the eyes sooner, and it works one object at a time.

    There is no single right way — most good searchers mix all three: edges first, then a grid sweep, reading shapes as they go.

    Tap to open — The game's roots, and how this page is made

    Hidden-picture puzzles are a documented, generations-old form. Children's magazines — the Highlights tradition above all — have long printed one big, busy drawing with small objects concealed inside the lines of the scene itself: a spoon in the folds of a curtain, a fish in the planks of a fence. Picture-book hunts in the I Spy style work the same way from the other side: a cluttered scene, a short list in rhyme or plain words, and the reader's eye doing all the work.

    This page follows that form in its own words and its own drawings. All three scenes — the workshop, the garden, the kitchen — were drawn for this page as original vector art; no published puzzle, picture, or scene is copied or traced, and no brand's artwork appears anywhere in it. The hidden objects are the ordinary small things these puzzles have always hidden: a key, a bell, a fish, a heart.

    Your record (below) is kept in a clean, consistent shape — one entry per finished try, with the scene, the rules in play, the time, the hints, and the misses — the same intake shape the whole games shelf uses, so your searching patterns can be read honestly over time: which scenes run long, whether hints are falling, whether misses drop as a strategy beds in.
