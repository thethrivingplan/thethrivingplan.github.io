The Ruler's Test — James Marape, Prime Minister of Papua New Guinea

Subject: James Marape — Prime Minister of Papua New Guinea since 30 May 2019. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Papua New Guinea's Governor-General is a ceremonial head of state, while executive power — command of the government, the budget, and the disciplined forces — sits with the prime minister. Marape came to the seat from inside the government he replaced: he had been Peter O'Neill's finance minister, resigned from that government in April 2019, and defected to the Pangu Pati; when O'Neill resigned on 26 May 2019 to avoid defeat in a no-confidence vote, parliament elected Marape prime minister on 30 May 2019, and he became Pangu leader that October (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024; contemporaneous reporting, May 2019). He was returned at the July 2022 general election — Pangu the largest party with 38–39 of 118 seats — and re-elected prime minister by parliament on 9 August 2022, unopposed, with the support of 97 members (Freedom House, 2024; ANU observer reporting, August 2022). He is graded on seven and a half years in office — the longest tenure of the post-Somare era — spanning the 2020 parliamentary impasse, the 2022 election, the January 2024 "Black Wednesday" riots, at least nine motions of no confidence by his opponents' count, and, in the week of grading, the first parliamentary vote on ratifying Bougainville's 2019 independence referendum. The file is not thin; where the record is contested, the contest is named. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machinery of the state was built, in law, by his government, and built to be independent of him. The Organic Law on the Independent Commission Against Corruption passed on 20 November 2020, eighteen months after he took office, alongside the Whistleblowers Act and proceeds-of-crime legislation; the ICAC stood up in 2023 (Devpolicy, November 2025; PMNEC statements, 2022–2026). Marape insisted the commission's leadership be recruited by an external panel and headhunting firm, shortlisted to an appointments committee chaired by himself but comprising the opposition leader, the chief justice, the Public Services Commission chair and the Council of Churches chair — and that the inaugural commissioners be expatriates, precisely so that wantok obligations could not reach them: Australian Andrew Forbes as commissioner, with Australian Daniel Baulch and New Zealander Graham Gill as deputies, from July 2023 (Devpolicy, November 2025).
  • The largest corruption inquiry of his tenure was aimed at the government he himself had served in, and he did not exempt himself from it. The Ombudsman Commission's report on the 2014 K3 billion UBS loan named Marape — O'Neill's finance minister at the time — among those who had played a part; Marape told parliament he had not stood in the way of the report's tabling and would present his own case to the inquiry he then established (Ombudsman Commission UBS report, 2019; PNG press reporting, February 2020). The Commission of Inquiry under former Chief Justice Sir Salamo Injia reported in April 2022; its findings were tabled, O'Neill publicly accepted its recommendations and said he would defend himself before the ICAC or the courts, and police subsequently charged O'Neill with three counts of giving false evidence under oath to the inquiry — the police commissioner describing it as the first arrest arising from the findings, with investigations ongoing (contemporaneous reporting, April 2022 and June 2023).
  • The machinery has begun to bite inside his own tenure, not only his predecessor's. In 2026 the Prime Minister's office publicly welcomed front-page reports of ICAC investigations, arrests and charges involving senior public officials, with Marape stating he would not comment on the merits of individual cases, that every person charged is entitled to due process and the presumption of innocence, and that the commission must work "without fear, favour or political interference" (PMNEC statement, 2026). The ICAC's budget allocation rose to about K54.1 million in 2026, a 34.5% increase (PMNEC statement, 14 August 2026).
  • When corruption allegations reached his own flagship program, he commissioned an outside audit rather than only denying them. After opposition allegations about the Connect PNG roads program (below), the government engaged Deloitte and an international engineering firm to audit the entire program, with the physical inspection of Phase One projects across eight provinces beginning in February 2025, and Marape stated publicly that anyone found to have mismanaged funds would be prosecuted (PMNEC statement, 2025). In June 2023 he also brought to parliament amendments to the Leadership Code imposing stricter penalties, including five-year dismissal from office, on leaders who fail to file annual statements or who obstruct the Ombudsman Commission — passed at first vote 86–0 (PNG press reporting, June 2023).

Evidence against:

  • The Connect PNG allegations go to the source of the seat, and they remain allegations precisely because the audit he commissioned has not reported. In February 2024 O'Neill alleged, citing documents he said were provided by public servants, that contracts worth some K3.5 billion under the program had been awarded to Ipwenz Construction, owned by a man he named as a close relative of the Prime Minister, with over K45 million paid in eight months; he named four further companies he alleged were linked to Marape associates (OnePNG, 4 February 2024). These claims are recorded here as ALLEGATIONs by a political opponent, not findings; Marape's documented answer is the Deloitte audit, whose findings were not public at grading. The Public Accounts Committee has separately been reported as finding that the trust fund the program's law required was never established — a "legal vacuum" around billions of kina — and published figures for total program spending conflict, ranging from K4.5 billion to K9.8 billion (Public Accounts Committee findings as reported, 2026). A program of that size, run for years without its statutory trust account, is a one-law failure whether or not any individual contract is ever proven corrupt.
  • The ICAC he built imploded, and its suspended deputies accuse him personally of interference — an accusation he denies, recorded here as contested. In June 2025 all three inaugural commissioners were suspended by the Appointments Committee amid mutual allegations of corrupt conduct among themselves: Forbes had referred his two deputies to police over alleged financial misconduct, while a warrant for Forbes's own arrest, issued in November 2024 over alleged abuse of office in rewriting the commission's regulations to restrict his deputies' powers, was reported by the Post-Courier (Devpolicy, November 2025; Lekmak, June 2025). Suspended deputies Gill and Baulch subsequently alleged publicly that Marape meddled in the commission's affairs and that Gill learned of his suspension by an email from the Prime Minister; Marape denied interfering, stating the suspensions followed constitutional process and arose from the officers' allegations against each other (PNG Facts, 23 October 2025). The Lowy Institute's assessment is that the affair "damaged public confidence" and sidelined the commission (Lowy Institute, 2025). Whatever the truth of the interference claim, the referee his government created spent its second year fighting itself rather than the field.
  • Seven and a half years of machinery have produced no documented senior conviction. The UBS prosecutions promised in 2022 (Test 5) have yielded, on the record assembled here, charges of false evidence against one former prime minister and no verdicts on the loan itself; the IMF records that of the governance structural benchmarks in its program only one had been fully met, and that governance indicators "continue to deteriorate" (Lowy Institute summary of IMF program, 2025).
  • The outcome measures point the wrong way. Papua New Guinea scored 26 of 100 in Transparency International's 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index — down from 31 in 2024, its lowest score in a decade, the lowest in the Pacific, 142nd of 182 (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released February 2026). On 13 February 2026 the Financial Action Task Force placed PNG on its grey list for strategic deficiencies in anti-money-laundering enforcement, including too few money-laundering prosecutions — a listing PNG had previously exited in 2016 and has now re-entered on his watch (FATF plenary outcomes, 13 February 2026; Bank of Papua New Guinea statement, 17 February 2026). The ICAC itself has stated publicly that the country may lose up to K4 billion a year to corruption (ICAC Commissioner Forbes, August 2023).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts have ruled against his government at the highest-stakes moments of his tenure, and the government has obeyed. In December 2020 a five-judge Supreme Court bench ruled that the Speaker's recall of parliament on 16 November 2020 was unconstitutional, that the 17 November sitting — at which his government, shorn of defectors, had passed the 2021 budget and adjourned the house for five months — was "unconstitutional, invalid, and ineffective," and ordered parliament to reconvene on 14 December 2020 (Supreme Court ruling, 9 December 2020). Parliament reconvened as ordered. In March 2025 the same court declared unconstitutional parliament's procedural blocking of the November 2024 no-confidence motion and ordered parliament recalled by 8 April 2025 to debate it; parliament complied, debated the motion on 15 April 2025, and defeated it 89–16 (Supreme Court ruling, 31 March 2025; Lekmak analysis, May 2025). A judiciary that can void his budget sitting and compel his parliament to sit and vote on removing him is not a gripped judiciary.
  • The same court also ruled for him where the law was his: in 2025 a unanimous five-judge bench upheld Constitutional Amendment No. 48 (below) against the opposition's challenge, holding it did not tamper with the Constitution's basic structure — a court ruling in his favor on the merits, after ruling against him on the motion that preceded it, in the same dispute (PMNEC statement on the ruling, 2025). The pattern is of a court deciding both ways, which is what an ungripped court looks like.
  • The central bank's autonomy was, in the end, strengthened under his government rather than captured. Under the March 2023 IMF program (US$918 million ECF/EFF) his government committed to amend the Central Banking Act to strengthen the Bank of Papua New Guinea's mandate and autonomy, appointed a permanent governor, deputy governor and board members, and let the bank move the kina to a crawl-like arrangement and tighten monetary policy in its own name (IMF, second reviews, May 2024). No documented instance appears in the record of the bank being directed to finance the government or of a governor removed for refusing.
  • Media pressure from the government (below) was answered by the media, in the open, and the government moderated. After the Media Council of PNG's lobbying, the government softened its media-law plans; a parliamentary inquiry's recommendations were adopted by parliament — described by the Media Council's secretary as the first time in the country's history that had happened — and PNG's RSF ranking recovered from 91st in 2024 to 73rd in the 2026 index (RSF World Press Freedom Index 2026 reporting; Media Council of PNG statements).

Evidence against:

  • He changed the removal rules in the middle of being challenged under them. On 11–13 March 2025, with a no-confidence motion against him pending and litigation over it live, his government passed Constitutional Amendment No. 48: a prime minister who defeats a no-confidence motion may not face another for 18 months. The amendment was prospective — it did not save him from the pending motion, which he still had to win on the floor — but Devpolicy's analysis states its practical effect plainly: having won the April 2025 vote, Marape is shielded from further motions effectively to the 2027 election, and 84 MPs voted for the amendment against 4 (Devpolicy, 20 March 2025). The Supreme Court upheld the amendment's constitutionality; that settles its legality, not its character. Writing your own 18-month immunity into the constitution, from the seat the immunity protects, is gripping the rules even when a court lets you keep them.
  • The 2020 impasse began with his side gripping the parliamentary machinery. When a dozen of his ministers defected in November 2020, the Speaker — presiding over a government-aligned majority — recalled parliament over the majority's own adjournment vote, rushed the budget through a sitting of government MPs alone, and adjourned the house to April 2021, past the window in which a no-confidence motion could first be moved; the opposition's December 2020 motion then stalled when the Private Business Committee did not meet to vet it, and a 2021 Supreme Court ruling held that delay non-justiciable, leaving the loophole open (Supreme Court rulings, December 2020 and 2021; Lowy Institute analysis, October 2021). Marape's government benefited from each of these maneuvers and complied with the one ruling that went against it; both halves are the record.
  • His government reached for the media twice. In February 2023 it released a draft National Media Development Policy that envisaged licensing and registering journalists and recasting newsrooms as instruments of national development; RSF called on the government to withdraw it, PNG's press-freedom ranking fell 32 places to 91st in 2024, and RSF recorded that Marape had repeatedly criticized journalists for reporting the country's problems and said he would look to hold them accountable (RSF/BenarNews reporting, May 2024). In January 2024, during the state of emergency after the Port Moresby riots, his ICT minister threatened a complete shutdown of social media for the remainder of the emergency and prosecution for spreading "opinions that are wrong" (Post-Courier reporting, January 2024). The shutdown was not carried out and the media policy was moderated after lobbying (Test 3); the reach is recorded here.
  • His government first loosened the central bank's mandate before the IMF required it re-tightened. In late 2021 parliament, under his government, unanimously amended the Central Banking Act to broaden BPNG's objectives from price stability toward growth; the IMF judged the amendments to have "weakened" the Act and made reversing them a condition of the 2023 funded program, and the reversal benchmark then ran late — still outstanding at the IMF's May 2024 review (Devpolicy, November 2023; IMF, May 2024). The autonomy the bank now enjoys was restored under external pressure, not volunteered.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He submits to the votes he could lose. Ordered by the Supreme Court to recall parliament for the motion his own side had blocked, his government recalled it, debated it, and won it on the floor, 89–16, on 15 April 2025 (Lekmak, May 2025). Facing a further motion in 2025, Marape publicly welcomed it — "I welcome any challenge… Members of Parliament have the full freedom to make their choice without coercion, without fear, and without inducement" — stated he would not run political camps or behind-the-scenes lobbying, and said he would travel out of the country during the vote period to give MPs total freedom to choose in his absence (PMNEC statement, 2025). The statement is self-serving, as incumbents' statements are; it is also an on-record commitment against the camp-and-coercion methods that decide PNG confidence votes, made by the man with most to gain from them.
  • When the street exploded, he suspended his own officials first. On 10 January 2024, after a police and public-service stop-work over a pay reduction the government attributed to a payroll glitch, Port Moresby and Lae descended into rioting and looting in which 16 people were killed on the first count and 22 in later tallies. Within a day Marape declared a 14-day state of emergency in the capital, suspended the Police Commissioner and the secretaries of Finance, Treasury and Personnel Management pending investigation, and ordered administrative and criminal investigations into the payroll failure and the stop-work (AAP and PMNEC reporting, 11–12 January 2024). Six of his MPs resigned from the government in the aftermath; he did not resign, and told parliament the government would be removed on the floor of parliament or not at all.
  • He adjusted the media policy when the media pushed back — through a parliamentary inquiry whose recommendations parliament adopted, after which the government's position moderated and the country's press-freedom ranking partially recovered (RSF 2026 reporting; Media Council of PNG). Adjustment by inquiry and adoption is slower than adjustment by announcement; it is also more durable.
  • On the largest question before the state, he eventually moved the file rather than burying it. Bougainville's referendum result — 97.7% for independence in 2019 — was formally received by parliament on 27 August 2026, debated from October 2026 with Marape opening the debate ("we have no choice but to deal with this issue"), and set down for a ratification vote in the sitting week of 24 November 2026, with no result announced at the time of grading (Business Melanesia, 24 November 2026; Islands Business reporting, October 2026). The delays that preceded this are on the other shelf; the fact that the vote is happening at all, in his term, on his motion, belongs on this one.

Evidence against:

  • His first answer to the November 2024 no-confidence motion was not a vote but a procedural block: parliament's Private Business Committee disallowed the motion as substantively identical to the one defeated in September 2024, the opposition had to go to the Supreme Court to get its own motion heard, and the Court ruled the blocking unconstitutional (Supreme Court ruling, 31 March 2025). The open adjustment came only after the court ordered it.
  • His second answer was to change the rules (Test 2): the March 2025 constitutional amendment converting a defeated motion into 18 months of immunity was passed while the blocked-motion litigation was live. Answering a challenge by amending the constitution so the challenge cannot recur is a form of doubling down that happens to be lawful.
  • The emergency answer to Black Wednesday carried the grip with it. The state of emergency was accompanied by announced powers of warrantless search of homes, property, vehicles and phones, and by his ICT minister's threat to shut down social media entirely and prosecute "false information… opinions that are wrong… or hearsay" (Post-Courier and WSWS reporting, January 2024). The pay "glitch" explanation for the stop-work that triggered the riots was disputed by the affected public servants at the time — the government said administrative error, the strikers said a tax by another name — and the investigation he ordered has produced no public accounting in the record assembled here. Six MPs resigned from his government over the handling; the adjustment shelf shows suspensions and inquiries, the against shelf shows no published findings.
  • On Bougainville, the wobble was answered years late and with conditions added. The 2022 Era Kone Covenant committed the two governments to tabling the referendum result in parliament by the end of 2023; it was tabled in August 2026, almost three years late (ABG statement, 2026; Five Takes analysis, 2026). In June 2026 his government set a ratification threshold of a three-quarters majority — 89 of 118 MPs — which Bougainville's leaders dispute was ever agreed under the Melanesian framework, and Marape has proposed a negotiated transition of up to 15 years with Bougainville required to fund at least half (at points stated as 70 percent) of its own budget first, against Bougainville's actual internal revenue of about 5.5 percent of its spending (RFA and The National reporting, 2026; GCIRD analysis, July 2026). President Toroama has said publicly that "where agreed processes have been departed from, concerns have naturally arisen" and that trust is under strain (Five Takes, 2026). Bougainville's leaders have resolved on self-government by 1 September 2027 with or without Port Moresby, and have said they will pursue their own constitutional route if parliament fails to ratify.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Bougainvilleans, the highlands communities, the rural poor, women, the opposition's voters, and those who share the streets — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The one completed renegotiation of his tenure redistributed a resource project's take toward the people on whose land it sits. The government declined to renew the Porgera gold mine's lease in 2020 and closed the mine; the New Porgera that restarted on 22 December 2023 is owned 51% by PNG stakeholders — the state through Kumul Minerals, the Enga provincial government, and local landowners holding 15% equity plus a royalty raised from 2% to 3% — with PNG shareholders to receive 53% of the mine's economic benefits over its 20-year life, against the foreign partners' 49% equity share (Barrick and PMNEC statements, December 2023). Devpolicy's analysts note the detail of the 53/47 benefit split remains scantily documented; the equity structure itself is a matter of public record, and it is the first majority-PNG ownership of a major resource project.
  • Bougainville's 97.7% was, in the end, counted. Whatever the delays (Tests 3 and 5), it was Marape's government that brought the referendum result into the national parliament, opened the ratification debate in October 2026, and put the question to the 89-vote test in November 2026 rather than leaving the result unacknowledged in a drawer — the outcome Bougainville had been promised since the 2001 Peace Agreement (Business Melanesia, 24 November 2026).
  • The stated purpose of his largest spending program is the unconnected majority: Connect PNG's mandate is to link a country where roughly 85% of people live rurally and many districts are reachable only by air or sea. The program's delivery and integrity are contested (Test 1); the counting intention — roads to the counted-out — is the program's documented rationale, and physical road openings across multiple provinces are documented even by its critics.
  • The fiscal consolidation his government ran under the IMF program was executed without the state ceasing to function for the poor: the deficit narrowed from 4.3% of GDP in 2023 to 3.4% in 2024 while growth recovered to 4.6% in 2024 on the Porgera restart and commodity prices, and the IMF recorded all end-2023 quantitative performance criteria met (IMF, May 2024; Lowy Institute, 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The count itself failed at the election that returned him, and he was its beneficiary. At the 2022 general election, the electoral roll had not been updated: up to a million voters — in a population of about nine to ten million — were missing from it and thousands were turned away from polling stations; Freedom House records manipulation of voter rolls, politically compromised returning officers, fraudulently completed ballots and hijacked ballot boxes, with results declared in districts where boxes had been hijacked or burned rather than the elections declared failed; about 50 people were killed in election-related violence, including a young woman reportedly shot dead by police while queuing to vote in Port Moresby (CIVICUS Lens, 2022; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024). Marape did not create the roll's decay — responsibility is shared with predecessor administrations, as CIVICUS notes — but the election was run by his government, its failures fell overwhelmingly on ordinary voters rather than on incumbents, and no electoral-commission reckoning followed that appears in this record. The 2027 election preparations are, at grading, again his government's assurance to give (PMNEC, May 2026).
  • The highlands are counted in condolences more reliably than in protection. On 18 February 2024 an ambush in Wapenamanda, Enga Province, killed at least 49 people, combatants and bystanders — the toll was initially reported as 64, revised to 26, then rose to 49 as bodies were recovered (Wikipedia/AAP reporting, February 2024). Enga's governor stated the provincial government had warned the security forces the fight was coming and asked them to act beforehand (AAP, 19 February 2024). Marape responded with public anger, arrest powers for the military, and promises of life sentences; tribal fighting in Enga had already killed some 60 people in 2023, continued through 2024 — including fighting near Porgera that prompted a further state of emergency — and the government's legislative answer, the Counter-Terrorism Act 2024, reframes communal violence as terrorism rather than policing it preventatively (AAP, February 2024; analysis of the Counter-Terrorism Act 2024, January 2026). More than 50,000 illegal firearms are estimated in circulation (same analysis). The state he heads cannot currently protect the highlands' villages from each other, and the dead accumulate there uncounted by any inquiry.
  • The poor's share of the growth story is thin and contested. Roughly 37–40% of Papua New Guineans live below the poverty line on World Bank estimates, a share that has not materially moved across his tenure, and only about a fifth of the population has electricity (World Bank PNG Economic Update 2025, as reported). The kina's managed depreciation under the IMF program — more than a fifth against the US dollar since 2019 on the opposition's figure, a correction of a decade's overvaluation on the IMF's and the bank's — has raised the price of imported fuel, food and medicine for households with no hedge against it; former prime minister O'Neill's charge that "GDP does not put food on the table" is a political opponent's framing, recorded as such, and the cost-of-living pressure underneath it is documented independently in the January 2024 riots' own trigger: public servants took to the street over a pay cut of US$26–80 a fortnight (AAP/WSWS reporting, January 2024; IMF program documents, 2023–2024).
  • Women are nearly absent from the count of the counted. Two women were elected to the 118-member parliament in 2022 — of 167 women candidates against 3,458 men (Freedom House, 2024; CIVICUS, 2022) — and no reserved-seats or equivalent reform has been delivered in his seven years to change the structure that produces that number.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 30 May 2019, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The ICAC — promised 2019, delivered in law and in operation. Marape took office promising an independent anti-corruption commission; the Organic Law passed in November 2020, the commissioners were appointed through the independent process in July 2023, and by 2026 the commission was conducting investigations and arrests of senior officials with a rising budget (Devpolicy, 2025; PMNEC, 2026). The institution's internal collapse (Test 1) is a failure of the commission's first leadership, not of the promise to create it — though the promise's purpose, a working referee, remains only partly redeemed.
  • Porgera on new terms — promised under "Take Back PNG," delivered December 2023. He said resource projects would be renegotiated for majority PNG benefit; the mine he closed in 2020 reopened with 51% PNG equity and a 53% benefit share (Barrick/PMNEC, December 2023). The other projects in the promised wave — Papua LNG, Wafi-Golpu, P'nyang, Pasca — had not reached final investment decision at grading, seven years after he described them in December 2023 as "almost at their mature stage" (Lowy Institute address, 11 December 2023); the flagship kept, the wave still coming.
  • The IMF program — signed March 2023, substantially performed. PNG passed review after review of the US$918 million arrangement — the Lowy Institute notes four reviews passed where almost half of IMF programs fail before completing the first — with the deficit cut from 4.3% to 3.4% of GDP across 2023–2024 and all end-2023 quantitative criteria met (IMF, May 2024; Lowy Institute, 2025). Keeping an austerity program's word is a promise kept to a creditor rather than to voters, and the governance benchmarks lagged (Test 1); the fiscal word, as written, was largely kept.
  • The economy grew toward the target, on the nominal measure he chose. The K200 billion economy he set in his maiden speech of 30 May 2019 was measured from below K80 billion in 2019; the IMF put the economy at about K113 billion in 2022, and Marape stated in October 2026 that it stands at about K140 billion (PMNEC, 2023; OnePNG, 26 October 2026). The number moved in the promised direction at roughly half the promised speed, and how much of the movement is real rather than the kina's fall is contested between the government and the opposition (O'Neill statement, November 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • His own self-test, failed on its own terms. In his maiden speech of 30 May 2019 Marape promised to make PNG "the richest black Christian nation on earth" and set the K200 billion economy "by 2029"; in a speech of 19 June 2019 he was reported as saying he would assess himself as having failed if PNG was not the richest black nation by 2029 (maiden speech as quoted in his own December 2022 Sydney address; reported June 2019 remarks). By October 2026 the target had been re-dated in his own statements — "K200 billion by 2029–2030," then "K200 billion economy by 2030," with K300 billion by 2040 and K500 billion by 2050 added beyond it (PMNEC Reset@50 address, 2025; OnePNG, 26 October 2026). PNG in November 2026 has ~40% of its people in poverty, a corruption ranking at a decade low, and a fresh FATF grey-listing. The promise was always rhetoric-sized; he chose the size, and the re-dating is documented in his own words.
  • The UBS jailings — promised for after the 2022 election, not delivered. In 2022 Marape stated that all those implicated in the UBS deal "will be prosecuted and sent to jail," explaining that prosecutions had been put on hold until after the election and would proceed once his government was re-formed (PNG Facts reporting, 2022). Four years on, the documented yield is the false-evidence charges against O'Neill (June 2023) — charges about testimony to the inquiry, not the loan — and no completed prosecution of any participant in the transaction itself appears in the record assembled here.
  • The Era Kone deadline — missed by almost three years. The 2022 Era Kone Covenant between his government and Bougainville set the tabling of the referendum result in parliament by the end of 2023. It was tabled on 27 August 2026, after a further assurance — welcomed by the ABG — that it would come in the first half of 2026 (ABG statement, 2026; Five Takes analysis, 2026). The vote itself was scheduled for the week of grading, seven years after the referendum.
  • Law and order — the standing promise against the standing record. Marape has promised order at every crisis of his tenure; the record is Black Wednesday's 16–22 dead in the capital in January 2024, at least 49 dead in one Enga ambush the following month, continuing tribal wars through 2024–2026, and a Counter-Terrorism Act whose own analysts record death counts "persisting in the dozens" (AAP, January–February 2024; Counter-Terrorism Act analysis, January 2026). On the promise most felt by ordinary people, delivery has not come.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

James Marape is the first prime minister in this series whose file is defined by a paradox the evidence supports from both ends: he built referees stronger than any of his predecessors built, and the referees keep ruling against him — and he keeps obeying them. The Supreme Court voided his budget sitting in 2020 and he reconvened parliament; it ordered his parliament to debate removing him in 2025 and he won the vote it forced, 89–16; it upheld his self-protective constitutional amendment in the same dispute, because the amendment was lawful even though it was his. That is the grip test in one tenure: the rules were bent toward the incumbent repeatedly — a speaker's maneuvers in 2020, a media policy that reached for the register, an 18-month immunity written into the constitution mid-challenge — and the institutions bent back, every time, without being broken. His anti-corruption record has the same shape at larger scale: the ICAC, the UBS inquiry that named himself, the audit of his own roads program — real machinery, genuinely aimed — against an ICAC that imploded in mutual accusation, a flagship program run for years without its statutory trust account, a corruption index at a decade low of 26, and an FATF grey-listing earned on enforcement, the one thing the machinery was for. What he counts is real too: landowners holding equity in Porgera, Bougainville's 97.7% finally before the parliament that must answer it — against a million voters missing from the roll at the election that returned him, 49 dead in an Enga ambush the state was warned about, and two women in a parliament of 118. And his word is the file in miniature: the dated promises he made to institutions (the ICAC law, the IMF reviews, the Porgera terms) were kept with unusual fidelity, while the dated promises he made to the nation — richest black Christian nation by 2029, K200 billion by 2029, the UBS players in jail, Bougainville tabled by 2023 — were each, in his own later words, re-dated. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: the Bougainville ratification vote of this sitting week and what follows it, the Deloitte audit of Connect PNG, whether the ICAC's 2026 arrests reach verdicts, whether the FATF action plan is completed, and whether the 2027 election is run on a roll that counts the voters.
