      Purpose flights — somebody is waiting

      There is no money anywhere in this simulator — nothing to earn, buy, or win. Flying's real stakes were never money. They are these: a person at the other end who needs what you are carrying, a passenger trusting your hands, and your standing with the people who taught you. That is what the top of the ladder is for.

      Purpose flights unlock with trust. Earn the circuit endorsement (challenge 4) and the instructor starts handing you real jobs — the usefulness reward is capability itself: each sign-off below lets you do something you could not do before. A purpose flight only counts flown the way the waiting person needs it flown — the medicine run wants a checklist-clean, on-time arrival; your passenger wants a smooth one. Fail the standard and the flight honestly does not count. Nobody pays you. Somebody needed you, and either you showed up well or you did not — that is the whole stake, and in real flying it is usually the true one.

      The medicine run load — capacity is a real limit

      An aeroplane can only carry so much and stay inside its limits. Before the medicine run, choose the load — and feel what the choice costs. Heavier means the clinic gets more, and the aeroplane gives you less: a longer roll, a higher stall speed, a weaker climb. Sometimes the honest answer is two trips. Sometimes it is leaving the crates. It is never "everything, always" — that refusal is weight and balance doing its job.

      The why — all of it

      Every behaviour of the aeroplane above is explained here, in plain words. Tap a heading to open it.

      The four forces — and why a wing lifts (tap to close)

      Four forces act on every aeroplane, always: lift (up, from the wings), weight (down, gravity on the mass), thrust (forward, from the propeller), and drag (backward, the air resisting). In steady level flight they balance: lift equals weight, thrust equals drag. Change any one and the aeroplane accelerates — climbs, sinks, speeds up, or slows down — until they balance again. That single idea explains almost everything you will feel in the simulator.

      Why does the wing lift at all? A wing is shaped and angled so it pushes air downward as it passes. Push air down, and the air pushes the wing up — Newton's third law, the same reason a rocket rises by throwing exhaust down. The curved top surface also speeds the air over it, lowering pressure there (Bernoulli's effect), which adds to the lift — but the honest core is deflection: a wing flies by throwing air at the ground. No downward push, no lift, whatever the shape.

      Lift grows with the square of speed — double the speed, four times the lift — and with the wing's angle to the airflow, up to a point. That point is the stall, and it is the most important idea on this page.

      Angle of attack and the stall — it is about angle, not speed (tap to open)

      Angle of attack is the angle between the wing and the air coming at it — not the angle of the nose to the horizon. Pitch the nose up while climbing steeply and the airflow still comes from ahead-below; the angle of attack is the difference between where the nose points and where you are actually travelling.

      Raise the angle and lift rises — until about 15 degrees. Past that, the air can no longer follow the wing's top surface. It breaks away in turbulence, lift collapses, drag soars, and the wing stalls. The nose drops because the tail, still flying, pushes it down — the aeroplane's built-in plea for a lower angle.

      Because a stall is about angle, it can happen at any speed: fast in a steep turn (where banking raises the angle the wing needs), or slow and level on approach. "Stall speed" is just the speed where level flight demands the critical angle. Recovery is always the same first move: reduce the angle — nose down — then add power and let speed rebuild. Pulling up harder in a stall is the one instinct that makes it worse, and unlearning it is why students practise stalls at altitude.

      Ground effect — the cushion near the ground (tap to open)

      Within about one wingspan of the ground — roughly 35 feet for a trainer — the ground interferes with the wing's downwash. The air cannot be pushed down as freely, so the wing gets more lift and less drag for the same speed and angle. Pilots call it ground effect, and you will feel it twice: floating long on landing when you arrive too fast (the cushion refuses to let you down), and lifting off too early on takeoff, then sagging as you climb out of the cushion before the wing can truly fly. The simulator models it inside 40 feet — watch the badge, and watch a fast approach float.

      Weight and balance, in plain words (tap to open)

      Weight is simple and unforgiving: a heavier aeroplane needs more lift, so it must fly faster or at a bigger angle. Its stall speed rises, its takeoff roll lengthens, and its climb weakens. Try the Heavy setting below and measure all three — the numbers move exactly as the theory says.

      Balance is where the weight sits. An aeroplane balances on its centre of gravity like a seesaw. Too far forward and the nose is heavy — hard to rotate, hard to flare. Too far back and the nose is twitchy-light and, far worse, a stall can become unrecoverable because the tail lacks the leverage to push the nose down. That is why real loading is calculated, not guessed: every seat, bag, and litre of fuel has a known weight and a known distance from the datum, and the sums must land inside a printed envelope. This simulator fixes balance at a safe middle point and varies only weight — the envelope maths itself is the named simplification.

      Why checklists exist — and how pilots really run them (tap to open)

      Checklists were born from a crash: in 1935 a prototype bomber, flown by one of the most experienced test pilots alive, crashed on takeoff because a control lock was left in. The aeroplane was judged "too complex for any one memory" — and the checklist was the fix. The lesson stands: checklists are not for beginners; they are for humans. Memory fails under stress, hurry, and interruption, in experts most dangerously because they trust it.

      Real pilots run them two ways. For routine phases they use flow then check: do the actions from a practised pattern, then read the list to catch what the pattern missed. For anything interrupted, they restart the list — an interrupted checklist resumed from memory is how items get skipped. And they say items out loud, touching each control, because a spoken, physical check catches what a silent glance slides over. The two lists on this page are shortened versions of a real trainer's, and running them clean is counted in your record because the habit — not the items — is the skill.

      Circuit discipline — the pattern around the field (tap to open)

      Airfields run a one-way system in the sky: the circuit (called the pattern in the US). Take off into wind, climb straight, turn left onto crosswind, left again onto downwind — flying back parallel to the runway at circuit height, about 1,000 feet — then left onto base, and left once more onto final, lined up and descending to land. Everyone flying the same rectangle at the same height in the same direction is what makes a busy field predictable instead of chaotic.

      The discipline inside it: fixed heights, fixed turn points, radio calls at each leg, and a constant scan for the aeroplane ahead of you in the sequence. Downwind is where the before-landing checklist belongs — gear, flaps, speed — finished before base, so final is flown with both hands and full attention. Challenge 5 asks you to fly exactly that, and the map above draws the rectangle you are aiming for.

      The flight model, honestly — what is real here and what is simplified (tap to open)

      What is modelled for real: lift rising with the square of airspeed and with angle of attack up to a critical angle near 15°, then collapsing (the stall); drag rising with speed and steeply with angle; thrust falling as speed builds; weight set by the loading you choose; banking tilting lift so turns cost height and raise the stall speed; ground effect strengthening lift and cutting drag below 40 feet; headwind adding to airspeed but not ground speed.

      The named simplifications: one fixed aeroplane, a generic two-seat trainer — no specific make or model, and no real aeroplane's numbers. Balance is fixed mid-envelope (weight varies, centre of gravity does not). No engine failure, no icing, no turbulence beyond the gusty setting's wind swings, no other traffic, no radio. The air is always at sea-level density. The world is flat, the runway endless in patience if not in length. Numbers are rounded to teach the shape of the truth — a real trainer's handbook figures will differ in detail, and the handbook wins.

      StrategyHowYou gainYou pay

      The landing grade reads three numbers, as an instructor does: approach speed in the last 200 feet (aim 60–70 kt with flaps), descent rate at touchdown (under 200 feet per minute is a greaser, 200–500 firm and fine, over 500 is a thump that teaches), and touchdown point (in the first third of the runway — float past halfway and the honest move was a go-around).

      The go-around is a strategy, not a failure. Any approach that feels wrong — too fast, too high, floating long — gets full power, nose to climb attitude, flaps away in stages, and another circuit. Instructors grade a timely go-around higher than a rescued bad landing. The simulator counts go-arounds in your record with respect, not shame.

      Stall recovery: the order matters

      Two schools argue the emphasis. Angle-first (the modern standard): reduce angle of attack first, then full power — because power alone, pitched up, can deepen the stall. Power-first (the older habit): throttle up immediately, minimise height loss — it worked in docile trainers but can fail exactly when the stall is aggravated. This simulator grades angle-first: recovery counts when the angle drops below 10° and the wing is flying again, and the height you lose is printed beside it. Try both orders in challenge 6 and compare the altitude bill.

      The theory underneath: wind, gusts, and density (tap to open)

      Headwind is a gift: it adds to airspeed for free, shortening the takeoff roll and steepening the climb — you take off into it, always. Tailwind is theft: even 5 kt behind you stretches the roll and flattens the climb, which is why runways change direction with the wind. Crosswind pushes you sideways off the centreline and demands a crab or a wing-down slip; every pilot and aeroplane has a limit, and a new pilot's personal limit is far below the aeroplane's. Gusts are sudden angle-of-attack changes delivered by the sky: on a gusty approach pilots add half the gust factor to approach speed — extra energy as insurance against a sudden lull dropping the wing toward the stall. Hot, high, and humid air is thin air (density altitude): the wing and engine both perform as if higher up, rolls lengthen, climbs sag. The judgment rule that covers all of it: personal minimums — limits you set on the ground, in calm and writing, that the excitement of a waiting aeroplane is not allowed to renegotiate.

      Settings — the rules are settings

      Change a rule and a banner appears over the aeroplane naming it a variant, so a score under changed rules never pretends to be standard. Standard is: calm wind, standard weight, honest stall.

      SettingChoiceWhat it teaches
      Wind

      Weight
      		Heavier means a higher stall speed, a longer roll, a weaker climb. Measure it.
      Gentle stall

      Loading for the weight setting, in plain words: Light is you alone with half fuel. Standard is two people and full fuel. Heavy adds bags to the limit — legal, but every number gets worse, which is the lesson.

      Pilot energy — fatigue
      		Real pilots check themselves before the aeroplane — the IMSAFE check: Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Emotion. Turn this on and a long session drains your energy; below half, your control inputs arrive late and imprecise, the way fatigue really degrades a pilot — slower, sloppier, and worst of all, less aware of it. The Coach will name it. Rest restores you. It is a variant because it changes how the controls answer, and the record labels flights flown tired.
