The Ruler's Test — Ulisses Correia e Silva, Prime Minister of Cabo Verde

Subject: José Ulisses de Pina Correia e Silva — Prime Minister of Cabo Verde from 22 April 2016 to 19 June 2026 (10 years, 58 days). The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Cabo Verde is a semi-presidential republic in which the prime minister heads the government and holds executive power — the government, the budget, and public policy — while the president is head of state with reserve powers (veto, constitutional review, dissolution under conditions), not the principal policymaker. The premise supplied with this batch expected exactly this seat, and verification confirmed it — with one major update the brief anticipated and this dossier states up front: executive power changed hands in 2026. In the parliamentary election of 17 May 2026 the opposition African Party for the Independence of Cape Verde (PAICV) won an absolute majority — 37 of 72 seats on 48.04% of the vote in the final Comissão Nacional de Eleições (CNE) count, against 33 seats and 44.75% for Correia e Silva's Movement for Democracy (MpD) and 2 seats for UCID; an earlier provisional count, at 98.2% of polling stations, had put PAICV at 46.7% — the two figures are printed separately here and never blended (CNE final results, published 29 May 2026; provisional reporting, 18–19 May 2026). Correia e Silva conceded shortly after 03:00 on election night, congratulated PAICV leader Francisco Carvalho by phone, and later resigned the MpD leadership (his party tenure is recorded as ending 20 September 2026). Carvalho was sworn in as prime minister by President José Maria Neves on 19 June 2026. Because Carvalho's tenure is five months old at grading, this dossier grades Correia e Silva's completed ten-year tenure as the substantial record, and names Carvalho only where the record bears directly on a test — chiefly the prosecution file that followed him into office. The second 2026 election is also verified: in the presidential election of 15 November 2026, President José Maria Neves (PAICV) was re-elected in the first round. The counts conflict slightly between sources and are printed separately, never blended: the CNE final proclamation of 21 November 2026 is reported at 82,621 votes and 63.96% for Neves against 40,568 votes and 31.40% for Joana Rosa (MpD), on turnout of 31.67% (132,883 of 419,532 registered); a second published table of the CNE count gives 82,602 votes and 63.98% for Neves against 40,530 and 31.39% for Rosa, on turnout of 31.69% (CNE via contemporaneous reporting, November 2026). Abstention of roughly 68% was the highest recorded in a Cabo Verdean presidential election. Neves's re-election gives the PAICV the presidency and the premiership together for the first time in over a decade; it post-dates the graded tenure and is recorded as context, not as evidence against the subject. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test, printed verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out an elected programme is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes and never allowed to launder each other. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The baseline integrity numbers held at the top of the continent for his whole tenure, and improved on his watch. Cabo Verde scored 62 of 100 in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025 (35th of 182), up from 58 in 2020 and 58 in 2021, peaking at 64 in 2023 — second in sub-Saharan Africa only to Seychelles (68), against a regional average of 32 (Transparency International, CPI 2020–2025). Freedom House scores the safeguards against official corruption at 3 of 4 and government openness at 3 of 4 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026). His government created a Corruption Prevention Council in 2019, tasked with identifying corruption-risk areas and evaluating existing measures (contemporaneous institutional reporting).
  • The prosecutor's office reached his own side. Paulo Rocha, his Minister of Internal Administration, was the subject of a long-running judicial investigation into a 2014 police killing of a criminal suspect — an investigation the Attorney General's office publicly confirmed in December 2021, while Rocha sat in his cabinet — and the criminal process against Rocha continued after he left the ministry and became an MpD deputy; by August 2026 reporting he was facing criminal trial for aggravated homicide in that case (Attorney General's office statement via CPJ, December 2021; Cabo Verdean press reporting, August 2026). The underlying allegation against Rocha is an ALLEGATION until a court rules; what Test 1 grades is that the process was not visibly stopped to protect a sitting minister of the governing party.
  • The prosecutor's office also reached the opposition's leading figure — and the prime minister defended the prosecutor rather than exploiting or disowning the case. In December 2025, police acting for the Attorney General's office searched Praia City Hall and seized documents in an investigation of Mayor Francisco Carvalho's municipal administration (an instruction process opened in 2024 after a finance-inspector complaint); Carvalho denounced the searches as an MpD abuse of power — his characterization, recorded as an interested party's claim. Correia e Silva publicly warned against attacks on the Attorney General's office and framed what was at stake as "the stability of the system" (Plataforma Media reporting, 2025–2026; ARIES strategic assessment, April 2026). In July 2026 — after Carvalho had become prime minister — the Public Ministry formally accused Carvalho and Praia councillors of dozens of crimes (reported as 26 crimes for Carvalho) relating to municipal management (Expresso das Ilhas, 19 July 2026). Those accusations are ALLEGATIONS, unproven at grading; the one-law point is narrower: the same prosecution service pursued a sitting minister of the prime minister's own party and the opposition's leader under the same criminal procedure, and the prime minister's public position in the opposition case was to defend the prosecutor's independence.

Evidence against:

  • The Alex Saab case is the tenure's hardest one-law file, because the law that was followed was the domestic one and the law that was set aside was a binding regional judgment. Saab, a businessman indicted in the United States on money-laundering charges and described in US reporting as a close associate of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro, was arrested on Sal on 12 June 2020 during a refuelling stop, on the basis of a US-issued Interpol red notice; there was no bilateral US–Cabo Verde extradition treaty, and Attorney General José Landim defended the transfer on "reciprocity" grounds. On 15 March 2021 the ECOWAS Court of Justice ruled the detention unlawful — Saab had been detained before the red notice issued — ordered his release, and ordered Cabo Verde to pay €200,000 in compensation; the court's judgments are technically binding on member states. Two days later, on 17 March 2021, Cabo Verde's Supreme Court approved the extradition; the Constitutional Court denied Saab's appeal; the Supreme Court also declined a UN Human Rights Committee request to suspend the extradition; and Saab was transferred to US custody on 16 October 2021 (ECOWAS Court ruling, March 2021; CRS, Cabo Verde: In Brief, July 2024; contemporaneous reporting). The government and courts' position — that domestic constitutional process governed — is stated on the record; the disputants are named: the ECOWAS Court and the UN Human Rights Committee on one side, Cabo Verde's Supreme and Constitutional Courts on the other. A state that complies with the court judgment it agrees with and not the one it does not is applying one law at home and a chosen law abroad, and the shelf records it as such. Saab's own guilt was never adjudicated in Cabo Verde and is not graded here.
  • When the state's commercial interest was pressed, it acted by decree first and litigated later. In July 2021 the government retook, by decree, the 51% of Cabo Verde Airlines (CVA) it had sold to Loftleiðir Cabo Verde (Icelandair group) in March 2019, saying the partner had "not demonstrated the ability to guarantee the sustainability and continuity of the company's operations." Loftleiðir states — its claim, recorded as an ALLEGATION-level position in a live dispute — that the state "unilaterally decided to expropriate, by decree," its stake and terminated a March 2021 restart agreement, and it took Cabo Verde to the ICC International Court of Arbitration in Paris, the forum the contracts themselves named; the state disputes breach and says the partner failed to provide the agreed USD 30 million restart financing (ch-aviation, 30 November 2021; government statements, June–July 2021). The dispute went to the agreed arbitral forum, which is the lawful door; the sequence — decree, then arbitration — is what the shelf records.
  • The clean national numbers coexist with a documented lower shelf: bribery and nepotism at municipal level are recorded as persisting problems in the standard institutional summaries of the country throughout the period (Transparency International country material). No documented case in the record assembled here shows the prime minister personally directing a prosecution or blocking one; the against-shelf is about selective compliance at the top of the state, not about a documented personal protection racket.

Why this grade: In plain words — inside Cabo Verde, the same criminal process visibly reached a sitting minister of his own party and the leader of the opposition, the integrity indices stayed at the top of Africa, and he defended the prosecutor when the prosecutor's target was his chief rival, which is the moment one-law claims are usually exposed as false. The grade stops at MIXED because twice, when a powerful external interest pressed — Washington's extradition request, and the state's own stake in the national airline — the binding rule that lost was the one that stood in the state's way: a binding ECOWAS Court release order was not complied with, and a private partner's stake was retaken by decree before the agreed arbitration ran.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — ordinary litigants and the prosecution service itself, whose independence the prime minister publicly defended in December 2025; the travelling public's interest in a functioning state, insofar as integrity scores underwrote credit ratings and concessional finance. Hurt — Alex Saab (named; a detainee held and extradited despite a binding regional court order for his release — his underlying US charges are ALLEGATIONS, and he was later pardoned in the United States in a 2023 prisoner exchange, outside this grading); Loftleiðir Cabo Verde and its investors (named company; stake retaken by decree, remedy left to arbitration); Cabo Verdean taxpayers, who carry the ECOWAS compensation order and the arbitration exposure.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The referees' headline scores are the strongest in the region and did not deteriorate under him. Freedom House rates Cabo Verde "Free" at 92 of 100 (38/40 political rights, 54/60 civil liberties), unchanged across the 2025 and 2026 reports, with maximum 4/4 scores for the electoral process, for whether the elected head of government actually determines policy, and for free and independent media (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025 and 2026). The EU's GSP+ assessment for 2023–2025 records high compliance with core governance obligations (European Commission Joint Staff Working Document, 16 July 2026).
  • Cohabitation — the system's built-in wobble — ran for nearly five years without institutional seizure. From 9 November 2021 to June 2026 a PAICV president, José Maria Neves, held the reserve powers while an MpD prime minister governed; relations were generally characterised by cooperation rather than blockage, the government legislated through its assembly majority, and the president's party's July 2023 no-confidence motion against the government was debated and defeated through the ordinary parliamentary door (contemporaneous analysis, 2025–2026; Parliament of Canada mission report, March 2025).
  • The electoral referee ran three national cycles under him and seated his defeat. The CNE administered the 2021 parliamentary election (MpD 38 seats), the 2021 presidential election (won by the opposition's Neves), the December 2024 local elections (PAICV took 15 of 22 municipal councils, up from 8), and the May 2026 parliamentary election that ended his government — and he conceded on election night rather than contesting the count (CNE results, 2021–2026).
  • The central bank's autonomy was strengthened in law on his watch, not weakened: amendments to the Banco de Cabo Verde Organic Law were passed and recorded by the IMF as "an important governance reform" in the February 2026 Article IV review (IMF Press Release 26/036, 6 February 2026). The judiciary's capacity was likewise added to rather than raided: in October 2025 the government established a specialised Criminal Investigation Court in Praia and a new district court for São Miguel to attack documented backlogs (International IDEA democracy tracker, October 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The state media — which dominate the landscape and employ more than 70% of the country's journalists — were gripped in practice, on the regulator's own record. RSF's country assessment states that although an independent council has appointed the heads of Radiotelevisão Caboverdiana (RTC) since 2019, "in practice the council's decisions always coincide with what the government wants," RTC outlets "nearly always prioritise coverage of what the government is doing," self-censorship under state pressure is widespread, and the government "does not hesitate to restrict access to information of public interest" (RSF country file, 2025–2026). In February 2023 the government, acting as sole shareholder, removed the sole administrator of the state news agency Inforpress — an outlet with no independent appointment mechanism (State Media Monitor, 2026). In August–October 2025 the media regulator ARC confirmed and fined RTC board interference in the editorial autonomy of the state television channel TCV, whose director was suspended (State Media Monitor, 2026). Cabo Verde's RSF World Press Freedom Index position moved from 30th of 180 in 2025 (score 74.98) to 40th in 2026 (score 71.98) — still "satisfactory" and still among Africa's best, and the direction on his watch was down (RSF Index 2025, 2026).
  • The prosecution was used once, visibly, against journalists reporting on the government. In January–February 2022 the Attorney General's office declared three journalists — Hermínio Silves of the privately owned Santiago Magazine, and Daniel Almeida and Alexandre Semedo of the privately owned A Nação — formal suspects ("arguidos") for "qualified disobedience" / violation of judicial secrecy, over reporting on the classified investigation into the 2014 police killing that implicated the sitting interior minister (see Test 1); the journalists' association protested outside the Attorney General's office, and CPJ called on prosecutors to drop the investigation (CPJ, January–February 2022). The investigation does not appear to have led to charges (CRS, July 2024), and no journalist has been detained in Cabo Verde since 1991 (RSF) — but the suspect declarations barred the journalists from reporting on the investigation and stood for months as the state's answer to scrutiny of a minister. A later civil judgment (August 2026, after the graded tenure) ordered Silves and his outlet to pay damages to Paulo Rocha over the same reporting; the defence announced an appeal — recorded here as the file's continuation, not as the subject's act.

Why this grade: In plain words — the big referees were never gripped: the election commission seated his opponents twice and then seated his own removal, the central bank got a stronger organic law, the courts were given new capacity, and a hostile-party president cohabited with him for five years without a constitutional fight. The grade is MIXED, not HOLDS, because the one referee within daily reach of the executive — the state broadcaster and news agency that dominate a small media market — was steered in practice on the regulator's and RSF's own findings, and because the prosecution's one documented move against the press was aimed at journalists investigating his interior minister.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — voters of both parties, whose elections were run by a commission nobody credibly accused of rigging the count; the PAICV opposition, which governed municipalities and then the country through doors the incumbent left open; the Banco de Cabo Verde, strengthened in law. Hurt — the journalists Hermínio Silves, Daniel Almeida, and Alexandre Semedo (named; declared formal suspects in 2022 over public-interest reporting, and barred from covering the investigation); TCV's newsroom and its suspended director (role only; the regulator documented board interference in editorial autonomy in 2025); audiences of state media across all ten islands, who received government-prioritised coverage from the outlets that employ most of the country's journalists.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • The largest wobble of the tenure was met with declared emergencies and published measures, not denial. When COVID-19 collapsed the tourism economy in 2020 (the size of the contraction is reported differently by source and the figures are printed separately, never blended: −20.8% on INE figures as carried in the UN Country Analysis 2025; −19.3% in an earlier World Bank/IMF debt sustainability analysis), the government borrowed concessionally, expanded social protection through the Single Social Registry, and ran the vaccination campaign both major parties had promised in the April 2021 election, which voters then ratified by re-electing him mid-crisis (World Bank/IMF DSA, 2023; UN Country Analysis, 2025). The drought wobble was answered the same way: after four failed rainy seasons, the government declared a national drought emergency on 16 February 2022 and a state of calamity in 18 of 22 municipalities, with support measures for rural households (IFRC DREF Final Report MDRCV003, February 2025).
  • When his own flagship policy failed in the moment, he reversed it openly and said why. The privatised airline stopped flying in March 2020 and stayed grounded; in June 2021 the prime minister announced the renationalisation himself, in public, giving the reason — the partner had not demonstrated the ability to guarantee the company's sustainability — and naming the interest he was protecting, the company's roughly 300 employees (government statements via AFP/Barrons, June 2021). Whatever the merits (Tests 1 and 5), the adjustment was made in the open, with reasons, when another option — keeping the grounded airline alive and its workers paid — had effectively ceased to be available.
  • The voters' final pushback was answered with the cleanest move the Test recognises. On the night of 17–18 May 2026, with the provisional count showing his party beaten, Correia e Silva conceded shortly after 03:00, congratulated the winner by phone, and announced his resignation from the party leadership in the same statement — the third consecutive Cabo Verdean head of government to leave his party after losing a national election (Portugal Brief and CNE reporting, 18–19 May 2026). Power transferred on 19 June 2026 without a contested count, a caretaker crisis, or a refusal.
  • Parliamentary pushback was absorbed through the chamber: the PAICV's July 2023 no-confidence motion was debated and voted, not circumvented, and the cohabitation with a PAICV president (Test 2) required five years of continuous adjustment to a head of state from the opposing camp — adjustment performed without a single documented attempt to strip the presidency's reserve powers (Parliament of Canada mission report, 2025; contemporaneous analysis).

Evidence against:

  • The strategic wobble he never truly adjusted to was named to him for a decade: the economy's dependence on tourism. Diversification was his stated answer in 2016 and again in 2021; the documented outcome by the end of the tenure is deeper concentration — tourism at roughly 25% of GDP and some 55% of exports, record arrivals of 1.18 million in 2024 celebrated by his government, and the World Bank's 2026 Economic Update still describing growth as resting "on a narrow foundation," with activity concentrated on Sal and Boa Vista (World Bank, Cabo Verde Economic Update 2026; Michigan State globalEDGE risk assessment, 2025). When another option became available — the post-COVID rebuild, the Portugal debt-for-climate swap of January 2023, the special economic zones — he considered and used pieces of it, but the central exposure the voters and the Fund kept naming was answered with more of the same engine, run better. Considering an option and declining it with reasons is not against-evidence under the Test; a decade of stated diversification ending in deeper concentration is recorded here as the limit of the adjustment, graded under his word in Test 5.
  • Inter-island connectivity — the pushback every islander outside the tourism hubs raised — was answered with structures more than with service: a new state domestic airline (CVsky) was decreed in 2024 but only certified on 24 December 2025 and began flying on 29 March 2026, weeks before he lost office, after years in which the World Bank records domestic air services as "thin, expensive, and unpredictable, marked by repeated operator failures" (World Bank Economic Update 2026; CVsky certification record). The 2026 campaign promise of "more ships, planes and higher wages" was, in substance, an admission that the wobble was still open at the end of ten years.

Why this grade: In plain words — every acute wobble of the decade was answered in the open: a pandemic, a five-year drought, a grounded airline, a hostile-party president, a no-confidence motion, and finally the voters themselves, whose verdict he accepted the same night it was delivered, by phone call and public concession. The two slow wobbles — tourism concentration and inter-island connectivity — he acknowledged and only partly adjusted to, and those failures are real; they are graded where the Test puts them, under his word (Test 5), because on the record he did look at the other options and said what he was doing. On the question this test asks — adjust openly, or deny and double down — the documented answer, crisis by crisis, is that he adjusted openly, and the last adjustment was handing over the office.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the roughly 300 Cabo Verde Airlines employees whose jobs the 2021 reversal was publicly justified as protecting (named group by role); rural households in the 18 municipalities placed under the 2022 calamity declaration, who became eligible for drought support; the whole electorate in May–June 2026, which received an uncontested transfer of power. Hurt — the same airline's passengers and the outer islands, for whom the connectivity adjustment arrived only in March 2026; tourism workers and small guesthouse operators on islands other than Sal and Boa Vista, who bore the concentration risk he acknowledged but did not unwind.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the outer islands, the diaspora, women, the young, the informal workers, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The poor were put on a register and reached at scale. Social protection coverage rose from 43.6% of the population in 2016 to 60.3% in 2023 (ILO social protection profile). The Single Social Registry recorded some 324,500 people classified by poverty level (government delegation to the UN Human Rights Council, November 2023), and the productive-inclusion programme reached over 150,000 people — 87% of them women — across 28,000 poor households, with 86.6% of the poorest (Group 1) households assisted through regular or emergency transfers since 2020 (World Bank, 2023). The Social Pension reached some 26,000 elderly beneficiaries by 2023, and in December 2025 the prime minister announced a further rise to 7,000 escudos from January 2026 and a national minimum wage of 25,000 escudos to be set in 2026 for application in 2027 (Lusa, 26 December 2025) — announced under his government; delivery of the wage falls to his successor and is not credited here.
  • The headline poverty record moved the right way across the tenure on the official national measure: poverty fell from 35.5% in 2015 to 24.8% in 2023 on INE estimates, with extreme poverty down from 23.7% in 2015 to 12.7% in 2019 before the pandemic pushed it back to 13.1% (World Bank country page; UN reporting). Unemployment fell to 8.0% in 2024 and, on the World Bank's 2026 update, to 6.2% in 2025 (World Bank Economic Update 2026).
  • Women were counted into the legislature structurally, on his government's watch: the 2019 Parity Law, requiring at least 40% of each gender in electable list positions, produced a National Assembly of 34 women in 72 seats (47%) in 2026 — the second-highest share in Africa — and the diaspora was counted as always, its three emigrant circles electing six deputies, the bloc that in fact completed PAICV's 2026 majority (EJS Center analysis, November 2026; CNE 2026). Cabo Verde was declared malaria-free by the WHO in January 2024, a public-health outcome that counts every island's residents equally (UN reporting, 2024).

Evidence against:

  • On the international upper-middle-income measure, half the country was still poor at the end of the tenure: poverty at the UMIC line ($8.30/day, 2021 PPP) was estimated at 53.8% in 2024 and 51.2% in 2025 — improving, and still a majority (World Bank Economic Update 2026). The two poverty pictures (24.8% national line, 2023; 51.2% UMIC line, 2025) measure different lines and are printed separately, never blended; both are his record.
  • The young were counted least. Youth unemployment (15–24) stood at 20.1% in 2024 on the World Bank's employment programme document (a UN figure puts 2024 youth unemployment at 13.2% on a different definition — printed separately, never blended), nearly one in four people aged 15–35 was not in employment, education, or training, and 47.5% of all employed people worked informally (World Bank CORRE programme document, November 2025). Emigration — young Cabo Verdeans leaving for work and education abroad — was a central theme of both 2026 campaigns, with the MpD's own presidential candidate arguing young people needed reasons to stay (Nation Africa, 16 November 2026). Turnout tells the same story from the other side: participation fell from 66% in 2016 to 57.4% in 2021 to 46.46% in the May 2026 parliamentary election, and to roughly 31.7% in the November 2026 presidential election — a growing share of the counted deciding the count is not theirs (CNE figures via contemporaneous reporting).
  • Geography still decided life chances. Tourism and its income remained concentrated on Sal and Boa Vista; 90% of the extreme poor lived on four of the nine inhabited islands; inter-island transport stayed thin and expensive for most of the tenure (Test 3); and as of December 2024, 8% of the population — 41,299 people — faced acute food insecurity after the drought years, with child stunting at 14.8% (Cadre Harmonisé via UN Country Analysis, 2025).

Why this grade: In plain words — nobody was counted out by law or by category: the poor were registered and transferred to at a scale unusual in the region, women were written into the lists, the diaspora's six seats were decisive and honoured, and national poverty and unemployment both fell across the decade. The grade is MIXED because the accounting that improved on paper left the same groups carrying the weight at the end as at the start — the young (a fifth to a quarter outside work and training, leaving the country in visible numbers), informal workers (nearly half of all employment), and the outer islands — and because a majority of the population still sat below the upper-middle-income poverty line in the year his government celebrated upper-middle-income status.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the 28,000 poor households (over 150,000 people, 87% women) reached by productive inclusion; some 26,000 social-pension recipients; women candidates and voters under the Parity Law; the diaspora, whose deputies decided the 2026 majority. Hurt — young people aged 15–35, roughly one in four not in employment, education, or training, for whom emigration became the practical option; informal workers, 47.5% of the employed, largely outside contributory protection; residents of the non-tourism islands and the four islands holding 90% of the extreme poor, who paid the connectivity and food-insecurity costs of the model.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Programme pledges and public statements vs. the record, 2016–2026, with dates. The two axes — word-keeping and helped/hurt — are graded independently, and all four crossings are named.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly delivered):

  • Stability and sound money — kept, and it helped. His standing pitch, from the 2021 campaign onward, was that "stability… is our oil, our diamonds," with freedom, institutions, and checks on power as the priority (AFP interview, April 2021). The delivery is documented: post-COVID growth of 17.7% in 2022 (World Bank/IMF DSA figure; a UN per-capita account gives 15.8% for 2022 — printed separately, never blended), 7.2% in 2024, and an estimated 5.2% in 2025; inflation back to 1.0% in 2024 and around the 2% target in 2025; public debt down from its pandemic peak (152% of GDP at end-2021 on the DSA measure; a Fitch-cited series gives 147% for 2021 — printed separately) to 112.0% in 2024 and a projected 102.5% in 2025, with reserves at €1.06 billion in December 2025; every end-June 2025 IMF programme target met except one indicative target on social spending (IMF Press Release 26/036, 6 February 2026). Cabo Verde was reclassified an upper-middle-income country on 1 July 2025, and his government recorded the first fiscal surplus since 2007 in 2025 (World Bank, 2025–2026). This is the crossing kept + helped: the word was macroeconomic discipline, and households were helped through the price stability and resumed growth that followed the 2022 inflation shock.
  • The privatisation programme — pursued as written, for as long as it ran. The MpD's elected platform was economic liberalisation and a smaller state footprint, and per the mandate rule that platform is not graded as a failure — only its keeping is. He kept it: TACV's domestic routes were unbundled to Binter CV in 2017, 51% of the airline was sold to Loftleiðir in March 2019, and the seven airports were placed under a 40-year Vinci Airports concession (finalised 2023 after COVID delay) (contemporaneous reporting; IMF First Review, January 2023). This crossing is kept + hurt in part: the keeping earns its credit here, and the hurt is recorded in full — the privatised airline never achieved the promised Atlantic-hub viability, was grounded from March 2020, and its roughly 300 employees and its passengers carried the risk of a model that failed in the market it was sold into.
  • Eradicating extreme poverty — the machinery was built as promised. The PEDS II plan (2022–2026) and the National Strategy for the Eradication of Extreme Poverty were adopted, the Single Social Registry built, and the inclusion programme scaled toward the stated aim of reaching every extremely poor household by 2026 (Test 4). The promise's delivery machinery exists; its target is graded below.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or reversed):

  • Extreme poverty by 2026 — the tenure's clearest dated word, not kept. The government committed, in its own strategy documents and to the UN Human Rights Council in November 2023, to eliminate extreme poverty by 2026. The World Bank's current projection puts extreme poverty at 9.9% in 2026 — down from 13.1%, and nowhere near eradication (World Bank country page, 2026). This is the crossing broke + hurt: the word was broken on its own date, and the people hurt are the roughly one-in-ten Cabo Verdeans still in extreme poverty whom the promise named. No no-good-choice defence covers the whole gap: COVID and drought were genuine traps in 2020–2022 (see below), but the target was reaffirmed in the 2022–2026 strategy written after those shocks, with the shocks already known.
  • The airline privatisation — reversed by his own hand. Having sold 51% of TACV in 2019 as the centrepiece of the programme, he renationalised it by decree in July 2021 (Test 1). This is the crossing broke + helped, and both halves are recorded: the break — a flagship privatisation undone within one term, leaving an arbitration claim — and the help — a grounded national airline and its workforce kept in existence through the pandemic's end, returning to international service from December 2021. The no-good-choice clause applies to the timing, by its mechanics: the options actually open in mid-2021 were (a) leave the airline with a partner the state judged unable to fund the restart, risking liquidation of the flag carrier and its jobs in a country whose economy is air connectivity; (b) inject further state-guaranteed loans (USD 24.4 million had already been guaranteed) into a structure that had not flown in fifteen months; or (c) retake control and restructure under the state. Each carried real harm. He chose (c), stated his reason publicly, and did not build the original trap — the pandemic grounded the airline, not his government — though he had built the exposure by selling the carrier into a single foreign partner without a survivable fallback. Graded as the best available choice in the moment; the broken word still stands on this shelf, because the Test records the break and the help independently.
  • Diversification — promised in both elections, not delivered. Both the 2016 and 2021 campaigns pledged to diversify income away from tourism dependence; the 2026 World Bank verdict is a growth model still resting "on a narrow foundation," with SOE reform and privatisation timelines repeatedly pushed back — the IMF recorded in January 2023 that completion of the privatisation process for eleven key SOEs had slipped to 2026, and in 2025–2026 still flagged delayed SOE reforms as a fiscal risk (IMF First Review, January 2023; IMF Press Release 26/036, February 2026; World Bank Economic Update 2026). The COVID trap is named here in fairness: in 2020 the options were to defend the one engine the country had or to let it fail while alternatives were built — no good choice, and not held against him. What is held is the decade around the trap: the dependence deepened in the good years 2016–2019 and again in the record-arrivals years 2023–2025, when the room to diversify existed and was used instead to expand the same sector.
  • The last promises belong to the successor's watch. The December 2025 commitments — minimum wage of 25,000 escudos to be set in 2026 for 2027, social pension at 7,000 escudos from January 2026, 1,000 further social housing units — were made by a government with five months left to live (Lusa, 26 December 2025). They are recorded so the record can grade them later; they earn no credit here.

Why this grade: In plain words — the word he kept best was the one at the centre of both his elections: stability, discipline, and institutions, delivered through a pandemic, a drought, and an inflation shock, with debt falling and the country's income classification rising. The words he broke were the dated, checkable ones: extreme poverty was not eradicated by 2026, the economy was not diversified, and his flagship privatisation was reversed by his own decree — albeit in a genuine no-good-choice moment, and to the benefit of the airline's workers. A record with the central promise kept and the dated promises missed is a MIXED record, and the four crossings all appear in it: kept+helped (stability), kept+hurt (privatisation pursued into failure), broke+helped (renationalisation), broke+hurt (the 2026 poverty target).

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — all Cabo Verdean households, through inflation returned to ~2% and debt on a falling path; the airline's workforce, kept employed through the reversal; creditors and partners, whose programme targets were met. Hurt — the roughly 9.9% of the population projected to remain in extreme poverty in 2026, the named beneficiaries of the broken promise; tourism-dependent workers and outer-island households, who carry the undiversified model's risk into the next government; Loftleiðir Cabo Verde (named company), whose purchased stake was retaken.

Overall Summary

Ulisses Correia e Silva governed Cabo Verde for ten years and left the way the Test most wants a ruler to leave: beaten at a free election, conceding by phone the same night, and handing the office to his opponent on schedule. The decade behind that exit is genuinely two-sided. On one law, the criminal process reached his own interior minister and the opposition's leader alike, and he defended the prosecutor against his rival's attacks — but a binding ECOWAS Court order was set aside in the Saab extradition, and a private partner's airline stake was retaken by decree before arbitration (helped: the prosecution service and ordinary litigants; hurt: Alex Saab, Loftleiðir, and the taxpayers carrying both files). Without gripping, the election commission, the courts, and a strengthened central bank were never seized, and five years of cohabitation with a PAICV president passed without a constitutional fight — but the dominant state media were steered in practice on the regulator's own findings, and three journalists were made formal suspects for investigating his minister (helped: voters of both parties; hurt: Hermínio Silves, Daniel Almeida, Alexandre Semedo, and the state-media audience). The wobble is his strongest test: pandemic, drought, a grounded airline, a no-confidence motion, and electoral defeat were each answered openly, with declared emergencies, stated reasons, and a same-night concession (helped: drought-declared municipalities, the airline's workforce, the whole electorate; hurt: outer-island passengers who waited until March 2026 for the connectivity fix). Counts everyone is split between a real inclusion machine — a social registry covering most of the country, transfers reaching 150,000 people, women at 47% of parliament, the diaspora's seats honoured — and the groups still carrying the model at the end: the young, the informal half of the workforce, and the non-tourism islands, while a majority still sat under the upper-middle-income poverty line. His word divides the same way: the central promise of stability and discipline was kept and helped; the dated promises — extreme poverty gone by 2026, a diversified economy, a privatised airline — were missed or reversed, with the airline reversal the one break that also helped. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: the ICC arbitration over Cabo Verde Airlines, the criminal process against Paulo Rocha, the Public Ministry's case against his successor Francisco Carvalho (an ALLEGATION at grading, and the first one-law test of the new government, not of this one), and whether the Carvalho government delivers the minimum-wage and pension promises the outgoing government announced in December 2025.
