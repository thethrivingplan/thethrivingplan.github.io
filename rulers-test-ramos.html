The Ruler's Test — Américo Ramos, Prime Minister of São Tomé and Príncipe

Subject: Américo d'Oliveira dos Ramos — Prime Minister of São Tomé and Príncipe since 12 January 2025, reappointed on 12 November 2026 to head the XX Constitutional Government. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly, because this premise needed unusual care. São Tomé and Príncipe is semi-presidential. The president is head of state; the prime minister heads the government — the ministries, the budget, the state utility, the IMF programme, and day-to-day decision-making sit with the premiership, and the presidency is described in the electoral record itself as "in large part ceremonial, with power in the hands of the prime minister" (Portuguese Wikipedia, 2026 presidential election, summarising the constitutional order; corroborated by Afrikactus, July 2026: the presidency "remains a guarantor of institutional stability rather than a centre of day-to-day decision-making"). President Carlos Vila Nova (in office since 2 October 2021) is therefore not graded — but his hand is heavy in this file, and it is recorded wherever it bears on a test, because Ramos owes his seat to it twice over. Vila Nova dismissed the elected government of Prime Minister Patrice Trovoada on 6 January 2025, rejected two successors proposed by Trovoada's Independent Democratic Action (ADI) — Hélio Vaz de Almeida, then Adelino Pereira — saw his next pick, Ilza Amado Vaz, resign after three days (9–12 January 2025), and appointed Ramos, then governor of the central bank, who had not been endorsed by the ADI leadership (Africa Yearbook 2025, Brill; Lusa reporting, January 2025). On 15 January 2026 the Constitutional Court declared that dismissal decree unconstitutional, with effect only from that moment onward — the Ramos government stayed (UN Secretary-General's report on Central Africa, via ecoi.net, 2026; Lusa, January 2026). Ramos, born in 1957, is a banker: former finance minister (2014–2018, under Trovoada), former ADI secretary-general, and central bank governor until his appointment. On 27 June 2026 his faction held an extraordinary ADI congress, not recognised by Trovoada's faction, at which he proclaimed himself party leader; the Constitutional Court affirmed the congress and his leadership on 21 July 2026 (Lusa, 27 June 2026; Tela Non, 21 July 2026). Vila Nova was re-elected president on 19 July 2026 as an independent backed by the MLSTP–PSD, with 55.88% of valid votes on the Constitutional Court's final count (31,613 votes) against 41.46% (23,457) for Nito Abreu of the ADI; the electoral commission's provisional figures, printed separately and never blended with the final count, were 55.94% and 41.42%. Turnout was 41.43% on the final count — a record low, with abstention of 58.57% and diaspora abstention of 79.97% (Tribunal Constitucional final results; CEN provisional results via Lusa/Africanews, 20 July 2026). In the legislative election of 27 September 2026 the MLSTP–PSD won a plurality — 24 of 55 seats on 18,639 votes (37.45%) — ahead of Ramos's ADI on 23 seats and 18,168 votes (36.50%), a final margin of 471 votes; the commission's provisional count, again printed separately, gave the MLSTP 17,551 votes to the ADI's 17,184, a margin of 367. The MCI–PS/PUN coalition took 6 seats and the Basta movement 2; turnout was 37.26% (54,741 ballots of 146,918 registered), abstention 62.74% (Tribunal Constitucional, general count edital, 6 October 2026; CEN provisional announcement, 28 September 2026). No party held the 28 seats a majority requires. Vila Nova invited the MLSTP first — the presidency says it was given 24 hours, in one account 30 days, to produce written guarantees of parliamentary support, and did not; the MLSTP's leader Américo Barros disputes that account, saying the party proposed his name and could count its 24 deputies, Basta's two, and four others he did not name (Presidency statement via Lusa, November 2026; MLSTP press conference via Bugô/Lusa, November 2026; the 30-day version is African Street Journal's, November 2026 — the two accounts of the deadline are in conflict and both are recorded). The ADI produced a written parliamentary-incidence agreement with MCI–PS/PUN worth 29 seats, and on 12 November 2026 Vila Nova reappointed Ramos by Presidential Decree No. 32/2026; Ramos was sworn in on 16 November and an 11-minister cabinet (with one secretary of state) was appointed on 18–19 November 2026 (Presidency; Lusa/Africanews, 12–19 November 2026). MLSTP deputies petitioned the Constitutional Court on 13 November 2026 to annul the appointment decree, alleging the president had not heard the parties with parliamentary representation as the constitution requires; the court had not ruled as of this grading (Lusa via African Street Journal, November 2026; Apex Africa Strategy summary, 24 November 2026). Ramos is graded on roughly twenty-two months in office — January 2025 to November 2026 — spanning two governments (XIX and XX). The English-language record on this country of some 240,000 people is thin, and Portuguese-language reporting (Lusa, Tela Non, STP-Press) carries much of the file; where a shelf is genuinely thin after real effort, this dossier says so and grades accordingly rather than padding. Trovoada (prime minister 11 November 2022 – 6 January 2025) and Vila Nova appear as context where the record Ramos inherited, or the acts that seated him, bear directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic printed on the Test card governs the grading: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one; the Test grades the holding, never the platform. Ramos's position under that rule is unusual and is stated plainly: he was not elected to the premiership in 2025 at all — he was appointed against his own party leadership's nominees — and in September 2026 the voters gave his party second place; what mandate evidence exists is therefore his February 2025 government programme (Test 5) and the ADI's 2026 campaign, not a personal electoral platform.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The one clear cross-party act of the first weeks ran against his own camp's predecessor, not against the opposition: one of the new government's first decisions was to cancel the controversial airport-tax increase the Trovoada government had introduced in November 2024 — a charge that fell on every traveller regardless of party (Africa Yearbook 2025, Brill). Four of his first ten ministers had served under Trovoada, and his first cabinet kept office-holders from across the ADI's internal divide rather than staffing only his own faction (Africa Yearbook 2025; 360 Angola/Lusa, January 2025).
  • The fiscal law he governs under has been applied as written, to his own government first. The IMF Extended Credit Facility arrangement (approved 19 December 2024, under Trovoada) continued under Ramos with its targets published and its misses published: at the second review (19 December 2025) four of six end-June 2025 quantitative criteria were met and waivers for the two misses were granted openly, on stated grounds; at the third review (staff-level agreement, 22 June 2026) most quantitative targets were met (IMF press releases, December 2025 and June 2026). A government that publishes its own missed criteria is, on this file, giving by a stated rule.
  • The outcome measure has not deteriorated on his watch: São Tomé and Príncipe scored 45 of 100 on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025, unchanged from 45 in 2024 (70th of 182) — a mid-table African score, neither a purge's theatre nor a collapse (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released February 2026). No documented case in the record assembled here shows a Ramos-era prosecution aimed at an opposition figure, nor one shielding a named ally from a documented charge.
  • Ramos himself has stood on the receiving end of the law and been cleared by it, before this tenure: detained in 2019 over an alleged corruption file linked to a USD 30 million agreement, he was later cleared of the charges and awarded compensation of approximately €400,000 (360 Angola/Lusa, January 2025). The 2019 allegation is recorded here as an ALLEGATION that the courts resolved in his favour; it is evidence about the system's capacity to clear a man, not evidence against him.

Evidence against:

  • The seat itself was allocated by a process in which one law for all candidates did not operate — the same defect recorded at the source in the Iraq file, and stated here for the same reason: Test 1 grades the source. The ADI, holding an absolute majority from 2022 (30 seats), proposed Hélio Vaz de Almeida; the president rejected him as unsuitable. The ADI proposed Adelino Pereira; the president ignored the proposal and appointed Ramos, who had not been endorsed by the party leadership (Africa Yearbook 2025). The ADI's then leadership called the appointment a "palace coup" (360 Angola/Lusa, January 2025) — an interested party's characterisation, recorded as such. The Constitutional Court unanimously rejected the ADI's appeal against the appointment on 13 February 2025 (Africa Yearbook 2025), which settles the appointment's legality as the court then stood; it does not change the fact that the premiership changed hands by presidential preference among one party's names, not by the party's or the voters' choice. Ramos accepted the seat on those terms. Accepting is an act, and it is his.
  • His signature revenue measure created, on its face, two classes of citizen. Decree-Law No. 07/2025, approved by his Council of Ministers on 10 May 2025 and in force from 1 August 2025, sells São Toméan nationality by investment or donation — a minimum contribution of USD 90,000 for a single applicant, USD 95,000 for a family of two to four — administered through a Citizenship by Investment Unit under the Minister of Justice, with a ten-year exclusive public-service concession granted on 21 May 2025 to Passport Legacy, a company set up under Dubai law, as exclusive administrative partner (Lusa via Forbes África Lusófona and Golden Passport Record programme file, 2025–2026). The reported revenue split — 56% to the state, 44% to the private partner — comes from trade and agent sources, not from a published official text, and is recorded as reported, not verified (CitizenX programme analysis, 2026). The decree-law route meant the measure never passed the National Assembly as a law; ADI deputies — at that point the Trovoada wing of his own party — asked the Constitutional Court in August 2025 to declare it unconstitutional, and a group of lawyers including the Bar Association president sought an injunction in September 2025 to suspend the concession; no ruling on either challenge was found in the record as of grading (Forbes África Lusófona citing Lusa, August–September 2025). A nationality that a São Toméan holds by birth and a foreign buyer holds by wire transfer, under a decree the Assembly never voted, is a second law at the source — whatever the programme's fiscal merits, which are graded on Test 5.
  • Inside his own party, the succession was settled by a congress one faction does not recognise. The extraordinary congress of 27 June 2026 at which Ramos proclaimed himself ADI leader was not recognised by Trovoada's faction, which rejected it as illegitimate; the Constitutional Court affirmed it on 21 July 2026 (Lusa, 27 June 2026; Tela Non, 21 July 2026; African Quarters, 2026). A court ruling makes the leadership lawful as the court stands; the underlying fact — the party's crown taken at a congress its sitting leadership boycotted as unlawful — is the kind of insider track Test 1 exists to record.

Why the grade lands here: The governing record shows no selective prosecution, a fiscal programme applied and published even when missed, and a corruption-perception score that held steady. Against that stand two source defects that are his, not only the president's: he took a premiership his party's leadership had filled with other names, and his government then created a purchasable nationality by decree, through an exclusive foreign concessionaire, without an Assembly vote. Neither defect is a verdict of personal corruption — none is documented — but both are departures from one law for insiders and outsiders at the exact point where the law is made. MIXED.

Helped: Travellers and the tourism trade, by the cancelled airport-tax increase (January 2025); the treasury and the state's creditors, by a published IMF programme kept on track (IMF, 2025–2026); applicants under the citizenship programme — 548 applications from 72 countries in its first year, 350 approved and 12 rejected on the Investment Unit's own first-year report (UCID report, 1 September 2026, via trade coverage). Hurt: Hélio Vaz de Almeida and Adelino Pereira, the ADI's proposed candidates passed over for a man the leadership had not proposed; Patrice Trovoada and his faction, removed from both the premiership and, by June–July 2026, the party leadership; and São Toméan citizens by birth, whose nationality was made a priced product by decree while the Assembly's deputies and the Bar Association's lawyers were left to challenge it in court after the fact.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The referees have ruled against the executive that seated him, in public, and the rulings stand in the record. On 15 January 2026 the Constitutional Court declared the president's January 2025 dismissal decree unconstitutional — a judgment against the act that created Ramos's government, delivered by the court as then composed, signed by four of its five judges (UN Secretary-General's report via ecoi.net, 2026; Lusa, January 2026). On 13 February 2025 the same court had unanimously rejected the ADI's appeal against Ramos's appointment (Africa Yearbook 2025) — the court ruling for him once and against his maker once is the shape of a referee that is not simply held.
  • The electoral referees ran two national votes in 2026 under heavy outside observation, and election days were calm. The EU deployed an Election Observation Mission for both the 19 July presidential and 27 September legislative/local/regional elections (chief observer Sérgio Humberto; 30 observers in July, 49 on the September election day), alongside African Union (29 members), ECCAS, CPLP and g7+ missions; the EU mission reported orderly voting, slight opening delays at a small number of stations, improved procedures in September compared with July, and no incidents in its midday sample (EEAS, 2026; EU EOM diary and UAN World election-day reporting, September 2026). The UN Special Representative for Central Africa commended the authorities and electoral institutions for a generally peaceful campaign atmosphere ahead of the presidential vote (UNOCA, 17 July 2026). The EU final report, due within two months of the cycle's completion, had not been published as of grading; the preliminary record is what this shelf rests on, and it is labelled as such.
  • Civic space, on the monitors' counts, remained open under his government: CIVICUS rated São Tomé and Príncipe one of only two sub-Saharan African countries with civic space assessed as "Open" in 2025 (PEN International Africa overview, 2025, citing CIVICUS Monitor). Critical coverage of the government — including of the February 2026 court crisis and the November 2026 appointment dispute — circulated freely in Lusa, Tela Non and international outlets throughout the tenure.

Evidence against:

  • In February 2026 the parliamentary bloc that sustains his government moved directly on the Constitutional Court. On 2 February 2026, after the Assembly's speaker, Celmira Sacramento, blocked access to the parliament building, 29 deputies of the pro-government ADI faction together with MLSTP and Basta deputies met at the University of São Tomé and Príncipe under heavy police protection and voted to remove the speaker, to revoke the interpretative law on the judicial system, and to dismiss all five sitting judges of the Constitutional Court, and to elect the president of the Electoral Commission (UN Secretary-General's report via ecoi.net, 2026; Lusa, 10 February 2026). The revocation bill they passed would have terminated the judges' mandates early and recomposed the court; it was sent to the president for promulgation on 6 February 2026 (Lusa, 10 February 2026). This was seventeen days after that court had declared the dismissal that seated Ramos unconstitutional. The court declared the 2 February plenary unconstitutional and its deliberations null the same day (Channel Africa, February 2026) — but the speaker was nonetheless replaced by the first vice-speaker, and the deliberations were not accepted as null by the deputies who took them (Lusa, 10 February 2026).
  • The court's own headquarters was physically closed to its judges. The Constitutional Court reported that its premises, behind the presidency, had been closed since 2 February 2026 "by members of the Presidential Guard and the National Police, without any authorisation from the president or vice-president of this sovereign institution," preventing the presiding judges and all staff from entering, "with serious damage to the regular exercise of constitutional jurisdiction"; its vice-president, Kótia Menezes, ruled that the president was constitutionally barred from promulgating the judges'-mandates bill until the court could decide the preventive-review request filed by one-fifth of the deputies (Constitutional Court order via Lusa, 9–10 February 2026). The Presidential Guard denied interfering and affirmed its commitment to the law (Presidential Guard statement via Lusa, 10 February 2026) — the denial is recorded; Lusa's reporters verified armed National Police at the premises. The guard answers to the president, not to the prime minister, and this dossier does not lay the closure at Ramos's door as an order. What is laid at his government's door is the sequence: his sustaining bloc voted the judges out, police held the building, and no documented word or act by Ramos in defence of the court's access appears in the record assembled here.
  • The court that then decided his files was a recomposed court. Before the 2026 elections, parliament elected five new Constitutional Court judges — Artur Vera Cruz, Jonas Gentil, Liudmila Santos, Marta de Sacramento and Rolando Costa Neto (Channel Africa, 2026). It was this court that affirmed Ramos's contested ADI congress and leadership on 21 July 2026, the day after the presidential result was announced; that proclaimed the final results of both 2026 elections; and that, as of grading, holds the MLSTP's petition to annul Ramos's own reappointment (Tela Non, 21 July 2026; Tribunal Constitucional, 2026; Lusa, November 2026). Each step is individually lawful on its face — parliaments elect judges. The pattern is what Test 2 grades: the bench that ruled against the executive in January 2026 was voted out by the executive's bloc in February, and the bench seated afterwards has ruled for the executive's head in his party case and sits on his appointment case.
  • The electoral referee's lists were cut before the September vote, in ways that fell on smaller parties. The Constitutional Court rejected the MCI–PS/PUN coalition's lists in most constituencies over falsified candidate signatures, leaving it able to stand only in Água Grande, Caué and Príncipe; four further parties said they were kept off the ballot, two of them — Força Santomense and Partido Força do Povo — having been declared dissolved by Constitutional Court ruling 8/2026 of 29 January 2026 (ElectioMap compilation from court and commission records, 2026). The signature findings may be sound — falsified signatures are a lawful ground — but the record assembled here contains no independent audit of them, and the timing, weeks after the January ruling against the executive, is part of the pattern above.
  • The media file carries one documented punishment: in May 2025, after more than 200 people protested the arrest of a lawyer (Test 4), a journalist covering the demonstration was suspended by the national television network — the state broadcaster (US OSAC Country Security Report for São Tomé and Príncipe, 2025). No documented reversal of the suspension, and no statement by Ramos, appears in the record assembled here.

Why the grade lands here: A referee that rules against you in January and is voted out by your bloc in February, whose building is then held by police while its judges are locked out, and whose replacement bench affirms your party leadership and judges your appointment, is not holding its autonomy — even though every individual step wears a legal costume, and even though the January ruling itself proves the old court was free. The credit on the FOR shelf is real: two calm, heavily observed elections and an open civic-space rating. But Test 2 grades the grip, and between February and July 2026 the grip closed on the one referee that had bitten. The presidency shares this grade's weight — the guard is the president's and the appointment power is his — and that sharing is recorded; it does not lift the grade for the head of the government whose bloc cast the votes and who benefited from each ruling of the new bench. FAILS.

Helped: Américo Ramos himself, twice — a party leadership affirmed (21 July 2026) and an appointment challenge heard by the post-February bench; the ADI's pro-government faction, which kept the Assembly's chair after the speaker's removal. Hurt: The five Constitutional Court judges dismissed by the 2 February plenary and the court's staff, locked out of their own headquarters from 2 February 2026; Celmira Sacramento, removed as Assembly speaker by a plenary her own court-called-unconstitutional session produced; the smaller parties whose lists and legal existence were ended by the January–September 2026 rulings (MCI–PS/PUN in most constituencies; Força Santomense and Partido Força do Povo, dissolved); the suspended state-television journalist (role only — the record does not name the journalist, and this dossier does not either); and every future litigant whose case against the executive will be heard by a bench seated after the last one was removed for ruling the wrong way.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The sharpest institutional pushback of the tenure — the Constitutional Court's 15 January 2026 declaration that the decree seating him was unconstitutional — was absorbed without retaliation against the ruling itself and without a manufactured crisis. The ruling's prospective-only form was the court's own choice; the government's side of the bargain was to continue governing under a judgment that named its origin unlawful, and the Assembly majority adopted a resolution keeping Ramos in office ahead of the elections rather than forcing a confrontation (UN report via ecoi.net, 2026; Américo Ramos biography summarising São Toméan reporting, 2026). No emergency, no dissolution, no refusal appears in the record.
  • When the voters pushed back — his party lost the September 2026 plurality — he answered with negotiation in the open rather than denial of the result. The ADI concluded a written parliamentary-incidence agreement with the MCI–PS/PUN coalition (29 seats combined), whose leader Domingos Monteiro stated publicly that the agreement was intended to support the government for four years; Ramos presented that documented majority as the basis of his reappointment and, after being sworn in, said the rule of the majority is decisive (Lusa/Africanews, November 2026; Afrikactus, 13 November 2026). The MLSTP's competing claim — 24 seats plus Basta's two plus four unnamed others — was never documented in writing in the record; Ramos's was. If another option becomes available, the Test asks whether it was at least considered openly: the coalition door was the other option to a minority government or a forced MLSTP executive, and it was taken openly, with its arithmetic published.
  • The energy wobble was answered with adjustment machinery rather than denial. When the Turkish company producing electricity broke off contract renegotiations and ceased operations in August 2025, blackouts followed; the government's documented responses ran through the IMF programme's energy benchmarks — publication of the state utility EMAE's key performance indicators, alignment of domestic fuel prices with international benchmarks, a debt-for-climate-investment swap with Portugal announced in May 2025 to fund solar generation and transmission networks, and the Security Sector and energy-transition work reported to partners (Africa Yearbook 2025; IMF first review, 31 July 2025; Lusa, 27 May 2025). The crisis was not solved (Test 5), but it was not denied either: the IMF's published reviews name the electricity shock as the central drag on the economy, in documents his government co-signed.
  • The no-good-choice clause applies to the fiscal file, and its mechanics are recorded rather than used as a demerit. The trap: São Tomé and Príncipe entered 2025 assessed by the IMF as in debt distress due to prolonged unsettled external arrears (debt nonetheless sustainable), with a technically insolvent diesel utility whose fuel imports the state financed at 2.4% of GDP in 2025, reserves thin, and an ECF programme approved in December 2024 — under his predecessor — as the price of partner support (IMF, July and December 2025; World Bank country overview, 2026). The options that actually existed were: align fuel prices and consolidate, hurting households now (chosen); or hold prices and accumulate arrears and utility debt, hurting the same households later through blackouts and a reserves crisis (the 2022–2023 path, when net international reserves fell to near zero). Hindsight options — cheap renewable generation at scale — did not exist in the moment; the transition is a decade-long build. Ramos did not build this trap: EMAE's insolvency and the arrears predate him by years, a fact the IMF and World Bank records state plainly. He was, on the published record, honest inside it — the missed targets and waivers are in the IMF's own releases. What remains gradable, and is graded on Tests 4 and 5, is the distribution of the pain and the pace of the exit, not the choice of the least-harm door itself.

Evidence against:

  • His party's first answer to the September 2026 count was to dispute it. Before the final count, Ramos stated on his party's own tally-sheet count that the ADI had won 24 seats and the commission had called a "technical tie," while the commission's provisional figures already had the MLSTP ahead by 367 votes (Afreeque/Lusa reporting, September 2026). The final Constitutional Court count settled it — MLSTP 18,639, ADI 18,168 — and Ramos governed on the final count thereafter; but the first move, when the wobble arrived, was a rival count, not acceptance.
  • The street's one documented pushback of the tenure met silence in the record. In May 2025 more than 200 people protested the arrest of lawyer Miques João Bonfim — the legal representative of the sole survivor of the November 2022 barracks attack (Test 4) — and a journalist covering the protest was suspended by state television (US OSAC Country Security Report, 2025). No documented response by Ramos — no statement, no inquiry, no reversal — appears in the record assembled here. The protest was not dispersed by force on any account found; it was simply not answered.
  • The blackouts returned when the campaign ended. Social-media reporting from São Tomé in late October 2026 describes power cuts resuming on alternate days after a period of stability during the campaign period, with residents eating by candlelight and blaming EMAE (Facebook posts from São Tomé, 29 October 2026 — recorded as public perception, not as a verified outage log). A government that stabilises supply for the vote and not after it has answered the electoral wobble, not the energy one; the perception is recorded as perception, and the underlying fact it rests on — growth of 0.4% projected for 2026 "as the energy crisis continues to weigh on economic activity" — is the World Bank's (World Bank, 2026).

Why the grade lands here: Ramos absorbed the worst ruling a court can hand a government — your origin was unconstitutional — without breaking anything, and answered an electoral defeat with a published coalition rather than a stolen count. Those are real adjustments, openly made. Against them: a rival tally claimed before the final count, a protest and a journalist's suspension met with no documented answer, and an energy crisis managed in documents while the lights, on residents' accounts, followed the electoral calendar. He adjusts to institutions; the record does not show him adjusting to the street. MIXED.

Helped: The MCI–PS/PUN coalition, brought into a four-year governing agreement from six seats; development partners and creditors, given a government that negotiates in published documents (IMF reviews completed July 2025, December 2025, and at staff level June 2026); voters who wanted continuity of the economic programme, who got it. Hurt: MLSTP voters — 18,639 of them, the plurality — whose party's claim to form the government was answered first with a rival seat count and then with a coalition that locked them out; the May 2025 protesters and the suspended journalist, whose pushback received no documented answer; and households whose electricity, on their own accounts, was steadier during the campaign than after it.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Príncipe, the diaspora, the opposition plurality, women, the poor, and the people caught in the state's files — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The 2026 electoral accounting included everyone on paper, and the machinery was widened on his government's watch: a new electoral framework introduced automatic and permanent voter registration and made the National Electoral Commission a permanent body, and a parity law was applied for the first time, with women accounting for 41.4% of confirmed candidacies, above the 40% legal threshold (Channel Africa, 2026; São Toméan government statement to the UN General Assembly, September 2026, via Citizen Portal). Príncipe's Regional Assembly and regional government were renewed in the same 27 September vote, and EU observers were deployed to every district of São Tomé and to the Autonomous Region of Príncipe (EEAS/EU EOM, 2026).
  • His cabinets have carried women into the sovereign portfolios, by name: in the January 2025 government, Ilza Amado Vaz (foreign affairs), Vera Cravid (justice, parliamentary affairs and women's rights), Nilda Borges da Mata (environment) and Isabel de Abreu (education) held four of ten ministries; da Mata was elected president of the National Assembly on 10 November 2026 with, on one report, all 55 votes (360 Angola/Lusa, January 2025; CLSTR news summary, November 2026). The justice minister carried the country's conflict-prevention report to the UN Peacebuilding Commission in June 2026, including work toward a first National Action Plan on Women, Peace and Security (UNODC, 12 June 2026).
  • The diaspora was counted in the result that beat him: São Toméans abroad elect two Assembly seats, and in 2026 both went to the MLSTP (Konsulat von São Tomé e Príncipe, Berlin, reporting CEN results, 29 September 2026) — the governing party accepted a final count in which the diaspora's seats strengthened the opposition.
  • The social floor was named in his programme and partly defended in the fiscal file: his February 2025 programme's five axes included "promoting social justice and social inclusion" as a named axis (Lusa, 10 February 2025), and the IMF programme his government executed was renegotiated in December 2025 toward "a more gradual and less front-loaded fiscal adjustment" after the energy shock — the adjustment's pace was eased rather than the poor being handed the whole bill at once (IMF, 19 December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The largest counted-out group in 2026 is the non-voter, and the numbers are the story of his tenure's legitimacy. Abstention reached 58.57% in the July presidential election (diaspora abstention 79.97%) and 62.74% in the September legislative election — 92,177 registered voters of 146,918 did not cast a ballot in the vote that returned his government (Tribunal Constitucional, 2026). Turnout fell from 65.36% in the 2022 legislative election to 37.26% in 2026 on the comparative record. A government reappointed on 18,168 party votes in a country of some 240,000 people, after a term that began with an unconstitutional dismissal, governs a majority of the registered who stayed home. Disengagement is not solely a ruler's doing — it is recorded here as the accounting he now holds, not as a crime charged to him.
  • Two communities were counted out of the presidential vote entirely. Voting was cancelled in Ilhéu das Rolas and Praia Messias Alves, in the south of São Tomé island, after residents erected barricades and prevented ballot boxes from reaching polling stations (CEN via international reporting, 20 July 2026). The barricades were the residents' own act — a protest, on its face, about being counted at all — and no documented follow-up, rescheduled vote, or government answer to those communities appears in the record assembled here.
  • The fiscal adjustment was distributed downward, in the programme's own numbers. Fuel-price alignment with international benchmarks raised prices at the pump; inflation, after falling to a four-year low of 8.6% in April 2026, rose back to 9.2% year-on-year in May 2026 following the fuel adjustments; the fiscal balance swung from a 0.3%-of-GDP surplus in 2024 to a 3.3% deficit in 2025 as the state financed EMAE's fuel imports at 2.4% of GDP; and 13.1% of the population lived below the international poverty line (USD 3/day, 2021 PPP) in 2025, with limited job opportunities fuelling emigration (IMF reviews, 2025–2026; World Bank country overview, 2026). The young leaving are a counted-out group in the most literal sense: the September election was fought, on the reporting, over scarce jobs and "the continuing departure of young people" (IO Digest/AFP reporting, 27 September 2026).
  • The state's oldest open file counts four dead men and one survivor, and it did not close on his watch. In the 25 November 2022 attack on the army headquarters — before Ramos's tenure, under Trovoada's government and Vila Nova's presidency — four detained attackers died in custody: three, the armed forces chief of staff said, of wounds from an "explosion," and Arlecio Costa after he "jumped from a vehicle"; images appearing to show tortured suspects sparked outrage and calls for a probe (Reuters, 25 November 2022; AFP, December 2022). The sole surviving attacker was convicted and sentenced to 15 years (US OSAC report, 2025). Soldiers charged over the torture and killings saw the former army chief's charges dismissed in 2023 for lack of evidence; on 22 August 2025 the Constitutional Court rejected a prosecutor's request to decide whether the charged military personnel should be tried by a civil or military court (Africa Yearbook 2025) — the forum question, and with it the trial, still unresolved three years on, under Ramos's government and his justice ministry. In May 2025 the survivor's lawyer, Miques João Bonfim, was himself arrested — facing allegations of sexual misconduct, recorded here as ALLEGATIONs — and more than 200 people protested the arrest (US OSAC report, 2025). Bonfim subsequently stood as a presidential candidate in July 2026 and won 900 votes (1.59%) (Tribunal Constitucional, 2026). Whatever the merits of each file, the families of the four dead men have had no completed accounting from any government, including this one.

Why the grade lands here: The formal accounting genuinely widened — automatic registration, a parity law that put women at 41.4% of candidacies, Príncipe and the diaspora voting and counted, women in sovereign ministries. The material accounting narrowed: a plurality of the country abstained, two southern communities never voted, the adjustment's costs landed on fuel buyers and the poor while 13.1% lived under the international poverty line, and the 2022 custody deaths still have no completed trial. Everyone is on the roll; too many are missing from the count that matters. MIXED.

Helped: Women candidates and office-holders, under a parity law applied for the first time (41.4% of candidacies) and a cabinet with women in foreign affairs, justice and education; Príncipe's voters, whose regional institutions were renewed in the same vote; the diaspora, whose two seats were counted for the opposition that won them. Hurt: The 92,177 registered non-voters of September 2026, whose absence decided the election's shape; the residents of Ilhéu das Rolas and Praia Messias Alves, whose presidential vote was cancelled and not, on this record, restored; fuel buyers and the poor (13.1% under USD 3/day in 2025), who carried the price alignment; young São Toméans leaving for work abroad; and the families of the four men who died in custody in November 2022, still without a completed accounting.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Programme pledges and public statements vs. the record since 12 January 2025, with dates; the seat's inherited commitments stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The programme was delivered to parliament and its governance axis was partly executed. Ramos submitted his government's programme to the National Assembly on 10 February 2025, built on five axes — strengthening governance and proximity to the people; stimulating economic and social development; promoting social justice and social inclusion; administrative reform and modernisation; regional integration and proactive diplomacy (Lusa, 10 February 2025). Under the first and fifth axes, the documented record includes the National Conflict Prevention Strategy (2025–2029) presented to the UN Peacebuilding Commission in 2025 and reported on, with a first Security Sector Review approved by the Council of Ministers on 8 May 2026 and publicly released on 26 May 2026 — a review that, on the UN's account, "did not flinch" in naming political meddling in appointments and eroded public trust (UNODC, 12 June 2026).
  • The state's word to its creditors was kept, at the published standard. The ECF programme approved in December 2024 was a commitment of the São Toméan state that Ramos inherited and chose to honour rather than repudiate: first review completed 31 July 2025 (eight of ten end-December 2024 criteria met, two waivers openly granted), second review completed 19 December 2025 with a 12-month extension and augmentation his government requested and received, third review at staff-level agreement 22 June 2026 with most targets met (IMF press releases, 2025–2026). Inflation fell from 14.4% average in 2024 to single digits — 8.6% year-on-year in April 2026, a four-year low, before the fuel-driven rise to 9.2% in May (IMF; World Bank, 2026).
  • The citizenship programme was built as announced and reported on. Whatever its Test 1 defect, the programme his government decreed in May–August 2025 operated as described — applications from September 2025, first passport issued January 2026, and a first-year report (1 September 2026) publishing 548 applications, 350 approvals and 12 rejections (UCID first-year report via trade coverage, 2026). A government that publishes its own programme's numbers, including rejections, is keeping a reporting word.
  • The newest word, recorded so the record can grade it later: on taking office for the XX Government in November 2026, Ramos promised an inclusive government based primarily on competence, open to civil society and other parties, and stated: "I don't promise a government without mistakes… I promise a government that doesn't hide its mistakes" (Tela Non via Mundi Fact news summary, 23 November 2026; campaign statements via RTP/Lusa, November 2026). The 11-minister cabinet appointed 18–19 November 2026 includes returning ministers and new names (Presidential Decree No. 34/2026, via Konsulat von São Tomé e Príncipe, November 2026); whether it is inclusive or competence-based cannot be graded days after its appointment, and is not.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The development promise is receding in the programme's own numbers. Ramos told Lusa on 10 July 2025 that the country should "look forward to a better future," with improved economic and social indicators, increased wealth and employment, and better health and education (Lusa, 10 July 2025). The record since: growth of 1.1% in 2024 and 1.0% in 2025, revised down at each IMF review — the December 2025 review cut the growth forecast from 2.9% to 2.1%, and the June 2026 staff agreement cut 2025 to 1% and 2026 to 0.4%, with recovery deferred to 2027 (IMF, December 2025 and June 2026; World Bank, 2026). The direction of the revisions is the opposite of the promise's direction; the causes (electricity shock, external prices, emigration) are documented and partly outside his control, which is why this is recorded as receding, not as a lie.
  • The energy word is the clearest unkept one. The programme's development axis and the government's May 2025 announcements — a Portugal debt-for-climate swap whose conditions were, Ramos said, "now in place for signing," funding solar generation and the transmission network (Lusa, 27 May 2025) — promised a way out of the blackout economy. Eighteen months on: the Turkish producer's August 2025 exit was followed by frequent power cuts; the World Bank's 2026 overview states the energy crisis "continues to weigh on economic activity" with 0.4% growth expected for 2026; and no signed-and-delivering swap, nor a restored stable supply, appears in the record assembled here (Africa Yearbook 2025; World Bank, 2026). The swap may yet sign; on this record the word was given in May 2025 and the lights were still rationed in October 2026.
  • He never had the voters' word to keep in 2025, and the 2026 word is a coalition's, not a mandate's. This is stated as a structural fact, not a personal fault: appointed in January 2025 without an election, programme written after appointment, reappointed in November 2026 after his party finished second. The mandate rule protects a ruler who governs on the platform the voters elected; Ramos's platform was never put to the voters as a premiership bid until 2026, when it won 36.50% and second place. Test 5 therefore grades a thinner set of promises than in most dossiers — a February 2025 programme and public statements — and the grade carries that thinness openly.

The two axes, named separately, as the method requires: Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded independently, and the crossings documented in this file are: (1) Kept his word AND it helped — the inherited IMF programme honoured in public, which kept partner financing flowing (total ECF disbursements about USD 19.9 million by the third review) and brought inflation to single digits (IMF, 2026). (2) Kept his word AND it still hurt — the same programme's fuel-price alignment, a stated programme commitment executed as written, which raised pump prices and pushed inflation back up in May 2026, with the cost falling on households and the poor (World Bank, 2026); the keeping earns the credit above, and the hurt is recorded in Test 4's ledger in full. (3) Went against his word AND it helped — not documented in the record assembled here: no clear case was found of Ramos breaking a stated commitment to the public's benefit, and none is invented. (4) Broke his word AND it hurt — the energy file comes closest: the May 2025 "conditions in place" assurance on the debt-for-climate swap and the development promise of July 2025, followed by eighteen months of cuts and downward revisions borne by households and businesses (Lusa, May and July 2025; World Bank, 2026); it is recorded as a receding word with documented hurt, short of a proven deliberate break.

Why the grade lands here: The verifiable word — the creditor programme, the published targets, the programme delivered to parliament, the citizenship scheme's own reported numbers — was kept at the standard of a careful central banker, which is what he was. The aspirational word — a better future, more wealth and jobs, a way out of the blackouts — has receded at every published checkpoint for twenty-two months. A ruler who keeps the small print and misses the headline is not a liar; he is a man whose promises outran a 0.4%-growth economy he did not create but did volunteer to run. MIXED.

Helped: Creditors and development partners, whose programme was honoured and whose disbursements continued; price-stability's beneficiaries — every household and business that plans in dobras — from inflation brought from 14.4% (2024 average) to single digits; the 350 approved citizenship applicants, who received what the programme promised them. Hurt: Households and small businesses that planned on the July 2025 "better future" and the May 2025 energy assurances, and got 1% growth, 0.4% projected, and rationed power; job-seekers, above all the young, whose employment promise remains, on the September 2026 reporting, the election's unmet centre; and MLSTP voters, whose plurality word — 18,639 votes for a change of government — did not produce one.

Américo Ramos is a central banker placed in a politician's chair by another man's hand, and the dossier's shape follows from that origin.
