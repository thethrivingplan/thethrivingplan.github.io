The Ruler's Test — Évariste Ndayishimiye, President of Burundi

Subject: Évariste Ndayishimiye — President of the Republic of Burundi since 18 June 2020. The seat graded is the presidency, and it is the executive seat as actually held: head of state and government in substance, commander-in-chief, appointer and dismisser of the prime minister and government (the current prime minister, Nestor Ntahontuye, is his appointee of 5 August 2025), and general secretary turned dominant figure of the ruling CNDD-FDD, the former rebel movement that has governed Burundi since 2005. Ndayishimiye, a career CNDD-FDD general known as "Neva," won the May 2020 presidential election with roughly 71 percent of the official vote and was sworn in early, on 18 June 2020, after President Pierre Nkurunziza — whose disputed third term in 2015 triggered a crisis of killings, a failed coup, and the flight of some 500,000 Burundians — died suddenly on 8 June 2020, officially of cardiac arrest. He is graded on six and a half years in office. Two premise points are stated plainly at the top. First, the holder is confirmed: as of November 2026 Ndayishimiye remains president, was nominated by the CNDD-FDD in April 2026 for a second term, and filed his candidacy in November 2026 for the presidential election scheduled for 3 May 2027 (Africa Briefing, November 2026; Crisis Group CrisisWatch, October 2026). A second seven-year term is permitted by the 2018 constitution's two-term limit, so the candidacy itself breaches no rule and no promise, and is not graded against him anywhere in this file. Second, the June–July 2025 elections were legislative, senatorial, and local — Burundi held no presidential election in 2025, the presidency having moved to a seven-year cycle under the 2018 constitution. His predecessor Nkurunziza (president 2005–2020) appears as context where the record Ndayishimiye inherited — above all the Imbonerakure youth league's role and the post-2015 international isolation — bears directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic (printed on the Test card, above the five tests): "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one; the Test grades the holding, never the platform. Carrying out the CNDD-FDD program Ndayishimiye ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Marks are lost only for how power is held: a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4).

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The law under Ndayishimiye has, at moments, reached the very top of his own system. His first prime minister, Alain-Guillaume Bunyoni — a former police chief and one of the most powerful figures in the CNDD-FDD — was dismissed in September 2022, arrested in April 2023, and on 8 December 2023 sentenced by the Supreme Court to life imprisonment on charges including attempting to overthrow the government, illicit enrichment, and destabilizing the economy, with confiscation of houses, land, and 14 vehicles; the sentence was upheld on appeal in 2024 with a fine of 22.7 billion Burundi francs added (AFP via TRT Afrika and Burundi Times, December 2023 and 2024). Whatever else is said of that case below, the second-most powerful man in the state was not untouchable.
  • Other senior insiders have faced process: the central bank governor, Dieudonné Murengerantwari, was dismissed and arrested in October 2023 on charges of money laundering and misappropriation of public assets; the commerce minister Immaculée Ndabaneze was dismissed in 2021 after implication in the embezzlement of proceeds from selling aircraft of the defunct Air Burundi; in December 2022 police in Rumonge arrested three local officials and a governor's adviser, all ruling-party members, for alleged misappropriation of building materials, days after Ndayishimiye publicly vowed to combat corruption (U4 Anti-Corruption Resource Centre overview, June 2024; International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, December 2022).
  • The party's own youth league is not wholly outside the courts: in December 2022 the Bubanza High Court sentenced an Imbonerakure member to 20 years in prison for stabbing a man to death (International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, December 2022). In October 2026 the president launched the implementation of a national strategy against corruption and economic malpractice, naming embezzlement, concealment of revenue, and bribery as targets (LionWeek, 15 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The documented pattern of the anti-corruption drive is presidential discretion, not one law. The U4 overview records that the National Intelligence Service (SNR) conducts high-profile arrests of senior officials accused of corruption, that the government typically communicates nothing about the cases, that those arrested are interrogated secretly and occasionally forced to return funds, that they are rarely given prison sentences, and that some are reinstated while others are dismissed (U4, June 2024). The commerce minister dismissed in 2021 faced no judicial proceedings at all (U4, June 2024). Punishment falls by decision from above, in the dark, and can be lifted the same way — the opposite of a rule that binds the ruler's own hand.
  • The institutions built to apply anti-corruption law impersonally were dismantled on his watch: in April 2021 the National Assembly transferred away the powers of the Special Anti-Corruption Brigade, its prosecutor's office, and the Anti-Corruption Court created in 2006. Burundi's best-known anti-corruption campaigner, Faustin Ndikumana of PARCEM — an organization whose suspension Ndayishimiye's government had earlier lifted as a reform gesture — assessed the October 2026 relaunch as a reworking of earlier failed drives and warned that measures taken outside the existing legal framework "risk being used to punish some people while sparing others" (LionWeek, 15 October 2026).
  • The Bunyoni case, the for-shelf's flagship, cuts both ways on inspection. Bunyoni was dismissed days after Ndayishimiye publicly warned of a coup plot against him; the charges included using witchcraft to threaten the president's life; the Supreme Court sat at the prison where he was held; he pleaded not guilty and said the evidence did not exist; and human rights organizations reported his torture and mistreatment in detention — reports recorded here as ALLEGATIONs, contested by the authorities, who maintain justice was served (AFP reporting, December 2023; Great Lakes Eye, 2025). In early 2026 Bunyoni was provisionally released on medical grounds, the life sentence standing but its execution suspended by discretion (Burundi Times, 2026). A law that can imprison a former prime minister on a witchcraft charge and then quietly release him when his health fails is functioning as an instrument of the seat, in both directions.
  • Against the ruling party's own armed wing, the law barely operates at all. UN experts reported that between January 2024 and May 2025 there were at least 605 extrajudicial killings, 892 arbitrary detentions, 58 enforced disappearances, 62 acts of torture, and at least 200 reported cases of sexual violence including the rape of children in Burundi, with state agents or their proxies often implicated and the Imbonerakure playing a prominent role, especially around elections (UN experts, cited in Human Rights Watch World Report 2026). Human Rights Watch documented killings, enforced disappearances, arbitrary arrests, beatings, extortion, and intimidation by Imbonerakure members throughout the pre-2025-election period, with near-total impunity (HRW, 2025; International Crisis Group, 2025). The December 2022 Bubanza conviction stands in this file precisely because it is the exception that shows the rule.
  • One law also fails at the cash register: from 19 August 2024 the CNDD-FDD imposed compulsory "contributions" on the whole population — 1,000 to 5,000 Burundi francs per person, up to 100,000 for businesses — collected by Imbonerakure members and local officials through threats, denial of public services, and reprisals (Human Rights Watch World Report 2026). A levy demanded by the ruling party from every citizen, enforced by its youth league outside any tax law, is a second fiscal law for the party standing above the state's.

Why this grade: The deciding evidence is the contrast the record itself draws. The state's legal machinery is swift, secret, and severe when the president points it — at a former prime minister, a central bank governor, detained officials who are never publicly tried — and it is nearly inert before the documented, counted abuses of the ruling party's own youth league, whose members staff the ruling party's compulsory collections from the population. Severity tracks proximity to the president's favor, not the act committed. That is two laws, and the test fails.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the treasury and the public in the individual cases where embezzled assets were confiscated or officials removed (the Bunyoni confiscations; the Rumonge arrests); citizens in the rare localities where an Imbonerakure killer was actually prosecuted. Hurt — officials detained secretly by the SNR without public process, named and unnamed; Alain-Guillaume Bunyoni, convicted on charges including witchcraft and reported tortured in detention (ALLEGATION, contested); every Burundian household paying the party's compulsory levy under threat of losing public services; and the victims in the UN experts' counts, whose cases produced no prosecutions.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The referees have, on individual occasions, ruled against the script. In June 2025 the Mukaza Court of Appeal annulled the conviction of journalist Sandra Muhoza on jurisdictional grounds, declaring her first trial and arrest warrant null — a court voiding a prosecution the state had pursued for over a year (RSF/IFEX, April 2026; Human Rights Watch World Report 2026). The Bubanza court's 20-year sentence of an Imbonerakure member (Test 1) is a second instance of a court ruling against the ruling party's own.
  • Early in the tenure the grip visibly loosened: the media regulator (CNC) lifted the ban on the Iwacu news site's comments in February 2021 and authorized banned outlets such as Bonesha FM to resume broadcasting; several new radio and television channels were authorized; the suspension of the anti-corruption organization PARCEM was lifted (Human Rights Watch World Report 2022). The African Union praised the June 2025 elections as peaceful (International IDEA election assessment, 2025), and Burundi's national human rights commission (CNIDH) appeared before the UN Human Rights Council in September 2026 presenting data on investigations into abuses by public officials (GICJ report on HRC63, September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The electoral referee was gripped before the 2025 vote was cast. The main opposition party, the CNL — which had won 27 of 100 elected seats in 2020 — was suspended by the Interior Ministry in June 2023, and its leader Agathon Rwasa was removed from the party leadership in a process Human Rights Watch described as a government-orchestrated internal coup (HRW, 2025). In December 2024 the electoral commission (CENI) disqualified the candidates of the opposition Burundi Bwa Bose coalition, including Rwasa, applying an eligibility rule — a two-year bar on party-leavers running — that had been extended by presidential decree (Brill Africa Yearbook 2025; IPU Parline, 2025). The April 2024 electoral code set the presidential candidacy fee at 100 million Burundi francs, roughly USD 34,700 and more than 100 times GDP per capita (ARIES Consult Burundi report, May 2026; UN Special Rapporteur, September 2026). The result: on 5 June 2025 the CNDD-FDD won 96.5 percent of the official vote and all 100 directly elected National Assembly seats, and on 23 July 2025 all 13 Senate seats (CENI results via IPU Parline and International IDEA, 2025). The Constitutional Council confirmed the results on 20 June 2025 and rejected the opposition's appeals as unfounded (Brill Africa Yearbook 2025). A new administrative division introduced before the elections was widely characterized by Burundian public opinion as gerrymandering (Brill Africa Yearbook 2025).
  • On election day the grip was physical: Imbonerakure members stood at polling stations openly instructing voters to choose the ruling party, and Catholic Church observers — the country's most credible domestic monitors — reported ballot stuffing and forced voting, with some observers turned away from polling places (HRW, 2025; International IDEA, 2025; Catholic observer reports via MEWC, 2025). Official turnout was reported at 98.9 percent (International IDEA, 2025).
  • The judiciary answers to the presidency in the open. In 2021 Ndayishimiye publicly accused the judiciary of corruption, and 40 judges were dismissed and sentenced for misconduct or corruption (U4, June 2024); in August 2022 the dismissal of 40 magistrates by presidential decree was publicly praised by the presidential press office (PANA, 12 August 2022). The Muhoza case shows the limit of the for-shelf's court ruling: after the Mukaza Court of Appeal annulled her conviction in June 2025, she was not freed — she remained in Mpimba prison, was transferred to Ngozi, and was retried on the same charges and sentenced to four years in January 2026, before a further appeal in April 2026 reduced the sentence to six months already served and she was finally released, two years after her arrest for a message in a journalists' WhatsApp group (CPJ, January 2026; RSF/IFEX, April 2026). The referee whistled; the game continued anyway.
  • The external referees are refused entry. Burundi has kept the UN human rights office (OHCHR) in the country closed, refuses to cooperate with the UN Special Rapporteur on Burundi, and denounced his mandate at the Human Rights Council in September 2026 as a "targeted witch hunt"; UN special procedures issued a joint statement in December 2025 on the refusal of cooperation, and the UN Committee Against Torture found in 2025 that Burundi had imposed reprisals on lawyers who cooperated with it (GICJ, September 2026; UN joint statement, December 2025). Most Burundian human rights organizations investigating violations work from exile (HRW World Report 2026). In September 2025 the country director of the Belgian development agency Enabel was reportedly ordered to leave the country (HRW World Report 2026).
  • The legislature the referees produced contains no opposition at all — 108 of 111 Assembly seats held by the CNDD-FDD, the remaining three co-opted Twa representatives — and ahead of the May 2027 presidential election the field has narrowed further: the FNL endorsed Ndayishimiye, Sahwanya-FRODEBU announced on 10 October 2026 it would not field a candidate for lack of electoral guarantees, and in November 2026 eleven small opposition parties pledged to campaign for the incumbent (Crisis Group CrisisWatch, October 2026; Anadolu via Africa Briefing, November 2026).

Why this grade: Every referee that could check the seat has been suspended, priced out, disqualified, dismissed by decree, retried around, or locked out of the country: the opposition party suspended and decapitated, its candidates barred by the commission, the results confirmed by the council, forty magistrates removed after presidential accusation, a journalist kept in prison after her conviction was annulled, and the UN's monitors refused entry altogether. The surviving for-shelf items are real but they are courtesies the grip permits, not checks the grip survives. The test fails.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the CNDD-FDD's candidates and officeholders, who now face no institutional competition; the eleven parties that traded endorsement for proximity. Hurt — the CNL's voters, roughly a quarter of the 2020 electorate, whose party was suspended and whose candidates were barred; Agathon Rwasa, removed from his party's leadership and disqualified as a candidate (he has since filed an independent candidacy for 2027, reported November 2026); Sandra Muhoza, who spent two years in detention after a court voided her conviction; the dismissed magistrates; Catholic election observers turned away from polling stations; and Burundian voters generally, offered a 98.9-percent-turnout election with one possible winner.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, he at least considers it openly; considering and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence, refusing to look is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest wobble of the tenure — total international isolation after 2015 — was answered with genuine adjustment in 2020–2022. Ndayishimiye resumed political dialogue with the EU in February 2021 (suspended since 2016), produced a reform roadmap, pardoned the four Iwacu journalists in December 2020, released the human rights defender Germain Rukuki in June 2021 after his 32-year sentence was overturned, decreed pardons or early release for more than 5,000 prisoners in March 2021, and lifted media and civil-society bans (HRW World Report 2022; World Bank country review, April 2023). The pushback's demands were, in part, met — and the response came: the UN Security Council ended mandatory reporting on Burundi in December 2020, the United States lifted its sanctions in November 2021, and the EU lifted its sanctions and resumed direct support in February 2022 (World Bank, 2023; EU Council statement, February 2022).
  • Under economic pressure he changes personnel and says why, in public. When the revenue office reported a deficit of 110 billion francs, he dismissed finance minister Audace Niyonzima in December 2024 and appointed Nestor Ntahontuye; after the June 2025 elections he replaced Prime Minister Gervais Ndirakobuca with Ntahontuye on 5 August 2025 and reshuffled the government around the economic file (Burundi Times, August 2025; Brill Africa Yearbook 2025). Inflation, which peaked at 45.5 percent in April 2025, had eased to 15.2 percent by December 2025 (ARIES Consult, May 2026).
  • When another option became available in regional diplomacy, he has at times taken it: Burundi reopened its border posts with Rwanda in 2022 without its standing demand — extradition of the 2015 coup suspects — having been met, and Burundian and Rwandan foreign ministers met at the border (East African Report retrospective, 17 November 2026). In November 2026, officials of both countries met and agreed that Rwandan forces and M23 would not occupy Uvira, the Congolese town on Burundi's border (East African Report, November 2026). At home, when five opposition parties sought a meeting in May 2025 over the electoral commission's composition ahead of 2027, he received them (Anadolu via Africa Briefing, November 2026).
  • The December 2025 fall of Uvira is graded under the no-good-choice clause. When M23 seized Uvira on 10 December 2025, the options that actually existed were these: keep Burundian forces — reported at up to 18,000 in the DRC in 2025 — fighting a Rwanda-backed force at the border, risking a direct Burundi–Rwanda war and heavy casualties in a city of Congolese civilians; or pull back, concede the border town, and absorb the consequences at home. Each carried real harm. Burundi reduced its exposed presence, and absorbed over 90,000 Congolese refugees in a matter of weeks (UNHCR, December 2025–February 2026). The choice is recorded as the best possible available in the moment and is not held against him. What remains gradable is the trap's origin: the scale of the DRC deployment was his own strategic choice, made over years, and its costs — reported discontent and rifts within the army as war costs mounted (Critical Threats, September 2026) — are part of this file's record, as is the separate question of the deployment's honesty, graded in Test 5.

Evidence against:

  • The adjustment of 2020–2022 was reversed when the pushback stopped being about entry and started being about evidence. Faced with the UN Special Rapporteur's documented findings — including, in September 2026, a warning to "act before it's too late" ahead of the 2027 election, citing exclusion, intimidation, and impunity as a repeating pre-election pattern — the government's answer at the Human Rights Council was to denounce the mandate as a Western "witch hunt," describe the 2025 elections as regular and peaceful, and refuse cooperation; the OHCHR office remains closed (GICJ, September 2026; UN joint statement, December 2025). That is refusing to look, and under this test it counts.
  • The May 2025 meeting with the five opposition parties ended with the parties stating their concerns about the electoral commission had not been adequately addressed, and nothing in the commission's composition or the candidacy rules changed afterward (Anadolu via Africa Briefing, November 2026). He heard the option; the record shows no stated reasons for declining it and no change — the weakest form of considering, short of refusal.
  • On Rwanda, the public line escalated rather than adjusted. After the December 2023 RED-Tabara attack near the DRC border — an attack that killed 28 people across December 2023–February 2024 (ARIES Consult, 2026) — Burundi closed the entire border with Rwanda on 11 January 2024, accusing Kigali of backing the group; Rwanda denied it (East African Report, November 2026). In March 2025 Ndayishimiye told the BBC he had "credible intelligence" that President Kagame planned to attack Burundi; Rwanda's foreign minister responded that both sides had already agreed military and verbal de-escalation (BBC, March 2025, via East African Report). The border remains closed as of November 2026, with Catholic bishops of both countries publicly pleading on 12 November 2026 for its reopening (ACOREB meeting report, November 2026). The plea is unanswered on the record assembled here.
  • The fuel crisis — nearly six years of shortages, the defining wobble of daily life — has been answered mainly with blame assigned downward. Ndayishimiye attributes the shortages to foreign-exchange constraints, smugglers, and dishonest traders (Great Lakes Eye, 2026); in March 2026 Burundi received roughly 35 percent of its estimated petrol requirement and 56 percent of diesel (FEWS NET, June 2026), motorists were rationed to 20–30 litres at a time in 2026, and citizens smuggling fuel across the Ruzizi river faced arrest and up to two years in prison (The Continent, 30 August 2025; IMF via Great Lakes Eye, 2026). The constraint is real and partly inherited — the 2015 donor withdrawal gutted the foreign-exchange position — and under the no-good-choice clause the shortage itself, and the hard allocation choices inside it (subsidize from an empty treasury, ration, or let prices and food costs run), are not held against him. What is gradable is what he built around the trap: the IMF identifies the dual exchange-rate system — official rate beside a far higher parallel rate — as a continuing driver of shortages, smuggling, and weak investment, and it remains in place (IMF via Great Lakes Eye, 2026; ARIES Consult, 2026).

Why this grade: The record splits cleanly in time. From 2020 to 2022 he answered the world's pushback with releases, unbannings, and dialogue, and the world answered back with sanctions relief — adjustment, openly done, that worked. Since 2023 the answers have narrowed to personnel changes inside his own camp and denial aimed outward: the rapporteur refused, the election findings rejected unexamined, the border held shut against his own bishops' plea. He still adjusts tactics and people; he no longer adjusts to evidence about the holding itself. MIXED is where that split lands.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the prisoners released in the 2020–2021 opening (the Iwacu four, Germain Rukuki, and the roughly 2,600 of the 5,000-plus pardoned who had actually been released by late May 2021, per HRW); Burundian exporters and the treasury when sanctions lifted and EU support resumed; border communities during the 2022 reopening; Congolese civilians of Uvira, spared a two-army battle for their city in December 2025. Hurt — Burundian traders, transporters, and divided families along the Rwanda border, closed since January 2024; motorists, bus passengers, and businesses living under rationing and black-market prices near USD 7 a litre (The Continent, August 2025); the opposition parties whose May 2025 concerns were heard and left unaddressed; and the Burundian public, whose documented grievances about the 2025 elections were answered at the UN as a foreign plot.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition, the Tutsi and Twa minorities, the Banyamulenge, the refugees on his soil and the returnees, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The constitutional architecture of counting survived his tenure intact and was applied in 2025: the National Assembly's 111 seats include 11 co-opted members to ensure ethnic and gender balance under the Arusha settlement, among them three Twa representatives, and women won 39 of the 100 elected seats, rising to 44 of 111 (39.6 percent) with co-option — up from 35 percent in 2020 (IPU Parline, 2025; MEWC election monitoring, 2025). The vice-presidency is held by Prosper Bazombanza of UPRONA, a Tutsi (cabinet records, 2025).
  • Toward the foreigner on his soil, the record is genuinely open-handed. Burundi granted prima facie refugee status to Congolese fleeing the M23 offensives (UNHCR, September 2025); it received more than 36,000 arrivals in February 2025 and, after Uvira fell, over 90,000 more in weeks — UNHCR counted over 110,000 Congolese refugees in Burundi by February 2026, with total hosted Congolese refugees reported above 200,000 (UNHCR, 2025–2026). The government publicly acknowledged the camps had far exceeded capacity and, on 18 December 2025, launched an emergency response plan with a USD 33.2 million interagency flash appeal (Christian Post/UNHCR reporting, December 2025). Burundian refugees abroad were also counted back in: voluntary repatriation from Tanzania accelerated, with 9,815 returning between October 2025 and mid-January 2026 (UNHCR data via RVO, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • In the autumn of 2026 the state counted one community out by name. Through September–October 2026 the National Intelligence Service arrested members of the Banyamulenge community — Congolese Tutsi — including religious leaders in Bujumbura and refugees at the Bwagiriza camp; more than 25 Banyamulenge refugees were detained from October; and on 9 October 2026 the government publicly accused 13 detained Banyamulenge of spying for Rwanda and M23 and urged the public to monitor Banyamulenge and stop renting them homes (International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, October 2026; SOS Médias Burundi via Crisis Group). The spying charges are ALLEGATIONs, unproven on the record here; the official call for an ethnic community to be surveilled and denied housing is documented government speech, and it counts a whole people as suspects.
  • The opposition's voters were counted out of the country's future in 2025 (Test 2): the CNL barred, its leader disqualified, Imbonerakure instructing voters at polling stations, and a parliament seated with zero opposition members. A count in which 96.5 percent is the only available answer has not counted the other answers.
  • The poor are counted as a revenue source before they are counted as citizens: the CNDD-FDD's compulsory contributions (Test 1) are extracted from a population of whom 65.1 percent live below USD 2.15 a day (World Bank figure via ARIES Consult, 2026) — a separate count puts 74.3 percent below the USD 3 line (World Bank via Great Lakes Eye, 2026); the two figures are printed separately because they measure different lines. Some 56 percent of Burundian children under five are chronically malnourished, among the world's highest rates, and in 2025 the World Food Programme cut daily rations for the vulnerable from 75 to 50 percent of recommended intake for funding reasons (World Bank/AfDB/WFP via ARIES Consult, 2026). Average inflation of roughly 34 percent in 2025 fell hardest on those with nothing (World Bank via Great Lakes Eye, 2026).
  • Even the welcomed refugees were counted into conditions that killed some of them. At the Gatumba transit camp, more than 40 refugees died in a short period in December 2025, deaths attributed by the refugee deputy mayor of Uvira to cholera, poor sanitation, hunger, and lack of medical care — an attribution recorded as his statement, not an official finding (AfroVera, December 2025). At the Busuma site, local sources reported 22 refugees dead in a single day in late January 2026 amid overcrowding and water and sanitation failure (Le Journal Afrique, January 2026). The two tolls are kept separate, as reported, and neither has been officially confirmed or refuted on the record assembled here. The government's own acknowledgment that capacity was exceeded is the mitigating fact, and it is on the for-shelf; the deaths are on this one.
  • The baseline count underneath everything remains the UN experts' tally for January 2024–May 2025 — 605 extrajudicial killings, 892 arbitrary detentions, 58 enforced disappearances, 62 acts of torture, at least 200 reported cases of sexual violence — in which the victims are overwhelmingly Burundians who fell outside the ruling party's protection: opposition supporters, suspected dissenters, and the rural poor (UN experts via HRW World Report 2026).

Why this grade: The for-shelf is real — the Arusha quotas held, women advanced, and a poor country opened its border to 200,000 fleeing neighbors when richer states might have closed theirs. But the test asks whether everyone is counted, and in 2025–2026 the record shows three groups counted out in three different ways: the opposition's electorate, disenfranchised wholesale; the Banyamulenge, marked by name for surveillance and housing denial by their own host government; and the victims in the UN experts' counts, for whom no counting — no investigation, no prosecution, no acknowledgment — has followed. A state that urges its citizens to stop renting homes to an ethnic minority has answered this test in its own words. FAILS.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the 110,000–200,000+ Congolese refugees granted prima facie status and shelter, however strained; the 9,815 Burundian returnees from Tanzania; women candidates, whose Assembly share rose to 39.6 percent; the Twa, whose co-opted seats were preserved. Hurt — the Banyamulenge community, detained by the intelligence service and publicly marked for surveillance and housing boycott; the refugees who died at Gatumba and Busuma in conditions the state acknowledged were beyond its capacity; the CNL's electorate; child nutrition across the country (56 percent stunting); and every household paying party levies and 34-percent inflation out of incomes among the lowest on earth.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 18 June 2020, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The reopening — kept, and it delivered. Ndayishimiye came to office promising to end Burundi's isolation and restore relations with its partners. He resumed the EU dialogue in February 2021, produced the reform roadmap the EU asked for, and made the early releases and unbannings that partners cited; the United States lifted sanctions in November 2021 and the EU lifted its sanctions and resumed direct financial support in February 2022, expressly citing the process begun with the 2020 elections and the government's commitments (EU Council, February 2022; World Bank, 2023). This is the mandate rule's cleanest case in the file: he said he would bring Burundi back into the world, and the world came back.
  • The pardons — performed. The March 2021 decree pardoning or releasing more than 5,000 prisoners was issued as promised; roughly 2,600 had been released by late May 2021, with the gap between decree and release recorded here as part of the record (HRW World Report 2022).
  • The second-term candidacy — no word broken. He has never promised to serve one term; the 2018 constitution permits two seven-year terms; his November 2026 filing for the 3 May 2027 election is within the word of the constitution he operates under, and this file records it as such.
  • The anti-corruption word — repeatedly spoken and repeatedly enacted in form. From his December 2022 vow to fight corruption through the October 2026 launch of the national anti-corruption strategy, the promise has been restated and institutionally performed — dismissals, arrests, confiscations (Tests 1 and 5 against-shelf carry why the performance does not amount to the promise's substance: a fight conducted by one law).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or reversed):

  • The Imbonerakure pledge — reversed. On taking office Ndayishimiye promised to rein in the ruling party's youth league, a promise partners and Burundians both recorded (HRW World Report 2022). Six years on, the league enforces the party's compulsory levies on the population, stands at polling stations directing votes, is documented by UN experts as prominent in killings, disappearances, torture, and detentions through 2024–2025, received paramilitary training in Cibitoke province ahead of deployment to the DRC (UN Special Rapporteur, September 2025), and in October 2026 its provincial leaders were urging members to monitor their communities — in line, SOS Médias Burundi reported, with the president's stated ambition to transform the Imbonerakure into "commandos" (Crisis Group CrisisWatch, October 2026). The promise was not merely unkept; its opposite was built.
  • Justice and political tolerance — reversed. The 2020 pledges of justice and tolerance produced, by 2023–2025: the main opposition party suspended and its leader removed in a government-orchestrated internal process (HRW), its candidates disqualified, a candidacy fee set above 100 times GDP per capita, and a parliament with no opposition. The roadmap commitments on human rights that unlocked EU support were described even at the time as vague and noncommittal on impunity (HRW World Report 2022); the impunity the roadmap avoided is the UN experts' 2024–2025 record.
  • The development word — receding. His governing promise, repeated across the tenure, is an emerging Burundi by 2040 and a developed one by 2060, in a country where, in his own slogan, every mouth would have food and every pocket money. Six years in: GDP per capita is roughly USD 292–320, among the world's lowest; inflation averaged about 34 percent in 2025; fuel — the economy's blood — has been short for nearly six years, with March 2026 supply at about a third of petrol needs; foreign-exchange reserves stand at 1.6 months of imports (World Bank, IMF, FEWS NET via ARIES Consult and Great Lakes Eye, 2026). Direction is a defense available to a new government; at six years, with a second term being sought, the gap between the word and the wallet is the record.
  • The word to a fellow president — contested, and printed as contested. At an August 2026 press conference in Kigali, President Kagame stated that he had telephoned Ndayishimiye and asked whether Burundian forces were in North Kivu, that Ndayishimiye answered "We have no troops there," and that "a couple of weeks after, his forces were captured in North Kivu" (Kagame press conference, 24 August 2026, via East African Report). Burundi's large deployment in South Kivu alongside the Congolese army is documented and openly conducted (Critical Threats, September 2026); the North Kivu denial, and the alleged capture, rest on Kagame's account, which Burundi disputes. The item is recorded as a contested claim by an interested party — Rwanda's president, mid-rivalry — not as a finding; it is included because a head of state's word to another head of state, disputed on the record, belongs in this test's file with its disputants named.

Why this grade: His word divides by audience. To foreign partners, in 2020–2022, he promised an opening and performed it, and the receipts — sanctions lifted, support resumed — are on the for-shelf in full. To Burundians he promised the three things that would have changed the holding itself: the youth league reined in, justice and tolerance, and bread-and-money development. The first was reversed into expansion, the second into exclusion, and the third has receded every year of the tenure. A ruler who keeps his word to the referees abroad while inverting it at home has kept the smaller half. FAILS.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Burundi's treasury, exporters, and aid-dependent services, by the kept reopening word; the prisoners freed under the performed pardon decree; partners who dealt with him in 2020–2022 and got what was promised. Hurt — Burundians who voted in 2020 for the tolerance and justice pledges and received the 2025 exclusion; communities living under an expanded, levy-collecting, election-staffing Imbonerakure; households whose pockets the development word was supposed to fill, now paying black-market fuel prices and 2025's inflation; and — if Kagame's contested account is accurate, which this file does not find — the region's last channel of direct presidential trust.

The four crossings, named: 1. Kept his word and it helped: the reopening — the dialogue resumed, the roadmap produced, the releases made, and sanctions lifted on the strength of it (2021–2022). 2. Kept his word and it still hurt: the Rwanda border. He vowed the border would stay closed while Rwanda shelters the 2015 coup suspects Burundi demands be extradited; the vow has been kept since 11 January 2024, Rwanda has not extradited, and the keeping falls on Burundian and Rwandan border traders, transporters, and divided families — while informal trade quietly continued (Rwandan exports to Burundi of USD 13.39 million in Q2 2026, via East African Report) and both countries' bishops pleaded in November 2026 for the door he keeps shut. 3. Went against his word and it helped: the 2022 border reopening — the extradition condition was quietly set aside, the foreign ministers met at the border, and border communities traded and moved again for two years. The departure from the stated condition is recorded here as a break; the help is recorded in full beside it. 4. Broke his word and it hurt: the Imbonerakure pledge — promised restraint, delivered expansion into levies, polling stations, and paramilitary training, with the hurt counted in the UN experts' figures in Tests 1 and 4.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Évariste Ndayishimiye inherited a state that had spent five years as a pariah: sanctions on, donors out, a youth militia doing the ruling party's violence, and half a million citizens in exile. His first two years were the most promising in this series' African files — the Iwacu journalists pardoned, Rukuki freed, thousands pardoned, banned stations back on air, the EU and US sanctions lifted on the strength of promises he was visibly beginning to keep (kept-word-and-helped, crossing 1, in full). The grades fail because of what the same record shows next. On Test 1, the law moves by presidential pointing — life imprisonment for a former prime minister on charges including witchcraft, secret intelligence-service arrests for officials, confiscations and quiet medical releases — while the party's own league collects compulsory levies from the population and answers for almost none of the UN experts' counted 605 extrajudicial killings, 892 arbitrary detentions, and 58 enforced disappearances of January 2024–May 2025 (helped: the confiscating treasury; hurt: the secretly detained, the levy-paying public, the unprosecuted dead). On Test 2, every referee was gripped before it could rule: the CNL suspended and decapitated, its candidates disqualified by the commission under a decree-extended rule, a candidacy fee above 100 times GDP per capita, forty magistrates dismissed by decree, a journalist held two years past the annulment of her conviction, the UN's monitors denounced and locked out — leaving a parliament of 108 ruling-party seats and three co-opted Twa (hurt: a quarter of the 2020 electorate, first of all). On Test 3, the split is temporal: real adjustment in 2020–2022, reshuffles and an easing inflation rate under economic pressure since, but denial at the center — the rapporteur refused a look, the bishops' November 2026 plea unanswered, the fuel crisis blamed downward while the dual exchange rate the IMF names as a driver stays in place; the December 2025 Uvira withdrawal and the fuel shortage itself are graded under the no-good-choice clause and not held against him, while the DRC deployment that built the Uvira trap, and the exchange regime that deepens the fuel one, remain his (helped: Uvira's civilians, spared a two-army battle; hurt: border communities under a kept closure — crossing 2 — and a rationed nation). On Test 4, the Arusha quotas held and a poor country sheltered over 200,000 Congolese refugees — and then, in October 2026, urged its citizens to surveil the Banyamulenge and stop renting them homes, while refugees died of cholera and crowding in camps the government admitted were beyond its capacity (helped: the sheltered and the returning; hurt: the Banyamulenge, the Gatumba and Busuma dead, and the 56 percent of children growing up stunted). On Test 5, the word kept abroad was inverted at home: the league expanded instead of reined in, tolerance became exclusion, and the promise of food in every mouth and money in every pocket meets a GDP per capita under USD 320 and a sixth year of empty pumps (crossing 4; the one softening entry is crossing 3, the 2022 reopening that broke his own extradition condition and helped the border live). The open files that could move these grades are named: whether the May 2027 election admits a real contest, whether any Imbonerakure member of rank is ever prosecuted, whether the bishops' door reopens, and whether the Banyamulenge are counted back in.
