The Ruler's Test — Andris Kulbergs, Prime Minister of Latvia

Subject: Andris Kulbergs (first name verified from official and reference sources as Andris, not Andrejs) — Prime Minister of Latvia since 28 May 2026. A businessman from the motor trade (Lattelecom marketing; managing director of Inchcape's Latvian arm and of Baltic Motors; president of the Latvian Authorised Automobile Dealers Association), he entered parliament in 2022 as a formally non-partisan figure on the United List (AS) ticket, chaired the Saeima's parliamentary inquiry into Rail Baltica's cost overruns, and ran unsuccessfully for the European Parliament in 2024. When Evika Siliņa resigned on 14 May 2026 — after the Progressives withdrew over her dismissal of their defence minister, and on the morning KNAB detained her agriculture minister in the timber-industry case — President Edgars Rinkēvičs tasked Kulbergs with forming a government. His first cabinet (United List + New Unity + National Alliance + Union of Greens and Farmers) was confirmed on 28 May 2026 by 66 votes, explicitly as a bridge to the election of 3 October 2026. In that election the United List won 35.3% and 41 of 100 seats — the largest single-list result since independence — on turnout of 51.79%, the lowest in Latvian history. His second cabinet (United List + National Alliance, 51 seats) was confirmed on 17 November 2026 by 57 votes to 34. He is graded on roughly six months in office, across two cabinets a week apart at grading, plus his prior public record where it bears on the tests. The file is thin by construction: six months, one of them as an election bridge, is not enough time for most of these tests to be proven in either direction, and the grades below say so where that is the honest finding. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The timber-industry criminal process — opened against figures of the predecessor coalition — continued uninterrupted under his government, including against people inside his own first coalition. The ten persons holding procedural status include Armands Krauze, leader of the Union of Greens and Farmers (ZZS), which sat in Kulbergs' first cabinet; Prosecutor General Armīns Meisters stated in June 2026 that the investigation was progressing, evidence was growing, and no procedural status had been added or removed. No interference by Kulbergs' government in the criminal process is documented (Prosecutor General's Office statements, June–August 2026). The underlying offences remain ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated, and are graded to the predecessor government in its own file, not to Kulbergs.
  • Competition enforcement reached a state champion on his watch: on 13 August 2026 the Competition Council fined state forester Latvijas valsts meži (LVM) more than €7.8 million for granting six wood processors an unjustified competitive advantage over more than six years through guaranteed long-term contracts outside auction, and ordered equal sales conditions for all qualified bidders in future (Competition Council decision, 13 August 2026).
  • His first cabinet shared power with the camp he replaced: New Unity — the former prime minister's party — held four portfolios including Foreign Affairs and Transport, and the cabinet was approved by 66 votes, well beyond his own alliance's strength. Whatever its other character (below), the bridge government was not a winner-take-all construction (Cabinet of Ministers, 28 May 2026).
  • He holds office without holding a party card: he is formally non-partisan, elected and re-elected on the United List ticket without joining any of its component parties — a structural limit, if a modest one, on party-machine favouritism (Saeima and Cabinet records, 2022–2026).

Evidence against:

  • His first majority repeated the bargain his predecessor was graded down for: it included ZZS, whose component party For Latvia and Ventspils is led by Aivars Lembergs — convicted of bribery, money laundering and abuse of office (appeal judgment upholding a four-year sentence, 27 September 2023) and sanctioned by the US (Global Magnitsky, December 2019) and the UK (November 2024). The documented structural fact is the same as in the Siliņa file: for five months his government's survival depended in part on the party Lembergs leads, with the same coalition distancing clauses managing rather than removing the link (coalition composition, 28 May 2026; US Treasury, 2019; UK Government, 2024).
  • His second government rests partly on a second oligarch-era figure's pledged support. The AS–NA coalition holds 51 of 100 seats; Ainārs Šlesers, leader of Latvia First (17 seats), publicly pledged his party's support, and the confirmation's 57 votes included Latvia First MPs — the Centre for Eastern Studies (OSW) records tacit support from around seven of the 17, including Šlesers himself. Šlesers is described across the reporting as an oligarch of the pre-2011 era; his support was accepted, not refused (OSW analysis, 20 November 2026; LETA, 17 November 2026).
  • The second cabinet's appointments ran visibly through his personal circle. As documented in the confirmation debate and by Delfi's reporting: Foreign Minister Ģirts Jaunzems is his former classmate (a career diplomat, latterly ambassador to Uzbekistan); Health Minister Normunds Staņēvičs is his fellow student from Concordia International University (an Amber Beverage Group board member and crisis manager); Finance Minister Kārlis Purgailis was his non-staff adviser in the first government; Transport Minister Krišs Lipšāns was his legal adviser and a colleague from the automobile association. Opposition MPs accused him in the sitting of appointing acquaintances; the Providus think-tank's director commented that "the main selection principle was classmates." Kulbergs rejected the accusation, saying the ministers are experts in their fields, not friends — his denial is recorded here at full strength; the documented relationships are not disputed (Saeima confirmation debate and LETA/Delfi reporting, 17 November 2026).
  • Accountability for a scandal-adjacent state executive was softened inside his own government. LVM board chair Zane Drinķe had publicly promised to resign at the end of May 2026 over the timber-support scandal; in October 2026 she stayed, with the shareholder ministry's agreement, the documented reasoning being that she had just won the competition for the post and the enterprise would be left without top management. The decision was taken under his first government, whose Agriculture Ministry held the shares (inbox.lv/LETA reporting, October 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the prosecutor's office, KNAB, the election commission, the media and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The prosecutor's office acted directly against his government's position, in the open, and was answered through the courts rather than punished. On 16 June 2026 Prosecutor General Meisters filed a formal protest demanding the Cabinet revoke, from the moment of adoption, the predecessor government's timber-pricing decisions. The government considered the demand, rejected it by letter of 27 July 2026 with stated reasoning, and the Prosecutor General then applied to the Constitutional Court (filed 20 August 2026) — the channel the law provides. Meisters remains in post, publicly stating the criminal investigation "will certainly not take years"; no retaliation against him, the prosecutor's office, or KNAB is documented (Prosecutor General's Office statements, June–August 2026).
  • The election that hugely empowered him was administered independently and accepted by everyone. The 3 October 2026 election was run by the Central Election Commission under the fully manual counting his predecessor's parliament had legislated after the 2025 municipal scanner failure; results were certified on 16 October 2026; ODIHR deployed an Election Assessment Mission (15 September – 8 October 2026; final report pending at grading); German Eurofighters guarded Latvian airspace over the election period. No party, including the defeated former governing parties, disputed the result, and power arrangements proceeded on the certified numbers (CVK records, October 2026; ODIHR mission records).
  • No move against the other referees is documented in six months: the Bank of Latvia's governor, restored by the previous parliament over coalition bargaining, serves on untouched; the Constitutional Court's docket — including the timber case his government is a party to — proceeds on the court's own timetable; and media coverage of his government, including the hostile "classmates cabinet" reporting on LSM, Delfi and TV3, ran continuously and unpunished through both formations (public record, May–November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The one direct executive–referee confrontation of his tenure ended with the executive refusing the referee's demand. The Prosecutor General's protest was not a press release but a statutory instrument: it concluded that Cabinet decisions disposing of state assets below contract price were unlawful and incompatible with the Constitution and EU law, and demanded their annulment from adoption. Kulbergs' government declined, defending decisions taken by the predecessor cabinet in which his own coalition partner ZZS's minister was the responsible portfolio-holder (Test 1). The refusal was lawful in form — the Constitutional Court will now decide — but the documented fact stands that when the state's chief prosecutor told his government a set of decisions was unlawful, his government kept them in force (Prosecutor General's protest, 16 June 2026; Cabinet reply, 27 July 2026).
  • The second government's confirmation was compressed in a way that limited scrutiny of the referees' future overseers: the cabinet line-up was made public only on the morning of the 17 November vote, the government declaration was presented shortly before a sitting that ran past 22:00, and opposition MPs protested they had no opportunity to examine candidates or programmes. Speed is not capture, but it is the executive choosing how much examination it receives (LSM and LETA reporting of the sitting, 17 November 2026).
  • The file is six months long. No appointment to a court, the prosecutor's office, KNAB, the election commission or the central bank has fallen due and been tested on his watch; the absence of grip documented above is partly the absence of occasion, and this grade carries that thinness openly.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, prosecutors, partners or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He owned the airBaltic collapse in public, with the uncomfortable parts stated by himself. When the state airline — majority state-owned, carrying about $583 million in funded debt and an unpaid €30 million emergency state loan from April 2026 — filed for Chapter 11 protection in New York on 14 September 2026 after bondholders holding over 70% of the debt opted for liquidation over the company's 25%-interest refinancing plan, Kulbergs explained the sequence himself, called the filing "one of the best options for ensuring airBaltic's viability," and elsewhere called it a gamble the airline now had to justify to creditors and taxpayers. He did not blame the predecessor government that had extended the loan, announce a rescue the EU state-aid rules would not permit, or pretend the fleet plan (reduction toward ~36 aircraft) was anything but a shrinkage (Reuters, 14–16 September 2026; prime minister's statements, September 2026).
  • On Rail Baltica — the file he built his parliamentary reputation investigating — pushback produced a decision, not a defence of the status quo. On 1 October 2026 his government capped the core route at €20 million per kilometre (against current €28–32 million), backed a minimum first phase — single-track embankment, at most four regional stations — and set the Transport Ministry to produce a minimum scenario by December. His finance minister stated publicly that if Latvia cannot sustain the costs the project could be frozen rather than funded at the expense of roads and schools (Cabinet decision, 1 October 2026; Ministry of Finance statements, November 2026).
  • On the Istanbul Convention he slowed his own camp down. The United List's MPs had voted for withdrawal in October 2025; as prime minister he stated the alliance was not committed to withdrawing immediately and proposed a consultative, non-binding referendum on the question, discussed with the President. The Convention remains in force; the deferred bill sits with the new Saeima. Whether this is adjustment or postponement is CONTESTED between his supporters and the treaty's defenders; the documented movement is from his party's withdrawal vote to his government's referendum proposal (LETA reporting via Via Baltica, 2026).
  • The electorate's verdict was accepted as the government's mandate and its limit: he had told parliament on taking office that "the October 3 election will be the verdict on this government," campaigned on the caretaker record, and formed his second government strictly on the certified result (Reuters, 28 May 2026; CVK, October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The prosecutor's protest (Test 2) is also a wobble fact: confronted with a formal finding of unlawfulness against decisions benefiting a politically connected industry, the adjustment his government made was a reasoned refusal. The decisions stayed; the prices stayed; the matter went to court on the prosecutor's initiative, not the government's.
  • The Rail Baltica cap was decided in a closed extraordinary meeting two days before the election, on the basis of an Alvarez & Marsal review that has not been published; the Finance Ministry stated on 30 October that it had no estimate of the cap's effect on the state budget, and the cap as adopted is an "indicative target" excluding large structures. The transparency criticism is documented (a requester who has pursued the study since 13 August 2026 and litigated over documents; his interest — a brother stood, unsuccessfully, on the United List's Riga list — is disclosed in his own publication and noted here). The adjustment was real; its basis was not shown (Cabinet decision, 1 October 2026; Ministry of Finance and Transport replies, October 2026).
  • The second formation repeated the pattern of speed over examination: six weeks of coalition negotiation ended with a line-up published the morning of the vote and a declaration MPs had an hour to read, after the President had publicly hoped for a government by Independence Day, 18 November. The deadline was met; the scrutiny was the cost (LSM/LETA, 17 November 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Russian-speaking minority, Latgale, the poor, women, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The election he presided over counted everyone on the same rules: universal citizen franchise, manual counting adopted after the previous administration's scanner failure, diaspora voting administered across 38 postal commissions, and results — including 32 seats for the two Russian-speaker-oriented lists (Latvia First 17, Sovereign Power/New Latvians 15) — certified without dispute. The voters of the parties he defeated took their seats in full (CVK final results, 16 October 2026).
  • His political vehicle is the regionalist alliance: the United List's core is the Latvian Regional Alliance and the Liepāja Party, and its 41 seats include representation from every constituency including Latgale — the poorest region, whose at-risk-of-poverty rate (35.8%) is more than double Riga's. Regional development and the reorganisation of regional administration sit in his second government's stated priorities, and he holds the Climate and Energy portfolio — the file that decides heating costs in the east — himself (election results by constituency, October 2026; Cabinet of Ministers, 17 November 2026).
  • The inherited social floor continued to rise on schedule under his first government: the Welfare Ministry's 2027 minimum-wage process proceeded under the statutory framework (below for its shortfall), pensions and benefits continue under existing law into the provisional-budget period, and no group was singled out for a benefit cut in his six months (Ministry of Welfare and Finance statements, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • No documented step in six months addressed the Russian-speaking minority as anything other than the inherited security framework. The residence-permit language-test regime, the Latvian-only schooling transition and the closed Russian-language public broadcaster all continued on the predecessor's settings; no naturalisation, integration or outreach initiative from his government appears in the record. The parties that Russian-speaking voters actually chose — Latvia First and Sovereign Power/New Latvians, 32 seats between them — sit outside his government; Latvia First's support was accepted for his confirmation (Test 1) without its voters receiving any portfolio or programme concession that is documented. Non-citizens, roughly a tenth of residents, remained unaddressed, as under his predecessor (status quo, documented by omission from both government declarations, May and November 2026).
  • The minimum wage for 2027 was proposed at €820 — €15 below the €835 figure the statutory reference calculation produced — after the tripartite council failed to agree (30 July and 8 October 2026 meetings, under his first government). The ministry fell back on the 2024 medium-term schedule; the unions' €835 proposal, matching the formula, was not adopted. The shortfall is small and lawful; it is also a documented case of the lowest-paid being counted at slightly less than the state's own formula said (Ministry of Welfare draft regulations, October–November 2026).
  • His second cabinet counts women at one in fourteen. Ilze Indriksone is the only woman among the prime minister and 13 ministers — a fact opposition MPs raised in the confirmation debate and the reporting recorded. Whatever the individual ministers' merits, the composition is a documented narrowing of who is seen at the table (Cabinet composition, 17 November 2026; LETA/Delfi reporting).
  • Latgale's ledger did not move in six months: no region-specific measure from either of his governments is documented, and the €2.875 billion savings programme his second government announced for 2027–2030 (Test 5) will be tested precisely in the regions least able to absorb it. That is a forward risk, stated as such, not a present finding.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record since May 2026, with dates; his prior public record where it bears on the seat.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (word kept):

  • Safe elections — kept. His first government's declaration named safeguarding secure elections among its few priorities for the bridge term; the 3 October 2026 election was held on schedule, securely (including allied air policing over the vote), administered by manual count, certified on 16 October, and accepted by all parties (government declaration, May 2026; CVK, October 2026).
  • Defence at 5% — kept as policy, on the inherited path. His May declaration pledged raising defence spending to 5% of GDP; the 2026 budget he executed stood at €2.16 billion, 4.91% of GDP, continuing the targeted move to 5% projected for 2027, and his second government's declaration again names 5% of GDP for defence as its commitment, with Ukraine support continued (Ministry of Defence budget records, 2026; government declarations, May and November 2026).
  • Rail Baltica cost discipline — acted on. The parliamentarian who chaired the Rail Baltica inquiry became the prime minister who imposed the €20 million-per-kilometre cap and a minimum first phase (1 October 2026) — his signature scrutiny position carried into office, whatever the transparency caveat graded in Test 3.
  • airBaltic decision by autumn — kept in form. The May declaration pledged to assess the airline's business plan by August and decide on scenarios; the decision that emerged — government support for the Chapter 11 restructuring over liquidation or a 25%-interest refinancing — was taken and announced in September 2026 (government declaration, May 2026; Reuters, September 2026).
  • Non-partisan professionals in cabinet — kept in form. He promised to bring people from outside party politics into government; four of his second cabinet's 13 ministers are non-partisan. The personal-ties caveat on which outsiders were chosen is graded in Test 1, not double-counted here (Cabinet of Ministers, 17 November 2026).

Evidence against (word not kept, or receding):

  • A zero-based 2027 budget — not delivered on time. His second government pledged to draw up the 2027 state budget on a zero-based principle with €2.875 billion of savings targeted across 2027–2030. On 24 November 2026 — a week after his confirmation — the Finance Ministry announced Latvia will start 2027 with a provisional budget because the budget cannot be drafted and approved by the year's start. The procedure is lawful and pensions, benefits and salaries continue under it; but the centrepiece fiscal promise of the new government begins its term unmet on its own timetable, with new initiatives delayed (LETA, 24 November 2026; coalition agreement, November 2026).
  • Stability was the offer; the arithmetic is one seat. He won the election promising an end to governmental turbulence after three governments in four years. His coalition holds 51 of 100 seats and was confirmed with votes from an opposition oligarch-led party whose support is pledged, not contracted — a structure whose stability depends on the continued goodwill of a partner outside the coalition agreement (OSW analysis, 20 November 2026).
  • The tracker tally, stated as a tally: the independent promise-tracker kept.vote, which follows his stated commitments, records 12 tracked promises at grading: 2 kept, 1 compromise, 6 in progress, 1 broken, 2 with no action. Six months into a term that began as a five-month bridge, that distribution is neither a kept word nor a broken one — it is a word mostly still outstanding (kept.vote, November 2026).
  • His pre-office scrutiny standard vs. his office's practice: the MP who chaired inquiries into Rail Baltica's hidden costs now heads a government that set the project's cap on an unpublished study and published its own cabinet line-up on the morning of its confirmation. The positions are not contradictory in law; the distance between the scrutiny he demanded and the scrutiny he offered is documented (Tests 2 and 3).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED

Andris Kulbergs' first six months are the record of a bridge that became a landslide. He took office as a caretaker chosen to hold the state steady for five months, ran the election he had promised would be the verdict on his government, and won the largest single-list mandate in Latvia's history — then formed a one-seat-majority government whose confirmation leaned on an oligarch-era party's pledged votes and whose cabinet runs, in documented part, through his own classroom and advisory circle. The institutions around him worked and were left working: the prosecutor his predecessor appointed protested against decisions his coalition partner was implicated in, and his government answered in the Constitutional Court rather than against the prosecutor; the election commission delivered a result nobody disputed; the competition authority fined the state forester. Against that stand the refusals and the shortcuts: the prosecutor's protest rejected and the timber decisions kept in force; the scandal-promised LVM chair kept in post; the Rail Baltica cap set behind closed doors on an unpublished study two days before the vote; a cabinet of classmates and advisers published on the morning of its confirmation; a zero-based budget that begins as a provisional one; and a Russian-speaking third of the country governed, as under his predecessor, entirely inside a framework written about it rather than with it. Six months is too short to fail a man on trajectory or to clear him on it — no institution has been captured, and none has yet been tested by a hard appointment or a lost vote. The record is correctable, and the open files that will move these grades are named in it: the Constitutional Court's timber judgment, the 2027 budget when it comes, and whether a 51-seat coalition held up by pledged support governs by one law when the pledges are priced.
