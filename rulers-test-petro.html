The Ruler's Test — Gustavo Petro, President of Colombia (2022–2026)

Subject: Gustavo Petro Urrego — President of Colombia from 7 August 2022 to 7 August 2026, the country's first left-wing president, elected at the head of the Historic Pact (Pacto Histórico) coalition; previously a senator and, in his youth, a member of the M-19 guerrilla movement, which demobilized in 1990. Constitutionally barred from re-election, he endorsed Historic Pact senator Iván Cepeda as his successor. Cepeda lost the 21 June 2026 runoff to right-wing outsider Abelardo de la Espriella, 49.7% to 48.7% — the closest presidential election in Colombia's history. Petro disputed the result, announced on 7 July 2026 that he would not recognize de la Espriella's victory, and boycotted the inauguration, but he left office on schedule and de la Espriella was sworn in on 7 August 2026. This dossier grades Petro's completed tenure. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • In March 2023, Petro publicly asked the Attorney General's Office to investigate his own eldest son, Nicolás Petro, and his brother Juan Fernando Petro, after press reports linked both to approaches from imprisoned traffickers and irregular campaign money. The investigation of Nicolás proceeded, and he was arrested on 29 July 2023 on charges of illicit enrichment and money laundering (Fiscalía statements and contemporaneous reporting, 2023). Petro did not pardon, shield, or publicly direct the case; the prosecution ran through his entire term, under an attorney general (Francisco Barbosa) who was his open political adversary and then under his own nominee (see Test 2). As of November 2026 the case remains in pre-trial proceedings in Barranquilla, with the Superior Tribunal ruling on 23 November 2026 that disputed evidence — including Nicolás Petro's August 2023 Semana interview and his former partner's cell phone — stays admitted for trial (court reporting, November 2026). The charges remain ALLEGATION; there is no verdict.
  • Prosecution also reached his cabinet. In the UNGRD disaster-agency scandal, former UNGRD director Olmedo López and his deputy Sneyder Pinilla admitted receiving kickbacks and entered cooperation agreements; prosecutors then indicted Petro's former finance minister Ricardo Bonilla and former interior minister Luis Fernando Velasco on bribery charges, accusing them of "leading, promoting, and directing" a scheme that steered some COP 612 billion (~$161 million) in UNGRD and Invías projects to districts of lawmakers whose votes the government needed in 2023–2024 (Fiscalía indictment, reported November–December 2025). Both ministers deny the charges; the case is an ALLEGATION as to them and has produced no verdict. The Supreme Court separately opened investigations into the congressional leaders named in the cooperators' testimony. Petro did not block any of it.
  • The electoral authority's process against his own campaign ran to a conclusion: on 27 November 2025 the National Electoral Council (CNE) sanctioned the 2022 Petro campaign — the first presidential campaign sanctioned in Colombian history — ruling 6–3 that it had exceeded spending limits by COP 5.3 billion (~$1.4 million) and accepted financing from prohibited sources, including the Ecopetrol workers' union (USO) and the teachers' federation Fecode; his parties Colombia Humana and Unión Patriótica were fined, as were campaign manager Ricardo Roa, the campaign auditor, and the treasurer (CNE ruling, 27 November 2025). A June 2025 Constitutional Court ruling had held that the CNE could sanction the campaign administratively but that only Congress's Accusations Commission could investigate Petro personally; the CNE complied with that division.

Evidence against:

  • The violation the CNE found was his own campaign's. The overspend and prohibited-source findings are an administrative ruling against the campaign he headed, not an allegation by opponents; the sanctioned parties may still appeal to the Council of State (CNE ruling, November 2025). His campaign manager Ricardo Roa — personally fined in that ruling — remained president of the state oil company Ecopetrol, appointed under Petro, throughout the proceedings and to the end of the term.
  • His son's case sits at the edge of the campaign itself. In a leaked August 2023 interview with Semana, Nicolás Petro said he had received money from Samuel Santander Lopesierra, a convicted trafficker known as "El Hombre Marlboro," and from contractor Gabriel Hilsaca, and that part of it entered his father's 2022 campaign; he later qualified and partly recanted those statements, and prosecutors' theory that campaign money was involved remains ALLEGATION, contested by Nicolás Petro's defense and denied by Gustavo Petro, who states he knew nothing of any illegal financing. No court has ruled on the question.
  • The UNGRD scheme, on the prosecution's account, was vote-buying for his legislative program: disaster-relief and infrastructure contracts allocated in exchange for congressional support for government bills — a use of state resources for the governing coalition's benefit that, if proven, is one-law failure at the center of his administration, not at its edge. Petro denies directing or knowing of the scheme; Bonilla and Velasco deny the charges; López's further claim that officials pressed him to steer contracts to the ELN to keep peace talks alive is an ALLEGATION by a cooperating defendant, denied by the government, under Supreme Court investigation.
  • Appointments repeatedly went to figures under active investigation: Armando Benedetti, facing multiple investigations (including, later, the campaign-financing file), was made ambassador and then, in February 2025, interior minister — the government's chief political operator; in January 2026 former Medellín mayor Daniel Quintero, facing more than 40 criminal and disciplinary complaints from his mayoralty, was appointed national health superintendent (appointment reporting, January 2026). No rule barred these appointments; the pattern is documented.
  • After leaving office, Petro himself came under a Supreme Court investigation opened in late 2026 on a complaint alleging he traded public contracts for sexual favors during his congressional and presidential years. This is an ALLEGATION under preliminary investigation; no charge has been filed, and Petro denies it categorically, stating he never used a state contract to obtain favors (Supreme Court chief justice's public statements and Petro's response, October–November 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, the central bank, Congress, the prosecution service, and the media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts ruled against him on the largest questions of his term and he complied. The Constitutional Court in June 2025 returned his pension reform (Law 2381 of 2024) to the House of Representatives to cure a procedural defect, suspending its entry into force; Petro publicly celebrated the ruling — "the pension reform has been saved" — and the re-debate proceeded (Constitutional Court ruling, June 2025). The Council of State on 18 June 2025 suspended his decree calling a labor-reform referendum because it lacked the Senate's prior favorable opinion required by Article 104 of the Constitution; the government withdrew the decree days later rather than defying the court (Council of State ruling, 18 June 2025; government statements, June 2025). The same Constitutional Court also ruled for him in June 2025, holding that only Congress could investigate him personally over campaign financing — a limit on the CNE that he accepted and used, and which shows a court ruling on law, not on sides.
  • Congress was never his instrument. The Senate sank his health reform in April 2024 and again, in revised form, in December 2025; a Senate committee archived his first labor reform in March 2025; the Senate voted on 14 May 2025 against his proposed popular consultation. Coalition partners (the Conservative Party and La U) left his alliance in April 2023 without reprisal. His reforms that did pass — tax (2022), pension (2024), labor (2025) — passed by negotiation and vote, in watered-down form.
  • The central bank defied him openly for most of his term and survived doing so. Governor Leonardo Villar and the board held and cut rates on their own timetable while Petro publicly demanded faster cuts for years; Villar stated publicly in September 2025 that he would not yield to presidential pressure (Bloomberg/Reuters reporting, September 2025). The bank's independence was a fact of his tenure, demonstrated against his wishes.
  • The prosecution service's leadership changed by the institutional route: Petro presented an all-women shortlist (terna), and the Supreme Court elected Luz Adriana Camargo on 12 March 2024, after Barbosa's term expired. Her office continued the prosecution of Nicolás Petro and brought the UNGRD indictments of Petro's ex-ministers (see Test 1).
  • The media remained free, hostile, and untouched in law: no outlet was closed, no journalist imprisoned by his government, and Colombia's large private media groups campaigned against him in the open through the 2026 election. In September 2024 he signed a decree — described as the first of its kind in the Americas — barring public officials from stigmatizing journalists.

Evidence against:

  • The pressure on the courts was rhetorical but sustained and sometimes physical in setting. In February 2024, as the Supreme Court deliberated over the attorney-general election, Petro called supporters into the streets and pro-government protesters surrounded the Palace of Justice, trapping magistrates inside for hours; the UN and the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights called for the election to proceed "without any additional pressure on the court" (IACHR and UN statements, February 2024). Petro framed adverse rulings throughout his term as a "soft coup" (golpe blando) — the CNE's October 2024 decision merely to open a campaign-finance investigation drew his public response: "The coup d'état has begun" (Petro on X, 8 October 2024).
  • Where Congress refused his health reform, he governed around the refusal through the regulator. After the Senate shelved the reform on 3 April 2024, the health superintendency (Supersalud) intervened — took over the administration of — the country's two largest insurers, Sanitas and Nueva EPS, and interventions eventually covered insurers serving more than 23 million affiliates (CRS report, May 2025; Supersalud records). The interventions were within the superintendency's statutory powers and were litigated in the courts, but their sequence — legislative defeat followed within days by administrative takeover of the reform's main targets — is documented, and his former finance minister José Antonio Ocampo's criticism stands on the record: "The response to the failure of the health reform… should not be to take over the system."
  • The term ended in open institutional warfare with the central bank. After the board raised the benchmark rate to 11.25% on 31 March 2026, Finance Minister Germán Ávila withdrew the government from the board, Petro wrote that the board "continues to pursue a policy that is killing the Colombian economy" and that the government was "not part of a suicidal opposition stance," and his labor minister threatened further minimum-wage increases if rates rose. Because the finance minister's presence is required for the board's quorum, analysts and constitutional lawyers warned the boycott could paralyze monetary policy; Governor Villar stated the board could not properly meet without the government member. Ávila returned to the board for the April 2026 meeting, at which rates were held (Reuters, 31 March–1 April 2026, and April 2026 reporting).
  • His speech toward journalists crossed lines his own courts then drew. On 30 August 2024 he called women journalists "muñecas de la mafia" ("dolls of the mafia"); the Constitutional Court's ruling SU-432 of 2025 required him to refrain from stigmatizing language toward women journalists, and in May 2026, under a further court order, he publicly apologized (Constitutional Court SU-432/2025; El País reporting, May 2026). The press-freedom organization FLIP documented a pattern of presidential stigmatization of critical media across his term; no sanction beyond the courts' orders followed.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Congress, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He adjusted, in public, at several of the term's hardest points. He accepted the Constitutional Court's return of the pension reform and the re-debate it ordered (June 2025). When the Senate passed an acceptable labor reform on 17 June 2025, he withdrew the referendum-by-decree that the Council of State had suspended, rather than forcing the confrontation (June 2025). He accepted his coalition's poor results in the October 2023 local elections, including losses in traditionally left-leaning cities. When the ELN's January 2025 Catatumbo offensive killed more than 100 people and displaced over 53,000, he suspended the ELN talks on 17 January 2025, declared a state of internal commotion, and deployed more than 9,000 soldiers — reversing his own negotiating posture in response to events (government statements, January 2025). After the 26 January 2025 deportation-flight standoff with Washington (see below), his government negotiated and accepted the flights the same night.
  • He ultimately handed over power on schedule. Whatever his rhetoric (below), he did not extend his term, did not obstruct the 7 August 2026 inauguration date, and left office as the Constitution requires; de la Espriella was sworn in before Congress on that date (inauguration record, 7 August 2026).
  • He corrected himself under court order in the journalists case, delivering the public apology the Constitutional Court required in May 2026.

Evidence against:

  • His characteristic first answer to institutional defeat was to contest the referee's legitimacy. The CNE investigation was "the coup d'état has begun" (October 2024); the Senate's 14 May 2025 vote against his popular consultation was, in his May 2025 characterization, "fraudulent" — after which he issued the referendum decree anyway, on the theory that the Senate's vote was void, until the Council of State suspended it. The health reform's two Senate defeats were answered not with a renegotiated bill that could pass but with the administrative interventions described in Test 2.
  • The pattern culminated in the 2026 election. After the electoral authorities certified de la Espriella's victory on 24–25 June 2026 and the EU observation mission rejected fraud claims, Petro on 7 July 2026 announced he would not recognize the result, alleging — without presenting evidence to judicial authorities — that "algorithms from California" made by "private intelligence companies from Israel" had altered the count and that the true winner was Cepeda (Petro statements, 7 July 2026). He filed an annulment suit before the Council of State, compared his successor to Hitler in public remarks, ordered that no military installation host the inauguration, and confirmed on 15 July 2026 that he would boycott the ceremony: "I cannot accept a president… because it is a fraud." The transition process (empalme) broke down in July, with de la Espriella suspending it on 7 July citing Petro's non-recognition, and Petro's own finance minister suspending the government's side. Thirteen Colombian universities and the Attorney General's Office publicly noted the absence of fraud evidence (AP, EU EOM, and Colombian reporting, June–July 2026). Petro also stated his rejection was not a call to violence and that he would leave office on the scheduled date — and he did. Cepeda, the defeated candidate, conceded after the recount; the president did not.
  • The January 2025 deportation standoff shows both movements in one day: on 26 January 2025 Petro turned back two previously authorized U.S. military deportation flights, demanding dignified treatment of deportees, and announced retaliatory 25% tariffs; within hours, facing U.S. tariff and visa measures, his government accepted "the unrestricted acceptance of all illegal aliens from Colombia returned from the United States, including on U.S. military aircraft" (White House statement, 26 January 2025), framing the outcome as an agreement guaranteeing decent conditions and sending Colombia's presidential plane to fetch deportees. The adjustment was real; so was the same-day reversal of a position announced as a matter of national dignity.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Indigenous and Afro-Colombian communities, coca farmers, ex-combatants, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The poor were counted, measurably. Monetary poverty fell from 39.7% in 2021 to 33.0% in 2023 (DANE) and to 28.0% in 2025, with extreme poverty falling from 11.7% (2024) to 9.6% (2025) (DANE figures as reported 2026). He raised the minimum wage sharply every year — 16% for 2023, 12% for 2024, 9.5% for 2025, and a 23.7% "vital wage" decree for 2026 (provisionally suspended by the Council of State in early 2026, with Petro pledging to sustain it by temporary decree). His pension reform's core was a non-contributory pillar for poor seniors; his labor reform restored night and Sunday surcharges phased in through 2027.
  • Communities historically outside the count were placed inside his government's structure: Francia Márquez, an Afro-Colombian environmental defender, served as vice president and first minister of the new Ministry of Equality and Equity; a June 2023 constitutional amendment recognized peasants as subjects of special constitutional protection and created agrarian courts; land delivery under the 2016 Peace Agreement accelerated relative to predecessors — 291,030 hectares delivered to landless peasants, victims, ethnic communities, and rural women by November 2025 and 323,579 hectares by mid-March 2026 (Ministry of Agriculture figures via UN Verification Mission reports), with land-tenure formalization reaching 3.9 million hectares, 56% of the Agreement's seven-million goal.
  • Venezuelan migrants — some 2.8–2.9 million people in Colombia — kept the regularization pathway (the PPT temporary protection statute) his predecessor created and his government maintained; Petro declined, in his January 2025 words, to return irregular U.S. residents "in chains," and his deportation dispute with Washington was fought, on its face, over the dignity of Colombian deportees.

Evidence against:

  • The communities his peace policy was meant to protect were overrun while it ran. The Catatumbo offensive of January 2025 — by the ELN, the group his government had been negotiating with — killed at least 100 people, including demobilized FARC signatories, and displaced 53,444 people, with 32,000 more confined (UN/OCHA figures). Killings of social leaders and ex-combatants continued throughout his term: 187 human rights defenders were murdered in 2025 alone (Indepaz figures as reported), and the UN Verification Mission verified continuing killings of former combatants in every reporting period. By the end of his tenure, per the International Crisis Group, irregular armed groups fielded more combatants than at any moment since the FARC's 2016 demobilization (Crisis Group Briefing N°56, 7 August 2026).
  • Coca farmers were counted as partners in substitution while the coca economy — and the armed groups taxing it — grew past every record: cultivation reached 253,000 hectares in 2023, with potential cocaine production up 53% in a single year, and about 261,000 hectares in 2024 (UNODC). Forced eradication was wound down by design; substitution programs reached only a fraction of growing families, and the United States decertified Colombia's counternarcotics efforts on 15 September 2025 (with a national-interest waiver) for the first time since 1997, citing record cultivation.
  • His opponents' voters were counted at the ballot box and discounted afterward. The 12.7 million Colombians who voted for Cepeda were his constituency; the 12.96 million who voted for de la Espriella got, from the sitting president, a declaration that their victory was an algorithmic fraud, that their president-elect was illegitimate and comparable to Hitler, and a boycotted inauguration (see Test 3). Whatever the legal transfer of power, the public accounting of the winning half of the country, by the man still holding the office, was that their votes did not count.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record, August 2022 – August 2026, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly delivered):

  • Pension reform — kept. Passed by Congress in June 2024 (Law 2381 of 2024); returned by the Constitutional Court in June 2025 for a procedural cure; re-debated; and in August 2026 the Court approved most of the reform (Reuters, 28 October 2026). Its solidarity pillar extends a basic income to poor elderly Colombians who never qualified for a pension — the promise as made.
  • Labor reform — kept, modified. After the Senate committee archived his first bill (March 2025) and the consultation fight (Test 3), a negotiated reform passed both houses on 20 June 2025 and became law — night work from 7 p.m., Sunday surcharges rising to 100% by July 2027, indefinite contracts as the norm.
  • Tax reform — kept. His 2022 tax reform on high earners, oil, and mining passed Congress in November 2022, early in the term.
  • No new oil and gas exploration contracts — kept. Announced in 2022 and held for the full term, against sustained industry and opposition pressure (his successor campaigned on reversing it).
  • Minimum wage and poverty — kept in direction and largely in fact (see Test 4): poverty down from 39.7% to 28.0% across the period his policies governed.
  • He left office when his term ended — no re-election bid (he was constitutionally barred), no term extension, departure on 7 August 2026.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "Total Peace" — not delivered. His flagship promise was negotiated settlements with all armed groups. The ELN talks — the centerpiece — were suspended in September 2024 after an ELN attack killed soldiers, restored, and collapsed finally after Catatumbo in January 2025; no agreement was signed with the ELN or with any major group, dissident FARC factions expanded, and he left office with more irregular combatants in the field than Colombia had seen since 2016 (Crisis Group, August 2026). His successor was elected explicitly to dismantle the policy.
  • Health reform — never enacted. The promise to replace the EPS insurer system by law failed in the Senate in April 2024 and again in December 2025; the system he left is the one he promised to abolish, altered by administrative intervention rather than by the reform he campaigned on.
  • Land — a fraction delivered. He campaigned on implementing the Peace Agreement's three-million-hectare land fund. Cumulative delivery under the Agreement stood at about 10% of that goal by late 2025 (291,030 hectares, Ministry of Agriculture via UN), most of it delivered in his term but an order of magnitude below the promise; the PARES Foundation, using the outgoing government's own handover figures, put delivery with definitive titles at 98,148 hectares against the National Development Plan target (PARES report, 2026).
  • Coca and drugs — the record ran opposite to the promise. He promised a new drug policy that would shrink the coca economy by developing the countryside; cultivation and potential production set records instead (253,000 hectares in 2023; ~261,000 in 2024; UNODC), and Colombia was decertified by the United States in September 2025.
  • Fiscal word — not kept. He promised to fund his program with the 2022 tax reform while respecting fiscal sustainability; instead the fiscal deficit roughly doubled on his watch (to a projected 7.1% of GDP in 2025, per Finance Ministry figures), the fiscal rule was suspended in 2025, and Moody's and S&P downgraded Colombia's credit in 2025 citing the debt trajectory.
  • Relations with the United States — the rupture is the record. Beyond decertification: on 19 October 2025 President Trump called Petro an "illegal drug leader" and halted subsidies; the U.S. State Department had revoked Petro's visa in September 2025 after his New York remarks urging U.S. soldiers to disobey orders; and on 24 October 2025 the U.S. Treasury's OFAC designated Petro personally under Executive Order 14059 — the illicit-drugs authority — for having "engaged in, or attempted to engage in, activities… that have materially contributed to… the international proliferation of illicit drugs," designating his wife Verónica Alcocer, his son Nicolás Petro, and Interior Minister Armando Benedetti derivatively for supporting him (Federal Register notice, FR Doc. 2025-20152). Petro denies any involvement in drug trafficking — no court, Colombian or foreign, has convicted or charged him with such conduct — and characterizes the designation as political retaliation for his positions on Gaza and drug policy; in a July 2026 phone call he asked Trump to remove him and his family from the list, and Trump responded, per Petro's account, that he "will do his best." The designation remained in force when he left office and after (it caused Avianca to cancel his ticket for a Bogotá–Mexico City flight on 24 August 2026). Both governments' positions are stated here as they stand; the designation is a U.S. executive action, not a judicial finding.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Gustavo Petro governed for four years against institutions that were never his: a Senate that sank his health reform twice, a central bank that raised rates over his objections, courts that returned his pension law, suspended his referendum decree, and ordered him to apologize to journalists — and, in the main, those institutions still stood at the end, and he obeyed their rulings and left office on the day the Constitution set. That is the strongest thing on his record, and it is real. Against it: a campaign sanctioned for breaking finance law, a son prosecuted for money allegedly raised in that campaign's shadow, disaster funds allegedly traded for congressional votes, a health system restructured by regulator's decree after the legislature said no, a central-bank board boycotted in his final months, and a flagship peace policy that left armed groups larger than he found them while coca set records. And the wobble, when it came at the end, failed in the specific way his whole term had rehearsed: defeated institutions were answered first as conspiracies — a "coup" in an electoral investigation, "fraud" in a Senate vote, and finally fraud in the vote of the country itself, which he refused to recognize even as he vacated the office it had reassigned. The poor, to his credit, were poorer by the millions fewer when he left; the voters who replaced him were told their choice was not real. Both are on the record, and the record is correctable in the open.
