The Thriving Plan — a field guide

Spot It, Check It, Block It

How to spot a scam in ten seconds, check a number in twenty, and block and report it in thirty.

Scams run on volume and cheap reach. Every person who can spot one, block it, and report it raises the scammer’s cost and feeds the filters that protect the next person. Ruining the operation is a numbers game — so this page teaches the numbers game.

Built from our own verified catches first, general guidance second. Every method here carries its limit printed beside it. Nothing on this page asks you to call a strange number, tap a strange link, or engage a scammer to “check.”

1. Spot it — the five tells

A message earns the label “scam” from its actions, not from vibes.

Any one tell = caution. Two or more = treat it as a scam until it proves otherwise — and the proof never comes from inside the conversation. It comes from a door you open yourself: the company’s real app, or the number printed on the back of your card.

1

Pressure

Urgency or rarity: “final notice,” “don’t let this one slide,” “act before midnight.” Real help does not expire in an hour.

2

A vague prize — or a vague problem

“Something special for you.” “Your account will be locked.” Never anything concrete you can independently check.

3

A borrowed face

A celebrity, their mother, a company, a government office, a friend — arriving through a channel that face never uses: a cold DM, a random text, a brand-new account.

4

The pivot

Get you off the platform and onto their ground: a phone number to call, a link to tap, a different app, a code word to say.

5

The step that costs

Dressed as “verification” or “processing”: a gift card, crypto, a wire, a deposited check to forward, banking details, or a code read aloud.

Real catches — from our own inbox

The “Elon Musk’s mom” DM

A fake Maye Musk account (“Musk_Universe🌍”) offers “something special,” hands out a phone number, and gives the code word “Tesla.” That is tells 1, 2, 3, and 4 in one short thread — with the fee step, tell 5, waiting on the phone call. The account was not Maye Musk, and the number was not Elon’s.

The political fear text

“Surrounded by death and destruction… you’re in danger,” plus a look-alike link. Tells 1, 3, and 4. What a message is selling — including fear — is marketing, not evidence.

Robocall weather

Dozens of unknown numbers, each “connected” for almost exactly 35 seconds, rotating area codes. Nobody real calls like that. A dialer does.

2. Check it — how to run a number, safely

Checking is done from your ground — never from theirs.

The safe checks
  • Search the exact thing. Search the full phone number, and search a distinctive sentence from the message in quotes. Scam scripts repeat across thousands of victims, so the script usually has a public record even when the number is fresh.
  • Read the account. Age, follower counts that don’t fit the face, no verification where the real person has it, a profile photo that also appears on other “fan” pages.
  • Use free lookup tools for what they can actually tell you — spam and robocall checkers and carrier lookups can show an area code, a carrier, a line type (mobile or internet/VoIP), and whether other people have reported the number.
  • Verify a claim through the real door you open yourself. A bank claim gets checked in your banking app or by the number on your card — never by a number or link inside the message.

The two questions that kill most scams on contact:
1. “Would the real person actually do it this way?”
2. “If this were genuine, would they have to sell me on it?”

The honest limit, printed in the open: a lookup can tell you a number’s area code, carrier, line type, and whether others have reported it. It cannot give you the scammer’s name. A number with no reports is “not rated” — not “clean.” Untraceability is their product design; anyone selling you the name is running a second scam on top of the first. A platform account (TikTok, Messenger, Instagram) is traceable by the platform and by law enforcement through records the public cannot see — which is exactly why reporting it matters.

Never check by engaging. Don’t call back to “see who it is” (the one-ring trick can bill you, and any answer logs you as a live, responsive number). Don’t tap the link to “look.” Don’t reply “STOP” to a scammer — to them it does not mean stop; it confirms a human is reading.

3. Block it — the twenty-second moves

A block protects you. A report protects the next person. Do both when they’re offered.

On your phone and in your apps
  • Samsung / Android: Phone app → Settings → turn Caller ID and spam protection ON. For repeat offenders, use Block numbers and feed the list — the block list works; the trick is feeding it.
  • iPhone: Settings → Phone → Silence Unknown Callers. Block directly from the call or the message.
  • Carrier layer: your carrier’s spam app (for example AT&T ActiveArmor) filters network-side, before the phone rings.
  • Messenger / Instagram / Facebook: open the thread or profile → Block. In the same flow, Report → scam or impersonation where offered.
  • Gmail: Report spam — and Report phishing for impersonation. Both also train the filter for everyone.

4. Report it — the wrench that ruins operations

This is the part that actually ruins every scammer’s operation.

Forward scam texts to 7726
7726 spells SPAM. Forwarding goes to carrier spam databases and helps get the number shut down.

reportfraud.ftc.gov
Report to the U.S. Federal Trade Commission. Attempts count, not just losses — the FTC’s database is the pattern map enforcement works from.

In-app reports feed the platform’s detection for everyone — the same platform records that make an account traceable when authorities ask.

Why reporting works — the economics, plainly

Their economics need a million cheap touches and a tiny hit rate. Blocks shrink the reachable pool. Reports poison their numbers and accounts faster than they can mint new ones. And an educated reader is a dead end they will stop paying to reach. One report looks small; a million readers reporting is the operation ending.

5. If you already engaged — the damage-control order

In this order. Speed is the whole game.

  1. Stop contact. Don’t announce it. Just stop.
  2. Money sent? Your bank or card company immediately. Gift cards → the card’s issuer, keeping the card and the receipt. Crypto or wire → report anyway; recovery is unlikely, but the record matters.
  3. Information given? Change the passwords that matter, turn on two-factor authentication, and consider a credit freeze — free, at each credit bureau.
  4. A code given, or remote access allowed? Treat the device or account as compromised, and say so to your bank.

No shame in it. These scripts are professionally built and tested on millions of people. The record of what happened is useful. Hiding it only protects the operation.

6. How this page labels things

  • Labels are facts, not opinion. “Scam” is earned by actions on the record — pressure, a pivot, a payment step — never by a feeling about a person.
  • What a message is selling is marketing, not evidence — including messages selling fear.
  • Unverified prints as unverified. A number with no reports is “not rated,” not “clean.”
  • This page is living. New scripts get added as they’re caught, with the catch dated.
