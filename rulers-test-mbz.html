The Ruler's Test — Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan, President of the United Arab Emirates

Subject: Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan — President of the United Arab Emirates since 14 May 2022, when the Federal Supreme Council unanimously elected him under Article 51 of the constitution, a day after the death of his half-brother, President Khalifa bin Zayed Al Nahyan, on 13 May 2022 (UAE Ministry of Foreign Affairs / WAM statement, 14 May 2022). The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: the President is head of state, chairs the Federal Supreme Council, signs federal laws and decrees, appoints and dismisses the prime minister and cabinet, conducts foreign affairs, and is supreme commander of the armed forces (Congressional Research Service, The United Arab Emirates: Issues for U.S. Policy, updated 13 September 2023). The division of power is stated plainly, because the record shows a real one at the emirate level: the Prime Minister, Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum — Vice President and Prime Minister since 2006 and Ruler of Dubai — runs the federal cabinet day to day and is sovereign inside Dubai, and substantive Federal Supreme Council decisions require five of seven rulers including both Abu Dhabi and Dubai, giving Dubai an effective veto (electoral-framework record, 2025–2026). Federally, however, the prime minister's authority is derivative — he holds office by presidential appointment — and since 29 March 2023 he has shared the vice-presidency with the President's brother, Mansour bin Zayed Al Nahyan (Reuters reporting, March 2023). The presidency is therefore the seat graded; Mohammed bin Rashid appears as the President's chief federal operator and as Dubai's ruler, not as a co-graded seat, and where the federation's two-pole structure genuinely limits the President, that limit is recorded in the tests rather than smoothed over. Mohamed bin Zayed's mandate is election by the seven hereditary rulers of the Federal Supreme Council, not election by voters: there is no platform the voters elected, so the mandate rule is applied in its adapted form, as in this series' Morocco, Saudi, Bahrain, and Qatar dossiers — the Test grades the holding, and his word is measured against the constitution, his own public pledges, and the state's announced programs (see Test 5). He had been the UAE's de facto leader since Khalifa's stroke in 2014 (CRS, 2023; Reuters, May 2022), and he is graded principally on his presidency since May 2022, with the de facto decade named as context where a file began under it. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The Ethic: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The financial-crime law was tightened against the system's own hub, and an outside referee certified the tightening. After the Financial Action Task Force placed the UAE on its grey list in March 2022 for strategic anti-money-laundering weaknesses, the state built a specialist financial-crimes court, issued new AML guidelines, strengthened the penal code's anti-bribery provisions, suspended the licenses of 32 local gold refineries in July–October 2024 over 256 violations, and fined a local bank AED 5.8 million; FATF removed the UAE from the grey list on 23 February 2024 after recognizing significant progress (FATF record, February 2024; Legal 500 enforcement summary, 2024). Federal Decree-Law No. 10 of 2025 then replaced the 2018 AML law with tougher sanctions and expanded predicate offenses, including tax evasion (legal analyses of Decree-Law 10 of 2025, October 2025).
  • The perception measure is the region's strongest: Transparency International's CPI 2025 scores the UAE 69 of 100, the highest in the Gulf comparison set (Qatar 58, Saudi Arabia 57) (Transparency International, CPI 2025). Freedom House likewise scores its safeguards against official corruption at 2 of 4 — middling, but the highest-scoring functioning-of-government item in its UAE report (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026).
  • Some federal rules are written universally and enforced against firms regardless of ownership connections: the Wage Protection System requires electronic salary payment across the private sector; Federal Law No. 9 of 2022 on domestic workers defines wages, rest days, health insurance, and a right to complain (UN submission summarizing the domestic-workers law, August 2025); and in October 2026 an Abu Dhabi court sentenced eight men, most in absentia, to life in prison for unlicensed arms trafficking to Sudan's army — a case reaching a former Sudanese intelligence chief, recorded here as a court verdict against trafficking to the side the UAE is not accused of backing (Sudan–UAE relations record, checked 20 November 2026).
  • Clemency runs on a published, recurring rule rather than ad hoc favor: the President ordered the release of 1,295 inmates ahead of Ramadan 2025, pledging to settle their sentence-related financial obligations (WAM via National Media Office, 27 February 2025), 1,440 ahead of Ramadan 2026 (UAE press reporting, February 2026), and 956 ahead of Eid Al-Adha 2026 on the same terms (Aletihad / WAM reporting, 20 May 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The legal order is tiered at the source, before any discretion is exercised. Noncitizens make up approximately 88 percent of the population on one sourced figure (BTI 2026 country report; Genocide Watch country report, May 2026) and close to 90 percent on another (Human Rights Foundation country record, 2026) — the figures are printed separately, as sourced — and they hold no political rights, no general path to citizenship, and a legal status tied to an employer-sponsor under the kafala system (HRW, World Report 2026). Citizens receive the welfare state — subsidized services, public-sector employment preference under Emiratisation, land and housing support — as a class privilege of nationality.
  • Inside the labor law the tiers continue. Employers can file "absconding" charges even against workers who leave to escape abuse, exposing them to detention and deportation; wage theft, illegal recruitment fees, and passport confiscation persist and "may amount to forced labor"; trade unions are banned outright; and there is still no non-discriminatory minimum wage (HRW, World Report 2026). The midday summer work ban remains the primary heat protection despite evidence of its ineffectiveness for outdoor workers (HRW, 2026).
  • Criminal exposure is sorted by identity and by proximity to the seat. The Federal Penal Code and Cybercrime Law criminalize peaceful criticism of the state and its leaders — the charge on which Ahmed Mansoor, a human rights defender, was sentenced to 10 years in 2018 (US House Tom Lantos Human Rights Commission record) — while consensual "sodomy" with an adult male is criminalized and vaguely defined morality provisions reach public affection and gender-nonconforming expression (HRW, World Report 2026). Women gained ground in the new family and domestic-violence statutes — marriage age raised to 18 — but only non-citizen Muslim women can marry without a guardian present, and the domestic-violence law defines violence as acts "exceeding the guardianship authority" of the perpetrator, leaving disciplinary space for male guardians (HRW, World Report 2026).
  • The counterterrorism law operates as a second, executive-only law for dissidents. In January 2025 the authorities designated in absentia 11 political dissidents and their relatives, plus eight UK-based companies linked to them, as "terrorists" — without due process or prior notice, under a 2014 law that lets the executive impose the label without judicial oversight; only two of the 11 had ever been convicted or accused of terrorism, on HRW's account, and none appears on internationally recognized terrorism lists. Designation carries asset freezes and risks of life imprisonment for anyone in the UAE who communicates with the named people (HRW, World Report 2026). Relatives designated for their kinship is the opposite of one law applied to each person for their own acts.
  • No documented case appears in the record assembled here of a member of a ruling family, or of the President's house in particular, being prosecuted under these laws — in a system where the President's son is Abu Dhabi's crown prince and his brothers hold the co-vice-presidency, the national-security advisership, and the chairmanships of the two great sovereign funds (Test 2). The absence is recorded as an absence, not as proof of an offense; it is the pattern the one-law test asks about, and the shelf cannot fill it.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because, as in the Qatar file, the two-law problem is the statute book itself, not favoritism in applying one law. The FATF reforms, the CPI score, the wage-protection and domestic-worker rules, and the recurring published clemency are real, and they are credited above. But the same legal order assigns political rights, welfare, mobility, and criminal exposure by category — citizen or sponsored migrant, critic or loyalist, man or woman, dissident or dissident's relative — before any official acts. One law for all does not survive a population sorted into tiers at the source, with the top tier's own house never documented as reached by the laws it issues.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the financial system's legitimate users and the state's credibility, by the FATF exit of February 2024; workers actually paid through the Wage Protection System and domestic workers who can invoke the 2022 law; the 1,295, 1,440, and 956 inmates released under the 2025–2026 clemency orders and their families. Hurt — the noncitizen majority (approximately 88 percent, or close to 90 percent, of the population on the separate sourced figures) living under sponsor-tied status with no political rights and no citizenship path; migrant workers exposed to absconding charges, wage theft, and passport confiscation; the 11 dissidents and their relatives designated "terrorists" in January 2025; women under the surviving guardianship rules; LGBT residents under criminalization.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • A federal advisory body with an elected element exists and was used on his watch: the Federal National Council's 40 seats are split evenly — 20 elected by electoral colleges, 20 appointed by the emirate rulers — and the most recent election was held on 7 October 2023, with approximately 337,000 citizens in the electoral colleges and turnout around 27.5 percent on the country-report record (Federal National Council record; country-reports summary of the 2023 election). A 2018 presidential decree requires women to hold 50 percent of FNC seats, a quota met through the appointed half when elections return fewer women (CRS, 2023).
  • The FNC does question the government: it debates draft laws and the budget, may approve, amend, or reject bills in its review, and may summon and question any federal minister (electoral-framework record, 2025–2026). The Central Bank operates monetary policy through a professional structure, and it enforced the AML regime against a local bank with the AED 5.8 million fine recorded in Test 1 — a regulator acting against a domestic institution (Legal 500, 2024).
  • No documented case appears in the record assembled here of the President personally overruling a named court verdict, a central-bank decision, or an FNC vote. The grip documented below is structural and legal rather than a file of individual interventions, and the distinction is recorded.

Evidence against:

  • Freedom House's 2026 verdict is structural and scored: the UAE is Not Free at 18 of 100 (political rights 5, civil liberties 13), with head-of-government electoral process 0 of 4, political parties 0 of 4 (parties are banned), free and independent media 0 of 4, and independent judiciary 0 of 4; its overview states that all executive, legislative, and judicial authority ultimately rests with the seven hereditary rulers (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026). RSF ranks the UAE 158th of 180 in its 2026 Press Freedom Index.
  • The FNC's autonomy is limited by design at every joint: it is advisory — if it rejects or amends a bill, the President or the Supreme Council can issue the law anyway; it cannot remove a minister; its elected half is chosen not by citizens generally but by electoral colleges whose members are named by each emirate's ruler (electoral-framework record, 2025–2026; CRS, 2023). The referee that exists holds its whistle by the rulers' appointment, on both halves of its bench.
  • The judiciary's dependence was exercised, on the monitors' record, in the state's largest political case of his presidency. The UAE84 mass trial — 84 defendants, most already convicted in the 2013 UAE94 trial on similar grounds — ended on 10 July 2024 with 53 convicted; Ahmed Mansoor received a further 15 years on top of his 2018 sentence; and on 4 March 2025 the State Security Chamber of the Federal Supreme Court upheld the convictions — 43 life sentences among them — effectively ending appeal. HRW documents serious due-process violations: restricted access to lawyers, closed proceedings, unpublished charge sheets and rulings, credible reports of abuse and ill-treatment, and double-jeopardy concerns (HRW, World Report 2026; HuMENA advocacy dossier on Ahmed Mansoor, 24 November 2026). HRW counted at least 83 people imprisoned for peacefully exercising free expression in its October 2026 call on EU leaders (HRW, October 2026, via Tom Lantos Commission record).
  • Independence is punished beyond the courtroom. In January 2025 the poet Abdulrahman Youssef al-Qardawi was deported by Lebanon to the UAE over a social-media post criticizing Egypt, the UAE, and Saudi Arabia; on arrival he was detained and forcibly disappeared, his location undisclosed and family access denied, on HRW's account (HRW, World Report 2026). Amnesty International's joint statement ahead of COP28 recorded at least 60 Emirati defenders and dissidents detained since 2012 whom authorities refused to release even after sentences were served, alleging a continuing "terrorist threat," with some detainees' citizenship — and their families' — arbitrarily revoked, leaving them stateless (Amnesty International, MDE 25/6725/2023, 1 May 2023). In 2026 the grip tightened under wartime cover: Amnesty documented Gulf-wide arrests of more than 1,000 people for war-related expression, with the UAE among the states announcing arrests for filming or sharing videos of missile interceptions and damage (Amnesty International, 2026).
  • Power was further centralized inside the ruling house during his presidency. On 29 March 2023 the President appointed his eldest son Khaled bin Mohamed as Crown Prince of Abu Dhabi — making him, by convention, his presumptive successor as Abu Dhabi's ruler and UAE President — named his brother Mansour bin Zayed as co-Vice President alongside Dubai's ruler, and named his brothers Tahnoun bin Zayed and Hazza bin Zayed as deputy rulers of Abu Dhabi; Mansour and Tahnoun also chair Mubadala and the Abu Dhabi Investment Authority respectively, together overseeing more than USD 1 trillion in assets (Reuters reporting, 29 March 2023; Arab Gulf States Institute analysis, May 2023; Washington Institute analysis, 2023). Analysts at the time read the Mansour appointment as diluting Dubai's federal weight (Washington Institute, 2023) — recorded here as analyst interpretation, not as a documented act against Dubai.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the question is whether the referees keep their autonomy, and in the UAE the referees' autonomy is not constituted: the legislature's elected half is chosen by ruler-named colleges and its decisions can be overridden by the seat it advises; the judiciary is appointed at all levels by the rulers and, on HRW's documented record, ran a closed mass trial ending in 43 life sentences with appeal extinguished in March 2025; the media operate under penal and cybercrime laws that score 0 of 4 for independence; and the central appointments of March 2023 concentrated the succession, the co-vice-presidency, the security portfolio, and the sovereign funds in the President's own branch of his own family. That the Central Bank fines a bank and the FNC questions ministers shows institutions functioning inside the grip, not outside it.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the President and the Abu Dhabi ruling house, whose hold on every federal referee and on the succession is now formally complete; the system's stability and predictability for investors, the benefit analysts cited for the March 2023 clarification (Reuters, March 2023). Hurt — the 53 defendants convicted in the UAE84 trial and the 43 serving life sentences, including Ahmed Mansoor; Abdulrahman Youssef al-Qardawi, forcibly disappeared on HRW's account; the at-least-60 detainees held past their sentences on Amnesty's count; citizens, whose only elected channel is an advisory council chosen by ruler-named colleges; journalists and residents arrested in the 2026 war-expression sweep; Dubai's federal weight, diluted on analysts' reading by the second vice-presidency.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The FATF grey listing of March 2022 was answered by adjustment, not denial. The state accepted the outside referee's findings, built the specialist court and the enforcement machinery listed in Test 1, suspended gold refineries and fined a bank, and earned removal from the list on 23 February 2024 (FATF record, 2024; Legal 500, 2024). This is the cleanest documented case in the file of external pushback producing open, structural change — and it happened across the Khalifa–Mohamed transition, completed under Mohamed bin Zayed's presidency.
  • The February–March 2026 Iran war was answered in public, in person, and toward protection rather than escalation. After US–Israeli strikes on Iran began on 28 February 2026 and Iran launched missiles and drones at Gulf states hosting US forces, the President made his first public comments while visiting people injured in the strikes — the injured he visited included two Emirati nationals and one Indian, one Sudanese, and one Iranian resident — saying the UAE was in a time of war but "well," that it has "thick skin and bitter flesh — we are no easy prey," and pledging: "We will carry out our duty towards our country, our people, and our residents who are also part of our family" (Reuters, 7 March 2026). He also appeared publicly at Dubai Mall to reassure residents as flights resumed (Circuit News daily report, March 2026). The no-good-choice clause is applied here by its mechanics: the options actually open were joining offensive operations against Iran (inviting deeper strikes on the airports, ports, and desalination plants his residents depend on), public acquiescence while under fire (abandoning deterrence and the residents he had just called family), or air defence, public reassurance, and diplomacy while the war ran its course. Each of the first two carried real harm; he took the third, stated the position honestly and in public while in it, and the trap's origin — hosting US bases under a decades-old security posture he inherited and has maintained, not a decision of that month — is recorded as the situation's origin rather than held as a demerit for the response itself. The episode is graded as the best possible choice available in the moment.
  • When another option became available in that same war, the record shows it being considered openly — and set aside by the partners, not suppressed by him. Bloomberg reported, via Iranian state-media relay, that early in the war the President telephoned fellow GCC rulers to propose a coordinated Gulf military response to Iran's strikes, and that the other rulers declined, reminding him the US–Israeli attack "wasn't their war," after which he dropped the coordination push (Bloomberg reporting as relayed by Press TV / GlobalSecurity, 15 May 2026 — a second-hand relay through an interested state's media, recorded at that weight). The item is double-edged and is shelved here because the sequence — propose, hear refusal, adjust — is the Test 3 motion, whatever one thinks of the proposal.
  • Sudan diplomacy shows a late, partial adjustment under pressure: after Sudan cut relations in May 2025 (below), the UAE joined the US–Saudi–Egyptian Quad statement proposing a three-month humanitarian truce leading to a permanent ceasefire, and at the Washington donors' conference of 3 February 2026 pledged USD 500 million to a Sudan humanitarian fund — the largest share of its USD 700 million launch total (Sudan–UAE relations record, checked 20 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The central Sudan file was answered with denial, maintained against a growing body of named findings. Sudan's government accuses the UAE of arming the Rapid Support Forces; a UN Panel of Experts report of January 2024 called reports of weapons flown to the RSF through Amdjarass, Chad, "credible"; HRW and France 24 found the RSF had used munitions previously acquired by the UAE military; Amnesty International's secretary general in November 2026 called the UAE "the primary backer of the RSF"; and a leaked UN Panel report reported by Middle East Eye in November 2026 described a UAE-based firm hiring Colombian mercenaries who helped the RSF take El Fasher — where RSF fighters reportedly massacred approximately 2,000 civilians in October 2025 — some after training at bases in the UAE. The UAE denies arming any party, says its Chad flights carried humanitarian aid, and told the panel the mercenary claims were baseless; Sudan filed at the International Court of Justice on 5 March 2025 alleging complicity in genocide, and on 5 May 2025 the Court removed the case from its list, 9 votes to 7, for manifest lack of jurisdiction — a jurisdictional ruling, not a finding on the merits. All of the support claims are recorded here as ALLEGATION, contested, with the disputants named: Sudan, the UN panels, HRW, and Amnesty on one side; the UAE's denial on the other (HRW, World Report 2026; Sudan–UAE relations record, checked 20 November 2026; Refugees International statement, November 2026). What Test 3 grades is not the allegation's truth but the response's shape: across three years of escalating findings by named bodies, the documented response is a flat denial, no announced independent investigation, and no published accounting of the Chad airbridge — denial and doubling down, if the findings are credited; a wronged state's refusal to confess to what it did not do, if they are not. The dossier records the shape and the dispute, and grades the shape.
  • Sudan's escalation was met in kind rather than absorbed: when Sudan's Security and Defence Council cut diplomatic relations and declared the UAE an "aggressor state" on 6 May 2025, the UAE declined to recognize the break and the relationship remained ruptured through November 2026, with Sudan's military leadership repeatedly rejecting the UAE's membership of the Quad mediation format (Sudan–UAE relations record, checked 20 November 2026).
  • Domestic pushback is answered with the instruments of Test 2, not with adjustment. There is no street politics to answer — assembly and parties are banned — but the documented responses to the pushback that does occur (the UAE84 defendants' advocacy, war-related posting in 2026, al-Qardawi's single critical post) are prosecution, designation, and disappearance, each recorded in Test 2. The monitors' labor findings have likewise produced no documented second adjustment: HRW's 2026 report repeats, nearly verbatim, its findings on absconding charges, wage theft, and heat protection from prior years (HRW, World Report 2026).
  • Climate pushback produced a consensus abroad and expansion at home (developed in Test 5): the COP28 presidency delivered the "UAE Consensus" of 13 December 2023, the first COP text to name transitioning away from fossil fuels — and ADNOC, chaired at board level within his house and led by the COP28 president himself, continued expanding production capacity, with Global Witness projecting fossil-fuel spending of roughly USD 1.14 billion per month through 2030, a projection ADNOC disputes as based on flawed assumptions (COP28 record, December 2023; Global Witness analysis via Impact Wealth, updated August 2026, with ADNOC's dispute recorded).

Why: The grade lands on MIXED because the record splits by direction, as it did in the Qatar file. Pushed by a technical referee (FATF) he adjusted structurally and in the open, and earned the delisting. Pushed by war in March 2026 he answered publicly, protected residents of all nationalities in word and in the hospital visit's composition, and — on the Bloomberg-relayed account — proposed a joint response, heard his partners refuse, and adjusted. Pushed on Sudan by Sudan, the UN panels, and the rights monitors, the answer for three years has been a flat denial with no independent accounting, maintained while the findings grew; and pushed at home, the answer is the prosecutor. Test 3 grades all three directions, and they do not point the same way.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — residents of all nationalities during the March 2026 strikes, by the defence-and-reassurance posture and the explicit inclusion of residents as "part of our family"; the financial system's users, by the FATF adjustment; Sudanese civilians, prospectively, by the Quad truce proposal and the USD 500 million aid pledge of February 2026. Hurt — Sudanese civilians in Darfur, above all at El Fasher in October 2025, if the ALLEGATION of UAE support to the RSF is credited — recorded as the wobble's gravest claimed cost, with the UAE's denial standing beside it; residents arrested in the 2026 war-expression sweep; migrant workers whose documented grievances have produced no second reform round.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the noncitizen majority, the workers, the women, the dissidents, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Inside the citizen perimeter, provision is broad and comparatively even: free healthcare and education, subsidized utilities, housing and land support, and public employment — the standard rentier settlement, applying to citizens as a class across all seven emirates, with Abu Dhabi's wealth funding federal transfers to the smaller northern emirates.
  • Women are counted into the state's visible institutions at a level unusual in the region: the 2018 decree's 50 percent FNC quota, women in the cabinet and judiciary, and the 2024–2025 family-law reforms raising the marriage age to 18 and expanding domestic-violence protections to cover sexual exploitation and economic violence (CRS, 2023; HRW, World Report 2026).
  • The tolerance architecture is real and was built under his de facto and formal leadership: the Abrahamic Family House in Abu Dhabi, housing a mosque, a church, and a synagogue, opened in 2023; long-established churches and a Hindu temple serve resident communities; and the Abraham Accords of 2020, concluded under his de facto leadership, normalized relations with Israel (COP28-era and accords record).
  • In the March 2026 war the count was stated to include everyone physically present: "our people, and our residents who are also part of our family" — and the hospital visit that carried the words covered injured residents of Indian, Sudanese, and Iranian nationality alongside Emiratis (Reuters, 7 March 2026). Abroad, the February 2026 Sudan pledge of USD 500 million was the largest share of the Washington fund's launch total (Sudan–UAE relations record, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The foundational count excludes most of the counted. The noncitizen majority — approximately 88 percent of the population (BTI 2026) or close to 90 percent (Human Rights Foundation, 2026), printed separately as sourced — builds, cleans, drives, cares, and staffs the economy, and holds no political rights, no vote even for the advisory council's elected half (whose colleges are citizens only), no path to citizenship, and no right to organize: trade unions are banned (HRW, World Report 2026). They are counted as labor and as protected residents in a war; they are not counted as people with a stake.
  • Their material count is discounted by the system's own rules: no non-discriminatory minimum wage, wage theft and recruitment fees documented as widespread, absconding charges hanging over flight from abuse, passport confiscation persisting despite prohibition, and heat protection resting on a midday ban the monitors call ineffective — with outdoor migrant workers "among the most vulnerable to heat-related illnesses and death" (HRW, World Report 2026). Authorities in Dubai and Abu Dhabi have raided partitioned workers' housing and evicted residents without addressing the low wages that make such housing necessary (HRW, 2026).
  • Women are counted at less than full weight in the law's own terms: marriage without a guardian is open only to non-citizen Muslim women, and the domestic-violence statute's definition leaves male guardians disciplinary authority "within limits accepted by authorities" (HRW, World Report 2026). LGBT residents are counted as criminals under the sodomy and morality provisions (Test 1).
  • Dissidents and their families are counted out by name: the January 2025 terrorism designations reached relatives who had committed no documented act (Test 1); Amnesty records citizenship revoked from detainees and their families, leaving them stateless (Amnesty International, May 2023); and the UAE84 sentences of 2024–2025 removed 53 more people — most already imprisoned since 2013 — from the count for life or for 10–15 further years (HRW, World Report 2026).
  • Beyond the border, the gravest counting charge is the Sudan ALLEGATION: if the findings of the UN panels, HRW, and Amnesty are credited, Sudanese civilians — the Masalit of Darfur above all, and the approximately 2,000 reported killed at El Fasher in October 2025 — were counted as acceptable costs of a proxy policy. The UAE denies arming any party; the ICJ's May 2025 ruling was jurisdictional only. The charge is recorded here as ALLEGATION, contested, with disputants named in Test 3 — and it is recorded in this test because Test 4 is where a dossier states whose lives the accounting, on the accusers' evidence, left out.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because counting everyone is the test, and the UAE's accounting has a designed perimeter drawn around roughly one resident in nine. Inside it, provision is generous, women sit in half the advisory council's seats, and three faiths pray in one Abu Dhabi complex; in a war, the President's words explicitly extended the family to every resident. Outside the perimeter stands the overwhelming majority of the population — counted for their work, protected in a missile attack, and counted out of every political right, every union, every minimum-wage floor, and every path to belonging — alongside women discounted by guardianship, LGBT residents criminalized, dissidents' relatives designated, and, on the contested Sudan evidence, a foreign civilian population allegedly priced into a proxy war.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Emirati citizens, who receive the full welfare settlement and federal transfers across all seven emirates; Emirati women in public institutions, under the 50 percent FNC quota and the 2025 family-law gains; resident religious minorities worshipping in the tolerance architecture; all residents, by the March 2026 protection pledge made in inclusive words. Hurt — the noncitizen majority (approximately 88 percent, or close to 90 percent, on the separate figures) with no political rights, no unions, no wage floor, and sponsor-tied status; outdoor workers exposed to heat under an ineffective ban; women under surviving guardianship rules; LGBT residents; detainees' families stripped of citizenship or designated by kinship; Sudanese civilians, on the ALLEGATION recorded above with the UAE's denial beside it.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 14 May 2022, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • "We the UAE 2031" — the state's announced program, in motion on its own numbers. The vision, launched 22 November 2022 at the UAE Government Annual Meetings, pledges to double GDP from AED 1.49 trillion to AED 3 trillion by 2031, raise non-oil exports to AED 800 billion, and lift foreign trade to AED 4 trillion (UAE Vision 2031 program record). The trajectory is documented: real GDP reached AED 1.776 trillion in 2024, up 4 percent, with non-oil sectors at 75.5 percent of the economy (Federal Competitiveness and Statistics Centre figures via Ministry of Economy, June 2025). The target is not yet met and cannot be graded as kept; the motion toward it is real, published, and measured by the state's own statistics centre. Crossing 1 in part: a word being kept, and the keeping — diversification, trade growth — helping residents and businesses who live in the non-oil economy.
  • The FATF word — kept, and certified by the referee. The commitment made under grey listing in 2022 to remedy the AML regime's strategic deficiencies was discharged to FATF's satisfaction by 23 February 2024, and then extended unprompted by Decree-Law No. 10 of 2025 ahead of the 2026 mutual evaluation (FATF, 2024; legal analyses, 2025). Crossing 1: word kept, and it helped — the delisting lowered the cost of every legitimate transaction in the federation.
  • The residents pledge of March 2026 — kept in the moment it was made. "We will carry out our duty towards our country, our people, and our residents who are also part of our family" was stated while visiting the injured of several nationalities, and the documented conduct of that month — air defence, public reassurance in Dubai, no collective blame of any resident community, including Iranian residents — matches the words (Reuters, 7 March 2026). A short-horizon word, recorded as kept at that horizon.
  • The Abraham Accords posture — kept under pressure. The 2020 normalization with Israel, concluded under his de facto leadership, was maintained through the Gaza war and its regional fallout rather than traded away when maintaining it became costly in Arab opinion — the posture stated in 2020 is the posture held in 2026.
