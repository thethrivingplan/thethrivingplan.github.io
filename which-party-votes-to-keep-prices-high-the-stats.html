Which party votes to keep prices high?

No party votes on a bill called “keep prices high.” There is no such bill. What does exist is a paper trail — votes that directly change what you pay for medicine, gas, health coverage, and imported goods, plus executive actions Congress voted to stop or let stand. This page puts all of it in one place, with the numbers attached.

The short answer — in one box

On the clearest price votes — letting Medicare negotiate drug prices and capping insulin — Democrats voted yes, Republicans voted no, almost to a person. On tariffs, the price-raising policy wasn’t a congressional vote at all: it was a Republican administration’s executive action in 2025, and Democrats (plus 3–5 Republicans) voted to end it while most Republicans voted to keep it in place. In the other direction, Republicans argue Democratic spending — above all the $1.9 trillion American Rescue Plan in 2021, passed with no Republican votes — helped fuel the 2021–22 inflation surge. Economists credit that spending with part of the surge, not all of it. Both records are below, at full size.

1. Prescription drugs — the cleanest record

This is the one price where Congress voted directly on whether the government may bargain it down. Before 2022, Medicare was barred by law from negotiating drug prices. The IRA removed that bar for selected drugs.

Medicare negotiation + inflation rebates + $35 Medicare insulin + $2,000 annual Part D out-of-pocket cap (from 2025)

What the negotiated prices actually did

  • First 10 drugs (Eliquis, Jardiance, Xarelto, Januvia, Farxiga, Entresto, Enbrel, Imbruvica, Stelara, NovoLog/Fiasp) — negotiated prices took effect Jan 1, 2026, at 38% to 79% below 2023 list prices.
  • CMS estimate: those 10 prices would have saved Medicare ~$6 billion if in effect in 2023 (≈22%), and save beneficiaries ~$1.5 billion in out-of-pocket costs in 2026.
  • 15 more drugs for 2027 — including Ozempic / Rybelsus / Wegovy ($959 → $274 for a 30-day supply in CMS’s published comparison) — bring the program to 25 drugs. Up to 20 more per year from 2029.
  • CBO’s 10-year score for negotiation overall: roughly $98–100 billion in Medicare savings (estimates vary by scoring window; CMS and CBO figures are cited separately in sources).

Sources: Medicare Rights Center — negotiated prices take effect · CMS — 15 additional drugs selected

The Republican argument, at full size: negotiation is government price-setting that will chill drug development, shift costs elsewhere, and — per CBO — the IRA as a whole would have a “negligible” effect on overall inflation in 2022 and only ±0.1 point in 2023. That CBO point is correct about overall inflation; it does not change the per-drug price cuts above, which are specific, not economy-wide.

All Democrats plus 7 Republicans — Collins, Hyde-Smith, Hawley, Kennedy, Cassidy, Murkowski, Sullivan — voted to keep the $35 cap for private insurance. 43 Republicans voted to strike it after the Senate parliamentarian ruled it didn’t fit reconciliation rules. Because waiving the rule needed 60 votes, 57 wasn’t enough. Result: $35 insulin applies in Medicare; private plans set their own price.

2. Tariffs — the price rise Congress didn’t vote to start

Important distinction: Congress never voted to impose the 2025 tariffs. They were imposed by executive action under emergency powers (IEEPA). The congressional votes were attempts to terminate those emergencies — i.e., to end the tariffs. Voting “no” kept the tariffs in place.

Canada — Apr 2, 2025

51–48 to end the tariffs. All Democrats + Collins, McConnell, Murkowski, Paul (R). House Republican leadership blocked a House vote. Tariffs stayed.

Global tariffs — Apr 30, 2025

49–49 — defeated. 46 Democrats + 3 Republicans for ending; 49 Republicans against. A tie loses. Tariffs stayed. GovTrack record

Brazil — Oct 28, 2025

52–48 to end the 50% tariffs. All Democrats + 5 Republicans (Collins, Murkowski, Tillis, McConnell, Paul). House unlikely to take it up; a veto was expected.

What changed in prices?

Average U.S. tariff rate rose from 2.6% to 13% during 2025 (NY Fed). Import prices for tariffed goods rose ~11% more than untaxed goods.

The administration’s argument, at full size: tariffs protect U.S. manufacturing, raise revenue ($264B collected in 2025 per Tax Foundation reporting), pressure trade partners into deals, and foreign sellers do absorb a growing share (about 14% by late 2025 in the NY Fed data). Supporters also note inflation cooled on other fronts in 2025 even as tariffs rose. None of that changes the pass-through finding: most of the cost, in every study above, landed on the American side.

3. Gas & price gouging

Consumer Fuel Price Gouging Prevention Act (H.R. 7688)
Would have made it unlawful to sell fuel at an “unconscionably excessive” price during a declared energy emergency, and empowered the FTC to investigate — aimed at large oil & gas companies.
Party-lineHouse 217–207 · May 19, 2022
Dems for
217
Reps for
0

217 Democrats voted yes; 0 Republicans did. 203 Republicans and 4 Democrats voted no. The context that day: national average gas was $4.58 / gallon for regular, $5.57 for diesel, after Russia’s invasion of Ukraine spiked global prices. The bill passed the House, was placed on the Senate calendar, and was never brought to a Senate vote — it expired with the 117th Congress. There is still no federal price-gouging statute; ~40 states have their own.

Source: GovTrack — H.R. 7688 roll call

Republican argument: there was no evidence of gouging — prices reflected supply & demand — and the bill’s undefined “excessive” standard could punish small stations and worsen shortages. Four Democrats who voted no made a version of the same supply argument.

4. Health coverage — where a vote raises or lowers premiums indirectly

Coverage votes don’t set a sticker price, but CBO scores translate them into how many people keep coverage and what the system costs — which feeds back into premiums for everyone.

One Big Beautiful Bill Act (H.R.1, 2025)
Extended 2017 tax cuts; added work requirements & more frequent eligibility checks for Medicaid; did not extend the enhanced ACA subsidies (those expired separately at end of 2025).
Party-line the other waySenate 51–50 · House 218–214
  • Senate: 51–50, VP Vance tie-break. No Democrats yes; Republicans Collins, Paul, Tillis joined Democrats in voting no.
  • House final: 218–214. Only Republicans Fitzpatrick & Massie joined Democrats in voting no.
  • CBO: deficit +$3.3 trillion over 10 years; Medicaid spending −$930 billion to ~$1 trillion; 10 to 11.8 million more uninsured by 2034 (range across CBO releases).

Source: HIMSS breakdown of H.R.1 healthcare impacts & votes

Republican argument: work requirements target able-bodied adults, eligibility checks cut waste/fraud, and tax-cut extension prevents a price-like tax hike on families. Democratic argument: paperwork requirements, not ineligibility, drive most coverage loss — and losing coverage raises uncompensated care costs that shift onto insured patients.

Lower Costs, More Transparency Act (2023)
Hospital & insurer price disclosure, PBM rebate transparency, easier generic-drug entry.
BipartisanHouse 320–71

Worth including because it breaks the pattern: when the tool is transparency rather than negotiation or a cap, both parties vote for lower prices in large numbers. It passed the House overwhelmingly and then stalled in the Senate — a reminder that “didn’t become law” isn’t always a party-line story.

Source: IA Magazine — House passage 320–71

5. Inflation & spending — the Republican case, with numbers

The strongest counter-argument isn’t a price vote — it’s that one party’s spending raised all prices. Here’s what the timeline and the research actually support.

American Rescue Plan Act (2021) — $1.9 trillion
$1,400 checks, expanded Child Tax Credit & ACA subsidies, $350B to state/local governments, extended unemployment benefits.
Senate 50–49 · House 220–211

No Republican voted for it in either chamber (final House vote: 1 Democrat, Golden, also voted no). It passed in March 2021 — immediately before the steepest climb in prices.

  • What research credits it with: Bernanke & Blanchard (2023) and San Francisco Fed work find most of the 2021–22 surge came from supply / price shocks (pandemic, energy, Ukraine), with fiscal stimulus adding demand on top — estimates cluster around ~1–3 points of the rise, varying by model. It was a contributor, not the sole cause.
  • What it also did: Census data show child poverty fell to 5.2% in 2021 under the expanded Child Tax Credit, then rose to 12.4% in 2022 after it expired — the trade-off at the center of the argument.
  • On the IRA’s name: CBO said the Inflation Reduction Act would have a “negligible effect on inflation” in 2022 and between −0.1 and +0.1 point in 2023. Penn Wharton: “statistically indistinguishable from zero” in the near term. The law’s measurable price effects are the specific ones (drugs, insulin, caps), not the overall CPI.

Sources: American Rescue Plan — votes & provisions · BLS historical CPI-U (PDF) · CBO director on IRA inflation effect

What actually drives most prices, most of the time: Federal Reserve interest-rate policy, global energy & food supply, housing supply (shelter was the stickiest part of post-2022 inflation), supply chains, and wages/labor markets. Congressional votes move specific prices powerfully — a drug’s list price, a tariff line, an insurance cap — and move the overall inflation rate only at the margins, through large spending or tax changes. Anyone who tells you one vote explains your grocery bill is selling you a framing, not a stat.

Bottom line, by price

Medicine

Democrats voted to let Medicare bargain and to cap insulin for everyone. Republicans voted no — unanimously on negotiation, 43–7 against the private insulin cap. Negotiated prices are now 38–79% below list for the first 10 drugs.

Imported goods

A Republican administration raised tariffs by executive action; ~90% of the cost landed on Americans (~$1,000/household in 2025). Democrats + a handful of Republicans voted to end them; most Republicans voted to keep them.

Gas (gouging bill)

Democrats voted 217–0 in the House to empower the FTC against fuel price-gouging; Republicans voted 0–203 for it. It died without a Senate vote.

Everything at once (inflation)

Democrats passed $1.9T in stimulus with no Republican votes, right before inflation peaked at 9.1%. Research credits that spending with part of the surge — alongside supply shocks and energy, which no vote controlled.

So: “which party votes to keep prices high the most?” depends on which price you mean — and now you don’t have to take anyone’s word for it. On direct price votes (drugs, insulin, gouging) and on votes to end price-raising tariffs, the Republican record is the one voting no. On the big-spending vote Republicans blame for broad inflation, the Democratic record is the one voting yes. The tables above are the whole argument, with receipts.
