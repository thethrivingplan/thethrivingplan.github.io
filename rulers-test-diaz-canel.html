The Ruler's Test — Miguel Díaz-Canel, President of Cuba

Subject: Miguel Mario Díaz-Canel Bermúdez — President of the Republic of Cuba since 10 October 2019, and First Secretary of the Communist Party of Cuba (PCC) since 19 April 2021. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: Cuba's 2019 constitution restored a separate prime minister as head of government — Manuel Marrero Cruz, in office since 21 December 2019, chairs the Council of Ministers and is accountable to both the National Assembly and the president, who nominates him (Constitution of Cuba, 2019, Art. 142, via reporting on the prime minister's office). Day-to-day government runs through Marrero. Executive power in the sense this Test grades — who can overrule, who commands the armed forces, who sets the line the government follows — sits above him: the president is head of state, and since April 2021 Díaz-Canel also holds the First Secretaryship, described in reference sources as the highest office in the party and its holder as the most powerful person in the Cuban party-state, the PCC being constitutionally the "superior leading force of society and the state." No premise correction to the brief's expectation is needed on the holder: Díaz-Canel still holds both offices in November 2026, confirmed by his public acts through August–November 2026 in those titles (Granma, August 2026; CPC International Department reporting, August 2026). The tenure framing does need one precision the brief did not state: he first became head of state on 19 April 2018 as President of the Council of State, became President of the Republic under the new constitution on 10 October 2019, and was re-elected by the National Assembly on 19 April 2023 with 97.7% of the Assembly vote for a second five-year term running to 2028, the constitutional maximum of two consecutive terms (CIA World Factbook, January 2026 edition; Associated Press, 19 April 2023). He is therefore graded on a tenure in progress — April 2018 in substance, October 2019 in the graded office, party leadership from April 2021 — not a completed tenure. His predecessor Raúl Castro appears as context where the system Díaz-Canel inherited bears directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform or mandate the system granted is not in itself a failure — the Test grades the holding, never the platform; carrying out that mandate is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The law reached his own economy minister. Alejandro Gil Fernández — deputy prime minister, minister of economy and planning, and one of Díaz-Canel's most trusted and publicly promoted officials — was dismissed in February 2024 and, after two closed trials in November 2025, was sentenced by the Supreme People's Court on 8 December 2025 to life imprisonment for espionage and related offences, plus a concurrent 20-year sentence for bribery, falsification of public documents, influence peddling and tax evasion, with confiscation of assets (Reuters, 8 December 2025; Supreme People's Court statement via Granma, December 2025). Whatever else is said below about that trial's secrecy, the defendant was an insider at the top of the government, not an opponent.
  • One major law was made with the counted-in public and applied to everyone alike: the 2022 Family Code went through public consultation that produced over 300,000 suggestions and modified some 48% of the draft's articles across 25 versions, was approved in a referendum on 25 September 2022 by 66.85% to 33.15% on 74.12% turnout (National Electoral Council figures), and on taking effect legalised same-sex marriage and adoption and strengthened protections for women, children, the elderly and people with disabilities for all Cubans on the same terms (referendum results; Díaz-Canel signed it into law 26 September 2022).
  • The January–March 2025 prisoner releases were completed on the announced number even after the other side of the bargain collapsed: Cuba had committed, through Vatican mediation, to release 553 persons; when the incoming Trump administration reversed the Biden administration's removal of Cuba from the US terrorism list and Havana paused the process, it then resumed and completed it — the Supreme People's Court's vice president confirmed on state television on 10 March 2025 that all 553 were free (Reuters, March 2025; Vatican News, March 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Political loyalty is written into the source of the law itself. The 2019 constitution — adopted under Díaz-Canel and still the frame he governs under — makes the PCC the only legal party and the system's socialist character unamendable; Freedom House's 2026 assessment states the position plainly: the one-party state "outlaws political pluralism, bans independent media, suppresses dissent" (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026, Cuba scored 9/100, Not Free). A supporter of the system and an opponent of it do not stand before the same law because one of them is forbidden to organise at all.
  • The July 2021 protests were answered with two laws in practice. After the 11 July 2021 demonstrations — the largest since 1959 — Díaz-Canel broadcast the words: "We call on all revolutionaries to go to the streets to defend the revolution. The order to fight has been given" (Human Rights Watch, Prison or Exile, July 2022). What followed, on HRW's year-long documentation of 155+ cases, was a pattern of arbitrary detention, incommunicado holding, summary and military trials, and sedition sentences of up to 25 years. The counts are printed separately, as sourced: Cubalex reported over 1,400 detained, more than 700 still behind bars as of July 2022; Cuban courts had confirmed convictions of more than 380 protesters and bystanders, including several children (HRW, July 2022). The government's own figure, given later, was some 500 people sentenced for participation (Cuban official figures via France 24 reporting, January 2025) — a different, lower count, printed alongside, not blended. One protester, Diubis Laurencio Tejeda, died, seemingly at the hands of police (HRW, July 2022). Government supporters who took to the same streets under the president's call faced no comparable prosecutions in the documented record.
  • The penal code and speech laws supply the second law's machinery: the 2022 penal code punishes "propaganda against the constitutional order" and criminalises receiving funds for acts the government deems contrary to state security, and the Social Communication Law, in force from October 2024, restricts content to what aligns with the "purposes of socialist society" (US Department of State human rights reporting; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025). These are laws whose trigger is the speaker's position toward the government.
  • An economic insider class stands outside the rules applied to ordinary economic actors. The military conglomerate GAESA controls an estimated 40–55% of tourism, retail and ports activity (leaked-document reporting, August 2025 — an estimate, recorded as such), while private micro, small and medium enterprises (MSMEs), legalised only in September 2021, were capped at 100 employees, barred from a list of prohibited activities (125 activities until the June 2026 package cut it to 55), and denied direct foreign-trade access until 2026 (Decree-Law on MSMEs, 2021; Rosa Luxemburg Stiftung analysis of the June 2026 package). The Gil case (above) also cuts this way on process: his trials were closed-door, no other officials implicated were named by the court, and independent verification of the espionage evidence was not possible — the verdict is a court's verdict and is recorded as such, but a secret trial for an insider and public exemplary trials for protesters is not one visible law for both (Reuters, December 2025; elTOQUE editorial note, December 2025).

Why: The grade lands at FAILS because the split is structural, not incidental. Where the law is neutral in content — family law, a corruption verdict against a minister — it can and does apply to all. But at the source, the constitution itself reserves legality to one party, and the defining enforcement event of the tenure, 11J, prosecuted one side of the street and deputised the other. One law for insiders and outsiders does not survive a system in which being an outsider is itself unlawful.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: same-sex couples and families across Cuba, who gained marriage, adoption and protection rights on equal terms (2022); ordinary defendants in the 553-release process, including protest-linked prisoners among them (2025).
  • Hurt: 11J protesters and bystanders — over 1,400 detained on Cubalex's count, hundreds convicted, sentences to 25 years; independent journalists, artists and activists prosecuted or harassed under the penal and communication laws; private entrepreneurs held for a decade under caps and prohibitions the military conglomerate never faced.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The seat itself is term-limited, and the limit has so far been respected in form: the 2019 constitution caps the presidency at two consecutive five-year terms; Díaz-Canel is serving his second (2023–2028) and no move to amend the cap appears in the documented record through November 2026 (CIA World Factbook, 2026).
  • One referee was allowed to record a result the government disliked in scale if not in outcome: the National Electoral Council published the Family Code referendum's 33.15% "no" vote — by far the largest recorded opposition vote of the revolutionary era, against a government campaign — rather than the 90%+ margins routine in Cuban votes (National Electoral Council, September 2022; NPR/AP reporting, September 2022).
  • The June 2026 reform package, whatever its motives (Test 3), loosens the executive's economic grip in law: state enterprises are to set their own prices and convert to joint-stock companies, private banks are authorised under central-bank supervision, the foreign-trade monopoly is abolished, and general price controls are lifted (National Assembly approval, 18 June 2026; Rosa Luxemburg Stiftung and UPI reporting, June 2026). Implementation was still pending at grading — 32 implementing regulations were still to be written — so this is recorded as a loosening enacted, not yet a loosening lived.

Evidence against:

  • The referees are appointed inside the same house. Judges are elected by the National Assembly, the judicial ethics code requires loyalty to the Socialist Revolution, and Human Rights Watch records that courts remain subordinate to the executive, with no effective complaint mechanism for detainees; the US Department of State records pervasive impunity, with no known prosecutions of officials for human rights abuses including torture arising from the 11J crackdown (Freedom House, 2025–2026; HRW; US Department of State reporting summarised in the Canadian IRB National Documentation Package for Cuba, May 2026).
  • The media referee does not exist as an independent body: formal media are state-owned, the constitution prohibits privately owned media, independent outlets operate outside the law and are treated as "enemy propaganda," and their journalists are routinely harassed, detained and barred from travel (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025). In November 2025 Amnesty International recorded a state campaign against the independent outlet El Toque and 18 of its contributors (Amnesty International, via IRB package, 2026). Freedom on the Net 2026 scores Cuba 19/100, Not Free, and records continued blocking of independent news sites, multiyear prison sentences for online expression, and systematic digital surveillance.
  • Elections offer no independent count to grip or release: National Assembly candidates are vetted through the National Candidature Commission, all 470 deputies elected 26 March 2023 ran within the single-party framework, and the president himself was re-elected by that Assembly 460-odd votes to a handful — 97.7% — as the only candidate (CIA World Factbook, 2026; AP, April 2023).
  • Grip tightened on his watch, by law: Decree-Law 35 (2021) on telecommunications, the 2022 penal code's speech offences, and the Social Communication Law (effective October 2024) each extended state control over expression after he took office; the November 15, 2021 protest march called by the Archipiélago platform was banned in advance and its organisers detained or confined — independence of assembly punished before it could occur (Freedom House; Civicus Monitor, 2024–2025).
  • Party and state leadership are fused in his own person: since April 2021 the president, the party's First Secretary, and the commander-in-chief's authority sit in one man, and the party's Political Bureau — which includes the prime minister and the armed forces leadership — is the body whose plenary endorsed the June 2026 reforms the day before the Assembly unanimously approved them (PCC Central Committee plenum and National Assembly, June 2026). Unanimity in 24 hours is the documented signature of a legislature ratifying, not checking.

Why: The grade lands at FAILS because there is no referee in the Cuban system that Díaz-Canel could grip harder than it is already held — courts, electoral bodies, media and prosecution are constitutionally and practically inside the party-state he heads, and his tenure added new legal instruments of control rather than loosening any. The term limit and the honestly reported 2022 referendum count are real, and they are recorded above; they are limits he inherited and observed, not independence he protected.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: the party-state apparatus itself, whose control of courts, media and elections was consolidated; state and private economic actors who stand to gain if the June 2026 loosening is implemented.
  • Hurt: independent journalists and outlets (El Toque's contributors among them), judges and prosecutors whose professional autonomy is conditioned on political loyalty, voters offered no competitive choice in 2023, and every defendant whose judge answers to the same authority that prosecutes.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The economic wobble was eventually answered with the largest adjustment of the revolutionary era, owned in his own words. Announced by Díaz-Canel on 12 June 2026 and approved unanimously by the National Assembly on 18 June 2026, the package of 176 measures (174 in some press counts — the figures are printed as sourced, not blended) authorises private banks, private currency exchanges and a digital foreign-exchange market, ends the 100-employee cap and the one-business rule for entrepreneurs, abolishes the state foreign-trade monopoly, permits foreign investors to hold stakes without a state partner, converts state enterprises into joint-stock companies, and replaces the universal libreta ration with targeted subsidies (Rosa Luxemburg Stiftung, June 2026; UPI, June 2026; Human Rights Foundation, 2026). Díaz-Canel conceded on the record: "The mistake was not in proposing them, but in postponing them — and this phase of postponement must come to an end" (via Rosa Luxemburg Stiftung, June 2026). He also stated the reforms were "not doing this because of pressure from the Yankees, but because we have reached a moment of maturity and reflection" — a stated reason for declining to credit foreign pressure, given openly (National Assembly reporting, June 2026).
  • An earlier, smaller adjustment followed the street directly: within weeks of the 11J protests, the government legalised MSMEs (in force 20 September 2021) — the first legal private companies with employees since 1968 — and temporarily lifted customs duties on food, medicine and toiletries brought in by travellers (Decree-Law on MSMEs, August–September 2021; AFP reporting, 2021).
  • The 553-prisoner commitment was honoured after its diplomatic basis collapsed (Test 1): when Washington reversed the terrorism-list removal in January 2025, Havana paused, then resumed releases on 27–28 February 2025 and completed them in March — an adjustment to a foreign partner's reversal that kept the undertaking to the Vatican rather than abandoning it (Reuters, March 2025; Civicus Monitor, 2025).
  • The voters' one recorded pushback was accepted without reprisal in the count: the Family Code's 33.15% "no" — much of it organised by evangelical churches against a government campaign — was published, and Díaz-Canel's public response was acknowledgment ("it still has issues that our society as a whole does not understand") before signing the approved code (AP/NPR, September 2022).
  • The 2026 oil shock is the record's clearest no-good-choice situation, and it is graded by that clause's mechanics. The options that actually existed after January 2026 were these: after US forces captured Nicolás Maduro on 3 January 2026, Venezuelan oil shipments stopped; on 29 January 2026 a US executive order authorised tariffs on any country supplying oil to Cuba (the tariff measure was terminated 20 February 2026, the national emergency and other restrictions remaining) (White House executive orders, January–February 2026; Atlas Institute/ACAPS reporting, 2026). Cuba produces roughly a third of its oil needs; Venezuela and Mexico together had supplied 86% of its imported crude in 2025. The doors open to Havana were: ration and endure nationwide blackouts (chosen, with blackouts of 22–30 hours documented through 2026), seek emergency cargoes (a Russian tanker of some 700,000 barrels reached Matanzas in late March 2026, after Díaz-Canel stated the island had gone three months without a single tanker), or concede political conditions attached to US relief (declined). Each door carried real harm; no hindsight door is counted here. What remains gradable under the clause is graded: whether he built the trap — in part, yes: dependence on a single patron's oil and a decade of postponed reform (his own June 2026 admission) left no reserve when the patron fell; and whether he was honest inside it — largely, yes: the three-months-without-a-tanker statement and the March 2026 Council of Ministers' call for "immediate" transformation were public admissions of the position, not denials of it.

Evidence against:

  • The defining wobble of the tenure — 11J — was answered with denial and doubling down, in his own voice. Beyond the "order to fight" (Test 1), Díaz-Canel in August 2021 conceded only that "complex situations" can yield "some excess," and stated: "there is no one missing or tortured, I tell you responsibly. All families were told as soon as possible about the whereabouts of their people" (via HRW/Washington Post reporting, 2021). HRW's documented record — incommunicado detention for days to months, beatings, cases it assessed as torture — contradicts each clause of that statement; the contradiction is recorded here as documented evidence against a public denial, not as rhetoric.
  • The follow-up wobble was pre-empted rather than answered: the peaceful march called for 15 November 2021 was declared illegal, organisers were detained or besieged in their homes, and several were later prosecuted or driven into exile — HRW's summary of the post-11J choice offered to critics was its title: Prison or Exile.
  • The street kept pushing and the answer kept being detention. In March 2024, hundreds protested in Santiago de Cuba and elsewhere over blackouts of up to 18 hours and food shortages; the Cuban Conflict Observatory recorded 5,604 protest actions from March to October 2024 (a monitor's count, printed as sourced). After the total nationwide blackout of 18–22 October 2024 — the Antonio Guiteras plant failure that left some 10 million people without power — at least 66 demonstrations were recorded across seven provinces and police arbitrarily detained at least 23 people linked to protests in four provinces (Civicus Monitor, 2024; grid-collapse reporting, October 2024). In November 2024 a writer was detained for allegedly leading a peaceful protest in Encrucijada over electricity and remained in detention into 2025 (IACHR, December 2024).
  • Releases were used as a valve, then partially reversed — the adjustment was not held. Cubalex documented that between 2025 and early 2026 at least 14 released political prisoners were returned to prison through revocation of parole or extra-penal licences, and recorded 91 incidents of denial or revocation of prison benefits in 2026 alone, running at 13–20 per month since April 2026 (Cubalex, 17 November 2026). Among those returned were prisoners released under the very Vatican-mediated process completed in March 2025. An adjustment that can be revoked person by person, without judicial guarantees Cubalex could identify, is a wobble managed, not answered.
  • On the other-option test in its exact form: when another option became available in economic policy — the reform menu his own economists had proposed for years — he declined to look at it for most of the tenure, then adopted nearly all of it at once in June 2026, conceding the postponement was the mistake. Considering it late, under collapse, earns the FOR shelf its largest item; refusing to look at it from 2019 to 2025, while the crisis deepened, is the against-evidence. In political policy — competitive elections, independent media, legal opposition — no other option has been openly considered at any point in the tenure; each proposal from civil society (the 15N march, citizen legislative initiatives) was refused a hearing rather than declined with reasons.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because the record genuinely runs both ways, split by subject. On economics, the pattern is denial for years followed by real, openly owned adjustment — MSMEs after 11J, the 553 completed against the diplomatic weather, and a June 2026 package that adopts the long-refused menu with the postponement admitted aloud. On politics and protest, the pattern never changed: the street's pushback was met with an order to fight, a false public denial, pre-emptive bans, and detentions after every blackout protest through 2024, with releases afterwards revoked one by one. A ruler who adjusts on prices and doubles down on people earns both shelves in full.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: private entrepreneurs and their employees (MSME legalisation 2021; the June 2026 expansion); the 553 released prisoners and their families (2025); consumers if the 2026 market measures are implemented.
  • Hurt: 11J and later protesters met with detention rather than adjustment (2021–2024); released prisoners returned to prison by revocation (at least 14 documented by Cubalex, 2025–2026); the whole population during the years 2019–2025 when the reform option sat unconsidered while GDP contracted — a hurt Díaz-Canel's own "postponement" admission assigns, in part, to the delay itself.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the protesters, the dissenters, the Afro-Cuban and religious minorities, the east as well as Havana, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Family Code (Tests 1 and 3) counted in groups the old code had counted out: same-sex couples, children, grandparents, the elderly and people with disabilities gained enforceable rights, and the code's protections against gender violence apply to all women (Family Code, in force 27 September 2022).
  • The universal floor was defended as policy even as it frayed: free health care and education remained universal entitlements throughout the tenure, and the June 2026 package replaces the universal libreta ration not with nothing but with targeted transfers to the vulnerable — a stated intent to concentrate what remains on the poorest (National Assembly, June 2026; Rosa Luxemburg Stiftung analysis, 2026). The minimum wage was raised in the same package from 2,100 to 3,110 pesos.
  • The 2024 migration laws and the May 2026 abolition of the 24-month limit on time abroad (announced by the government, per the Canadian IRB package) count the emigrated as still belonging — Cubans abroad may retain residence rights rather than being struck from the roll for leaving.

Evidence against:

  • A whole category — those who protest — is counted as criminal, not as citizens with a grievance. Beyond 11J (Test 1): Amnesty International recorded in October 2026 that Afro-Cuban Yoruba religious leaders Loreto Hernández García and Donaida Pérez Paseiro remain arbitrarily imprisoned for peacefully protesting on 11 July 2021, with Pérez Paseiro reporting serious illness, inadequate medication and food, and official pressure to go into exile (Amnesty International, Urgent Action AMR 25/1617/2026, 28 October 2026). The counts of the still-imprisoned are printed as sourced, not blended: Prisoners Defenders counts over 1,300 political prisoners in Cuba (July 2026 reporting); Amnesty's regional researcher counts at least 300 people still serving 11J-linked sentences five years on, some exceeding 20 years; two US House members' letter to Díaz-Canel of 18 November 2026 speaks of "more than 200 political prisoners whose cases have been discussed" in US–Cuba exchanges and cites more than 1,300 political prisoners per human-rights advocates (USA Today, 23 November 2026). The shelves use each figure under its own source.
  • The exodus is the largest uncounting in the record. Cuba's own statistics office (ONEI) confirmed the population fell from 11,181,595 on 31 December 2021 to 10,055,968 in December 2023 — a drop of over 1.1 million, 10.1%, in two years — and to 9,748,007 by late 2024 (ONEI via National Assembly reporting, July 2024). Demographer Juan Carlos Albizu-Campos, using different methods, estimates the resident population at 8.62 million, an 18% fall between 2022 and 2023 (Albizu-Campos, 2024). The figures conflict and are printed separately; on either count, roughly a tenth to a fifth of the country left on this watch, driven, in the documented assessments, by the economic collapse and the post-11J repression together — people who voted with their feet and are no longer counted at all. Arrivals data corroborate the scale: 738,680 Cubans arrived in the United States alone between 1 October 2021 and 30 April 2024 (US border and parole records via Albizu-Campos).
  • The crisis's costs fell hardest on those with least, and the state's accounting protected the connected first. The October 2021–2026 sequence — inflation, blackouts, shortages — is detailed in Test 5; its distribution is the point here: blackouts of 22–30 hours a day were documented in the provinces (Zapata Swamp reporting, April 2026; Sancti Spíritus and Cienfuegos accounts, October 2026) while Havana was restored first in the October 2024 collapse (50% of Havana customers reconnected by 21 October, against 70.89% nationwide only by 22 October — state media figures); hospitals postponed operations, with a UN humanitarian reporting figure of surgeries postponed for more than 100,000 patients including 11,000 children (UN/WFP-sourced compilation, 2025–2026); and through it all the military conglomerate GAESA continued building luxury hotels while holding an estimated USD 4.3 billion in reserves (leaked-document reporting, August 2025 — ALLEGATION-grade as to the reserve figure, which the government has not confirmed; the hotel construction itself is documented).
  • Religious and racial minorities carry specific counts against: unregistered religious groups remain criminalised (membership punishable by up to three months, leaders up to two years; Jehovah's Witnesses have awaited a registration decision since 1994) (US Department of State, via IRB package, 2026), and the two named Yoruba prisoners above are Afro-Cuban religious leaders imprisoned for protest — the intersection of every counted-out category in two people.

Why: The grade lands at FAILS because the inclusions, though real, are inclusions inside the loyal population, while the exclusions are of whole classes defined by their relationship to the ruler: protesters imprisoned for years, dissenters offered prison or exile, and over a million people — on the government's own statistics — who removed themselves from the count entirely during his tenure. A Family Code that counts every kind of family cannot offset an accounting in which the fastest-growing category of Cuban is the departed, and in which peaceful religious leaders are still in prison in October 2026 for marching in July 2021.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: LGBTQ+ Cubans and their families; children, elderly people and people with disabilities under the Family Code; the vulnerable targeted by the 2026 subsidy redesign (intent documented, delivery not yet); emigrants whose residence rights the 2024–2026 migration changes protect.
  • Hurt: political prisoners — over 1,300 on Prisoners Defenders' count, including at least 300 remaining 11J prisoners on Amnesty's; Afro-Cuban Yoruba leaders Loreto Hernández García and Donaida Pérez Paseiro, named because Amnesty's documented record establishes their arbitrary imprisonment; provincial and poor households bearing 22–30-hour blackouts, postponed surgeries and collapsed purchasing power; the 1.1 million-plus (ONEI) who left in 2022–2023 and those who followed.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since April 2018 / October 2019, with dates; the two axes — word-keeping and helped/hurt — graded independently.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The 553 — kept, against the weather. In a letter to Pope Francis in early January 2025, Díaz-Canel committed to granting freedom to 553 persons "in the spirit of the Ordinary Jubilee of 2025" (Granma, 14 January 2025). The US side of the surrounding bargain was reversed on 20 January 2025; the releases paused, resumed, and were certified complete on 10 March 2025 (Reuters; Vatican News). Crossing 1 on the two axes: kept his word AND it helped people — 553 prisoners and their families, including protest-linked prisoners among them (the government never published how many of the 553 were 11J-linked; monitors verified at least 212 political prisoners among the releases (OCDH, March 2025)). The later revocations (Test 3) are recorded against the keeping's durability, not erased from it: at least 14 of the released were returned to prison by 2026 (Cubalex).
  • The Family Code — kept. Díaz-Canel declared his support for same-sex marriage in his first interview as head of state (Telesur, September 2018), promised the question would go to the people in a new Family Code, and delivered exactly that process and result in September 2022. Crossing 1: kept his word AND it helped people.
  • Term limits — kept so far. He took office under, and has governed within, the two-term constitutional cap his own constitution set; re-elected once, in April 2023, with his term ending in 2028 and no documented move to extend it. A word about limits kept by not being tested past its edge — recorded as kept to date.
  • Private enterprise — kept late. The 2011/2021 party lineaments and his government's 2021 pledges promised legal space for private economic actors; MSMEs were legalised in September 2021 and the June 2026 package extends the promise beyond its original terms. The keeping is real and the lateness is graded in Test 3, not double-counted here.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The Tarea Ordenamiento — the tenure's central economic word, broken in its stated purpose. The January 2021 currency unification was presented by the government as a reform that would raise real incomes, transparency and efficiency, with salary and pension increases (minimum wage raised roughly fivefold) designed to protect purchasing power. The documented outcome: official consumer inflation of 77% in 2021, 39% in 2022 and 31% in 2023 (ONEI figures via BTI/IEEE compilations), a consumption-deflator measure of 439% for 2021 (national-accounts deflator via Latin American Research Review), the informal dollar rising from 24 pesos at unification to some 480 pesos in January 2026 and 800 by October 2026 (informal-rate trackers via Cubanomics, 2026), and GDP more than 10% below its 2018 level by 2024, with the government itself acknowledging a cumulative contraction exceeding 15% since 2019 by 2026 and ECLAC projecting a further 6.5% fall in 2026 (ONEI; ECLAC, 2026). Crossing 4: the word was given, the word was not kept in substance, AND it hurt people — wage-earners and pensioners whose fivefold nominal raise was consumed within two years.
  • Electricity — promised stabilisation, delivered collapse. Through successive summers the government pledged that maintenance and fuel contracts would ease the blackouts; the record runs the other way: nationwide grid collapses on 18–22 October 2024, December 2024, September and December 2025, and repeatedly in March and September 2026 (grid-collapse chronology, 2024–2026), with only 34% of power-plant capacity available on an average day in January–May 2025 (IEEE Spectrum analysis of state electricity-company data) and generation down almost 14% in 2025 (Centre for the Study of the Cuban Economy). A September 2026 nationwide collapse affected approximately 9.4 million people (ACAPS, 2026). Crossing 4 again: word broken, hurt general.
  • "No one missing or tortured" — a factual word, contradicted. The August 2021 denial (Test 3) is also a Test 5 item: a specific public assurance against a documented record of incommunicado detention and abuse (HRW, 2022). Crossing 4 in its purest form in this dossier: the statement was not kept true, and the people it concerned were the detainees themselves.
  • Recovery and growth targets — receding on schedule. Successive annual plans pledged returns to growth; the economy contracted in 2023 (−1.9%, BTI 2026 country report), 2024 and 2025 (−5% in 2025 on government figures via 2026 reporting), with 2026 projected worse. Each year's plan was stated as direction; cumulatively they are a word the record has not met in any year since 2020's −10.9% (ONEI).
  • One crossing-2 entry, recorded because the axes are independent: the 2019 constitutional order itself — one party, irrevocable socialism — was a mandate he has kept faithfully, and keeping it has hurt the people graded in Tests 1, 2 and 4. The mandate rule applies: governing on the system's granted platform is not itself the failure; the hurt is recorded in those tests' ledgers in full, and the keeping is recorded here without laundering either way. Miguel Díaz-Canel is the first Cuban leader since 1959 who is not a Castro, and the record shows both what changed with the name and what did not. What changed: a Family Code that counted in families the revolution had counted out, delivered by genuine consultation and an honestly reported referendum (Test 4's FOR shelf, Test 5's cleanest keeping); a private sector legalised in 2021 and, in June 2026, a reform package of 176 measures that adopts — with the admission that "the mistake was not in proposing them, but in postponing them" — nearly the whole menu refused for a decade (Test 3's FOR shelf); and a numbered promise to the Vatican kept after Washington tore up its side of the bargain (Tests 1, 3 and 5). What did not change: at the source there are still two laws, because the constitution reserves legality to one party and the penal code prices speech by its loyalty (Test 1 — helped: loyal institutions and insiders; hurt: everyone prosecuted for position rather than act); no court, newsroom, electoral body or prosecutor stands outside the party-state he has personally headed since 2021 (Test 2 — helped: the apparatus; hurt: defendants, journalists and voters with no referee of their own); and the protesters of July 2021 — met with his broadcast "order to fight," a public denial that the documented record contradicts, sentences of up to 25 years, and at least 300 of them still in prison in 2026 alongside over 1,300 political prisoners on Prisoners Defenders' count — remain counted out, as do the more than 1.1 million Cubans who, on the government's own statistics, left the country in 2022–2023 alone (Test 4 — helped: the families the Code included; hurt: the imprisoned, the exiled and the departed). His word splits by kind (Test 5): numbered promises to named counterparties were kept and helped; outcome assurances — purchasing power, electricity, growth, "no one tortured" — were broken and hurt, and were renewed rather than corrected. The 2026 oil shock is graded under the no-good-choice clause: the doors open after Venezuela's shipments stopped and Washington's January 2026 tariff order were all harmful, his candour inside the trap was real — and so was his part in building it, a decade of postponed reform on a single patron's oil.
