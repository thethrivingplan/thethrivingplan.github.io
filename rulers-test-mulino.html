The Ruler's Test — José Raúl Mulino, President of Panama

Subject: José Raúl Mulino Quintero — President of Panama since 1 July 2024, in office at grading, his five-year term running to 30 June 2029. A maritime lawyer and former minister of public security (2009–2014) under President Ricardo Martinelli, he entered the 2024 race as Martinelli's vice-presidential running mate and became the presidential candidate only after Martinelli was disqualified; he won on 5 May 2024 with 34.2% of the vote. His standing with the public has fallen with the record: a CB Global Data regional survey conducted 4–9 November 2026 put his approval at 34.9%. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution of his own patron proceeded on his watch, under his own appointees. The Odebrecht money-laundering trial of Ricardo Martinelli — the man whose candidacy Mulino inherited and whose party he leads — opened on 12 January 2026 before Judge Baloisa Marquínez, with Martinelli participating by video from asylum in Colombia; prosecutors closed in February 2026 by asking for his conviction as a principal perpetrator, and as of late October 2026 the verdict was still awaited, the Supreme Court having rejected his defense's amparo petitions (Lava Jato Outcomes case record, updated November 2026; AFP and OCCRP, January–February 2026). Mulino did not stop it, pardon him, or remove the prosecutors.
  • He publicly disowned the legislative attempts to free Martinelli by statute. When RM deputy Luis Eduardo Camacho — Martinelli's self-described "faithful friend" — promoted bills redefining political crime and expanding amnesty and pardon mechanisms (bills 235 and 64, which opponents and Transparency International said would reach the convicted and accused in the New Business, Blue Apple and Odebrecht cases, including Martinelli and former president Juan Carlos Varela), Mulino said on 1 August 2024: "This law does not have my approval… I will act according to the law." Both bills were rejected in the Assembly's Government Committee, bill 64 on 24 April 2025 (La Prensa, August 2024 and April 2025).
  • Enforcement reached the previous administration's officials at scale: the IFARHU scholarship scandal — discretionary "economic aid" granted under the Cortizo government — produced the arrest on 7 July 2025 of former director Bernardo Meneses (held in preventive detention; the charges of embezzlement, unjustified enrichment and money laundering are ALLEGATIONs he faces, not verdicts) and, by November 2026, 36 people charged and an estimated fiscal damage of $76.2 million across ten investigations (Public Ministry reporting, July 2025–November 2026).
  • The Comptroller General's audit machinery was turned on a contract his own foreign policy needed resolved: the audit of the Panama Ports Company concession (covering 2014–2024) underpinned the Supreme Court's January 2026 annulment, and in November 2026 the Comptroller filed criminal complaints against former Maritime Authority board members and administrators of 2014–2023 and the company itself (Comptroller General's Office statement, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The single largest exception to one law in his term was made for the man at the source of his power. Martinelli — convicted in the New Business case on 18 July 2023 of money laundering, sentenced to 128 months in prison and a $19.2 million fine, the conviction made final by the Supreme Court on 2 February 2024 — never served a day of that sentence. After fifteen months sheltered in Nicaragua's embassy, he left Panama on 10 May 2025 under a safe-conduct granted by Mulino's government, traveling to asylum in Colombia granted by President Petro. An earlier safe-conduct for travel to Nicaragua, granted in March 2025, had lapsed when Nicaragua declined to receive him over the Interpol question. The Foreign Ministry framed both grants as compliance with the 1928 and 1933 asylum conventions; that the safe-conduct is a discretionary act of the territorial state, extended here to a convicted fugitive who is the president's party founder and political patron, is the documented shape of the act, and no comparably placed convicted person received one (Reuters and AP, March–May 2025; La Prensa, May 2025). Mulino had campaigned suggesting he would keep Martinelli out of jail and promising an end to "political persecution"; in office he also called the Bogotá party photographs of the asylee a "huge blunder" by his ambassador and stated his government had not negotiated the asylum — only issued the pass that made it effective.
  • The prosecution function itself is headed by his own former lawyer. Attorney General Luis Carlos Gómez Rudy, who took office in January 2025 on Mulino's nomination, had been Mulino's defense counsel in the Selex radar embezzlement investigation — the case in which Mulino was arrested on 28 October 2015 and which was annulled in 2016 on procedural grounds (out-of-term investigations without judicial authorization), leaving him never declared guilty or innocent (La Prensa, October 2024). Mulino publicly defended the nomination against the conflict objection. Whatever Gómez Rudy's individual conduct — and the Odebrecht prosecution above sits on his side of the ledger — the structural fact stands: the nation's prosecutor is the president's former personal defense attorney, a circumstance critics across the coverage identify by name, and one no opponent of the government enjoys in mirror image.
  • The criminal process fell hardest, in volume, on the government's opponents in the streets. The Attorney General's Office reported on 29 July 2025 that 281 people from the Bocas del Toro protests had been prosecuted (arson, apology of crime, attacks on public servants), and prosecutions of Law 462 protesters were still opening in November 2026. Union leaders were prosecuted in sequence: SUNTRACS secretary general Saúl Méndez ended in asylum abroad, historic leader Genaro López under house arrest, and Sitraibana president Francisco Smith was arrested days after signing the 11 June 2025 agreement that ended the banana strike, on charges tied to the roadblocks (the charges are ALLEGATIONs; the IUF and Global Labor Justice characterize the arrest as retaliation for the strike — contested between the parties) (Public Ministry, July 2025; Fruitnet and FreshPlaza, June 2025).
  • No comparably senior figure of Mulino's own government — no minister, no party official of his administration — appears in the documented record as charged with corruption through November 2026. The two great enforcement files of his term, IFARHU and Odebrecht, run against the camps of his two predecessors. That distribution may reflect where the documented misconduct sits; it is also, on its face, the pattern this test measures.
  • His inaugural address of 1 July 2024 used the presidency's first word on the country's largest corruption file to welcome the acquittal of the 28 Panama Papers defendants and to label the investigation "an international hoax to undermine the image and competitiveness of our country" — a signal, noted by the ICIJ at the time, about which side of the laundering question the seat would speak from (ICIJ, July 2024).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, the prosecution, the Comptroller, media, and autonomous institutions keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Supreme Court ruled against the centerpiece contract of his canal policy and he complied. On 29 January 2026 the Court declared Law 5 of 1997 — the Panama Ports Company concession for Balboa and Cristóbal, held by CK Hutchison's subsidiary since 1997 and extended in 2021 — unconstitutional, together with its addenda and extension, finding disproportionate favoritism harmful to the fiscal interest. Mulino's government executed the ruling: Executive Decree 23 of February 2026 ordered the Maritime Authority's temporary occupation of the terminals, and 18-month interim operating contracts went to APM Terminals (Balboa) and MSC's TiL (Cristóbal) on 23 February 2026. CK Hutchison rejected the ruling and pursued international claims of roughly $3.5 billion; Mulino rejected its accusations of unlawful seizure (Supreme Court ruling, 29 January 2026; Seatrade Maritime News, February–November 2026).
  • The electoral authority's independence was defended by his veto hand against his own coalition. On 12 November 2026 Mulino partially vetoed Bill 700, the Assembly's redrawing of electoral circuits for 2029 — approved 43–21 by an Assembly led since 1 July 2026 by his own party's Shirley Castañeda — on the ground that the Chiriquí changes rested on legislative criteria rather than the constitutional technical and demographic parameters of the Electoral Tribunal's proposal (Balboa News, November 2026).
  • For a full year the National Assembly was not his instrument: from July 2025 to June 2026 it was presided over by Jorge Herrera, elected with independent and opposition support over the RM preference, and it was the Assembly — not the executive — that brokered the 11 June 2025 banana-workers settlement Mulino had refused to negotiate on those terms (Banana Link, June 2025).
  • The Comptroller's office audited where it chose, including announcing a fiscalization of funds assigned to canal security and the endorsement audit of the fourth-bridge addendum, and its criminal complaints in the ports case named officials across three administrations (Panama Daily News, September 2026; Comptroller's statement, November 2026).
  • Courts checked the strike retaliation: a court overturned the measures against 32 teachers and ordered salaries paid and reinstatement — the documented failure, recorded in Test 3, is the Education Ministry's non-compliance, not judicial silence (Education International ILO complaint, June 2026). No media outlet has been closed, expropriated, or stripped of its license in the documented record; La Prensa and the critical press publish daily against him.

Evidence against:

  • The prosecution's structural independence is compromised at the appointment, as set out in Test 1: the Attorney General is the president's former defense lawyer, nominated by him over public objection. Every prosecution decision touching the president's circle, his patron, or his opponents is taken under that shadow, whether or not any individual decision is bent.
  • His government moved to extinguish the country's most combative union as a legal person. In July 2025 the Labor Ministry under Minister Jackeline Muñoz initiated the dissolution of SUNTRACS, the construction workers' union at the head of the 2025 strikes. The ministry's stated ground was legal non-compliance; SUNTRACS and international labor federations characterize the move as the administrative death penalty for a strike the government had already declared illegal — the characterization is contested, the dissolution proceeding is documented fact (union and labor-federation reporting, July 2025; Education International, 2026).
  • Autonomous institutions that opposed him were pressured in public. During the teachers' strike Mulino accused the rector of the University of Panama, Eduardo Flores Castro, of allowing "all kinds of political trenches and political conspiracies" inside the constitutionally autonomous university; the rector answered in print defending the university's autonomy (La Prensa, April 2025). His weekly press conferences have repeatedly been used to berate critical coverage, and his inaugural "hoax" framing of the Panama Papers investigation (Test 1) set the tone for official speech about scrutiny itself.
  • His two Supreme Court appointments followed the constitutional path — Gisela Agurto Ayala and Carlos Villalobos Jaén, nominated by Mulino and ratified by the Assembly with 65 votes in October 2025, taking their seats in January 2026 — but they mean that by the time the Court disposes of remaining Martinelli-era questions, two of its nine members are his nominees. That is the ordinary mechanism of the constitution, recorded here because the same Court's 8–1 ruling of 3 May 2024 — that his own substitute candidacy was constitutional, two days before the election — is the foundation of his title, a sequence his critics describe as the system's original favor to him and his defenders describe as the Court avoiding the greater harm of leaving two parties without a candidate (Electoral Tribunal Agreement 11-1, 4 March 2024; Supreme Court ruling, 3 May 2024). The description is contested; the sequence is not.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, unions, Congress, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Against Washington, at the height of the canal pressure campaign, he corrected the record in public three times. On 20 January 2025 he answered President Trump's inaugural claim that China operates the canal and the United States would take it back with a same-day statement that the canal "is and will remain" Panama's. In February 2025, when the US State Department announced that US government vessels would transit toll-free, Mulino publicly denied any such agreement — Panama's position, maintained since, is that its constitution bars waiving tolls and that the April 2025 joint statement's "First and Free" language means a cost-neutral compensation mechanism, not free passage. In March 2025 he said Trump was lying about reclaiming the canal, and in June 2025 he asked the US Embassy to stop publicly commenting on Panamanian decisions after it announced the replacement of Huawei telecommunications equipment (statements compiled in Panama–US relations records through November 2026; US Department of Defense joint statement, 8 April 2025).
  • The banana wobble he did absorb. Having refused the banana workers' terms and called their strike illegal, he accepted the Assembly-brokered 11 June 2025 agreement restoring the Law 45 pension benefits Law 462 had cut — the one substantive repeal-by-exception of his pension reform — and then negotiated Chiquita's return directly, signing the memorandum in Brasília on 29 August 2025 under which the company rehired 3,000 workers in a first phase and 2,000 in a second, with exports resuming in December 2025 (FreshFruitPortal and AP, June–September 2025).
  • The teachers' strike ended by agreement, not only by force: the 11–14 July 2025 settlement with the Education Ministry returned teachers to classrooms with written commitments on due process, non-retaliation, and continued dialogue (Education International, July 2026 summary of the agreement).
  • On the mine, he slowed his own evident preference into process: an environmental audit commissioned with IDB and World Bank advice in 2024 and executed by SGS (published June 2026: 361 of 370 environmental commitments met, ~88%, with gaps in reforestation and long-term tailings and water liabilities); stockpile-only exports authorized from May 2025 rather than a restart; an interministerial commission whose 17 recommendations of 30 September 2026 were led by an orderly, company-financed closure; and only then, on 16 November 2026, formal talks — conditioned on a definitive company-paid closure plan with no extensions, the withdrawal of all international arbitration, no new concession, and a public participation process (presidential address, 16 November 2026; First Quantum statement, 17 November 2026).
  • He used the veto as a constitutional instrument rather than a partisan one in the electoral-map case (Test 2, 12 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the central domestic wobble of his term he did not adjust at all. Law 462, his social-security reform of 18 March 2025, was answered from 23 April 2025 by an indefinite teachers' strike, joined by construction workers, banana workers, health workers, students, and Indigenous communities — the largest mobilization since the 2023 mining protests. Mulino refused to negotiate the law's repeal or revision with the movement as a bloc, declared the strikes illegal, docked strikers' pay, and held the law untouched; it stands unamended in its essentials today, praised by the IMF and Moody's and unrepealed (strike chronology, April–July 2025; Moody's, 17 November 2026).
  • When Bocas del Toro's unrest peaked, the answer was the suspension of constitutional government in one province. Cabinet Decree 27 of 20 June 2025 declared a state of urgency in Bocas del Toro, suspended at least eight constitutional norms — initially including habeas corpus — cut mobile telephony and residential internet across the province, and deployed "Operación Omega." At least 376 people were detained during the emergency per the Ministry of Public Security; the Attorney General reported 281 prosecutions arising from the Bocas protests. The emergency ran 20–29 June 2025 (IACHR statement, 11 July 2025, which took note of the state's constitutional framing and called for the judicial guarantees that may never be suspended).
  • The non-retaliation he signed in July 2025 was, on the documented union record, not honored. The ILO complaint filed by Education International in June 2026 documents 298 teachers from 23 schools in five provinces still unreinstated and unpaid a full year after the strike ended, disciplinary proceedings continuing against union leaders, and the Education Ministry's failure to comply with the court order reinstating 32 teachers. The complaint is the unions' filing — an interested source — but its central figures are specific, dated, and unanswered in the public record (Education International, ILO Committee on Freedom of Association complaint, June 2026).
  • The banana concession was paired with the arrest of the man who signed it: Sitraibana's Francisco Smith was detained days after the 11 June agreement, and the Chiquita rehiring he had struck for proceeded under a new operating model in which, the company stated, plantations would no longer be staffed by workers "affiliated with any union" (Banana Link, September 2025).
  • Under street pressure over the April 2025 US security memorandum — which lets US personnel rotate through three former US sites for training, on three-year renewable terms, with Panama able to exit on six months' notice — he refused any reassessment: "Panama's sovereignty is not at stake, it's not handed over, it's not given" (press conference, May 2025). The memorandum's text supports his legal position that no bases were granted — Panama's security minister publicly rejected Defense Secretary Hegseth's suggestion of bases during the visit itself — but the wobble was answered by refusal, not review (AFP, April–May 2025).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — the Ngäbe-Buglé and Guna peoples, Afro-Panamanians, campesinos, the poor, migrants in transit, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The macro-accounts improved for the whole flock on paper. Real GDP grew 4.4% in 2025 after 2.7–2.9% in 2024, and 5.5% in the first half of 2026; the fiscal deficit narrowed from 6.2% of GDP in 2024 to 3.7% in 2025; poverty at the World Bank's $8.30 line fell from 19.8% (2024) to 18.6% (2025); Moody's restored Panama's outlook to stable on 17 November 2026, citing the pension reform and normalized canal operations (World Bank country page, October 2026; INEC, 2026; Moody's, November 2026).
  • The Darién closure counted in the people who live there. Crossings fell from 520,942 in 2023 and 302,203 in 2024 to 3,091 in all of 2025, under his July 2024 repatriation agreement with the United States (22 charter deportation flights in 2025) and the fencing of the jungle paths — ending, for the Indigenous and Afro-descendant communities of the Darién, years of being the corridor of a half-million-person migration (National Migration Service figures, 2025; academic and press compilations, 2026).
  • Bocas del Toro's jobs were substantially restored: under the August 2025 Chiquita agreement, more than 1,600 workers had been rehired by late 2025 toward the 5,000 committed, with the special banana pension protections expressly preserved in the new model (Labor Ministry statements, 2025; FreshPlaza, 2025–2026).
  • His pension reform's stated purpose is itself a counting argument: Law 462 was defended by his government, and credited by the IMF and Moody's, as the measure that keeps the Caja de Seguro Social — the health and pension system covering nearly the whole population — solvent rather than collapsing onto the poorest (Moody's, November 2026; MEF statements, 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The emergency of June 2025 fell on the province where the counted-out live. Bocas del Toro is majority Indigenous (Ngäbe and Afro-Antillean) and was the site of the banana strike. The Ombudsman's Office and the National Torture Prevention Mechanism, after interviewing 186 detainees, reported excessive and indiscriminate force — tear gas, pepper spray, and rubber bullets used at close range contrary to UN standards — acts of humiliation, and detainees deprived of food, water, and medical care; 99 of those interviewed reported cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment and 26 reported coercion to confess. The Ombudsman confirmed two deaths during the unrest — a minor and an adult — and filed a criminal complaint over the alleged death of a girl of one year and eight months attributed to tear-gas exposure; the Attorney General reported 64 investigations into alleged police abuse in Bocas alone, 95 nationwide (Ombudsman's reports, 3 July and 9 September 2025; Attorney General's statement, 29 July 2025). These are the state's own oversight organs' findings, not the opposition's.
  • The teachers who struck were still being counted out a year later: 298 unreinstated and unpaid (Test 3), in a labor force drawn heavily from the rural and Indigenous provinces; and the banana workers were counted back into employment on the express condition that their union not come with them (Banana Link, September 2025).
  • The Río Indio reservoir advances over the campesino communities in its basin. The Canal Authority's $1.6 billion project — backed publicly by Mulino as part of an $8.5 billion canal expansion — will flood more than 11,000 acres including homes, schools, churches, and eight cemeteries, and requires the relocation of some 2,500 people (ACP figures). The Coordinadora Campesina por la Vida filed a Supreme Court challenge on 31 July 2025 alleging unconstitutional failure to consult; the ACP's claim of 85% community support is contested by the communities' organizations, which say the process presented pre-drafted agreements rather than consultation. Burials at the first cemetery were ended by decree in July 2026 and protests continued in August 2026, with relocations scheduled from 2027 (Reuters, 31 July 2025; CNN, October 2026; AFP, August 2026). The project's beneficiaries are the canal's users and the national treasury; its costs are assigned, in the first instance, to the people in the flood zone.
  • For three weeks in February–March 2025, Panama held 299 third-country nationals deported by the United States — Afghans, Iranians, Pakistanis, Russians, Chinese, Cameroonians, Somalis, Ethiopians among them, some of whom said they had been denied the chance to seek asylum — first confined in a Panama City hotel, where some held "Help Us" signs to the windows, then in the San Vicente camp near the Darién. They were released on 8 March 2025 with short humanitarian permits only after civil-society pressure and proceedings before the IACHR (program records and press investigations, February–October 2025). Panama had agreed on 2 February 2025 to serve as the bridge for deportations Washington could not execute directly; the people in the bridge were, for those weeks, counted as cargo.
  • Growth has not yet reached the jobless: unemployment rose from 7.4% (2023) and 9.5% (2024) to 10.4% in 2025 even as GDP grew 4.4%, and Panama remains, in the IMF's and World Bank's standing description, one of the world's most unequal countries, with poverty concentrated in rural and Indigenous areas — the comarcas' poverty rates run multiples of the national figure (IMF World Economic Outlook, 2026; World Bank, October 2026). The "chen chen" — money in every pocket — was the promise; the distribution, two years in, is the grievance that fills his mentions.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since July 2024, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Close the Darién — kept, completely. He promised in the campaign to close the jungle route; he signed the US repatriation memorandum on his first day in office, 1 July 2024, fenced the paths, and crossings fell 99% by 2025 (Test 4).
  • Fiscal order — kept, at political cost. He promised to restore the public finances and Panama's investment grade. The deficit fell from 6.2% of GDP (2024) to 3.7% (2025) on the strength of the pension reform and spending discipline; S&P affirmed BBB- stable (22 September 2026) and Moody's moved the outlook back to stable (17 November 2026), though Fitch remains at BB+ (World Bank, October 2026; Moody's, November 2026).
  • The mine, audit first — kept as process. In 2024 he said the closed mine's fate would be decided on an environmental audit, not by decree; the audit was commissioned with IDB/World Bank advice, executed by SGS, published in June 2026, and the November 2026 talks follow its terms, with his stated conditions — no new concession, arbitration withdrawn, company-paid definitive closure — matching his 2024 framing (presidential statements, July 2024 and 16 November 2026). Whether the destination honors the 2023 Supreme Court ruling and the Law 407 moratorium on new concessions is the open legal fight his opponents have already joined; the talks' "not a concession" theory is contested, by named mining opponents and jurists, as a distinction without a difference.
  • Chiquita — kept. He promised to bring the banana company back after the strike he had refused to settle on the union's terms; the August 2025 memorandum, the phased rehiring, and the December 2025 resumption of exports are documented (Test 3).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "Chen chen" — not delivered where it was promised. The campaign's central offer, run under the slogan "Mulino is Martinelli, Martinelli is Mulino," was a return to the Martinelli-era boom: jobs and money in every pocket. Two years in, unemployment is higher than when he took office (10.4% in 2025, IMF), his approval stands at 34.9%, and the phrase is used against him in the street and online as the name of the shortfall (IMF, 2026; CB Global Data, November 2026). Growth came back; the broadly felt prosperity that was the actual promise has not, on the documented indicators.
  • A new constitution — promised, unbuilt, and now contested in its route. Constitutional reform was a campaign commitment. What exists by November 2026 is a presidential secretariat for state reorganization and constitutional affairs (SEPRESAC) that ran a 2025–2026 consultation and "constitutional literacy" phase, and a late-2026 government push for a constituent assembly in 2027 whose "originaria" framing jurists and the Electoral Tribunal's own public warnings describe as lacking a basis in Articles 313–314, which provide only for reform by successive assemblies or a Parallel Constituent Assembly that cannot alter sitting officials' terms. Opponents have filed criminal complaints against the process's promoters — the complaints are ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated — and an opposing citizen drive collects signatures for a parallel assembly instead (constitutional text, Article 314; legal commentary and complaint filings, September–November 2026). Two and a half years after the promise, there is no assembly, no draft, and a dispute about whether the chosen road exists.
  • No surrender of sovereignty — his word, Washington's reading differs in the particulars. He promised Panama would not cede an inch; the April 2025 memorandum nonetheless institutionalized a rotating US military presence at three former US sites for the first time since 1999, and the "First and Free" toll formula was announced by Washington in terms (free passage) that Mulino then had to correct in public. His defenders note the memorandum is revocable on six months' notice and the facilities remain Panamanian; his critics note that he signed, under threat of the canal's seizure, the substance of what was demanded in February–April 2025: Belt and Road exit, ports audit, migration enforcement, troop access (memorandum text, 9 April 2025; joint statement, 8 April 2025).
  • The trains and works — receding. The Panama–David railway and the Metro Line 3 completion were campaigned as near-term deliveries; by November 2026 the railway remains in studies and contracting stages with no construction start, and the works most visible to the public are the canal's, not his (campaign record, 2024; government works reporting through November 2026 — stated as absence from the documented record, which later evidence can correct).
  • The long arc, factually: Mulino's career is Martinelli's shadow and its correction. As security minister (2009–2014) he was the strongman of an administration that ended with its president convicted of laundering public money into a media purchase; in October 2015 he was himself arrested in the Selex radar embezzlement investigation, and freed in 2016 when a court annulled the case for the prosecutor's procedural violations — never acquitted, never convicted. He won the presidency as a substitute, on the borrowed popularity of a man in an embassy asylum, promising that man's prosperity without that man's legal fate. In office the two halves of the inheritance are both visible: the fiscal and canal statecraft are the competent half; the patron extracted by safe-conduct, the prosecutor who was his lawyer, and the emergency in Bocas are the other. His word on the largest material promise — shared prosperity — is the one the indicators grade, and they grade it not yet kept.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

José Raúl Mulino governs a country whose institutions have, at several decisive points, ruled against his preferences and been obeyed — a Supreme Court that voided the ports concession his diplomacy needed gone and was complied with, an Assembly that spent a year under a president not of his choosing and rewrote his pension reform for the banana belt, a Comptroller filing criminal complaints across three administrations. That is the strongest evidence that Panama's seat still holds without fully gripping. But the exceptions cluster where this test always looks: at the source. His convicted patron was carried out of the reach of a final sentence by his government's own safe-conduct; the prosecutor who decides which Panamanians face the law is the lawyer who once defended him; the prosecutions of his term run against his predecessors' people and against the protesters in his streets, and not, in the documented record, against his own. And when the flock pushed back hardest, the answer in the one province where the Indigenous and Afro-descendant poor are the majority was suspended habeas corpus, cut communications, and force that the state's own Ombudsman documented as excessive and, in two confirmed cases, lethal. The macro-economy he promised to repair is repaired; the prosperity he promised to share is not yet shared; and the mine, the constitution, and the Odebrecht verdict — the three files that will finish grading this presidency — are all, as of November 2026, still open.
