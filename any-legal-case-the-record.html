The Core Tenet
“I am only asking that the law be followed ethically.”

This tenet comes from the pilot case — one person's own record — and is offered to every person using this tool as their own summary line, if they choose to adopt it. It is adopted by the person, never imposed.

Noted Very Clearly — With the Tenet, Always Kept in Mind
Innocence can be proven — or disproven — if all records are released.

The complete record is what settles it, in either direction — that is why this tool asks for records, all of them, and labels every gap as a gap.

The Tool's Place
“This is just a tool in the deciding factor of a situation. It is never the complete deciding factor.”

The deciding belongs to the people and the process the law names — this tool informs it and never replaces it.

Proof Equals Action, Only
Words mean nothing. Proof equals action, only.

Trust is settled by documented actions, never by statements — for every party, this record's keeper included. What a person knows, wants, or is comfortable with cannot be verified from outside; only their actions can be. A claim about anyone's intentions is therefore filed as opinion, or left out — never printed as fact.

Errors Are Allowed
“Errors are allowed. The problem is the refusal to fix a known error.”

Mistakes happen on every side, this record's included — its own corrections are printed in place, at full size. What this record measures is what happens after an error is known: corrected, or defended. The remedy follows the error: correct the record, restore what can be restored, and where a person is held on a record now known to be wrong, release is the correction — not a favor.

Cooperation Runs Both Ways
If there is nothing to hide, cooperation should be a no-brainer — in both directions.

“Just cooperate” is advice usually aimed one way. Aimed back, it asks the same of the system: release the records, keep the recordings, state the reasons. Innocence can be proven — or disproven — if all records are released; that door swings for everyone.

True Means Provable
What is true can be proven. What cannot be proven is word against word — and is filed as exactly that.

This record denies what is not true and asserts only what a record can carry. An unprovable claim is never declared false and never declared fact; it carries the label UNRECORDED — WORD AGAINST WORD until a record settles it.

The Two Columns
What happened is one column. What was done afterward is another. This record never mixes them.

Nothing in a record built on this template admits, hints at, concedes, or denies the facts of the underlying incident — or any figure in it. The documented conduct of the process is examined on its own evidence, and stands or falls on that evidence alone.

It Runs Both Ways — Not an Escape Hatch
Guilty? Use at your own risk.

This tool is not a way out of anything. It builds a record, and a record runs both ways: the same release of records that can prove innocence can disprove it, and the same work that clears a person on one point can open a door that shows their guilt in another way. If it does, that stands on the record too — entered by the same rule, at the same size, in the same type as everything that helps. Nothing this tool surfaces about its own user is hidden, softened, or removed because it hurts. That is the price of the tool's one promise: that what it prints can be believed. Anyone unwilling to carry what the record shows about themselves should not start one — and anyone who starts one has accepted this term by starting.

Easier, Not Harder
If that is the stated goal, the process should make it easier to reach — not harder.

Every stated goal — rehabilitation, safety, fairness, compliance — is tested by a completion check: does what the process actually requires protect and advance that outcome, or does it obstruct it? A requirement that works against the goal it claims to serve is entered in the record as exactly that.

Trust Is a Ledger
Trust is built from documented track records — every party alike, in the open.

Who is more trustworthy is never settled by whose word a room is built to believe. It is settled the way Door 7 counts it: documented findings, proven actions, and corrections, kept for every party by one standard — the record keeper's own record first.

A licensed lie still counts in this ledger. Where the law permits an official to use deception — even to obtain a confession — permission changes the legal consequence of the false statement. It does not erase the statement from the trust record. In Door 7 it is entered exactly as it is: documented deception — legally permitted, at the same size as any other party's documented false statement, and weighed by the same tests: Was it documented? Was it material — did it aim at a decision or a confession? Was it under oath? Was it ever corrected? A party with no documented false statements does not fall behind a party with one documented deception merely because the room was built to trust the second party more, and a deception does not count for less merely because the law allowed it. Nor does it count for more than the same false statement by anyone else. One standard, counted the same, is the whole point of the ledger.

Change Takes a Decision
Change does not take time. It takes a decision. Point blank.

“It takes time to fix these things” is tested like any other claim: against documented action. A single person, carrying a full working life, can assemble a complete, sourced record of a case in days — not because the work is trivial, but because the decision to do it was made. The honest limit is printed beside the claim: one or two people coordinating is not the same task as an institution coordinating, and genuinely complex reform can be shown to be complex. But that limit answers a claim nobody made. The excuse as offered is that change itself takes time. Against that claim, the record asks a plainer question: for this known error, with this known fix, what — besides a decision — is still missing? If nothing is named, the entry reads: decision not yet made.

Neither Ledger Erases the Other
You should not have done it — and they should not have done what they should not do, either.

The snowball in Door 9 — the accumulating costs and consequences set in motion after an incident — will predictably be met with a deflection: “Well, you should not have done it.” The answer is entered in advance, in the nicest way possible: courtesy in the manner, with the contents untouched. The person's act, whatever the record proves about it, began the costs that belong to it — and it sits in the first column, where it is answered. The system's acts began the rest, and they sit in the second column. Neither column cancels the other. A deflection that points at the first column is not an answer about the second, and this record does not accept it as one.

Count the Whole Cost
Count the appearance costs — including the ripple.

A case costs more than its fines and fees. Parking, fuel, travel, lost time, missed work — and the people who absorb the morning with you: the family member who drives, the employer who covers, the household that rearranges. Those costs roll forward into the next obligation, like a snowball. All of it goes in the ledger, because all of it is real.

Name It Honestly
Name it honestly — and make it make sense.

Every entry is called what it is: an opinion is labeled opinion, an assumption is labeled an assumption, a gap is labeled a gap. And over any entry that does not add up, the record asks the standing question in plain words: make it make sense.

Explain the Video — and Fix What Is Known to Be Wrong

A recording can settle what words cannot. That is why every decision about it needs an explanation — in either direction. This section turns four plain questions into a record any person can build, and pairs them with the fix when the answer shows an error.

Four questions — asked of whoever holds the recording
  • 1

    Why was it erased — or why was it not erased? If a recording was erased, state when, by whom, under what schedule or order, and whether a preservation request had been received. If it was kept past its usual window, state why. Either answer is recorded as stated. No answer is entered as REASON NOT STATED.

  • 2

    Why was it not kept? If no copy was retained, state the retention window, who set it, and whether keeping it longer was possible. A short window is a policy choice, not a fact of nature — the holder names the choice and the reason for it, or the entry reads REASON NOT STATED.

  • 3

    Why was it not used? If a recording exists but was not viewed, not produced, or not shown to the decision-maker, state why. A recording that exists and is not produced is a gap at full size — it is never blurred with a recording that never existed.

  • 4

    If it was used, was it used correctly? Check the use, not just the existence: Was the complete recording used, or only an excerpt? Was it unaltered, with its timestamp and context intact? Is there a chain of custody? Was the other side able to view the same recording? Was what it shows described accurately in reports and in court? Each check is answered from a document or the recording itself — yes, no, or NOT VERIFIED.

How to fix it

Now, for this case: send the written preservation request first, through counsel (Door 3). Log every answer above in Door 3's inventory. If an excerpt was used, ask for the complete recording. If a description does not match what the recording shows, enter the correction beside the description, at the same size.

For past cases: the same audit applies backwards. Where a past recording decision, or any other past step, is now known to have been wrong, the correction is entered where the error stood — what was wrong, how it is known, and what was done about it. A correction is not complete until its consequence is addressed.

Where a person should be released: if the corrected record no longer supports holding a person — a charge, a condition, or a detention that rested on the error — then release is the fix. It is recorded as a correction of a known error, with the corrected record as its reason. Keeping a person held on a record known to be wrong is not caution; under this template's standard, it is the refusal itself.

Where the Line Stops — What Each Side Is Allowed, and Why

A fair question, asked plainly: what are they allowed to get away with that I am not — and why? The honest answer is not “there is no line for them.” A line exists on paper for everyone. What differs is where it is drawn, and how it is enforced. This section prints both, in the US federal frame, labeled as such; state numbering and labels vary. Door 6 holds the questioning record in full; Door 7 holds the trust count that follows from it.

The asymmetries — documented, not alleged
  • 1

    Lying to obtain a statement. An official may use deception in questioning — falsely stating what the evidence shows, or that someone else has confessed — as a permitted tactic, within limits (Frazier v. Cupp, 394 U.S. 731 (1969)). The citizen in the same room may not: a materially false statement to a peace officer or federal investigator is itself a crime (for example, Texas Penal Code § 37.08; 18 U.S.C. § 1001). The formal limits on the official's side are lies about the person's rights, threats, and false promises of leniency made by someone in authority.

  • 2

    Threatening to obtain agreement. A prosecutor may openly threaten to bring a heavier charge if the person insists on a trial instead of accepting a plea — that pressure, stated on the record, is constitutional bargaining (Bordenkircher v. Hayes, 434 U.S. 357 (1978)). A citizen who threatens another person to obtain their agreement commits coercion or extortion under ordinary law. An agreement produced under either pressure is entered in this record as what it is: an agreement made under pressure — and a person who reconsiders after time to think has changed their answer, not broken their word.

  • 3

    The powers of the office. Arrest, search, and seizure are lawful when an official does them under authority, and crimes — assault, trespass, unlawful restraint — when anyone else does the same acts without it. This asymmetry is the office itself, and no record disputes that an office needs powers. What the record asks is only that each use of them be documented and answerable, like everything else here.

  • 4

    A shield the citizen does not get. Under qualified immunity, an official whose act violated a person's rights is answerable in a civil suit only if the right was “clearly established” in a closely similar case. No equivalent gate stands in front of a claim against a citizen. The practical effect: the same wrongful act can be actionable in one direction and gated in the other.

Why — the law's stated reasons, labeled as stated

The reasons the law itself gives, entered from its own opinions and stated purposes — never as this record's guess at anyone's motives: the office must be able to investigate crime, to bargain cases to resolution, and to make hard decisions without personal ruin for every close call; and deception is framed, in the cases that permit it, as a tool aimed at the guilty, limited by the voluntariness test that surrounds it. A reader may find those reasons sufficient or insufficient. This record prints them at full size either way — and then prints the enforcement record beside them.

Where the line formally stops — for officials too, on paper

Perjury under oath. Tampering with or fabricating evidence. Official oppression — an official intentionally subjecting another to mistreatment or arrest the official knows is unlawful, under statutes of that name or kind. Deprivation of rights under color of law (18 U.S.C. § 242). Presenting testimony known to be false (Napue v. Illinois). Knowingly false statements in a warrant affidavit, open to challenge under Franks v. Delaware. These lines are real, and this record does not pretend otherwise.

It is not that no line exists for them. It is that one side's lines are enforced by default, at the window — and the other side's lines are enforced through gates.

That is the accurate summary, and it is the one this record uses: a charge for the citizen's false statement follows as a matter of routine; a consequence for the official's documented violation must usually be pursued — through a hearing, a disclosure rule, an immunity gate, or a prosecutor's decision to charge one of their own system's actors. A difference in enforcement, repeated daily, reads in practice as a difference in kind. Where a case tests it, the record enters which gate was reached, and what happened there — or NO ACTION RECORDED.

And “it takes time to fix”?

Tested by the same rule as everything else here — actions, not statements. Preserving a recording before its clock runs out takes a letter. Printing the reason for a decision takes the reason. Releasing a person held on a record now known to be wrong takes an order. Each of those takes a decision, not a season. Where a fix is genuinely complex, complexity can be documented like anything else — what steps remain, who owns them, what date they are due. An undated “it takes time,” with no steps named, is entered as it stands: REASON NOT STATED for the delay, and the delay itself counted in Door 9's ledger.

Who is more trustworthy — counted, not assumed

Word against word is never settled by the words, and never by whose word a room is built to believe. It is settled in Door 7, by the verification process this whole template runs: documented false statements, documented findings, documented corrections — every party alike, the record keeper first. Applied to the hardest case: if an official is documented to have lied — even lawfully, even only to obtain a confession — that party carries one documented deception, legally permitted in the ledger. If the other party carries none, then on the proven documentation the second party is, on that count, the more trustworthy of the two: one documented deception against zero is not a tie, and a license is not an eraser. The same count, reversed, rules the other way with equal force — if the person's own record carries the documented falsehoods, the ledger says so at the same size. Permission changes what the law does about a lie. It has never changed whether the lie happened, and trust — in this record — is counted on what happened.

The law already keeps a version of this count in secret: under Giglio v. United States (1972), an officer's documented dishonesty must be disclosed as impeachment material, and prosecutors keep credibility lists of officers whose proven record makes them unusable as witnesses. Door 7 is the open version of that same idea — every party's documented record in the open, by one standard.

Why this door? An interrogation is not a conversation between equals under one rule. The law permits one side tactics it forbids the other. That asymmetry is documented law, printed here so it can be read before it is met.

The law — the official's side (US federal frame)

In , 394 U.S. 731 (1969), the US Supreme Court held that police deception during questioning — there, falsely telling a suspect his cousin had confessed — does not by itself make a statement involuntary. Misrepresenting evidence is, within limits, a permitted interrogation tactic under federal law.

Frazier v. Cupp (1969). The Court treated the lie as one factor in the totality of the circumstances, not a disqualifier. The holding is the foundation most later deception rulings build on.

The limits are real, and printed at the same size. An officer who lies under oath faces perjury law (§ 37.02-style perjury statutes — state numbering and labels vary). Knowingly false statements in affidavits can be challenged under . A prosecutor who presents testimony known to be false violates due process under .

Franks v. Delaware (1978). Where a defendant shows an affidavit contained a false statement made knowingly, intentionally, or with reckless disregard for the truth, and the statement was necessary to the finding of probable cause, the defendant is entitled to a hearing — and the warrant falls if the claim is proven.
Napue v. Illinois (1959). A conviction obtained through testimony the prosecution knew to be false — including false testimony that merely affects a witness's credibility — violates the Fourteenth Amendment, whether or not the prosecution asked for the lie.

The law — the citizen's side (labeled by jurisdiction)

The same words, spoken the other direction, carry criminal exposure. Documented examples: Texas Penal Code § 37.08 makes a false report to a peace officer an offense; 18 U.S.C. § 1001 makes a materially false statement to federal investigators a felony — and in the Supreme Court rejected an "exculpatory no" exception, so even a bare false denial is covered.

Brogan v. United States (1998). A simple "no," spoken to federal investigators and false, is within § 1001. The Court declined to carve out mere denials.

Silence is the protected alternative. The right to say nothing is constitutional; a false statement is not. This asymmetry is the practical reason communication in a case runs through counsel and the written record (Statement 3, above).

The record — what questioning produces

The documented cost of the asymmetry, from the exoneration record: false confessions or admissions appear in roughly 30% of DNA exonerations; juveniles are two to three times more likely than adults to confess falsely; and the National Registry of Exonerations counts 89 exonerations involving false confessions in its documented tallies. These figures are entered as published by their sources and are re-checked when this page is updated.

The line this door draws

The law classifies the speaker and the direction, not the sentence. The identical false sentence is a tactic in one mouth and a crime in the other. This door accuses no individual; it prints the frame every person walking into questioning is actually standing in.

Word against word — and who the room is built to trust

Where no recording or document sits between two accounts — Door 1's label — the outcome turns on whose word is credited. That tie is not played on level ground: one side's false statement in the room can be licensed technique (Frazier, above), while the other side's is a crime (§ 37.08; § 1001, above). Pressure applied through a licensed deception is still pressure. Any statement or agreement produced under it is entered here with that label attached — the same discipline this door applies to an agreement signed under the pressure of a pending charge: an agreement made under pressure is recorded as made under pressure, and a person who reconsiders after time to think has changed their answer, not broken their word.

Where the line stops — pressure, powers, and the shield

Threatening to obtain agreement. In , 434 U.S. 357 (1978), the Supreme Court upheld a prosecutor's open threat to bring a heavier charge if the defendant refused a plea and insisted on trial. Stated pressure of that kind, from that side, is constitutional bargaining. The same threat made by a citizen to obtain someone's agreement is coercion or extortion. The line, again, classifies the speaker.

Bordenkircher v. Hayes (1978). The prosecutor offered a plea on a lesser penalty, stated he would seek a habitual-offender indictment — carrying a mandatory life sentence — if the offer was refused, and did so when it was refused. The Court held this did not punish the defendant for exercising a right; presenting the choice, however hard, was part of the plea-bargaining system.

The powers and the shield. Arrest, search, and seizure are lawful under the office's authority and unlawful without it — the office itself is an asymmetry no record disputes. What this door adds is the shield: under qualified immunity, an official who violated a right is civilly answerable only if that right was “clearly established” in a closely similar case. A citizen faces no such gate.

The lines that do bind officials, on paper. Perjury; evidence tampering and fabrication; official oppression; and deprivation of rights under color of law, 18 U.S.C. § 242. This door's honest summary, printed at full size: it is not that no line exists for them — it is that the citizen's lines are enforced by default, and the official's lines are enforced through gates — a Franks hearing, a Giglio disclosure, an immunity ruling, a prosecutor's choice to charge. Where this case reaches one of those gates, the record names the gate and its outcome, or enters NO ACTION RECORDED.

The law's stated why. Entered from the opinions themselves, labeled as stated: the office must investigate, bargain, and decide without personal ruin for close calls, and deception is framed as a tool aimed at the guilty, bounded by voluntariness. This door prints those reasons beside the asymmetries they are offered to justify — and beside the enforcement record, where the reasons meet practice.

Counting a licensed lie — more trustworthy, or less?

The verification question, asked directly: if a party is documented to have lied — even lawfully, even only to obtain a confession — are they more of a liar, or less, than a party with no documented lie? This door answers by counting, the only way it answers anything:

Permission changes the legal consequence. It does not change the count. A deception the law permits is entered as documented deception — legally permitted. It is not entered as zero, and it is not entered as worse than an identical deception by anyone else. Each entry is then weighed by the same four tests: documented (a record proves it, not a rumor), material (it aimed at a decision — a confession, a plea, a ruling), under oath or not, and corrected or defended once known.

So the comparison is arithmetic, not deference: one documented deception against zero documented false statements is one against zero — the party at zero is, on that count, more trustworthy on the proven documentation, whatever either party's role. Reversed counts reverse the answer, at the same size, by the same tests. What anyone intended by a false statement is interior and cannot be verified; the statement, its materiality, and its correction are actions and documents, and those are what this ledger counts.

The law already keeps this ledger — in secret

Under Giglio v. United States, 405 U.S. 150 (1972), a witness's documented dishonesty is impeachment material the prosecution must disclose, and prosecutors and agencies maintain credibility lists — sometimes called do-not-call lists — of officers whose documented record makes them unusable as witnesses. Documented findings, counted, deciding who is trusted: the law already runs this door's method. The difference is that theirs is kept behind a door, released only as it touches a case. This door is the open version: every party's documented record carried in the open, by one standard — the person's own row first, corrections included — so who is more trustworthy is settled by proven documentation, not by whose word the room was built to believe.

The deflection — answered in advance, in the nicest way possible

When this ledger is presented, a response can be expected, and this door enters it — and its answer — before it is given, so the exchange is on the record either way:

“Well, you should not have done it.”

The rebuttal, with courtesy in the manner and nothing removed from the contents: they should not have done what they should not do, either. Said in full, in the politest form this record uses:

“That may well be answered in its own column, and it will be. It does not answer this one. This entry is about what was done afterward, by the system, at its own choice — the requirement, the cost, the error, the delay. What is the answer for this entry?”

The discipline underneath is the Two Columns rule: the person's act began the costs that belong to it, and that ledger is kept honestly in Doors 1, 10, and 12 — nothing in this rebuttal denies, minimizes, or concedes anything about it. The system's acts began the rest of the snowball, and that ledger is kept here. Neither ledger erases the other.
