The Ruler's Test — Aleksandar Vučić, President of Serbia (2017–2026)

Subject: Aleksandar Vučić — President of Serbia from 31 May 2017 until his resignation on 27 September 2026; previously Prime Minister (2014–2017) and First Deputy Prime Minister (2012–2014). President of the Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) from 2012 to May 2023 and its de facto leader thereafter — the party's electoral lists bore his name in 2023 and again in 2026. On 9 September 2026 he dissolved the National Assembly and called snap parliamentary elections; on 27 September he resigned the presidency to head the "Aleksandar Vučić – United Serbia" list, with Assembly Speaker Ana Brnabić becoming acting president. In the election of 25 October 2026 the Student List won 53.69% of the vote and 140 of 250 seats; his list won 39.48% and 103 seats. He conceded defeat on election night. He is graded here on his completed tenure. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Organized Crime Prosecutor's Office (TOK) did move against insiders while he held power. It opened an investigation into former ministers Goran Vesić and Tomislav Momirović over suspected malfeasance on the Novi Sad–Kelebija high-speed railway (an alleged $115 million in damage to the state budget), and on 15 December 2025 it indicted sitting Culture Minister Nikola Selaković and three officials for abuse of office and forgery in stripping the Generalštab complex of its protected-monument status so it could be redeveloped by a Jared Kushner-backed firm. All four deny wrongdoing; the trial began in February 2026 (TOK statements; Special Court proceedings, 2026; Financial Times, 22 November 2026).
  • Prosecutors did indict 13 people over the Novi Sad canopy collapse, including former minister Vesić, and when a Higher Court panel halted proceedings against six of them in December 2025 for "insufficient evidence," the prosecution appealed and the Novi Sad Court of Appeal reinstated the indictment in full on 15 June 2026 (Higher Public Prosecutor's Office Novi Sad; Court of Appeal decision, June 2026).
  • His final pardons were not confined to his own side: of the 49 people he pardoned on 27 September 2026, twelve were the Novi Sad activists and students prosecuted in the "coup" case (Test 2) — the pardon power ended their prosecution too (Ministry of Justice list, published 2–3 November 2026; CRTA.Plus analysis). The seam is documented: the activists' lawyers said they had been close to acquittal, had not sought pardons, and the decisions took over a month to reach the court trying them.

Evidence against:

  • The same 49 pardons are the single clearest one-law document of his tenure. Signed on 27 September 2026, the day he resigned, with the names withheld until after the election, they released 40 of the 49 from criminal prosecution before any verdict — among them Marko Pavlović, charged with attempted aggravated murder for driving his car into student Sonja Ponjavić in January 2025, whose sentencing had been scheduled for 6 November 2026; eleven people in total charged over attacks on protesters; former Interior Ministry state secretary Dijana Hrkalović, on trial for trading in influence; and fifteen members of the Niš City Emergency Staff charged with abuse of office over 843 million dinars of damage — while their co-defendant, the former mayor of Niš, was not pardoned, a differential inside a single case. One convicted manager was pardoned although the trial court had formally opined, twelve days earlier, that there were no grounds to release him (courts' statements, November 2026; CRTA.Plus, "Vučić's 49 pardons," November 2026; BIRN, 20 November 2026). A commission of the Supreme Public Prosecutor's Office later found that the Belgrade Higher Prosecutor's Office had dismissed a criminal complaint over the pardons within five days without reviewing a single one of the 49 decisions (CRTA.Plus, November 2026).
  • The man who admitted organizing an armed attack received the system's gentlest track. Milan Radoičić, then vice-president of the Belgrade-backed Serb List, publicly assumed responsibility through his lawyer for organizing the 24 September 2023 Banjska attack in northern Kosovo, in which Kosovo police officer Afrim Bunjaku and three attackers were killed. In Serbia he was questioned in October 2023, released after one day, and subjected only to reporting duties and a travel ban; the European Parliament deplored the refusal to detain him. Three years on, Serbia's case remains an investigation without an indictment; Serbia refused Kosovo's request to hand him over. Kosovo indicted 45 people and convicted the three men arrested at the scene at first instance in April 2026 (two life sentences, one of thirty years); Radoičić, first among the 42 at large, cannot be tried there in absentia. On 15 November 2026 he failed to report as ordered; a Belgrade court ordered his detention and issued an arrest warrant, and a former intelligence inspector stated on N1 that he had left for Russia — a report no institution has confirmed, and the court states his passport is in its custody (Higher Court Belgrade statements, November 2026; BIRN; Kosovo Dispatch, 14 November 2026). Whether Serbian state structures were involved in Banjska is ALLEGATION — Kosovo alleges it, Serbia denies it, and no court has examined it.
  • When the organized-crime prosecutor began investigating former ministers, the law was changed around the prosecutor. The "Mrdić laws," adopted by parliament on 28 January 2026 under urgent procedure without inclusive consultation, subordinated TOK and the High-Tech Crime Prosecutor's Office to the Belgrade Higher Public Prosecutor's Office — headed by Nenad Stefanović, widely described as a Vučić loyalist — and terminated at short notice the assignments of all seconded prosecutors. The Venice Commission's ex-post opinion (April 2026) found the amendments removed safeguards for judicial independence and prosecutorial autonomy introduced by the 2023 constitutional amendments; the European Commission's 2026 report found no progress in the judiciary and increased political pressure (Venice Commission, April 2026; European Commission Serbia Report, 4 November 2026).
  • The canopy case itself shows the ceiling. Officials and pro-government outlets initially claimed the canopy had never been reconstructed, although footage showed Minister Vesić opening the renovated station. Two years after 16 people died, the proceedings are split across three prosecutor's offices and no trial has begun in any of them: the Novi Sad file sits at the Supreme Court on defense motions, and the Belgrade indictment has been overturned three times amid a jurisdictional dispute (Higher Court Novi Sad statement, 30 October 2026; Beta; N1). The European Public Prosecutor's Office opened its own investigation in March 2025 into potential misuse of EU funds in the station reconstruction (European Parliament resolution, 21 October 2025).
  • The rules of political competition were uneven by documented design: ODIHR found the December 2023 elections "dominated by the decisive involvement of the President," with pressure on public-sector employees, misuse of public resources, and vote-buying creating "unjust conditions" (ODIHR Final Report, 28 February 2024), and found the same pattern — "grave misuse of public resources," intimidation and pressure on voters — in the October 2026 elections he called (IEOM Preliminary Statement, 26 October 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the media regulator, public broadcasters, universities, and municipalities keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The grip was never total, and the surviving autonomy ended his rule. Independent outlets (N1, Nova, Danas, Vreme) published and broadcast throughout his tenure; courts occasionally ruled against his government, including the Constitutional Court's July 2024 ruling that the 2022 revocation of the Jadar project's spatial plan was unlawful (a ruling that served his own revival of the project, but was obeyed as law); and the electoral machinery in October 2026 counted a defeat he then accepted (below, Test 3).
  • The formal architecture of independence was, on paper, strengthened mid-tenure: constitutional amendments adopted by referendum in 2022 and implemented in 2023 were designed to insulate judicial and prosecutorial appointments from parliament — the safeguards the Venice Commission later found the 2026 amendments removed.

Evidence against:

  • The media regulator was neutralized. REM, the electronic-media regulator, was described by international observers in October 2026 as "effectively paralysed," having adopted not a single decision in the previous 22 months — spanning the entire protest period and the run-up to the election. A new REM Council was elected only on 12 November 2025; its four independent members resigned in December 2025 and returned only in July 2026 after an authentic interpretation of the media law (Media Diversity Institute open letter, 19 October 2026; State Media Monitor, October 2026).
  • The public broadcasters were captured in every review cycle. The State Media Monitor classifies RTS and RTV as "Captured Public/State-Managed" in every cycle since 2022. In the 2026 campaign, BIRODI's monitoring found 96% of the coverage time given to Vučić's list was positive and 95% of the time given to the Student List was negative; ODIHR found "a pronounced bias in monitored media coverage in favour of the incumbent party" (BIRODI, October 2026; IEOM Preliminary Statement, 26 October 2026). Pro-government tabloids and televisions routinely depicted protesters and critical journalists as "terrorists" and foreign agents (OCCRP, 2025; European Parliament resolution, 21 October 2025). Serbia ranked 96th on the RSF World Press Freedom Index 2025; the Media Freedom Rapid Response recorded 209 press-freedom violations in 2025, up from 84 in 2024 (RSF; MFRR).
  • The last independent broadcaster was worked on through ownership. OCCRP documented, from a source familiar with the talks and leaked correspondence, that state-owned Telekom Srbija's CEO Vladimir Lučić discussed with United Group's new CEO Stan Miller the removal of United Media's chief executive Aleksandra Subotić — the executive who had kept N1 independent — and that Miller said he knew the president was unhappy she had not yet been fired. Vučić had told pro-government Pink TV in February 2025 that some N1 staff would be dismissed in November. OCCRP stated it had seen no evidence Vučić instructed anyone; his spokesperson said he "did not interfere in the editorial policy of the media," and Lučić denied discussing the removal (OCCRP, August 2025). Telekom Srbija removed N1 and Nova S from its Total TV platform in April 2025; in 2026 Adria News Network was sold to Alpac Capital, which took control of the Serbian outlets in August 2026 amid documented concerns from MFRR partners about editorial independence (United Group statements; State Media Monitor chronology, 2026).
  • The security service's material went to television before it went to prosecutors. A BIA recording of a private meeting of Free Citizens Movement (PSG) members and STAV activists in Novi Sad on 12 March 2025 was aired on 13 March simultaneously on five pro-government televisions — captioned "Criminal plan uncovered," with names and photographs — before the BIA delivered it to the Higher Prosecutor's Office. Six activists were arrested on 14 March on suspicion of preparing acts against the constitutional order; six more, then abroad, were prosecuted in absentia, and Interpol refused Serbia's warrants for them, assessing the case as political. It emerged from the indictment motion that the BIA had monitored and wiretapped PSG vice-president and MP Anna Oreg for more than a year (CRTA.Plus, "12 branded," 2026; YUCOM statement, March 2025; Interpol decision as reported by CRTA.Plus).
  • Surveillance of critics was documented forensically. Amnesty International established that Serbian journalists and activists were targeted with Pegasus and with a domestic Android spyware, NoviSpy, deployed through police and intelligence channels, and later forensic work by Amnesty, Citizen Lab and the SHARE Foundation confirmed spyware on devices of at least 14 students, activists and opposition politicians (Amnesty International, December 2024 and 2025–26 follow-ups).
  • The universities were punished financially for their autonomy. After faculties backed the student blockades, a government regulation cut professors' salaries to 50% in February 2025 and to 12.5% from April 2025 by restricting paid research time; Vučić said on television that instead of salaries the professors would "get potatoes." Operational funding was withheld, defunding of blockading faculties was threatened, and a planned higher-education law was understood by the academic community to open the way to government-appointed deans (European University Association Board statement, 22 July 2025; AP reporting, May–June 2025). The European Commission's 2026 report records that in March 2026 police searching the Belgrade Faculty of Philosophy and Rectorate seized the entire Rectorate server contrary to a court order, along with student plenum minutes (Supreme Public Prosecutor's Office commission findings, via CRTA.Plus).
  • The prosecution service was left hollow as well as steered: as of August 2026, 166 of 899 public prosecutor posts were vacant, no public prosecutors had been elected since February 2025, and 26 of 62 chief prosecutor posts were held in an acting capacity; the new President of the Constitutional Court, appointed in January 2026, drew criticism for public remarks suggesting judges opposed to the government should be dismissed (European Commission Serbia Report, 4 November 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, students, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The protest movement extracted real concessions, in sequence. Construction Minister Goran Vesić resigned on 4 November 2024, three days after the collapse; Trade Minister Tomislav Momirović followed; Prime Minister Miloš Vučević resigned on 28 January 2025. The government published reconstruction documentation in tranches, and increased the higher-education budget — partially: the increase covered only the material-costs portion, not the full 20% demanded (protest outcome records, 2024–2026; student demand assessments).
  • Under external pressure he reversed a signature act of grip. After the Venice Commission's April 2026 opinion and the EU's suspension of Growth Plan payments (over €1.5 billion for Serbia), amendments adopted in May–June 2026 implemented seven of the Venice Commission's nine recommendations, restoring among other things the independent commission for objections to prosecutorial instructions and ending renewable mandates for court presidents and chief prosecutors (Venice Commission follow-up; National Assembly votes, June 2026).
  • He had adjusted to street pressure before: the Jadar lithium project was cancelled outright in January 2022 after mass protests, with Prime Minister Brnabić declaring "an end to Rio Tinto in the Republic of Serbia" (government statement, 20 January 2022).
  • The final wobble was answered in full. Having dissolved parliament on 9 September 2026 — conceding, sixteen months after the students first demanded it, the snap election he had refused — he lost, and conceded on election night, roughly ninety minutes after polls closed, announcing his opponents' projected seat count himself and stating he did so to counter claims he would refuse to acknowledge defeat. He had said after voting: "If we lose we will acknowledge the will of the electorate" (Reuters, 25 October 2026; Xinhua, 26 October 2026; IEOM Preliminary Statement).

Evidence against:

  • The first answer to the canopy protests was denial of a documented fact: the claim that the canopy had never been reconstructed, contradicted by footage of the minister opening the renovated station (protest timeline records, November 2024). The framing that replaced it — a foreign-directed "color revolution" run by Western powers through students and professors — was maintained by Vučić and pro-government media for the movement's entire two years, while the students' four original demands (documents, accountability for attacks on protesters, dropping charges, education funding) were met late, partially, or not at all (AP; European Parliament resolution, 21 October 2025).
  • The sonic-weapon episode is the denial pattern in one file. During the fifteen minutes of silence at the largest protest in Serbia's history (15 March 2025; 275,000–325,000 people by the Archive of Public Gatherings' count), a loud sound and airwaves sent thousands fleeing in panic with documented symptoms. The authorities first stated that Serbian agencies possessed no sonic devices, then admitted owning several; Vučić denied any use. No effective investigation followed: a commission of the Supreme Public Prosecutor's Office later found the Belgrade Higher Prosecutor's Office had decided in advance that no sound cannon was used, disregarding some 150 citizen statements, medical records and the Earshot expert analysis, and had instead asked the BIA to check 154 testifying citizens for party membership and NGO links; the commission proposed disciplinary and criminal proceedings against the responsible deputy chief prosecutor. The government invited Russia's FSB to co-produce the exculpatory account. On 29 April 2025 the European Court of Human Rights issued an interim measure ordering Serbia to prevent any use of sound devices for crowd control, noting such use was unlawful under Serbian law — while expressly taking no position on whether a device was used on 15 March. Whether one was remains ALLEGATION, denied by the state (ECtHR press release ECHR 110 (2025), 30 April 2025; CRTA.Plus sonic-weapon chronology; Supreme Public Prosecutor's Office commission findings, November 2026). In 2026 the state's answer extended to the claimants: military analyst Aleksandar Radić, the first to raise the claim, was criminally charged in July 2026 with endangering the constitutional order and disclosing classified data, and an opposition MP was questioned over his March 2025 tweet on the subject (Ministry of Interior complaint, 17 July 2026; CRTA.Plus).
  • The election demand was refused while pressure escalated. Students demanded snap elections from 5 May 2025; Vučić dismissed the demand for sixteen months — through the Vidovdan protest of 28 June 2025, waves of arrests, and the university salary cuts — before calling the vote in September 2026 on his own timing, resigning the presidency to lead his list (OSW analysis, 21 October 2026; protest timeline).
  • A protected counter-camp answered protest with counter-protest. The pro-government "Ćaciland" encampment in front of the Assembly was shielded by police while, per the European Parliament, "progovernment mobs and prior convicts have been shielded by police during violent counterdemonstrations, while opposition figures, students and members of Serbia's parliament have been beaten and abducted by plainclothes officers" (European Parliament resolution, 21 October 2025). Claims by an alleged former camp member in October 2026 that participants were paid and armed are ALLEGATION, unexamined by any court.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — students and protesters, Kosovo Serbs and Albanians, his opponents' voters, independent journalists — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • At the end, everyone's ballot counted. The 25 October 2026 election drew record turnout of 68.89%; the result — a defeat for the incumbent — was certified by the Republic Electoral Commission on 5 November 2026 after repeat voting at 53 polling stations, and power began transferring to the list his apparatus had spent two years calling foreign agents. National minority lists took their seats (five lists, seven seats) under the threshold exemption (RIK final results, 5 November 2026; IEOM Preliminary Statement, 26 October 2026).
  • The protest movement was never outlawed as such: over 10,700 protests took place in more than 630 communities between February and September 2025 alone, including the two largest demonstrations in Serbia's history, and the blockades ran for nearly two years (European Parliament resolution, 21 October 2025).
  • His pardons counted the other side back in as well as his own: the twelve prosecuted Novi Sad activists were among the 49 pardoned (Test 1).

Evidence against:

  • Students and protesters were counted as enemies of the state. Beyond the coup prosecution built on a leaked BIA recording (Test 2), the European Commission's 2026 report records hundreds of arrests with few indictments and no convictions of police officers for excessive force; protesters were rammed by cars in documented incidents (the January 2025 attack on Sonja Ponjavić among them — its charged perpetrator later pardoned, Test 1); the European Parliament recorded torture and sexual-violence allegations against detained students, threats by the police protection service, and Cobras special forces deployed against domestic clashes, firing live ammunition on one occasion (European Parliament resolution, 21 October 2025; European Commission, 4 November 2026). Those specific abuse claims are stated as the Parliament recorded them — ALLEGATIONs in form, documented in the resolution; the pattern of arrests without corresponding police accountability is the Commission's own finding.
  • Journalists were counted as targets: forensic spyware findings (Test 2), 209 MFRR-recorded press-freedom violations in 2025, and a campaign environment in which, per ODIHR, media bias and information manipulation "hampered voters' ability to obtain impartial and reliable information" (MFRR; IEOM, 26 October 2026).
  • His opponents' voters were counted under pressure. ODIHR documented, in both 2023 and 2026, pressure on public-sector employees, voter inducement and misuse of state resources; in 2026 it added ballot secrecy compromised at 15% of observed polling stations, marked ballots photographed at 4%, and distrust in a voter register whose discrepancies the authorities did not explain (ODIHR Final Report 2024; IEOM Preliminary Statement and press conference, 26 October 2026).
  • Kosovo Albanians were counted out of the settlement Serbia had accepted. Serbia accepted the Agreement on the Path to Normalisation (27 February 2023) and its Ohrid Implementation Annex (18 March 2023) verbally but declined to sign; in December 2023 Prime Minister Brnabić's letter to the EU stated Serbia's commitment did not extend to Kosovo's UN membership or territorial integrity — the two provisions that constituted the agreement's core exchange. The EU's position, stated at the time and since, is that the agreement is legally binding in its entirety. Implementation stalled; in October 2026 Interior Minister Dačić called Kosovo's membership in international organizations — which Article 4 of the agreement commits Serbia not to oppose — "completely unacceptable," and Vučić said on television on 21 October 2026: "I refused to sign" (EU spokesperson statement, December 2023; Kosovo Dispatch, 24 October 2026).
  • Kosovo Serbs were counted as leverage rather than as a flock. The community's political vehicle, the Serb List, operated as his party's extension; its vice-president organized the Banjska attack and was then sheltered by the mildest available process (Test 1), while Belgrade refused the handover Kosovo and the EU sought, and the north's Serbs lived the consequences — militarized policing, institutional withdrawal — of a confrontation their nominal protectors directed from Belgrade (Banjska record, Test 1; EU and NATO statements, 2023–2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record since 2017, and his SNS leadership since 2012, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The last promise was kept on the night it mattered. On 25 October 2026 he said, "If we lose we will acknowledge the will of the electorate," and conceded within hours, before official results (Reuters, 25 October 2026). It is the single most consequential kept word in the file.
  • The constitution was not bent for his continuation. He had long stated he would not change the constitution to extend his presidential tenure; he did not. Barred from a third consecutive presidential term, he resigned and sought the premiership instead — a route critics called circumvention of the term limit's spirit, but the letter of the commitment was kept, and the voters then decided (Le Monde, 26 October 2026).
  • Living-standard promises moved in the promised direction, late. In January 2024 he announced the average salary (€836 at the time) would exceed €1,000 in 2025, with €1,800 targeted by December 2027 and average pensions rising from €391 toward €650. The €1,000 threshold was, on his own finance minister's framing entering 2026, still to be crossed "for the first time in history" in 2026 — the deadline missed by months, the trajectory real (Government of Serbia, "Leap into the Future – Serbia EXPO 2027," January 2024; Finance Minister Mali via RTS, January 2026). EXPO 2027, the centerpiece promise of that program, was won, is under construction, and had 130 confirmed participating countries entering 2026.
  • He stepped down as SNS president when he said he would — announcing in 2022 that he would leave the party presidency, and doing so in May 2023 — though the form exceeded the substance: his name headlined the party's lists in December 2023 and October 2026 and his dominance of the party was never in documented dispute (SNS records; ODIHR 2024).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Jadar: an "absolute end" that lasted thirty months. In January 2022 his government revoked Rio Tinto's licenses and declared the project finished. In July 2024, days after the Constitutional Court ruled the revocation unlawful, his government restored the project's legal basis and he signed an EU strategic-partnership memorandum on raw materials with Chancellor Scholz in Belgrade; in June 2025 the European Commission designated Jadar a strategic project under the Critical Raw Materials Act. Mass protests resumed. In November 2025 Rio Tinto itself placed Jadar into care and maintenance, citing the government's failure to progress permits — the project stalled not by his word in either direction but by the country's refusal, twice expressed (government statements 2022–2024; European Commission designation, 4 June 2025; Rio Tinto memo as reported November 2025).
  • Ohrid: accepted aloud, disclaimed in writing, disowned on television. The sequence is in Test 4: verbal acceptance (February–March 2023), refusal to sign, the December 2023 letter carving out the agreement's core, and his own October 2026 summary — "I refused to sign." The EU holds the agreement legally binding; Serbia under his leadership implemented neither the signature nor the substance.
  • The European path receded as announced. Serbia's EU accession, the stated strategic goal of every government he led, opened no new chapters or clusters in his final five years; the European Commission's 2025 report recorded backsliding on freedom of expression and its 2026 report no progress in the judiciary. His own horizon moved with the record: the March 2026 "Serbia 2030" strategy projected EU membership by 2035 — "I see Serbia in 2035 as a member of the European Union. I do not know if we will have the right to vote or a commissioner" (presidential strategy presentation, 7 March 2026; European Commission reports, 2025–2026).
  • The founding promises of 2012 were not met. The SNS came to power on depoliticization of public institutions, anti-corruption enforcement and media freedom; fourteen years later the documented record is Tests 1, 2 and 4 — party-state fusion found by ODIHR in successive elections, a paralyzed media regulator, and prosecutions of the government's critics built on intelligence-service recordings. The gap between the 2012 platform and the 2026 record is the long arc of this test.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Aleksandar Vučić built the most complete party-state in Serbia since 2000 — a media regulator that stopped deciding, public broadcasters graded as captured in every review cycle, a prosecution service steered by law when it investigated his ministers, universities fined by salary regulation for siding with their students, and, on his last day in office, 49 pardons that lifted prosecution from a man charged with trying to kill a protester while sparing that man's co-defendants nothing of the sort in reverse. The canopy that fell on Novi Sad killed 16 people and exposed the system's operating principle: deny the documented fact first, release the documents in tranches, let the case circle the courts — and two years on, no trial has begun. What saves the file from a clean sweep is real and should not be minimized: the institutions he hollowed retained enough life to end him. A prosecutor indicted his culture minister; an appeals court reinstated the case against his construction minister; the Venice Commission's corrections were substantially enacted; and when the election he finally called produced a majority against him, he conceded on the night, in person, before the count was official. The tests measure the seat, not the exit — but the exit is on the record too, and it is the strongest evidence that what he gripped was never fully closed. The flock he counted out — students, journalists, Kosovo Albanians, his opponents' voters — voted, in the end, in record numbers, and the count was honored.
