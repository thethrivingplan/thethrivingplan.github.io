The Ruler's Test — Ali al-Zaidi, Prime Minister of Iraq

Subject: Ali Falih Kadhim al-Zaidi — Prime Minister of Iraq since 14 May 2026. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Iraq's presidency is a largely ceremonial head-of-state office, while executive power — command of the government, the budget, and the armed forces as commander-in-chief — sits with the prime minister. The president named in the original brief, Abdul Latif Rashid, no longer holds even the ceremonial office: parliament elected Nizar Amedi (PUK) president on 11 April 2026, and it was Amedi who tasked Zaidi with forming a government on 27 April 2026. Zaidi, born in 1985, is a Baghdad banker and businessman (banking, real estate, construction, food and agriculture) who had never held political office, led no party, and won no seat. He became prime minister as the Coordination Framework's compromise candidate after the 11 November 2025 parliamentary election: the incumbent Mohammed Shia' al-Sudani's Reconstruction and Development Coalition won the plurality (46 of 329 seats, ~1.32 million votes, official turnout 56.11%), but after five months of deadlock — during which the Framework first named former prime minister Nouri al-Maliki in January 2026 and Washington made Maliki's exclusion a condition — both Maliki and al-Sudani withdrew in April 2026 and the Framework settled on Zaidi. Parliament approved his fourteen-pillar government program and 14 of 23 ministers on 14 May 2026; the cabinet was completed only on 15 October 2026. He is graded on roughly six months in office, and the file's thinness is stated where it matters. His predecessor al-Sudani (prime minister 27 October 2022 – 14 May 2026, caretaker through the formation period) appears as context where the record Zaidi inherited — or the promises the seat carried into the election — bear directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption campaign launched within weeks of his taking office reached across the factional map, not only across his enemies'. It opened in May 2026 with the arrest of Adnan al-Jumaili, a deputy oil minister of the al-Sudani government, whose arraignment was televised; in "Operation Dawn" in late June 2026 the Counter Terrorism Service arrested more than 20 officials and legislators, including two deputy oil ministers and several MPs, seizing more than USD 100 million in cash and assets; more than 200 public figures were detained between June and August 2026 (Britannica, 2026; Konrad-Adenauer-Stiftung country report, August 2026). The prominent early detainees came from the Sunni Azm Alliance and from al-Sudani's networks — and the campaign then expanded to figures inside the Coordination Framework itself and to Iran-affiliated groups, in what the KAS assessment describes as a clear effort to counter criticism that it spared certain factions (KAS, August 2026).
  • The campaign ran with, and through, the judiciary rather than around it: it was built on ongoing investigations by the judiciary and a federal committee and publicly backed by Faiq Zaidan, president of the Supreme Judicial Council (Britannica, 2026; KAS, August 2026). Influential cleric Muqtada al-Sadr voiced support (KAS, August 2026).
  • The inherited flagship case was carried to verdicts under the same judiciary the campaign works with: in the "heist of the century" — the theft of some 3.7–3.83 trillion dinars (~USD 2.5 billion) of tax deposits from Rafidain Bank in 2021–2022, under a previous government — the Karkh Criminal Court on 25 November 2024 sentenced the scheme's organizer, businessman Noor Zuhair Jassim, to 10 years in absentia, former prime-ministerial chief of staff Raed Juhi to 6 years, former MP Haitham al-Jubouri to 3 years, and 12 tax-authority employees were also sentenced; the Supreme Judicial Council's July 2026 statement records 365 billion dinars recovered from Zuhair's companies and an Interpol file opened after he left Iraq and stopped repaying (Supreme Judicial Council statement, July 2026; contemporaneous reporting, November 2024).
  • The outcome measure has moved, slowly, the right way: Iraq's Corruption Perceptions Index score rose to 28 of 100 in the 2025 index (136th of 182), its highest ever, from 26 in 2024 and 16 in 2013–2015 (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The seat itself was allocated by a process in which one law for all candidates did not operate. The plurality winner of the November 2025 election, al-Sudani, was passed over; the Framework's first nominee, Maliki, was excluded after Washington "made it unmistakably clear that it would not accept" him and made his exclusion "a strict condition" (KAS, August 2026). Whatever its other merits, the premiership changed hands by factional veto and foreign veto, not by the election result — a fact about the source of the seat, stated because Test 1 grades the source.
  • The campaign's selectivity is contested by analysts and by its targets. The Middle East Council on Global Affairs assessed the crackdown and the disarmament deadline as functioning "less as coherent reform and more as a survival strategy for the Coordination Framework," leaving "powerful Iran-aligned factions untouched" in its first phase (ME Council, September 2026). Kataib Hezbollah, whose stronghold was raided in November 2026, accused Zaidi of running a "political extortion campaign" — an interested party's characterization, recorded here as an ALLEGATION-level claim, not a finding (Al Jazeera reporting, November 2026).
  • The enforcement instrument cuts against the one-law claim: the arrests were executed by the Counter Terrorism Service — an elite combat force — descending on the Green Zone and the provinces, rather than by ordinary judicial police process (KAS, August 2026). As of grading, the documented record shows detentions, dismissals, and seizures in the 2026 campaign, but no final court verdicts against the senior figures detained; the cases remain investigations, and the guilt of every detainee named in this dossier is an ALLEGATION until a court rules.
  • The recovery-for-leniency mechanism risks becoming a second law for those rich enough to repay. In its July 2026 statement the Supreme Judicial Council confirmed that heist-of-the-century convicts — including Zuhair, who had been released on bail in 2022, repaid a fraction (365 billion of some 1,618 billion dinars owed by his companies), and left the country — may gain amnesty eligibility by completing repayment, and that the judiciary is coordinating with the prime minister on applying the same recovery-for-reduced-measures approach to the northern refineries case involving the detained Jumaili (Supreme Judicial Council statement, 10 July 2026). The KAS assessment flags the same risk: if the General Amnesty Law is seen to let influential actors "buy their way out, the credibility and public support for the campaign might quickly wane" (KAS, August 2026). Public reaction to the July 2026 statement, as documented in Iraqi media coverage, was predominantly outrage at the contrast between leniency for large-scale theft and severity for petty crime — a perception recorded here as perception, not as a verdict on any individual case.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The institutions that seated him acted independently of him — he is their product, not their maker. The Independent High Electoral Commission ran the 11 November 2025 election (official turnout 56.11%, up from 43% in 2021, despite Muqtada al-Sadr's boycott call); the Federal Supreme Court ratified the results; parliament elected its speaker at the end of December 2025 and a president on 11 April 2026 before any prime minister could be named (IHEC final results, 17 November 2025; KAS, August 2026).
  • Parliament actively checks him and he submits to it. When his Council of Ministers approved the dinar devaluation on 6 October 2026, parliament suspended its agenda the next day to review the decision, MPs collected signatures to cancel it, a State of Law MP warned of a no-confidence move, and the finance minister and the Central Bank governor were hosted in the chamber to answer for it (Kurdpress and Iraqi press reporting, 7–8 October 2026). The Central Bank's media director defended the policy publicly and on the record — the bank speaking in its own voice, including disclosing that rates as high as 1,900 dinars per dollar had been proposed and rejected (Central Bank of Iraq statements via Iraqi press, October 2026).
  • The constitutional court's own house was put in order before he arrived, by judges rather than by the executive: in mid-June 2025 six of the Federal Supreme Court's nine permanent members and three reserve members resigned in protest at Chief Justice Jassim Mohammed Aboud's leadership; Aboud retired on 29 June 2025, officially for health reasons; Judge Munthir Ibrahim Hussein was sworn in as chief justice in early July 2025, and the resigned judges then withdrew their retirements, stating the reasons for them had been addressed (Rudaw and INA reporting, June–July 2025). The court that ratified the 2025 election was this reconstituted court.
  • Critical media and rights monitors operate openly against his government: the Iraqi Observatory for Human Rights publishes detailed violation reports naming his government's conduct and addressing appeals to him personally, and Iraqi and international outlets cover the anti-corruption campaign's critics at length (IOHR, 2026; Al Jazeera, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • His power base is a substitute built on the judiciary and the security services, which concentrates rather than distributes the seat's weight. The KAS assessment identifies his "close association with the judiciary" as the replacement for the parliamentary base he lacks — he commands not a single vote of his own in the chamber — and names the relationship one of the two that will decide his premiership (KAS, August 2026). A prime minister whose enforcement arm is the Counter Terrorism Service and whose political shield is the head of the Supreme Judicial Council holds the referees closer than the standard allows, even where no single act of interference is documented.
  • Freedom of expression narrowed on his watch, on a documented monitor's count. The Iraqi Observatory for Human Rights' report Voices Under Pressure, tracking the period since May 2026 — his tenure — documents 18 broadcast bans, prosecutions of journalists and activists over published content, the forcible dispersal of protests in Kut, and a security raid on a civic meeting: on 4 September 2026 security forces entered an Iraq Democracy Observatory preparatory meeting in Baghdad without judicial authorization, which the Observatory states violated Articles 17 and 38 of the constitution (IOHR, report released 11 November 2026). Iraq stood 162nd of 180 in RSF's 2026 Press Freedom Index, down seven places — an index whose reporting period substantially predates Zaidi, cited here as the environment he inherited and has not, on the IOHR record, reversed.
  • The state's grip remains contested from inside his own coalition. Until November 2026 the town of Jurf al-Sakhar had been sealed for years by Kataib Hezbollah — a formation inside the state-recognized Popular Mobilization Forces — barring state institutions from entering (Al Jazeera, November 2026). His completed cabinet seats Laith al-Khazali, a senior leader of Asaib Ahl al-Haq who appears on US sanctions lists, as a deputy prime minister (Reuters, 15 October 2026). The referees he does not grip are not all his to grip; but a seat that must negotiate entry into a town held by one of its own security formations is not holding the whole board.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The sharpest pushback of his tenure was answered in the open, against his own patron. After joint US–Saudi airstrikes hit PMF positions across seven provinces on the night of 28–29 July 2026, killing at least 20 PMF fighters and wounding 32 (PMF toll), President Trump publicly claimed the strikes had been coordinated with Baghdad. Zaidi's government replied that it had not been informed in advance and had not authorized them; the Ministerial Council for National Security condemned the strikes, rejected "all acts of aggression, regardless of the party responsible," instructed the Foreign Ministry to pursue legal action under international law, and stated there was no evidence for the Saudi claim that the provoking drone attack had been launched from Iraq; Zaidi cancelled his planned Riyadh visit and ordered maximum military alert while declining military retaliation (Iraqi government statements via Rudaw and Channels TV, 29–30 July 2026). He later rebuilt the relationship in person, visiting Riyadh on 14 October 2026 (Reuters, October 2026).
  • His signature deadline was missed, and the miss was owned rather than denied. When the 30 September 2026 disarmament deadline (Test 5) proved infeasible, the government announced the extension to 30 June 2027 openly, told Washington the new date was firm, and Zaidi defended it in person: "By June 30, 2027, no weapons will remain outside the framework of the state" (Iraqi Media Network interview, November 2026; Soufan Center IntelBrief, 19 November 2026).
  • At Jurf al-Sakhar in November 2026, faced with Kataib Hezbollah mobilizing some 8,000 fighters toward the town ahead of his investigative committee's arrival, he chose a negotiated entry over an armed clash: an ultimatum, then a compromise reached a day before the deadline — government forces entering, a joint operations centre established, phased militia withdrawal and phased deployment of army, federal police, and energy police — with the deal reportedly brokered with Iranian mediation (Al Jazeera and Reuters reporting, November 2026). Nine senior Babil province officials, including the provincial police chief and the director of the Oil Pipelines Company, were dismissed in the same file (Al Jazeera, November 2026).
  • The devaluation was defended in public, with reasons, against a parliamentary alternative he rejected openly: rather than pay salaries every 45 days as a finance-committee proposal suggested, the government chose the exchange-rate move and said why — the Central Bank calling it a "surgical operation" and a "bitter pill" on state television (Reuters, 8 October 2026; Central Bank statements, October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The July 2026 strikes also mark the limit of the adjustment: the decision to use force on Iraqi territory was taken outside his government's control twice over — by the militias whose alleged attacks provoked it and by the partners who answered — and his government's response, however openly stated, was words, a cancelled visit, and a legal file. Two weeks earlier he had stood in the White House and promised that after 30 September there would be "no justification" for armed formations outside state control (Tomorrow's Affairs analysis, 31 July 2026). The wobble was answered; it was not mastered.
  • The street's pushback under his government has been met, in documented cases, with force rather than adjustment. In Wasit governorate, protests demanding electricity were followed by what the Iraqi Observatory for Human Rights documents as widespread arbitrary arrests and raids on demonstrators and activists, forcible dispersals, and injuries — serious enough that the Observatory publicly called on Zaidi by name to intervene and stop the arrest campaign; no documented intervention by him appears in the record assembled here. Protests in Kut were likewise forcibly dispersed (IOHR, 2026).
  • Parliament's pushback on the devaluation produced scrutiny but no adjustment: the decision stood, food prices rose by up to 15% in the first days, and the government's answer was explanation, not revision (Kurdpress, October 2026; Reuters, 8 October 2026). Explanation is more than denial; it is less than adjustment, and the shelf records it as such.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Sunni, Kurdish, Turkmen, Christian, and Yazidi communities, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The cabinet completed on 15 October 2026 was distributed across the whole map, and included two structural firsts for the counted-out: a standalone Minister of State for Minority Affairs was created, and the three deputy prime ministers were announced as unpaid, field-based posts, with Zaidi telling parliament the deputies would receive no salary, allowances, or office appointments (Atlantic Council election tracker, updated 23 October 2026; Iraq Business News, 16 October 2026). Kurdish representation continued at the top of the state: Foreign Minister Fuad Hussein (KDP) was the one sovereign minister retained from the previous government (Reuters, 14 May 2026).
  • The Kurdish salary question was answered with an equality promise, on the record: Zaidi has promised that Kurdistan Region public employees will receive their salaries "on the same basis as other Iraqis," in direct discussions with KRG Prime Minister Masrour Barzani covering salaries, oil exports, and budget allocations (The Amargi, November 2026). During the summer 2026 electricity crisis, the federal government bought power from the Kurdistan Region as well as from Türkiye (KAS, August 2026).
  • The Jurf al-Sakhar file is, at bottom, a counting file: the town's roughly 140,000 displaced residents — overwhelmingly Sunni Arabs displaced in 2014 — have been barred from returning for a decade by the formation that sealed it. The November 2026 deal begins phased state entry and phased militia withdrawal, the documented precondition for any return (Al Jazeera, November 2026). The return itself had not been documented as accomplished by grading; the door is open on paper, and this shelf records the opening, not the homecoming.
  • The salary bill was protected as a first charge on a collapsing treasury: through the Hormuz closure the state kept paying a monthly wage and pension bill of about USD 5.3 billion covering more than nine million employees, pensioners, and social-welfare recipients, financing it by drawing on Central Bank reserves, and the prime minister rejected the proposal to stretch pay to every 45 days (KAS, August 2026; Reuters, 8 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Kurdistan Region was attacked, repeatedly, on his watch, and the state he heads could not protect it. Drone and missile attacks on Kurdish energy infrastructure ran through 2026; during Zaidi's own July 2026 Washington visit, militia attacks on the Kurdistan Region undercut his investment pitch in real time (KAS, August 2026). Most recently, on 8–9 November 2026 a suspected drone attack halted production at the Khor Mor gas field, cutting some 2.8 gigawatts of generation and blacking out much of the region; Kurdish officials blame Iran-backed militias — an attribution recorded here as an ALLEGATION, no group having claimed responsibility (Reuters, 9 and 16 November 2026).
  • The one ministry that exists for the displaced and the minorities was left empty. When the cabinet was completed on 15 October 2026, the migration and displacement portfolio — the Christian-quota ministry — was the single post left unfilled, its vote postponed over a dispute between the Christian Suyana bloc and the Babylon Movement (Reuters, 15 October 2026; Atlantic Council tracker, October 2026). The minority-affairs ministry was created the same day; the ministry for the displaced stayed vacant.
  • The fiscal adjustment was distributed downward. The October 2026 devaluation (official rate from 1,320 to 1,520 dinars per dollar) raised food prices by up to 15% within days, and Iraqi economists across the coverage describe its incidence as falling on employees, pensioners, and the poor — one calling it "the worst decision in the worst timing," another MP calling it "not economic reform but a failure to find solutions" that burdens "the citizen" instead (Reuters, 8 October 2026; Iraqi press, October 2026). Salary and pension arrears had already produced public protests before the devaluation (IBBC economic overview, 8 October 2026). The government conceded in late July 2026 that salaries would henceforth depend on available liquidity rather than the calendar (KAS, August 2026).
  • Protesters asking for electricity in Wasit were counted as a security problem (Test 3): arrests and raids documented by the national human-rights monitor, with dozens reportedly fleeing the governorate (IOHR, 2026). No Zaidi-specific documented step on the Yazidi file — Sinjar's reconstruction and the return of its displaced — appears in the record assembled here; it is recorded as an absence, not an act.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 14 May 2026, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The anti-corruption campaign — delivered at the promised scale. His fourteen-pillar program, ratified 14 May 2026, made fighting corruption a headline commitment; within weeks the campaign had detained a former deputy oil minister, raided the Green Zone in Operation Dawn, detained more than 200 public figures by August, and seized over USD 100 million (program text via Shafaq News, May 2026; Britannica, 2026; KAS, August 2026). Whatever its selectivity (Test 1), the campaign happened, at scale, on schedule.
  • The balanced foreign policy — followed as written. The program pledged balance — Iraq out of regional conflict axes, the US Strategic Framework Agreement activated, Gulf relations and the Development Road advanced. His first foreign visit was to Washington in mid-July 2026 (48 agreements and memoranda reportedly worth over USD 60 billion, mostly non-binding), Tehran followed on 23 July 2026, Riyadh on 14 October 2026, and Damascus in late October 2026, where a Baniyas pipeline agreement had been announced during the Washington visit (KAS, August 2026; Reuters, October 2026; Soufan Center, November 2026).
  • Disarmament machinery — built, and partially used. He announced understandings with five factions to lay down arms, with procedures completed for three (IMN interview, November 2026); Muqtada al-Sadr announced the dissolution of Saraya al-Salam, and Asaib Ahl al-Haq and Kataib Imam Ali signaled willingness to come under state control, with Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani's support for a state weapons monopoly on the record (KAS, August 2026; Britannica, 2026). On 7 November 2026 he cancelled militia-linked contracts at Mosul International Airport — check-in counters, car park, and taxi stand — cutting a financial pipeline rather than only announcing one (FDD analysis, November 2026).
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: al-Sudani's 2022 program promised provincial elections (held 18 December 2023, the first in a decade), a three-year budget (passed June 2023), a social-security law extending entitlements to all workers (passed 2023), and the Development Road project (launched May 2023 at a projected USD 17 billion) — a program substantially executed, which is part of why his coalition won the November 2025 plurality (UNAMI briefing to the Security Council, October 2023; BTI 2026 country report). The seat Zaidi holds was won, in part, on a kept word — and then transferred to someone who had made no promises to the voters at all.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The disarmament date — his clearest word, moved. The program's first pillar commits the government to consolidating all weapons under exclusive state authority. In June 2026 his government set the deadline at 30 September 2026, timed to the end of the US-led coalition mission; at the White House on 14 July 2026 he stated that after 30 September there would be no justification for armed formations outside full state control. The coalition mission ended on schedule on 30 September 2026; the militias did not disarm — Kataib Hezbollah, Harakat al-Nujaba, and Kataib Sayyid al-Shuhada rejected the timetable — and the deadline was extended to 30 June 2027, with several factions disputing that any final agreement exists (KAS, August 2026; IMN interview and Rudaw reporting, November 2026). The new date is stated firmly and may yet be kept; the first date was stated just as firmly, and was not.
  • The program's own fine print on the PMF. The same program that pledges a weapons monopoly also commits to enhancing the PMF's combat capabilities while defining its role in law — it does not dissolve the PMF (program text via Shafaq News, May 2026). The 2025 attempt to settle the PMF's status in law, under al-Sudani, ended with the government withdrawing the service-and-retirement draft in March 2025 amid Framework disputes and US pressure, and with Secretary of State Rubio warning al-Sudani directly on 22 July 2025 that the pending PMF bill would "institutionalize Iranian influence"; the legislation remained unresolved at the election (Washington Institute, March and August 2025). Zaidi inherited that stalemate and has so far answered it with deadlines and negotiation rather than a law.
  • The housing promise — announced at a scale the record cannot yet carry. The Watan initiative promises one million residential plots across 15 governorates; six months in, the documented record is an order to accelerate and initial groundwork in several governorates — in a state that has run all year without a budget, under the one-twelfth spending rule, with investment spending effectively suspended (The Amargi, November 2026; KAS, August 2026). Not yet broken; nowhere near kept.
  • The long targets are stated as direction, not yet as record: non-oil revenue to reach 30% of total revenue "over the remaining years of his term," and oil production capacity of 10 million barrels per day by 2032 (IMN interview, November 2026). Both are recorded here so the record can grade them later; neither can be graded now.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Ali al-Zaidi is the first prime minister in this series who was chosen precisely because he held nothing: no party, no seats, no faction, no electoral mandate of his own — the compromise every veto could accept after the plurality winner was passed over and Washington vetoed the alternative. Six months on, the record shows what that kind of seat can and cannot do. Where power runs through the executive, he has moved further and faster than his predecessors: an anti-corruption campaign that detained more than 200 public figures across the factional map, a negotiated state entry into a town a militia had sealed for a decade, contracts cancelled, deadlines set. Where power must be assembled — a budget, a completed cabinet, a disarmament the armed factions refuse — the seat stalls: five months to fill the interior and defence ministries, no 2026 budget at all, the weapons deadline moved from September 2026 to June 2027, and the Kurdistan Region blacked out by an attack his state could not prevent two weeks before this grading. The wobble defined his tenure in July 2026, when foreign aircraft bombed a state security formation on his soil and an allied president claimed his consent; he denied the consent publicly, condemned the strikes, and answered with law rather than force — openly, and without effect on the fact itself. The grades are all MIXED because the file is six months long and genuinely two-sided; the open files that will move them are named in the dossier: whether the June 2027 deadline is met, whether the 2026 detainees reach verdicts, whether the recovery-for-repayment mechanism becomes a second law for the rich, and whether the ministry for the displaced is ever filled.
