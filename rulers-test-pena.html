The Ruler's Test — Santiago Peña, President of Paraguay (2023–2026)

Subject: Santiago Peña — President of Paraguay from 15 August 2023 until his removal by impeachment on 6 November 2026; previously Minister of Finance (2015–2017) under President Horacio Cartes and a board member of the Central Bank of Paraguay. A Columbia-trained economist and former IMF official, he was handpicked by Cartes — his political patron — as the presidential candidate of Cartes's Honor Colorado faction, and won the April 2023 election with roughly 43% of the vote. His five-year term was scheduled to run to August 2028. On 2 November 2026 the Central Bank placed Ueno Bank, a digital lender serving some 3 million customers, into resolution after finding its solvency below half the legal minimum. Peña tendered his resignation on 5 November; Congress declined to accept it — acceptance would have made him a senator-for-life with parliamentary immunity — and instead impeached him (Chamber of Deputies 78–0; Senate, 6 November, 44 of 45 senators present voting to remove). He was arrested, charged, and placed in pretrial detention; on 16 November 2026 a judge ordered him moved from a suite with a private gym to the Viñas Cué military prison. Vice President Pedro Alliana was sworn in on 6 November 2026 to serve the remainder of the term. The criminal charges against Peña are ALLEGATIONS, unadjudicated; he has pleaded not guilty and denies wrongdoing. He is graded here on his completed tenure, 2023–2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The justice system did, in his tenure, move against sitting judicial insiders in the "mafia de los pagarés" (promissory-note mafia) scandal — a scheme in Asunción and Central Department peace courts in which, prosecutors allege, false notification reports let collection firms obtain garnishments against debtors who never knew they had been sued. The Supreme Court, under its Acordada N.º 1800/2025, reviewed and purged more than 23,000 executive cases and ordered garnishments lifted; prosecutors brought charges against peace judges, court staff, and lawyers, obtained abbreviated-procedure convictions of two former court ushers, and in November 2026 formally accused 12 more people, including two former judges, and requested oral trial (Supreme Court plenary reporting; Ministerio Público filings via Paraguayan press, 2025–November 2026). The underlying conduct is ALLEGATION as to those not yet convicted.
  • Law enforcement reached a sitting deputy of his own party: in August 2024 Colorado deputy Eulalio Gomes was killed in a police raid at his home in a narcotrafficking investigation (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025, key developments). The operation's own legality was debated in Paraguay; what is documented is that the raid on a governing-party legislator was carried out.
  • In the end, the law was applied to Peña himself without exemption: prosecutors opened a criminal investigation on 5 November 2026, Congress — including most of his own party — removed him the next day, and a judge ordered his pretrial detention and later his transfer to a military prison (below, Test 2). The removal is a fact; the charges remain ALLEGATIONs.

Evidence against:

  • The defining file of his presidency is a documented conflict of interest at the source. Peña took office in August 2023 disclosing a stake of roughly US$845,000 in Ueno Holding, part of the Grupo Vázquez conglomerate that owns Ueno Bank, and held it until March 2025 — his lawyer has said April 2025; the bank states he was never a direct shareholder in the bank itself (asset disclosure as reported by ABC Color, October 2024; Reuters and AP reporting, November 2026). During that same period, public money flowed into Ueno on a scale with no parallel at peer banks: state deposits at Ueno rose from near zero when he took office to roughly US$850–869 million by September–October 2026 — about a quarter to 29% of the bank's deposits, with public deposits in guaraníes up 130% in 2025 alone (Treasury records cited in the prosecutors' charge sheet; Bloomberg reporting; Central Bank data). The largest public depositor was the Instituto de Previsión Social (IPS), the pension and health fund for private-sector workers, whose exposure is put at roughly US$295–380 million across deposits and bonds. In November 2023, three months after Peña took office, the IPS council lowered the minimum score a bank needed to qualify for IPS deposits; Ueno ended 2023 holding IPS money, and on 29 August 2024 — days after Peña met IPS president Jorge Brítez on 22 and 27 August, per the charge sheet — the council approved buying up to 250 billion guaraníes (~US$32 million) of seven-year Ueno bonds (IPS regulations and ABC Color reporting, 2023–2024; charge sheet as reported, November 2026). Whether Peña directed any placement is an ALLEGATION he denies; the rule change, the meetings, and the flows are documented.
  • The bank's lending ran to its own owners while the state deposits grew. According to the Central Bank and prosecutors, between September 2025 and May 2026 Ueno disbursed about US$146 million to three companies linked to Grupo Vázquez — registered, a June 2026 Central Bank report found, in the names of former managers and employees of the group — putting it some US$41.5 million over its legal related-party lending limit; when supervisors ordered the loans counted in June 2026, Ueno sold them to a fourth group company, Vinanzas SA, which paid with money that had itself come from Ueno (ABC Color publication of Central Bank inspection files; prosecutors' filings, November 2026). These are the regulators' and prosecutors' documented findings and charges; the bank's owners have their own account, and criminal responsibility is unadjudicated.
  • The president was also the bank's personal borrower. In May 2024 Ueno granted Peña two simultaneous loans totaling about US$577,000, which prosecutors say partly financed his San Bernardino mansion; the charge sheet notes monthly installments of about US$38,880, roughly six times his presidential salary (Ministerio Público, as reported by AP and Paraguayan press, November 2026). ALLEGATION as to impropriety; the loans and the property are documented (ABC Color first reported the mansion in January 2025).
  • Prosecutors' formal charges against Peña — breach of trust (as instigator), administration for personal gain (as perpetrator), and criminal association, among 27 people charged in the case — allege that he helped place, renew, and keep public and pension money at Ueno while holding shares alongside its owners, and shielded the bank from intervention (prosecutors Nancy Salomón and Christian Benítez, as reported by CNN, AP, and Reuters, November 2026). Prosecutors put the damage to the state at more than US$809 million in state deposits locked inside Ueno at failure. Peña has pleaded not guilty, declined to testify before the judge, and his lawyer Ricardo Preda states he never ordered any institution to deposit money at Ueno and never held shares in the bank itself. All of this is ALLEGATION, not verdict.
  • Before Ueno, the pattern of favor ran through his patron. Peña's career was made by Horacio Cartes: Cartes moved him from the central-bank board to the finance ministry in 2015, Peña joined the Colorado Party in 2016 under Cartes's patronage, and Cartes handpicked him to run in 2023 (CRS, Paraguay: An Overview; Wilson Center, August 2024). On 22 July 2022 the US State Department designated Cartes and three of his adult children under Section 7031(c) for "significant corruption," citing obstruction of an international criminal investigation and ties to foreign terrorist organizations; on 26 January 2023 the Treasury's OFAC imposed Global Magnitsky sanctions, alleging Cartes paid cash bribes to legislators of US$5,000–50,000 per month while president and pledged US$1 million of his own money in 2017 to buy votes for a constitutional amendment allowing his re-election (US State Department and OFAC statements, 2022–2023). Cartes denies all of it; none of it has been tested in court. Peña's documented response as candidate was to defend his patron's procedural rights — he said he had been assured by the US ambassador that Cartes would have a "right to defence" and that the measures had "absolutely nothing to do" with his candidacy (Latin News, January 2023) — and, once president, his government in August 2024 asked the United States to "accelerate" the departure of Ambassador Marc Ostfield days after OFAC sanctioned Cartes's former tobacco company Tabesa for continuing to channel millions to him (Wilson Center, August 2024). The state thus used its diplomatic weight on behalf of the patron's file. OFAC delisted Cartes on 6 October 2025 and the US restored his visa on 9 July 2026; Peña publicly welcomed the delisting. The delisting is an administrative reversal, not a judicial exoneration, and the underlying allegations stand unresolved in both directions.
  • Analysts noted from inauguration week that the new government's first institutional concern appeared to be protecting Cartes from legal jeopardy (Inter-American Dialogue, Latin America Advisor, 15 August 2023), and the Cartes network's presence in the state was structural: Grupo Cartes is one of four conglomerates controlling most traditional media (RSF country file), Cartes's faction held the congressional leadership, and ABC Color reported in 2024 that at least 49% of lawmakers had placed relatives in government jobs (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025). Further ALLEGATIONs of favor — millions of dollars in public contracts to a company previously linked to Peña, and Itaipú contracts awarded to political allies — received, in CSIS's November 2026 assessment, "little institutional scrutiny during his presidency."

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the central bank, the prosecution, Congress, the electoral authority, and the media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • When the crisis came, the institutions acted — fast, in public, and against the president. The Central Bank's board voted unanimously on 2 November 2026 to place Ueno into resolution after its own supervision found capital below half the legal minimum; prosecutors ordered the arrest of the bank's top directors the day the bank was taken over; the press that had investigated the president's finances for years — ABC Color and the independent outlet El Surtidor — published throughout; and Congress, including most of Peña's own Colorados, impeached him within the week (Central Bank and Ministerio Público actions, 2–7 November 2026; CNN, NPR, AP, November 2026). Paraguay's deposit-guarantee fund paid out to 93% of eligible Ueno depositors by the legal deadline of 11 November (Asunción Times, November 2026). A system wholly gripped does none of this.
  • The electoral authority's calendar ran against the ruling party where voters so decided: in the 4 October 2026 municipal elections the opposition's Soledad Núñez won the mayoralty of Asunción, the capital (CSIS, November 2026).
  • The Supreme Court's pagarés purge (Test 1) was the judiciary disciplining its own lower courts at scale, on its own initiative.

Evidence against:

  • The single most important appointment of his presidency put the regulated in charge of the regulator. Peña appointed Carlos Carvallo — who had chaired Ueno's predecessor company and held executive roles in the group — as president of the Central Bank, the institution that supervises Ueno (El Surtidor, February 2024; CSIS, November 2026). Prosecutors have also charged Carvallo in the Ueno case (ALLEGATION; he denies wrongdoing), and he resigned as the bank was seized. Peña's economy minister, Carlos Fernández Valdovinos, likewise came out of the private banking sector (Banco Basa). El Surtidor documented this revolving door in February 2024, before any failure — the conflict was visible in advance and proceeded anyway.
  • Inside the supervisor, the documented record shows interference at the decisive point. A Central Bank supervision memo by supervisor Fernando Rivarola dated 10 July 2026 recommended that Ueno's sale of its related-party loans to Vinanzas not be admitted — the transaction that kept the loans off the bank's limit. Peña had appointed Rivarola to a different agency four days before Rivarola acted on Ueno's numbers (Central Bank memo as published by ABC Color; Marginal Revolution's summary of the Paraguayan record, November 2026). Prosecutors separately allege Peña "prevented regulators from intervening in the bank" (CSIS summary of the prosecutorial case, November 2026) — ALLEGATION, denied — but the supervisor's reassignment and the unheeded memo are documented.
  • The legislature under his faction punished the opposition's most prominent voice. On 14 February 2024, senators of the Colorado Party and allies aligned with Cartes expelled opposition senator Kattya González — elected months earlier with over 100,000 votes, among the Senate's highest totals — on charges of misuse of influence, in an extraordinary session of about four hours, using 23 votes despite a Senate rule adopted in December 2023 requiring 30 for such removals (Wikipedia/IDEA Global State of Democracy, February 2024; Agencia Presentes, 15 February 2024). González charged in her final speech that the removal proceeded "[t]hanks to the complicity of Mr. Santiago Peña"; the government did not intervene to defend the chamber's own rule. Prosecutors later dismissed the underlying complaint against her (as noted in Paraguayan commentary, October 2026). Her court appeals to recover the seat were still being pursued.
  • Civil society was put under a control law. In October–November 2024 his government enacted the NGO-control law — dubbed the "gag law" by the Paraguayan Human Rights Coordinator (Codehupy) — requiring nonprofits to document funding sources and destinations and empowering the government to shut down non-compliant organizations, while exempting churches, political parties, and football clubs (Codehupy 2024 report; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025). A law that grips associations and exempts the powerful's own vehicles is a grip instrument on its face.
  • The media environment thinned on his watch even as the best of it performed heroically. Paraguay fell in RSF's World Press Freedom Index from 115th (2024) to 84th (2025) and then 88th (2026), in RSF's "difficult" band; RSF recorded four investigative journalists attacked in three months in late 2025 with the journalist-protection law stalled, and threats and attacks — mostly linked to organized crime in the border zones — routinely unpunished (RSF country files, 2024–2026). The journalists who exposed the Ueno ties "faced attacks, harassment and other obstacles to their reporting" (LatAm Journalism Review, 18 November 2026). Peña's own documented response to ABC Color's October 2024 revelation of the IPS bond purchase was to call the reporting a "media campaign" (as reported in the prosecutorial record and press coverage).
  • The counterweight, stated fairly: the grip was real but never total, and its incompleteness is what ended him. Much autonomy Paraguay retained is structural — an opposition press owned by a rival conglomerate (Grupo Zucolillo's ABC), a prosecutor's office that moved once the Central Bank did, and a ruling party whose dominant faction withdrew its protection. The institutions that acted in November 2026 acted after three years in which the supervision file, the IPS file, and the press file had accumulated without consequence.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Congress, the press, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His final wobble produced a genuine concession. Facing mass protests, queues at banks, and an impeachment drive, Peña announced his resignation on 5 November 2026, saying he wanted "to avoid more harm to the country" and to restore tranquility, and apologizing: "I want to apologize to the people if I have failed them at any point. It was never my intention to betray the trust that they placed in me" (address on X, 5 November 2026; WSJ, November 2026). He stated he would present himself, exercise his right of defense, cooperate fully with institutions, and that "no one is above the law" (statement on X, 6 November 2026). Whatever its motive — graded below — the act of offering to leave rather than fight for the seat is on the record.
  • On Itaipú, he adjusted under pressure at least once: after Paraguay suspended the Annex C talks in April 2025 over suspected Brazilian intelligence espionage against Paraguayan officials, Peña met Lula in July 2025 and the talks resumed (bilateral reporting, 2025–2026).
  • He absorbed political costs inside his own camp late in the term: he backed Vice President Alliana against Cartes's chosen running-mate pick for 2028, saying through Alliana it was too early to decide, and promoted Marco Riquelme as a possible successor — defying his patron's succession plan (CSIS, November 2026; Última Hora, July 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Ueno file was a three-year wobble answered with denial until it broke. El Surtidor documented the revolving door in February 2024; ABC Color documented the IPS bond purchase in October 2024 and the mansion in January 2025; Peña's responses were to call the reporting a media campaign and continue. When Cartes-aligned senators began publicly questioning the Central Bank about Ueno's stability in September 2026, the presidency did not act; the bank failed six weeks later (press record, 2024–2026; Journal of Foreign Affairs at Carolina, November 2026).
  • The resignation itself is contested as a wobble-answer. Congress refused it precisely because acceptance would automatically have seated Peña as senator-for-life with parliamentary immunity from prosecution; lawmakers across factions argued the resignation was structured to obtain that shield, and chose impeachment — which strips immunity — instead (Bloomberg Línea, November 2026; CSIS, November 2026). This is the legislators' stated reasoning, printed as such; Peña's stated reason was the country's stability. Both are on the record; the institutional effect Congress prevented is a matter of constitutional text.
  • His defense in the impeachment was procedural objection rather than engagement: notified at 22:15 on 6 November with the accusatory brief and some 300 pages of annexes, he demanded a 72-hour extension, Congress refused (39–3), and neither Peña nor his lawyers appeared for the defense session; the Senate then rejected his due-process objection and removed him 44–0 of those present (Bloomberg Línea, November 2026; La Nación as cited by Sociedad Media, November 2026). His lawyer maintains the process violated basic due process — a claim now for the courts, recorded here as his position.
  • On Itaipú he doubled down against his own signature: the May 2024 agreement with Brazil fixed the tariff at US$19.28 per kW-month for 2024–2026 on the basis that from 2027 the tariff would reflect strict operating costs under a revised Annex C; Peña subsequently stated publicly that Paraguay does not intend to accept a tariff reduction after 2026, contradicting the trajectory of the deal he had announced as a triumph (Canal Solar, 2025; below, Test 5).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Indigenous communities, campesinos, the poor, pensioners, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The material count of the poor improved measurably and broadly during his tenure, on independent as well as official figures. Monetary poverty fell from 19.6% of the population in 2024 to 16.0% in 2025, and extreme poverty from 3.7% to 2.4% (INE household survey, as compiled in the World Bank's Paraguay Poverty & Equity Brief, October 2026). The World Bank attributes roughly two-thirds of the 2024–2025 reduction to rising labor income — jobs, not only transfers — with public unemployment falling to a historic low of 3.6% in the fourth quarter of 2025. Public transfers were the second driver: Tekoporã, the Adulto Mayor pension, and the expanded Hambre Cero school-feeding program — which now serves meals to over one million children in public schools — together kept an estimated 150,000 people out of extreme poverty and 239,000 out of poverty in 2025, with larger effects in rural areas (World Bank, October 2026 and November 2026).
  • The programs were universalist in design: Hambre Cero feeds public-school children without partisan test, and the poverty reduction was recorded across departments, not only in the capital.
  • When Ueno failed, the deposit-guarantee system — a standing institution his government then operated — moved to make small savers whole first: by 11 November 2026 the fund had transferred about US$644 million for advance payments to 1,812,348 eligible depositors, covering the full balances of roughly 97% of Ueno's customers, about US$38,000 per person at the cap (Central Bank figures, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The flock that was counted in was also the flock exposed. Ueno's 3 million customers — some 60% of Paraguay's adults, including about one million first-time bank users drawn in by the financial-inclusion expansion of his own economic model — woke on 3 November 2026 to frozen accounts, dead cards, and unpaid wages (CSIS and NPR, November 2026). The guarantee covered small depositors; it did not cover roughly US$2.1 billion of the bank's US$2.8–2.9 billion in deposits, and about one-third of deposits are not expected to be returned. The uncovered money belonged disproportionately to companies, brokerages — and the state itself, including the pension system (Fitch Ratings, 11 November 2026; Deposit Guarantee Fund figures).
  • Pensioners were counted as a funding source. Some US$295–380 million of IPS money — the retirement and health savings of private-sector workers — sat inside Ueno when it failed, placed there after the eligibility threshold was lowered in November 2023 (Test 1). Successor authorities preliminarily identified about US$600 million in viable Ueno assets to reimburse pension funds, which is an admission of the shortfall's scale (AP, 16 November 2026). In the weeks after the collapse, teachers walked off the job and blocked roads demanding the return of their retirement savings, and retirees' groups pressed Congress for an emergency guarantee law (AP, November 2026). Whether the pension money is recovered in full is, at grading, unresolved.
  • The rural and Indigenous count lagged the national story throughout. Rural multidimensional poverty stood at 30.3% in 2025 against 6.8% urban (INE via World Bank, October 2026); informality remained around 60% of non-agricultural employment; and Paraguay's human-rights coordinator documented neglect, stigmatization, and institutional violence affecting Indigenous peoples, campesino communities, and people living with HIV through 2024, in the same year the NGO law restricted the organizations that advocate for them (Codehupy, 2024 report). His own third report to Congress (July 2026) was criticized by legislators and analysts for omitting, among other things, responses for vulnerable sectors including Indigenous peoples (Pytagua summary of congressional reactions, 2 July 2026).
  • His opponents' voters were counted at the ballot box — elections ran and the opposition took Asunción in October 2026 (Test 2) — but the country's most-voted opposition senator spent the term out of her seat (Test 2), which is the counting of a hundred thousand votes as voidable by 23 of the other side's.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since August 2023, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Taiwan — kept, at real cost. Peña promised continuity with Taipei, and held it against sustained Chinese pressure and a domestic business lobby arguing the country loses its biggest market: Paraguay remains Taiwan's last diplomatic ally in South America; he made two presidential visits to Taiwan, including a state visit on 7–10 May 2026 at which seven cooperation agreements were signed and Taiwan opened its market to Paraguayan poultry (Reuters, May 2026). Beijing's foreign ministry responded by calling on Paraguay to "stand on the right side of history" and sever ties, and by citing polls it did not name claiming ~90% of Paraguayans favor relations with China — printed here as China's claim, contested by the government's own stated position. The economic cost is documented and was borne knowingly: Paraguay cannot sell beef or soy directly to China. On this promise his word and his record are the same.
  • Macroeconomic stewardship — substantially kept. He promised stability, growth, and integration into global markets. Paraguay won its first investment-grade sovereign rating from Moody's (Baa3) in July 2024 and a second from S&P (BBB–) in December 2025, with Fitch affirming BB+ on a positive outlook as late as 30 September 2026; growth averaged about 5.5% a year across his tenure and reached 6.6% in 2025, with inflation around 3.1% (Moody's, S&P, Fitch actions; IMF and government figures via CSIS, November 2026). These outcomes belong in part to a framework built over three decades — the fiscal-responsibility rule and inflation targeting predate him — but he campaigned on keeping that framework and, until the final fiscal year, broadly did.
  • No re-election project — kept, so far as it was his to keep. Paraguay's constitution bars presidential re-election absolutely. When amendment talk surfaced — a proposed amendment on municipal tax retention that opponents suspected was a vehicle — the Chamber of Deputies' president stated in October 2024 that "President Santiago Peña is not in any project to propose re-election" (La Nación, 30 October 2024), and on Constitution Day, 22 June 2026, Peña himself spoke of assuming the ex-president's role of senator-for-life after his term, presenting himself as the first to take that seat seriously (Paraguay TV coverage, 22 June 2026). No re-election amendment was ever formally proposed by his government. The suspicion that felled Senator González's hunger strike in February 2024 — that a constitutional change was being prepared — was never realized in a text.
  • Itaipú, first phase — delivered. He promised better terms from Brazil and in May 2024 obtained the tariff increase to US$19.28 per kW-month for 2024–2026, worth about US$1.25 billion a year to Paraguay in royalties, social investment, energy compensation, and capital gains, plus agreement in principle to let Paraguay sell surplus energy into Brazil's free market (Latin News; Paraguay's foreign ministry, May 2024).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • 500,000 jobs — not reached, and the term ended early. His central campaign promise was the creation of 500,000 jobs. His own third management report to Congress (July 2026) claimed more than 242,000 jobs created in the first half-plus of the mandate — roughly half the promise at the three-year mark, a figure opposition legislators contested as not reaching families' lived reality (government report, July 2026; congressional reactions via Pytagua, 2 July 2026). Unemployment did fall to record lows (Test 4), so the direction was kept and the number was not.
  • Itaipú, second phase — missed twice, then repudiated in direction. The 2024 deal committed both countries to conclude the Annex C revision by the end of 2024; Paraguay's presidency then announced a signing for 30 May 2025; both dates passed unsigned. Talks were suspended by Paraguay in April 2025 over the espionage allegation, resumed after July 2025, and as of November 2026 — with the 2024–2026 tariff period expiring — no revised Annex C had been signed, and Peña had publicly refused the post-2026 tariff reduction toward operating costs that the 2024 framework contemplated (Strature GPS risk file, November 2026; Canal Solar, 2025). The promise that headlined his foreign policy closed his tenure unkept.
  • Fiscal discipline — kept until it wasn't. The 1.5%-of-GDP deficit ceiling of the fiscal-responsibility law was the centerpiece of the model he sold to investors. On the last day of 2025 his government recognized some US$1.27 billion in outstanding bills to drug suppliers and road contractors; Fitch projects deficits of 3.2% of GDP for 2026 and 3.9% for 2027 and considers the 1.5% target unlikely to be met even in 2028 (Fitch Ratings, 11 November 2026; IANS summary of the fiscal record, November 2026).
  • The anti-corruption word — broken at the center. He campaigned on transparency and a modernizing, clean-government technocracy — "we will be better off" was the slogan's promise. The documented record of Tests 1 and 2 — his own stake in the bank's holding company held into his second year, his own loans from the bank, his own appointee from the bank chairing its supervisor, pension money placed after the qualifying rule was lowered, and a supervisor's blocking memo unheeded — is the record against which that word is measured, and it is also the substance of the criminal charges he now faces and denies (ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated). His government's August 2024 move to expedite the US ambassador's departure after the Tabesa sanction (Test 1) sits on the same shelf: when the anti-corruption instrument pointed at his patron, the state's word was spent against the instrument.
  • The long arc, factually: Peña's public life has two chapters. In the first, he was the technocrat — central-bank board member, then Cartes's finance minister at 36, an IMF résumé, the respectable face of a machine. In the second, as president, he attempted in his final year to build a power base of his own — the Ueno relationship, the promotion of his own successor candidate, the defiance of Cartes over the 2028 ticket — and the attempt and the fall were the same event: Cartes's senators began the Ueno inquiries in September 2026, Cartes publicly joined the calls for his ouster on 5 November saying the party would not side with "the plundering of public and private funds and pensioners' money," and the machine removed its own president in a week (WSJ; Journal of Foreign Affairs at Carolina; Inter-American Dialogue, November 2026). Some analysts have called the removal a "controlled implosion" preserving Colorado rule under Alliana — an interpretation, printed as such — while others read it as institutions working. Both readings agree on the fact beneath them: the word that governed his rise was loyalty to the patron, and it held until the patron withdrew it.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Santiago Peña governed Paraguay for three years as two presidents at once. The first delivered the best macroeconomic file in the country's modern history — investment-grade ratings from two agencies, growth above 5%, poverty down to 16%, a million children fed at school, a kept promise to Taiwan against the pressure of the world's largest buyer — and the second built, at the center of that success, a bank: his own money in its holding company, his own loans from its counter, his own appointee from its boardroom chairing its supervisor, and the pension savings of the country's workers placed inside it after the qualifying rule was lowered. When the bank failed, it took down nearly 3 million accounts, more than US$800 million in public money, and his presidency within four days. The tests measure the seat, and the seat's own record gives one law for the connected and another exposure for everyone else — while the same record shows courts, a central bank board, a prosecutor's office, a press, and a Congress that, at the end, all moved against him and were obeyed, which is why the grip grades mixed rather than failed, and why the final word on Santiago Peña now belongs, as he himself said in his last statement, to the justice system: "no one is above the law." The charges are unadjudicated; this record will be corrected in the open when verdicts replace allegations.
