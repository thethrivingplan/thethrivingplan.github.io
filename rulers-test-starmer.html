The Ruler's Test — Keir Starmer

Subject: Keir Starmer, Prime Minister of the United Kingdom, 5 July 2024 – 20 July 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Both shelves are shown for every test: the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION; contested items are described with who disputes them.

Framing note: Starmer's tenure is complete, not merely short. After poor local election results in May 2026 and the loss of confidence of his MPs, he announced his resignation on 22 June 2026; Andy Burnham succeeded him as Labour leader on 17 July 2026 and as Prime Minister on 20 July 2026 (BBC News, 22 June 2026; 2026 Labour Party leadership election record). This dossier therefore grades a finished premiership of just over two years. Where a 2024 manifesto promise was parliament-length, the grade notes that his early exit — itself partly a consequence of the record graded below — cut the delivery window.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Does he apply the same rules to allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders, with no favorites at the source?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The standards regime was applied at the very top of his own team. His deputy, Angela Rayner, referred herself to the Independent Adviser on Ministerial Standards, Sir Laurie Magnus, over a £40,000 stamp duty underpayment; Magnus found she had acted with integrity but had breached the Ministerial Code by failing to meet "the highest possible standards of proper conduct," and she resigned as Deputy Prime Minister, Housing Secretary and Labour deputy leader on 5 September 2025 (Magnus letter and resignation, 5 September 2025; reported by BBC/Alliance News, September 2025).
  • Transport Secretary Louise Haigh resigned on 29 November 2024 after it emerged she had pleaded guilty to fraud by false representation in 2014 — an insider removed over a standards issue within five months of taking office (BBC/Wikipedia scandal record, November 2024).
  • City Minister Tulip Siddiq resigned on 14 January 2025 after a Magnus review of her family's links to the deposed Bangladeshi regime; Magnus found no breach of the Code but judged she should have been "more alert" to reputational risk, and Starmer accepted her resignation (UCL Constitution Unit Monitor 89, 2025). Siddiq denies wrongdoing; a Bangladeshi court later convicted her in absentia in December 2025, a verdict she disputes (finwire.io guide to exits under Starmer, 2026).
  • He strengthened the machinery itself: the November 2024 Ministerial Code requires ministers to declare gifts and hospitality monthly rather than quarterly, and removed the Prime Minister's veto over the Independent Adviser initiating investigations (Reuters, 6 November 2024).
  • After the gifts row he repaid more than £6,000 of gifts received since becoming Prime Minister — including Taylor Swift and race tickets and a clothing rental — and said he would no longer accept donations for clothing (Downing Street statement, reported October 2024).

Evidence against:

  • His own conduct was the original problem. Reuters reported he had accepted more gifts than any other MP since 2019 — over £107,145 in gifts, benefits and hospitality, including Arsenal and concert tickets — and he had initially failed to declare clothing gifts for his wife from his biggest personal donor, Lord Alli (Reuters, September 2024; 2024 Labour Party freebies controversy record).
  • Lord Alli, the donor at the centre of the row, had been given a Downing Street security pass and hosted a party there for other Labour donors — access at the source for a benefactor that no ordinary supporter received (reported September 2024; freebies controversy record).
  • The Mandelson appointment: Starmer announced Peter Mandelson — a Labour grandee and ally of his chief of staff — as Ambassador to the United States in December 2024. Government documents later forced into publication showed a due-diligence memo of 11 December 2024 had warned Starmer that Mandelson's friendship with Jeffrey Epstein continued in 2009–11, after Epstein's 2008 conviction (Conservative-released "Mandelson files," reported 2026). Mandelson was sacked on 11 September 2025 when published emails showed the relationship was "materially different" from what was known at appointment (Foreign Office statement, 11 September 2025; UK Parliament written answer, 9 July 2026).
  • CONTESTED: In April 2026 the former head of the Foreign Office, Sir Olly Robbins, testified that he felt political pressure from Starmer's office to rush Mandelson's confirmation and that the security vetting agency had been "leaning toward recommending against" clearance. Starmer said he was not told of the vetting concerns and called that failure "staggering"; his chief of staff Morgan McSweeney resigned in February 2026 taking responsibility for the appointment (Associated Press, 21 April 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do Parliament, the devolved governments, Labour backbenchers and institutions keep their own autonomy — or is independence punished?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Backbench independence was real and effective: Labour rebellions twice forced major policy changes (see Test 3), and the four MPs suspended over the July 2025 welfare rebellion had the whip restored on 7 November 2025 after discussions with the Chief Whip (BBC News, 7 November 2025).
  • The devolved governments were treated as partners: in his first week Starmer visited Edinburgh, Cardiff and Belfast, and he established the Council of the Nations and Regions, bringing the PM together with the First Ministers and English mayors (Academy of Social Sciences analysis of intergovernmental relations, 2026). Scotland's First Minister John Swinney described relations as "incomparably better" than under the Conservatives (Scottish Parliament research briefing SB 25-28, July 2025).
  • Ultimately, the Parliamentary Labour Party's autonomy asserted itself over him: when his MPs withdrew confidence in June 2026, the institution removed the leader rather than the leader breaking the institution (BBC News, 22 June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Within three weeks of winning power, on 23 July 2024, Labour withdrew the whip from seven MPs — John McDonnell, Richard Burgon, Ian Byrne, Apsana Begum, Imran Hussain, Zarah Sultana and Rebecca Long-Bailey — for six months, for voting for an SNP amendment to scrap the two-child benefit cap (Labour Party leadership record, July 2024).
  • The pattern repeated a year later: on 16–17 July 2025 four more MPs (Rachael Maskell, Neil Duncan-Jordan, Brian Leishman, Chris Hinchliff) were suspended and three others stripped of trade envoy roles after the welfare rebellion. Starmer defended it personally: Labour "has to deal with" repeat rebels (BBC News, 17 July 2025). BBC political editor Chris Mason noted the suspensions were "designed to send a message" and risked appearing "petty and vindictive" (BBC News, 17 July 2025).
  • During the June–July 2025 welfare fight, rebel MPs reported — and No. 10 denied — whips' briefings treating the vote as a confidence issue and veiled threats about deselection (Guardian reporting, 24 June 2025, via the rebellion record).
  • Candidate selection was heavily centralised: NEC panels imposed candidates in 2024, sitting NEC members were themselves selected for seats — condemned by a union leader as "jobs for the boys" — and locally chosen left-wing candidates were removed days before the election, including Faiza Shaheen in Chingford and Woodford Green and Lloyd Russell-Moyle in Brighton Kemptown; an attempt to block Diane Abbott was reversed only after public backlash (LabourList selections record, June 2024; Brevia political briefing, 18 June 2024).
  • The grip reached his own succession: in January 2026 the NEC blocked Andy Burnham — the most popular potential challenger — from standing in the Gorton and Denton by-election, the only route by which he could return to Parliament to challenge Starmer. Burnham entered Parliament only in June 2026 via the Makerfield by-election, after which Starmer resigned within four days (2026 Labour Party leadership crisis record, 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When his own MPs, voters, markets or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Winter fuel: after months of pressure from his own MPs, unions and voters, Starmer signalled a reversal at Prime Minister's Questions on 21 May 2025, and on 9 June 2025 the government restored the payment to around 9 million pensioners, with only those earning above £35,000 excluded (Reuters, 9 June 2025).
  • Welfare: facing defeat by some 126 of his own MPs, the government conceded in late June 2025 that all existing PIP claimants would be protected, and on 1 July 2025 — an hour before the vote — further delayed the tightened PIP criteria pending the Timms review, gutting most of the planned £5 billion saving rather than losing (Irish Times, 1 July 2025; Alliance News, 26 June 2025).
  • Grooming gangs: having resisted a national inquiry, Starmer commissioned Baroness Casey's audit in January 2025 and, when it recommended a statutory inquiry, accepted publicly on 14 June 2025: "I've read every single word of her report, and I'm going to accept her recommendation" (Oldham Times, 14 June 2025; Casey audit published 16 June 2025).
  • He admitted error in his own words over the May 2025 "island of strangers" immigration speech, saying he should have read it more carefully and then expressing deep regret for the phrase (reported June 2025).
  • Farm inheritance tax: after more than a year of tractor protests, the government raised the agricultural relief threshold from £1 million to £2.5 million in December 2025 (government announcement, December 2025; BBN Times report).
  • The final wobble was answered completely: when his MPs' confidence went, he resigned rather than fight a contest he had initially intended to contest (Wikipedia, 2026 Labour leadership crisis; BBC News, 22 June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The pattern was deny first, double down, then reverse late. On winter fuel, as late as 7 May 2025 his spokesman said "there will not be a change to the Government's policy" and Starmer said the government would not be "blown off course" — weeks before the reversal (Glasgow Times, 7 May 2025).
  • On welfare, the concessions came only at the point of certain defeat, days after Starmer insisted to reporters he would "press on" with the cuts on a "moral case" (Forres Gazette/PA, June 2025).
  • On grooming gangs, the government spent roughly six months resisting a national inquiry — Starmer accused critics in January 2025 of jumping on a far-right "bandwagon" — before the Casey audit forced the change; opposition leader Kemi Badenoch said he "had to be led by the nose" (Oldham Times, 14 June 2025).
  • The reversals tracked electoral damage rather than preceding it: the winter fuel U-turn followed Labour's May 2025 local election losses to Reform UK (Reuters, 9 June 2025), and the sequence of U-turns was itself cited by his own MPs as a cause of the May 2026 collapse that ended his leadership (2026 leadership crisis record).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Who is inside his count — pensioners, disabled claimants, farmers, asylum seekers, the poorest — and who is outside it?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The single biggest counting-in measure of the parliament: the November 2025 Budget scrapped the two-child limit in Universal Credit from April 2026. DWP analysis estimates 301,720 children were no longer living below the poverty line as a direct result, with 450,000 projected by the end of the parliament (DWP analysis, reported November 2026; Budget statement, 26 November 2025).
  • The government's Child Poverty Strategy (5 December 2025) set a target of lifting 550,000 children out of relative low income by 2028/29, measured against DWP statistics (House of Commons Library briefing, 2026).
  • Pensioners were brought back inside the count: the June 2025 reversal restored winter fuel payments to about 9 million pensioners (Reuters, 9 June 2025).
  • Disabled claimants were protected by the rebellion-driven climbdown: existing PIP claimants kept their awards, protecting some 370,000 current recipients who had been set to lose out on reassessment (Forres Gazette/PA, June 2025).

Evidence against:

  • For his first year, pensioners were counted out: the July 2024 means-testing removed the winter fuel payment from around 10 million pensioners, including many just above the Pension Credit line; the IFS calculated about 85% of previously eligible pensioner households lost it (Reuters/IFS, 9 June 2025). Pensioner relative poverty then rose from 1.49 million to 1.69 million in 2024/25 (DWP Households Below Average Income, published 26 March 2026).
  • Disabled people were the original target of the March 2025 welfare package — restricting PIP eligibility and cutting the Universal Credit health element to save around £5 billion a year — and were only protected after his own MPs revolted (Irish Times, 1 July 2025).
  • Child poverty did not fall on his watch in the published figures: DWP statistics show 4.0 million children (27%) in relative poverty after housing costs in 2024/25, unchanged from the revised 2023/24 figure — with the two-child limit still in force for that entire period (DWP HBAI, 26 March 2026; CPAG analysis, 26 March 2026).
  • Farmers were counted out and then only partly back in: the October 2024 Budget ended full agricultural property relief above £1 million, provoking sustained national protests from November 2024; even after the threshold rose to £2.5 million in December 2025, the NFU and rural Labour MPs continued to demand full abolition (NFU campaign record, 2024–2026; Daily Express report, November 2026).
  • Asylum seekers and migrants were pushed furthest out. Starmer's May 2025 immigration speech warned Britain risked becoming an "island of strangers" — language his own MPs compared to Enoch Powell (reported May 2025). In November 2025 Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood proposed making refugee status temporary with reviews every 30 months and a 20-year wait for settlement — the longest in Europe — and extending the standard settlement wait from 5 to 10 years, with up to 30 years for those who arrived irregularly (Home Office "Restoring Order and Control" paper, 17 November 2025; Human Rights Watch assessment, 21 November 2025).
  • WASPI women — 1950s-born women the Parliamentary Ombudsman found had suffered maladministration over state pension age rises — were refused the compensation the Ombudsman recommended (£1,000–£2,950 each); the decision was re-taken by Pat McFadden and refused again in January 2026 (Liz Kendall statement, December 2024; Scottish Parliament Official Report, 19 February 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

His ten leadership pledges of 2020 and the 2024 manifesto, against the record.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • A substantial share of the 2024 manifesto was delivered or underway: VAT on private school fees began in January 2025; the rail public ownership law passed with operator transfers underway; Great British Energy was created in law; the Employment Rights Act passed on 18 December 2025; the Rwanda scheme was scrapped (Project Parly, Labour Manifesto Delivery Audit, updated 12 May 2026).
  • Channel 4 FactCheck's one-year assessment found the government had delivered or was "on track" on 22 of 36 checkable pledges, with 10 inconclusive and 4 not delivered or off track (Channel 4 News FactCheck, July 2025).
  • The government states it met its pledge of 2 million extra NHS appointments a year ahead of schedule, though FactCheck notes the pledge was designed in a way that makes it difficult to verify independently (Channel 4 News FactCheck, July 2025).
  • Employee rates of income tax, employee National Insurance and VAT were not raised in either of his Budgets — the government's own reading of its tax pledge was kept (Full Fact Government Tracker, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The ten pledges on which he won the Labour leadership in 2020 were repudiated by Starmer himself: asked on BBC Radio 4 in July 2022 whether they were dead, he answered "Yes. The financial situation has changed." Of the ten, most were dropped or replaced — public ownership of mail, energy and water; defending free movement; abolishing Universal Credit; raising income tax on the top 5%; abolishing the House of Lords (pledge card record, February 2020; analysis of the pledges' withdrawal, 2026).
  • Tuition fees are the clearest single reversal: the 2020 pledge promised "support for the abolition of tuition fees"; the pledge was dropped before the 2024 election, and in September 2025 his government raised English tuition fees from £9,250 to £9,535 — the first increase in eight years (Wikipedia, Tuition fees in the United Kingdom, citing the 2020 pledge and the 2025 increase).
  • The flagship green commitment shrank in stages: £28 billion a year of green investment, announced in 2021, was cut to £4.7 billion in February 2024 (documented pledge history, 2021–2024).
  • The 2024 manifesto's tax line — "we will not increase National Insurance" — was followed within four months by a rise in employer National Insurance from 13.8% to 15% with the threshold cut from £9,100 to £5,000, raising about £25 billion a year. The IFS argued this breached the commitment as written; Full Fact rates the National Insurance pledge as not kept. The government disputes this, saying the pledge referred to employee contributions — an interpretation the manifesto text did not state, and one it only articulated explicitly in November 2025 (Full Fact Government Tracker, 2026; Institute for Government explainer on Labour's tax pledges; House of Lords Library briefing, 2026).
  • Threshold freezes were extended instead — employee and employer NIC thresholds frozen further in the November 2025 Budget — raising tax by fiscal drag without touching rates (Full Fact, 2026).
  • WASPI: as opposition leader in 2022 Starmer called for fair and fast compensation for 1950s-born women; in government he refused any compensation in December 2024 and again in January 2026, against the Ombudsman's recommendation (Scottish Parliament Official Report, 19 February 2026; Kendall statement, December 2024).
  • Delivery gaps on headline outcome promises: the 1.5 million homes target was assessed off track, and asylum hotels, energy bills and living-standards promises had not visibly landed by May 2026 (Project Parly audit, 12 May 2026). His resignation in June 2026 also means the parliament-length promises can now never be delivered by him.

Overall Summary

On the documented record of a two-year premiership, Starmer's rule is a study in correction arriving late. He does answer the wobble — on winter fuel, disability cuts, grooming gangs and farm tax he reversed, sometimes in his own words of regret — but almost always only after defending the position to the point of defeat or electoral damage (Test 3, MIXED). His standards regime genuinely bit his own deputy and ministers, yet his own gift-taking and the Mandelson appointment show insiders favoured at the source (Test 1, MIXED). He gripped: rebels suspended in both Julys, selections centralised, and a leadership rival blocked by his party machine months before that same party removed him (Test 2, FAILS). He counted everyone in the end on child poverty — the two-child limit's abolition is the largest single counting-in measure of the parliament — but pensioners, disabled claimants, farmers, WASPI women and asylum seekers each spent long stretches outside his count first (Test 4, MIXED). And his word is the weakest shelf: the 2020 pledge card was repudiated by his own admission, tuition fees rose instead of falling, and the National Insurance pledge survives only on a reading its text never stated (Test 5, FAILS). Grades: One Law — MIXED. Holds Without Gripping — FAILS. Answers the Wobble — MIXED. Counts Everyone — MIXED. Keeps His Word — FAILS.
