The Ruler's Test — Isaias Afwerki, President of Eritrea

Subject: Isaias Afwerki — President of the State of Eritrea since 1993, and the only president the country has had. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: in Eritrea the president is the state. He is head of state and head of government, chairs the cabinet, commands the armed forces, and chairs the People's Front for Democracy and Justice (PFDJ), the only legal political party; since the National Assembly last met in January 2002, he is also the effective legislature, ruling by proclamation. The brief's premise was verified before grading: Afwerki still holds the seat in November 2026 — he delivered the keynote address for the 35th Independence Anniversary in Asmara on 24 May 2026, attended the Third Russia-Africa Summit in Moscow on 28–29 October 2026 and held talks with President Putin at the Kremlin on 30 October, and received China's Special Envoy for the Horn of Africa in Asmara on 5–6 November 2026 (Eritrean Ministry of Information, shabait.com, October–November 2026). No premise correction is required. Afwerki, born 2 February 1946 — he turned 80 in February 2026 — led the Eritrean People's Liberation Front (EPLF) to victory in the thirty-year independence war on 24 May 1991, headed the provisional government, and was elected president by the transitional National Assembly on 8 June 1993 with 95% of its vote, weeks after the UN-supervised referendum in which 99.8% of Eritreans voted for independence (CIA World Factbook record; US Senate Resolution 350, 2025). He has never faced the voters himself: no national election of any kind has been held in Eritrea since independence. The constitution ratified on 23 May 1997 has never been implemented. He has named no successor, and there is no visible succession mechanism. He is graded on the full tenure, 1993 to November 2026. A note on evidence, because this is the most closed state in the series: Eritrea admits no independent press, no UN investigator, and almost no outside researcher, and publishes almost no statistics of its own. Where a fact rests on the findings of UN-mandated bodies that Eritrea disputes, the finding and the dispute are both printed. Where the record genuinely cannot carry a finding — the exact number of political prisoners, deaths in detention, responsibility for specific fighting in November 2026 — the item is graded NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE inside the relevant test rather than filled in. The structural facts this dossier rests on — a dormant assembly, an unimplemented constitution, a service law whose limit is not applied, detentions acknowledged by no court record — are documented facts, not estimates, and the grades rest on them. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic of the Test, in Tristan's words: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Three standing rules of the method apply throughout. First, the mandate rule: governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one — and it is stated plainly here that no electoral mandate operates in this seat at all: Afwerki has never been elected by the public, so there is no platform to shield and none to attack; the Test grades the holding, and the absence of any mechanism by which Eritreans could give or withdraw a mandate is itself recorded under Tests 2 and 5. Second, the two axes are independent: keeping his word and helping people are graded separately, and neither launders the other; the crossings are named where they occur. Third, the no-good-choice clause: where the record shows a situation with no good choice — or none that appeared to be at the time — the dossier says so, names the options that actually existed and the harm each carried, and grades the choice as the best possible available in the moment; that cannot be held against him, though whether he built the trap himself, was honest inside it, and took the least-harm door actually open remain gradable. Every test closes with why the grade lands where it lands, and with who his actions on that test helped and who they hurt.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The written rules are formally uniform. Proclamation No. 82/1995 subjects every Eritrean between 18 and 50 to national service regardless of ethnicity, religion, or region, and the 2% diaspora "Recovery and Rehabilitation Tax" (Proclamations No. 17/1991 and No. 67/1995) falls on Eritreans abroad by the same formula whoever they are. On paper, the state's two great exactions make no favorites.
  • The punishment law reaches his own inner circle, which is a grim kind of even-handedness but a documented one. The G-15 detainees were his comrades-in-arms and senior ministers, not outsiders; Berhane Abrehe, his finance minister for six years, was arrested in 2018 after publishing a book critical of him and died in detention in August 2024 after six years without trial (BTI 2026; Freedom House 2025). The record shows no protected caste at the top: the offense that is punished is disloyalty, and it is punished in insiders first.
  • Internationally, he once accepted a rule-bound process and its discipline: Eritrea signed the Algiers Agreement in December 2000 accepting "final and binding" arbitration of the border, accepted the Eritrea-Ethiopia Boundary Commission's 2002 ruling, and pressed its claim for sixteen years by law rather than by renewed war (developed in Test 5).
  • Ordinary civil and minor criminal matters are handled by community and regional courts that do function for everyday disputes (BTI country reporting).

Evidence against:

  • For the cases that matter most, there is no stated law at all. On 18 September 2001 the government arrested eleven of the fifteen senior officials (the G-15) who had written to the president calling for the constitution's implementation, and closed every independent newspaper the same day; the journalists arrested in the same sweep — the group now known as the Eritrea 16 — have likewise been held incommunicado ever since. None has been charged, tried, or sentenced by any court; as of the 25th anniversary in September 2026, none has been released and the government has not confirmed which of them are alive (Committee to Protect Journalists, August 2026; PEN International, September 2026; US Senate Resolution 350, 2025). A rule that never has to be written down, applied for twenty-five years, is a second law for the connected and a void for everyone else.
  • Citizenship itself is two-tier by his own directive. In October 1994 a presidential directive stripped Jehovah's Witnesses of their citizenship rights because their faith forbade participation in the independence referendum and in military service; the community remains without identity documents, bank access, or government services as a class, and 64 Witnesses are currently detained without charge, several of them in their seventies and eighties (CSW written submission to the 62nd session of the UN Human Rights Council, 2026; USCIRF, 2025). No other Eritrean is required to surrender citizenship for an act of conscience; the Witnesses are.
  • Access to ordinary documents runs through loyalty paperwork that applies to one class of Eritrean only. Diaspora Eritreans must show full payment of the 2% tax — and those who left without completing national service must also sign a "regret letter" accepting punishment for having left — before embassies will renew a passport, issue a visa to visit home, or release an inheritance. The UN Security Council addressed the collection method directly in Resolution 2023 (2011), calling on Eritrea to "cease using extortion, threats of violence, fraud and other illicit means to collect taxes outside of Eritrea"; Canada expelled Eritrea's consul-general in 2013 and the Netherlands expelled its top Eritrean diplomat in 2018 after the collection continued in defiance of warnings (UN Security Council Resolution 2023; AEI review of the diplomatic record, 2026). Courts in Sweden (2018) and Germany (2022) ruled that the regret letter cannot lawfully be demanded of refugees under their protection.
  • Relief flows by grace, not by rule. In November–December 2025 the government released 177 Eritrean Christians, most of them conditionally — among them students arrested in April 2023 for recording worship music — in a step monitors welcomed (CSW, 2026, citing BBC Tigrinya). In the same season, the G-15, the Eritrea 16, and the 64 Jehovah's Witnesses were not released. A system in which freedom arrives as the ruler's gift to some prisoners while identically uncharged prisoners stay inside is the definition of a second track.
  • The law does not bind the state even when a court Eritrea recognizes says so. On 20 August 2025 the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights concluded that Eritrea had violated the African Charter in the case of three Jehovah's Witnesses detained from 1998 to 2021 — arbitrary detention, denial of legal status, inhumane treatment — and ordered compensation. Eritrea has not responded (CSW, 2026).

Why: The for-shelf is real and the dossier prints it at strength: the written exactions are uniform, and disloyalty is punished even in his own ministers. But Test 1 grades whether the law's incidence tracks conduct or tracks standing — and in Eritrea the operative law is unwritten. Detention without charge for the disfavored, denationalization for one faith, loyalty paperwork for documents, release by grace: each of these is documented, and each sorts Eritreans by their perceived loyalty rather than by anything a published law prohibits. That is two laws, and the second one is the one that governs. Helped: the PFDJ security apparatus and those currently in the president's favor, who operate without legal exposure; the party- and military-owned enterprises staffed by national-service labor, which the BTI describes as an unpaid labor force for those enterprises (BTI 2026). Hurt: the G-15 officials and the Eritrea 16 journalists, and their families, twenty-five years without charge or word; Jehovah's Witnesses as a class; diaspora Eritreans who must buy back their own documents; and the families of service evaders, against whom the UN Commission of Inquiry documented collective punishment — fines, eviction, confiscation, arrest of relatives (UN Commission of Inquiry on Eritrea, A/HRC/32/47, 2016).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The referees that exist at the lowest level function: community courts resolve ordinary civil disputes, and regional courts handle minor criminal matters without documented presidential interference in individual everyday cases (BTI country reporting).
  • Eritrea participates in some international referee processes rather than none. It underwent Universal Periodic Review cycles in 2019 and 2024, and it sat as a member of the UN Human Rights Council for 2023–2025 — while, as Human Rights Watch records, refusing to cooperate with the Council's own investigative mechanisms (HRW, World Report 2025). The African Commission process also ran to a conclusion against Eritrea in August 2025 (Test 1): the referee existed and ruled.
  • The one genuinely independent adjudication Eritrea ever accepted — the Boundary Commission under the Algiers Agreement — was accepted in advance as binding, and its ruling honored in Eritrea's own claim (Test 5).

Evidence against:

  • The legislature has not met since January 2002. The National Assembly — the body the unimplemented constitution makes the lawmaker, the budget's approver, and the president's elector — has been dormant for twenty-four years; the president legislates by proclamation, and the ruling party itself has not held a congress since 1994 (US Senate Resolution 350, 2025; Al Jazeera review of the record, 2018; BTI 2026). There is no legislative referee left to grip; it was switched off.
  • No national election has ever been held. Elections scheduled for December 2001 were postponed indefinitely, and none has been scheduled since (CIA World Factbook record; S.Res.350). Eritrea has an electoral law (2002) and no elections — the referee was designed and never allowed onto the field.
  • The judiciary has no independence to defend. There is no constitutional review, judges serve under executive authority, and political cases move through special and military channels outside ordinary process. The UN Special Rapporteur's reports record "the absence of an independent judiciary or a functioning legal code" for challenging detention, and a network of detention facilities "where due process is not observed" (Special Rapporteur reports, A/HRC/59 session, May 2025, and A/HRC/62/20, 2026).
  • The media referee was abolished in a day. All independent newspapers were closed on 18 September 2001 and none has reopened; the only domestic media are the state's. Since 2024 Reporters Without Borders has ranked Eritrea last of 180 countries in its World Press Freedom Index — below North Korea (RSF, 2024–2026).
  • The one referee he could not switch off, he tried to abolish. In July 2025 Eritrea tabled a resolution at the UN Human Rights Council to terminate the mandate of the Special Rapporteur on the situation of human rights in Eritrea — the only independent monitor of the country. The Council voted overwhelmingly to defeat the resolution and renewed the mandate (Amnesty International, 2025). No Special Rapporteur has ever been admitted to Eritrea.
  • The economic referees are the party. PFDJ- and military-owned enterprises operate on conscript labor outside any published budget; Eritrea has never conducted a census and publishes almost no official statistics, so there is no independent count of anything — money, people, harvests — against which the state's claims could be checked (BTI 2026; UN agency reporting).

Why: Test 2 usually grades whether a ruler grips referees that exist. Eritrea's file is the limiting case: the referees were prevented from existing or switched off — an assembly dormant since 2002, elections never held, a press closed in 2001 and ranked last on earth, a judiciary with no independence to lose — and the single external referee with a mandate, his government moved to terminate in 2025 and has never once let through the door. The thin for-shelf (functioning village courts, UPR participation, one honored arbitration) is printed in full; it describes the edges of the system, not a check on the seat. Helped: the presidency and the PFDJ-military economic network, which govern, tax, conscript, and spend with no institutional check, no published budget, and no independent count. Hurt: every Eritrean who might need a court against the state — the detained above all, who have no bench to appeal to; voters who have never been offered a ballot; readers served by a single state voice; and the country's own administrators, who must run a state with no published law in force and no statistics.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • June–July 2018 is the strongest wobble answer in this file, and it is genuinely strong. When Ethiopia's new prime minister Abiy Ahmed accepted the Boundary Commission ruling Eritrea had demanded for sixteen years and offered peace, Afwerki considered the option and took it within weeks: the Joint Declaration of Peace and Friendship was signed in Asmara on 9 July 2018, embassies reopened, flights and telephone links resumed, families separated for twenty years crossed the border, and in November 2018 the UN Security Council lifted the sanctions regime imposed on Eritrea in 2009 (UN Security Council Resolution 2444, 2018). A twenty-year posture — "no war, no peace" — was reversed openly, in person, when another option became available. Under the method's rule, that is exactly the consideration this test exists to record.
  • The pandemic produced a quiet adjustment inside the detention system. Between December 2020 and February 2021, as COVID-19 threatened the prisons, 32 Jehovah's Witnesses were released, among them three men — Paulos Eyasu, Isaac Mogos, and Negede Teklemariam — who had been held since 1994 without charge (OHCHR and Special Rapporteur statements, 2020–2021; USCIRF prisoner records). The releases were not announced as policy; they happened, under pressure of events.
  • Sustained external pressure produced marginal movement again in late 2025: the release of 177 Christians in November–December 2025 (Test 1) came in the same season as Sweden's foreign minister visited Asmara in December 2025 — the first visit by a Swedish official in thirty-two years — and raised the case of Dawit Isaak directly; she reported that Eritrean officials told her he is alive, though Sweden received no proof of life (Swedish Foreign Ministry, as reported December 2025; CSW, 2026).
  • In 2026 he adjusted to a shifting board rather than isolating against it: the United States decided to lift the sanctions imposed in November 2021 on Eritrean military and party entities for their role in Tigray (Reuters, 5 May 2026; the removal was reported effected by September 2026), and Asmara's diplomacy through the year — Moscow in October, Beijing's envoy in November, the New Alamein meeting with Egypt, Somalia, and Sudan's army chief on 4 October 2026 — was open engagement with every partner that offered it.

Evidence against:

  • The defining domestic wobble of his tenure was answered with disappearance. The G-15 letter of 2001 was not street unrest; it was fifteen of his own most senior officials asking, in writing, for the constitution to be implemented and reforms opened. The answer, within days, was arrest without charge for eleven of them, the closure of the entire independent press, and twenty-five years of silence about their fate (S.Res.350, 2025; CPJ, 2026). No domestic pushback since — not from the party, not from the churches, not from the diaspora — has ever produced a structural adjustment.
  • The 2018 opening was reversed unilaterally and without stated reasons. By April 2019 Eritrea had closed the reopened border crossings, barring the trade and family movement the peace had restored; no proper explanation was ever given (ISPI analysis; HRW, 2020). The border was never demarcated. The wobble was answered; then the answer was withdrawn, silently.
  • The gravest external findings were answered with rejection and non-cooperation, not examination. The UN Commission of Inquiry concluded in 2016 that there were "reasonable grounds to believe that crimes against humanity, namely, enslavement, imprisonment, enforced disappearance, torture, other inhumane acts, persecution, rape and murder, have been committed in Eritrea since 1991" (A/HRC/32/47). Eritrea's delegation dismissed the report as "legally indefensible" and a "politically motivated" witch-hunt, refused the Commission access throughout, and no Eritrean institution has ever investigated the findings (Eritrean Ministry of Information statements, 2015–2016; CSW, 2016). The findings are the Commission's and are contested by Eritrea; what is documented beyond dispute is the refusal to let anyone look.
  • The 2026 rupture with Ethiopia was answered at maximum speed and totality. On 1 October 2026 Ethiopia closed its embassy in Asmara and expelled ten Eritrean diplomats, accusing Eritrea of hostile acts; within hours, Eritrea severed all diplomatic ties — its foreign ministry stating it had "no option but to reciprocate in kind and sever all diplomatic ties with Ethiopia with all the negative corollaries that this entails" (Reuters, 1 October 2026). No channel was kept open by either capital. Ethiopia's foreign minister then wrote to the UN Security Council describing an Eritrean "all-out invasion" of parts of northern Ethiopia; Eritrea wrote in turn accusing Ethiopia of pursuing "an unprovoked war of aggression against Eritrea for more than three years" and of hosting and arming Eritrean opposition groups (UK House of Commons Library briefing, November 2026). Fighting resumed in Tigray from September 2026; clashes around Badme were reported on 11–13 November, and on 18 November Reuters reported both armies massing along the border. Neither government has published evidence for its central accusation against the other, and no independent body has access: responsibility for the November fighting is graded NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE in this dossier. What is gradable is the response pattern — total severance within a day, and no de-escalation channel thereafter.
  • The no-good-choice clause, applied and bounded. Eritrea's security dilemma was not, in its origin, of his building. Ethiopia refused for sixteen years (2002–2018) to implement the binding Boundary Commission ruling that awarded the flashpoint town of Badme to Eritrea — a documented fact, and the standing justification Asmara gives for its mobilization. A state of some four to six million people facing a neighbor more than ten times its size that would not execute a binding ruling had no cost-free door: accept the ruling's burial, return to a war that killed tens of thousands in 1998–2000, or hold a defensive mobilization. The dossier records that constraint and does not grade the existence of a defense posture against him. In 2026 the dilemma returned in a new form: Ethiopia's government speaks openly of securing Red Sea access and has not ruled out taking Eritrea's port of Assab by force (House of Commons Library briefing, November 2026) — and Eritrea's defensive mobilization in the face of a stated claim on its port is likewise recorded under the clause, not held against him. What remains gradable, and is graded in Tests 1, 4, and 5, is the posture's form and duration: indefinite whole-society conscription maintained unchanged for the eight years after Ethiopia accepted the ruling in 2018 and after the peace he himself signed — when the trap that justified it had, by his own account, been sprung open. The total diplomatic severance of October 2026, by contrast, was a choice among doors (reciprocity was one; protest with a channel kept open was another), and it is graded as a choice above.

Why: Abroad, when a genuine option appeared in 2018, he took it — quickly, openly, and at real scale — and the file credits that in full; the smaller adjustments of 2020 and 2025 are real too. At home, the record is unbroken in the other direction: the one internal pushback that mattered was answered with the permanent disappearance of the men who made it, and no finding, appeal, or anniversary since has moved the structure an inch. MIXED is the grade because the for-shelf contains the single largest adjustment in this dossier's region of the series — a twenty-year war posture reversed in a month — and a FAILS would erase it; but the against-shelf carries the greater weight, and it is stated plainly: the adjustments are events, and the refusals are the system. Helped: Eritrea's international position in 2018–19 and again in 2026 — sanctions lifted twice over, isolation ended twice by his willingness to take the offered door; the 32 Witnesses freed in 2020–21 and the 177 Christians freed in 2025; Eritrean and Ethiopian families who briefly reconnected across the reopened border in 2018–19. Hurt: the G-15 and the Eritrea 16, whose request for the constitution was answered with their lives in suspension; border communities whose reopened crossings were closed again without explanation in 2019; the subjects of the Commission of Inquiry's findings, who have never had a domestic hearing; and Eritreans and Ethiopians along the border in late 2026, facing massed armies with no diplomatic channel open between their governments.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the minorities, the faithful of every kind, the protesters, the poor, the diaspora, and the peoples next door — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The founding count included everyone. The April 1993 referendum was UN-supervised, open to the whole adult population, and returned 99.8% for the independence that was then delivered to all Eritreans without ethnic partition (S.Res.350, 2025). Whatever came later, the state began with a count in which every Eritrean was asked.
  • The state's great burden is not assigned by tribe or creed. National service falls on all nine ethnic groups and both major faiths, highland and lowland alike (Proclamation No. 82/1995; BTI 2026) — a universality of burden that several rulers in this series do not match, and which the for-shelf records at full strength. State health and education services are likewise formally open to all, and UN agencies documented substantial population-wide gains in malaria control and child survival in the 2000s–2010s.
  • The November–December 2025 releases returned 177 Christians to families who had counted them lost (CSW, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • One faith was counted out of citizenship itself. The October 1994 presidential directive against Jehovah's Witnesses (Test 1) did not merely disadvantage a group; it removed a class of Eritreans from the legal count — no citizenship rights, no identity documents, no bank accounts, no government services — for an act of religious conscience. Sixty-four remain imprisoned without charge, several in their seventies and eighties (CSW, 2026; USCIRF, 2025).
  • Since May 2002, only four religious communities are recognized — the Orthodox, Catholic, and Evangelical Lutheran churches and Sunni Islam. Adherents of every other faith, Pentecostal and evangelical Christians above all, have been detained in the thousands over two decades across more than 300 detention sites, some released only when privation had brought them near death (CSW submission to the Human Rights Council, 2026). And recognition is no protection for the disobedient: the Orthodox Church's own Patriarch, Abune Antonios, was deposed in 2006 and held under house arrest until his death in February 2022 for resisting state interference in his church (USCIRF and HRW reporting).
  • Families are counted as leverage. The Commission of Inquiry documented collective punishment as practice: relatives of service evaders and dissenters fined, evicted, dispossessed, and arrested for offenses they did not commit (A/HRC/32/47, 2016). The file's emblematic case is Ciham Ali — a dual US-Eritrean citizen detained in December 2012 at the age of fifteen, the daughter of a former information minister who had fallen from favor; 8 December 2025 marked thirteen years of her incommunicado detention (CSW, 2026).
  • The peoples next door were put outside the count. The UN's International Commission of Human Rights Experts on Ethiopia found in its final report (2023) that the Eritrean Defence Forces, with the Ethiopian federal forces and allied militias, were responsible in Tigray for mass killings, widespread and systematic rape and sexual violence including sexual slavery, deliberate starvation, and forced displacement — acts it characterized as war crimes and crimes against humanity, continuing after the November 2022 Pretoria cessation agreement to which Eritrea was not a party. Eritrea rejects the Commission's findings and denied wrongdoing; the Commission's mandate was allowed to lapse in October 2023 without Eritrean cooperation. The findings stand in this dossier as the findings of a UN-mandated body, contested by Eritrea and unexamined by any Eritrean institution; Tigrayan civilians are entered in this ledger because a documented army of this seat operated among them for two years. The US sanctions imposed on the Defence Forces and PFDJ entities in November 2021 over this file were lifted in 2026 — a change in Washington's policy, recorded as such, not a finding on the facts.
  • Women inside the universal burden carry a particular one. The Commission of Inquiry and successive Special Rapporteurs document sexual violence and harassment against female conscripts in a context of impunity (Special Rapporteur report, May 2025; Amnesty International, 2025). The government contests the monitors' work wholesale; no Eritrean body has investigated.
  • The exodus is the count the state refuses to read. UNHCR counted roughly 555,000 Eritrean refugees in 2025 — from a population the state has never counted itself, Eritrea having never held a census, with outside estimates running from under four million to over six. The Special Rapporteur reports that Eritreans deported home face interrogation, arbitrary detention, enforced disappearance, and indefinite conscription on arrival (May 2025); between December 2024 and February 2025 alone, more than 600 Eritreans were forcibly returned from Ethiopia (Amnesty International, 2025). A tenth or more of the nation has voted with its feet against the system that counts them only as conscripts and taxpayers; the state's answer is to count the returnees as traitors.

Why: A state counts everyone when no class is written out of the count by the ruler's own hand. In Eritrea whole classes are written out by decree and by practice: one faith denationalized, every unrecognized faith criminalized, families punished for their relatives, a detained child held thirteen years for her father's fall, returnees disappeared into the service, and the victims of his army's foreign war denied any standing in his accounting. The for-shelf's universality is real — the burden does not spare by tribe, and the founding referendum asked everyone — but a burden shared by all is not the same as all being counted. The count that Eritrea runs is a count of bodies for service and tax; it is not a count of persons with standing. FAILS. Helped: the recognized religious establishments, while compliant; the state's security and revenue position, which draws conscripts and diaspora tax from the whole population without exception; Eritreans of the founding generation, whose referendum was honored. Hurt: Jehovah's Witnesses, denationalized as a class; unrecognized Christian communities, detained in the thousands; families of evaders and dissenters, punished collectively; Ciham Ali and the other long-held detainees taken as minors or for their relatives' standing; returned refugees and deportees; female conscripts; Tigrayan civilians (per the UN Commission's findings, contested by Eritrea); and the roughly 555,000 Eritreans now counted as refugees abroad.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Founding promises, constitutional pledges, treaty signatures, and public statements vs. the record since 1991, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The founding word — kept. The EPLF promised independence and delivered it: military victory on 24 May 1991, a UN-supervised referendum in April 1993 that returned 99.8%, and recognized statehood. It is the largest kept promise in this file, and the whole of his legitimacy rests on it. The dossier records it at full weight.
  • The legal word on the border — kept. Eritrea accepted the Algiers Agreement's binding arbitration in December 2000 and the Boundary Commission's ruling in 2002, and held to the legal track for sixteen years while Ethiopia refused to implement the ruling. The refusal in that stalemate was Addis Ababa's; the record says so, and Eritrea's patience with the process is credited here.
  • The 2018 word — signed and, at first, performed. The Joint Declaration of 9 July 2018 ended the state of war in terms; embassies reopened, flights resumed, and the Security Council lifted the 2009 sanctions that November on the strength of the peace (Resolution 2444). For its first season, the word and the act matched.
  • The doctrine word — kept, for what it is. He has said for three decades that Eritrea would develop by self-reliance, take little aid, and accept no external instruction, and the state has behaved accordingly through drought, sanctions, and isolation. The keeping is documented and is credited on this shelf; the doctrine's costs are recorded in Test 4's ledger, and under the method the two axes are not merged.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The constitution — the seat's founding word, unkept for twenty-eight years. The constitution ratified on 23 May 1997 promises a president elected to a five-year term, an elected National Assembly, freedom of expression, and due process of law. It has never been implemented. The elections it requires, scheduled for December 2001, were postponed indefinitely; the Assembly has not met since January 2002 (Constitution of Eritrea, 1997; S.Res.350, 2025). He has faced no voters since the transitional assembly chose him in 1993.
  • The replacement promise — broken. In his Independence Day address of May 2014, Afwerki announced that a process to draft a new constitution would begin (USCIRF reporting; Al Jazeera, 2018). Twelve years on, no draft has ever been published, and as of November 2026 Eritrea has no constitution in force and none in public preparation.
  • The service limit — broken in practice every year since 2002. Proclamation No. 82/1995, the state's own law, sets national service at eighteen months. Under the Warsay-Yekalo campaign from 2002 the term became indefinite; conscripts serve for years and decades at nominal pay, with discharge at the state's unreviewable discretion (HRW, World Report 2025; Special Rapporteur, May 2025). After the 2018 peace removed the stated justification, expectations of an eighteen-month cap were allowed to circulate — and never enacted; monitors record no mass release and no reform through 2026 (HRW, 2020; BTI 2026: the unlimited-term obligation "continues without reform").
  • His own answer on elections — on the record. Asked in an Al Jazeera interview when elections would be held, he answered: "What elections?" — and said Eritreans might wait "three or four decades" to see the conditions for them (Al Jazeera, 2008–2010, recited in the record since). The sentence matters to this test because it is the seat's word about its word: the constitutional promise was not only delayed by events; it was publicly disowned by the man who made it.
  • The 2018 follow-through — eroded by his own hand. Eritrea closed the reopened border crossings by April 2019 without stated reasons, and the border whose demarcation the peace presupposed was never demarcated (ISPI; HRW, 2020). The peace's signature achievement was left to lapse by the signatory who had most demanded it.

The four crossings, named: 1. Kept his word AND it helped people: independence — the promise on which the state was founded, kept in full, and the help (a state of their own, voted for by 99.8%) belongs to the whole founding generation. The Boundary Commission track likewise: the legal word kept, and Eritrea's claim vindicated by law. 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt people: self-reliance, kept as doctrine through drought years and sanctions — the keeping is credited on this test, and the hurt (aid refused or restricted in hunger years, isolation's compounding costs) is recorded in Test 4 and not erased by the credit. The 2018 word belongs here too in its second act: the alliance he signed and honored with Abiy Ahmed became the partnership under which Eritrean forces entered Tigray, where a UN commission found them responsible for atrocity crimes (contested by Eritrea, Test 4) — a word kept, with grave hurt downstream of the keeping. 3. Went against his word AND it helped people: the releases. The government's standing word is that Eritrea holds no prisoners of conscience and detains only under law. The releases of December 2020–February 2021 and November–December 2025 freed more than two hundred people held without charge — an act that contradicts the standing word and, in the same act, returned fathers, mothers, and students to their families. The help is recorded in full; so is the contradiction. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt people: the constitution, the elections, the eighteen-month service law, and the 2014 drafting promise — the broken words on which the daily unfreedom documented in Tests 1, 2, and 4 is built.

Why: The kept shelf is not small — independence is the promise his whole rule rests on, and he kept it, and the border ruling shows a man who could wait sixteen years for a legal process to vindicate him. But this test grades a word over time, and for twenty-eight of his thirty-three years the operative words of the seat have been the broken ones: a ratified constitution never switched on, elections postponed past a generation, a service law whose limit exists only on its paper, and a replacement constitution announced in 2014 and never produced. The pattern is the same one this series found in Tunis, drawn out over three times the years: the words he kept enlarged or defended the state; the words he broke were the ones that would have limited the seat or freed the citizen. FAILS. Helped: the Eritrean founding generation, which received the state it was promised; Eritrea's legal position on the border, vindicated by patience; the more than two hundred prisoners released in 2020–21 and 2025, freed by the contradiction named in crossing 3. Hurt: every Eritrean who has lived a whole life under a constitution that was ratified before many of them were born and never once applied — which is to say, the entire population; conscripts who planned their lives around an eighteen-month law and have given the state decades instead; and the generation now in its early thirties that has never been offered a ballot.

Overall Summary

Isaias Afwerki is the only ruler in this series who has never faced an election, never permitted one, and never shared his seat with a sitting legislature, an independent court, or a free newspaper — and he is also the man who delivered the independence he promised and waited sixteen years on a legal ruling rather than reopen a war. The whys, test by test, with their ledgers in brief. On one law: the written exactions are uniform and disloyalty is punished even in his own ministers, but the operative law is unwritten — twenty-five years of detention without charge for the G-15 and the Eritrea 16, citizenship stripped from one faith by directive, documents sold back to the diaspora through loyalty paperwork the Security Council named in Resolution 2023, release by grace for some prisoners and not for identically uncharged others; helped, the security apparatus and the favored; hurt, the disappeared, the denationalized, and punished families. On gripping: the referees were not gripped but prevented — an assembly dormant since 2002, elections never held, the press closed in 2001 and ranked last of 180 since 2024, no independent judiciary or published budget or census — and the one external referee with a mandate, his government tried to terminate at the Human Rights Council in July 2025 and has never admitted; helped, the presidency and the party-military economy; hurt, every Eritrean who might need a court, a ballot, or a fact. On the wobble: the file's one great adjustment is real — the 2018 peace, taken within weeks when another option became available, sanctions lifted on its strength — with smaller movements in the prison releases of 2020 and 2025; against it, the G-15's letter answered with disappearance, the border reopened and silently re-closed, a UN commission's findings rejected unexamined, and the October 2026 rupture with Ethiopia answered by total severance within hours, leaving massed armies and no channel by November; the no-good-choice clause is applied to the security dilemma itself — a binding border ruling buried by a far larger neighbor for sixteen years, and a stated claim on Assab in 2026 — whose origin was not his building, while the dilemma's domestic form, indefinite conscription kept for eight years after the trap sprang open in 2018, is graded where it belongs, in Tests 1, 4, and 5. On counting: the founding referendum asked everyone and the burden of service spares no tribe, but whole classes are written out by his own hand — Jehovah's Witnesses denationalized, unrecognized faiths criminalized, families punished for relatives, a girl of fifteen held thirteen years, returnees disappeared into the service, some 555,000 refugees uncounted, and Tigrayan civilians placed outside his accounting by findings his government refuses to let anyone test (ICHREE, 2023, contested by Eritrea). On his word: all four crossings occur and are named — independence kept and helping; self-reliance and the 2018 alliance kept and hurting; the prisoner releases against his word and helping; and the constitution, the elections, and the eighteen-month law broken and hurting, for twenty-eight years and counting. The items this dossier grades NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE are stated so the gap is on the record: the true number of political prisoners and of deaths in detention (outside estimates range widely and no investigator has access), responsibility for the specific border fighting of November 2026 (both governments accuse, neither publishes evidence), and the state of his health and any succession arrangement (no documented record exists). The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the November 2026 confrontation becomes a war or a negotiation; whether the 2026 lifting of US sanctions produces any domestic opening or only revenue; whether the fate of the G-15 and the Eritrea 16 is ever acknowledged — the European Parliament was set to vote on Dawit Isaak's release in late November 2026, twenty-five years after his arrest; and whether a constitution, an election, or a census — any one of the three counts Eritrea has never run — ever takes place.
