The Ruler's Test — Peter Mutharika, President of Malawi

Subject: Arthur Peter Mutharika — President of Malawi since 4 October 2025, and the premise of the original brief is corrected on the record: the seat changed hands. Malawi's general election of 16 September 2025 was won outright in the first round by Mutharika of the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), with 56.8% of the presidential vote (3,035,249 votes) against 33.0% (1,765,170) for the incumbent, Lazarus Chakwera of the Malawi Congress Party (MCP); turnout was 76.4% of 7.2 million registered voters (Malawi Electoral Commission final results, 24 September 2025). Chakwera conceded publicly on 24 September, hours before the official declaration, describing the outcome as the people's democratic will, and the transfer was peaceful. Mutharika was sworn in on 4 October 2025 at Kamuzu Stadium in Blantyre, with Jane Ansah as Vice-President and Enock Chihana of the alliance partner AFORD as Second Vice-President. He is graded on his current tenure — roughly thirteen months at grading — with two bodies of context where the record requires them: his own first presidency (2014–2020), which ended when the Constitutional Court annulled his 2019 re-election and he lost the court-ordered fresh election, and Chakwera's completed tenure (2020–2025), against whose record he was elected. Mutharika, a former law professor who turned 85 the year of his return, is the first person to hold this office in two non-consecutive terms. The presidency is the executive seat: he appoints the cabinet, the service chiefs, and — through processes this dossier examines — the heads of the electoral, judicial-oversight and anti-corruption machinery. In parliament his DPP emerged as the largest bloc without a majority (counts by source put it between 78 and 81 of 229 seats, with a large independent contingent), so his budgets pass by negotiation, a fact that matters to Test 2. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic printed on the Test card governs the grading: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Under the mandate rule, governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one; carrying out that platform is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). Each test closes with why the grade lands and a ledger naming who was helped and who was hurt; word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes, and the Overall Summary names which of the four crossings occurred. Where the record shows a situation in which every available option carried real harm, the dossier says so, names the options, and grades the choice as the best possible available in the moment — while still grading whether he built the trap, and whether he was honest inside it.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The arrests of former MCP officials proceed through open courts, not around them: named charges, bail hearings, and bail granted on standard conditions — K1 million cash bail with periodic police reporting for former ministers Vitumbiko Mumba and Jessie Kabwila in the Lilongwe Senior Resident Magistrate's Court — and a Chief Resident Magistrate's Court ordered medical treatment for the detained MCP secretary general, Richard Chimwendo Banda, before remand (Malawian court reporting, December 2025 – January 2026).
  • The president's stated standard is evidence, given personally to his predecessor: when Chakwera telephoned him on 19 January 2026 to protest "political arrests," Mutharika replied that arrests under his administration are "based on clear evidence," that suspects will face trial, and that "the era of 'political arrests' has ended" (State House statement via presidential press secretary Cathy Maulidi, 20–21 January 2026).
  • The two largest files are attached to real paper, not invented offences: the East Bridge fertiliser-for-commodities barter, under which prosecutors allege some K25 billion was lost on fertiliser never delivered, and the alleged approval of roughly K29.4 billion in extra-budgetary funding for the Mega Farms Project in July 2025. Both transactions predate his government and had already drawn audit and parliamentary questions. The charges against the former officials named in them are ALLEGATIONs, unproven; the transactions themselves are documented (ACB spokesperson Jacqueline Ngongonda, May 2026; Maravi Post reporting, April–May 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The direction of prosecution is one-way, and it has been one-way for thirteen months. Every senior figure arrested since December 2025 sits on the other side: MCP secretary general Richard Chimwendo Banda (arrested 12 December 2025 on attempted-murder and abduction charges arising from a 2021 incident; freed when the statutory 30-day remand expired without a trial committal, charges outstanding); former ministers Ezekiel Ching'oma, Vitumbiko Mumba, Jessie Kabwila, Sosten Gwengwe and Sam Kawale; former Secretary to the President and Cabinet Colleen Zamba; and the sitting Leader of the Opposition, Simplex Chithyola Banda — arrested by Fiscal Police on 12–13 April 2026 in the East Bridge file, bailed, then made the subject of a second ACB arrest warrant on 8 May 2026 in the Mega Farms file, in reporting his camp contests (he denies evading arrest and states he was travelling without notice of the warrant) (CAJ News Agency, 22 January 2026; AFP via Pulse of Africa, April 2026; Maravi Post, May 2026). Mumba and Kabwila were charged with publishing false information likely to cause public alarm — over a press conference at which they alleged electoral fraud by the Electoral Commission in the September 2025 election. Whatever the merits, alleging a stolen election is now a chargeable act in Malawi, and no DPP figure has faced a symmetrical charge for symmetrical claims made in 2020.
  • At the same time, the cases against his own side were being closed. In February 2026 the Director of Public Prosecutions, Fostino Maele, discontinued the unexplained-wealth and money-laundering case against Norman Chisale — Mutharika's former bodyguard, by then a deputy minister in the government conducting the prosecution — "alongside several others involving politically exposed individuals" (Malawi Institute of Journalism analysis, 2026). Maele cited a prior professional connection, having represented Chisale in private practice; the recusal explains the signature, not the pattern. A discontinuance can be proper in a single case. Beside nine opposition arrests, it is what a second law looks like.
  • The seat's own file with the bureau completes the picture. The Anti-Corruption Bureau's cement investigation alleged that Mutharika's presidential taxpayer number was used in 2018–19 to import some 1.2 million bags of cement worth about K5.1 billion duty-free. He denied any involvement, was questioned under caution by Fiscal Police in August 2020, declined to answer the bureau's questions in July 2021, and was never charged; the bureau froze his and his wife's bank accounts for some 270 days before the restriction lapsed in June 2021 and a court refused a preservation order. The underlying allegation is an ALLEGATION, never adjudicated in either direction, and it is recorded as such. What is documented fact is the sequel: the two aides the bureau named as reportedly involved in the importation — Chisale and Peter Mukhito — are today his Deputy Minister and his Minister of Homeland Security, the ministry that oversees the police making the current arrests (ACB statements and court reporting, 2020–21; Cabinet of Malawi, October 2025).
  • The one law also reached down to a commenter: a citizen, Elija Mike Banda, was arrested in January 2026 after posting on Facebook about Chimwendo Banda's prolonged detention (CAJ News Agency, 22 January 2026).

Why: A law that only ever lands on the other side is two laws, however genuine each individual charge may prove. None of the MCP figures has been convicted of anything; several of the charges may be well founded; but in thirteen months the documented prosecutorial energy of this state runs in one direction only, and the documented relief — discontinuance — runs in the other, reaching a man who sits in the arresting government. That asymmetry, not any single case, is what Test 1 grades. FAILS.

Helped: The public record on two genuinely questionable transactions of the previous government (East Bridge, Mega Farms) is being assembled in court, where it belongs; taxpayers, if any of the ALLEGATIONs is ever proven and money recovered. Hurt: The named MCP officials under charge — secretary general, opposition leader, five former ministers — carrying unproven ALLEGATIONs with the liberty and reputation costs of arrest; the opposition's practical ability to allege electoral fraud without criminal exposure; and the credibility of every future claim by this state that prosecutorial direction is coincidence.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the anti-corruption bureau, the central bank, the media, and civic organisations keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts are demonstrably not his. Chief Justice Rizine Mzikamanda, his predecessor's 2022 appointee, remains in post, and at the October 2026 swearing-in of the president's own Judicial Service Commission appointees he used the ceremony to instruct them publicly on independence and accountability. Below him, magistrates granted bail to the Leader of the Opposition, enforced the 30-day remand cap that freed Chimwendo Banda, and ordered that detainee's medical treatment against the detaining power's convenience (court reporting, January–October 2026).
  • He filled a referee gap he inherited rather than created: the Judicial Service Commission had sat incompletely constituted for some 21 months from February 2025 — a vacancy that began under Chakwera — and Mutharika's appointments of 6 May 2026, made under section 117 of the Constitution, reconstituted it (Government press release, 6 May 2026; Nation reporting, October 2026).
  • Parliament checks him in the open and he submits to it. Holding the largest bloc but no majority, his government passed its 2026/27 budget by negotiation; the Public Accounts Committee chairperson reported the acting head of the ACB to the police and the Attorney General in April 2026 over alleged interference with a committee inquiry, and the health committee chairperson publicised the medicine stock-out figures in September 2026 — both on the record, neither punished in any documented way.
  • The press that documents every item in this dossier — the Nation, Nyasa Times, Malawi24, the Malawi Institute of Journalism's outlets — publishes against him daily and unharmed. The electoral commission that ran the 2025 election was staffed under his predecessor; he won under it and has not purged it. The Reserve Bank governorship passed in January 2026 to George Partridge, a serving minister moved across — a presidential appointment of the ordinary constitutional kind, recorded here because Test 2 watches the central bank, and no interference with monetary operations is documented in the record assembled.

Evidence against:

  • The Anti-Corruption Bureau — the one referee built specifically to investigate the powerful — has been headless for his entire tenure, and the vacancy is now his, not inherited. The bureau has had no substantive director general since Martha Chizuma's contract expired on 31 May 2024; her own tenure under Chakwera had included a police arrest in December 2022 and an interdiction the High Court declared illegal — the predecessor's grip on this referee, recorded as context. Mutharika's administration removed the inherited acting director, Hilary Chilomba, and installed Gabriel Chembezi as acting director general in November 2025. Chembezi's twelve-month acting term expired on 3 November 2026; the Justice Minister directed him to remain in office. Two recruitment exercises — interviews in April 2026 that produced no appointment, a third advertisement in August, seven more candidates interviewed by November — have produced no substantive appointment in thirteen months (Malawi24, 10 November 2026). An acting head serves at the pleasure of the hand that extends him, month by month. That is the grip in its administrative form, whoever holds it and whatever his intentions.
  • The acting head himself carries unresolved ALLEGATIONs: Malawi24 reported in March 2026 that Chembezi attended a 17 November 2025 meeting on the proposed Amaryllis Hotel purchase — a transaction his bureau is investigating — and the Public Accounts Committee chair reported him to the police in April 2026 over alleged attempts to influence the committee's investigation. Both are allegations, unproven and contested; the structural point stands without them: the referee investigating the powerful is an unconfirmed acting appointee whose continuation the executive controls.
  • The Human Rights Defenders Coalition, the country's principal civic referee, was deregistered by the Non-Governmental Organisations Regulatory Authority in 2026, in the same season it was organising nationwide demonstrations against the government. Its vice-chairperson states the deregistration does not extinguish its members' rights as citizens; the fact of the deregistration — a referee struck off the register while blowing the whistle — is documented (Nation and Zaku Malawi reporting, November 2026).
  • His deputy is the referee of a nullified election. Vice-President Jane Ansah chaired the Malawi Electoral Commission that administered the 2019 election the Constitutional Court annulled for widespread irregularities, including results sheets altered with correction fluid. Her place on the 2025 ticket was lawful and the ticket won clearly; it is recorded here because Test 2 grades the standing of referees, and the official most associated with a failed national count now sits one seat from the top — a fact the opposition cites, and the court record supports the association.

Why: This is a genuinely split file. The courts, parliament, the electoral commission and the press keep their autonomy and use it against him weekly — that is not nothing; it is most of what the test asks. But the two referees aimed most directly at the executive itself have been weakened on his watch: the ACB kept vacant-and-acting for thirteen months under an extendable acting head, and the leading rights coalition deregistered while mobilising against him. The grip is selective rather than total, which is what MIXED means — and it sits at the failing edge of MIXED. A substantive ACB director appointed through the advertised process would move this grade; another year of acting arrangements would end the argument. MIXED.

Helped: Detainees and litigants who met independent magistrates (bail, the remand cap, a medical order); parliament's oversight committees, whose public bites went unpunished; readers of an uncowed press; the judiciary, whose oversight commission exists again after 21 months. Hurt: The ACB's staff, cases and standing, run past his term by an acting head the executive can end at will; the Human Rights Defenders Coalition and the Malawians whose grievances it organised; and public confidence that the anti-corruption referee will be permitted a permanent head while its files point at politicians.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The strongest wobble answer in the file is his own, from his first presidency. In February 2020 the Constitutional Court annulled his re-election; in May 2020 the Supreme Court upheld the annulment. Mutharika attacked the ruling in words — and complied with it in fact: he contested the fresh election of 23 June 2020, lost it, and left office. The man graded here holds the seat because its previous holder — himself — submitted to the hardest pushback a court can deliver. No dossier in this series carries a cleaner precedent.
  • In this tenure he let an unpopular mechanism tell the truth. The automatic fuel pricing mechanism was allowed to operate through three increases — petrol from K3,499 per litre at his inauguration to K4,965 in January 2026 and K6,672 at the end of March, easing to K5,619 — against the plain politics of his own campaign promise of available, affordable fuel. The IMF's October 2026 mission records the fuel and sugar pricing reforms as restoring market functioning (IMF Press Release 26/323, 6 October 2026; Panafricanvisions first-year review, 7 November 2026).
  • When the street organised in November 2026, one door was dialogue. The Concerned Citizens of Malawi–People Power Movement suspended its 17 November demonstrations after the government signalled willingness to meet, its co-chairperson calling the postponement "a strategic decision from our end, not surrender" and warning the demands stand (CCM-PPM press briefing, November 2026).
  • He answered the whereabouts wobble in person, eventually: returning on 31 October 2026, he stated on State House media that he had remained in control, in contact with the Chief Secretary and ministers daily, that fuel shortages were a logistics problem with supply available at regional ports, that those responsible would be held accountable, that blackouts would end by December, and he asked Malawians for a year of patience (State House Media, 31 October 2026). The commitments are dated and checkable, and Test 5 records them as such.

Evidence against:

  • For twenty-five days at the height of converging crises, the wobble went unanswered. He left Malawi on 6 October 2026 for South Africa on a "private visit" — his fifth such trip of the year — with no duration or purpose disclosed, while fuel queues lengthened, blackouts continued and hospital medicine shelves emptied. The government's answers were refusals in form: "A private trip is private" and the president "will return when the time is right" (Information Minister Shadric Namalomba, 23 October 2026); social-media reports of onward medical travel to India were denied without the trip's actual purpose ever being stated. The Vice-President never addressed the nation, though the MCP and UTM president Dalitso Kabambe both formally asked her to. That the trips were for medical treatment is an ALLEGATION the government denies; this dossier does not grade his health, on which the public record cannot carry a finding. What is documented and graded is the communication: a head of state publicly unreachable for twenty-five days of crisis, and a government that treated the question as impertinence.
  • The silence has largely held since his return. On 24 November 2026 the Public Affairs Committee — the interfaith body that has mediated Malawi's national crises for three decades — issued a public statement that continued uncertainty about the president was undermining public confidence and demanded a live presidential address (PAC statement, 24 November 2026). Malawian commentary counts twenty-two days since the return with little direct engagement (Malawi Cables, November 2026). The address has not happened.
  • The street's other door was deflection and obstruction. The Information Minister announced that "we are investigating the sponsors of these demos... we know some politicians are behind this" (November 2026). The Deputy Minister of Homeland Security — who doubles as the DPP's director of youth — said on television that he would not allow the Human Rights Defenders Coalition's demonstrations. Police informed the HRDC that they could not provide security for its 17 November march because officers were committed to a governing-party parade scheduled the same day; the councils of Kasungu and Mulanje declined to facilitate marches in their districts. The HRDC postponed on stated safety grounds; its demand — the energy minister's resignation — stands unanswered (Nation, Zaku Malawi and Al Jazeera reporting, November 2026).
  • The no-good-choice clause, applied where it belongs — the fuel file. The options actually open in October 2025 were: (a) hold pump prices down by subsidy with gross reserves near two months of imports and no IMF programme in place — the price stays fictional until the fuel stops entirely; (b) let the automatic mechanism price fuel at cost — prices roughly double inside six months and transport and food costs follow; (c) formal rationing — the queue becomes a state allocation with its own corruption market. Each carried real harm. He chose (b), the least-harm door available in the moment, and that choice is graded here as the best possible available and is not held against him. Two things remain gradable and are graded elsewhere: the trap's origin is shared — the subsidy-and-scarcity structure was built over a decade that includes his own first government — and the promise he made against this exact trap is graded on Test 5, not excused by the trap.

Why: He has answered the biggest wobble of his political life by submitting to it, and the economic wobble of 2026 by letting prices tell the truth at his own expense. He answers the visibility wobble — a country asking, through its opposition, its former central banker and its churches, where its president is and whether anyone is in charge — with absence, refusal of detail, and ministers investigating the motives of the askers. Both halves are documented; neither cancels the other. MIXED.

Helped: The Concerned Citizens movement, whose dialogue door opened; the fiscus and fuel importers, by prices that reflect cost and a supply chain that can be financed; Malawians holding a dated, checkable commitment on blackouts. Hurt: The Malawians who queued overnight for rationed fuel while their president was privately abroad and publicly unaccounted for (Al Jazeera, 20 November 2026); the HRDC's marchers, postponed by a security shortage created by a party parade; public confidence, on the Public Affairs Committee's own 24 November account.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the regions that did not elect him, the opposition's voters, the refugees in the camp, the patients, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The hunger he inherited was counted nationally and immediately. A state of disaster was declared on 25 October 2025 — three weeks after his inauguration — and extended to all 28 districts, with about 4.0 million people projected to face Crisis (IPC Phase 3) or worse food insecurity between October 2025 and March 2026 (ACAPS summary of IPC projections, November 2025). The count then fell to about 1.9 million in April–September 2026 after a maize harvest of roughly 3.3 million tonnes, up from about 2.8 million — a recovery the IPC attributes largely to better rains after the El Niño drought, and which this dossier credits to the weather and to input pricing, not to the seat's virtue (IPC, 2026; Ministry of Agriculture final estimates, reported November 2026).
  • Two of his signature policies count by design rather than by loyalty. Free secondary education, from January 2026, covers every child including the opposition's; the National Advocacy Platform's independent first-year assessment credits the access expansion while warning on quality (NAP assessment, 8 October 2026). The K5 billion Constituency Development Fund is allocated per constituency — all 229, including MCP-held seats (government and DPP accounting, 2026).
  • In the IMF negotiation, the protection of the counted-out is written into the design his government is negotiating: the Fund records that a new programme would pursue fiscal consolidation "while protecting social spending" (IMF Press Release 26/323, 6 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The input map drew a regional question from his own civil-society reviewers. The National Advocacy Platform questioned why the largest share of Farm Input Subsidy Programme beneficiaries falls in Southern Malawi — the DPP's home region — at a time when seasonal forecasts pointed to below-normal and erratic rainfall in parts of the Southern and Central regions, and called for distribution aligned to district-level forecasts (NAP assessment, 8 October 2026). The question is documented and specific; a published government rebuttal is not in the record assembled here.
  • The refugees at Dzaleka are counted as a security file. The camp, built for 10,000 to 14,000 people, houses roughly 49,000 (UNHCR figures). In June 2026 six refugees were sentenced to six months' imprisonment with hard labour for leaving the camp without exit permits — the encampment regime enforced criminally under this government (ACAPS, June 2026) — while the World Food Programme was reduced to prioritising food assistance for 35,000 of the most vulnerable as aid funding shrank (WFP reporting via Dzaleka news services, November 2026). A Law Commission review proposing wider refugee rights, including work and business rights, was validated in February 2026; it remains a proposal, not a law, and the government's own commissioner was reported in August 2026 describing plans to relocate the camp to Chitipa as "at an advanced stage."
  • The sick were counted in the wrong currency. Medicine availability across public facilities fell from 51% in April 2026 to 40% in September, and in central hospitals from 34% to 18%, in a year when the medicines budget rose 13% to K108.3 billion — because the Central Medical Stores Trust owes foreign suppliers about $18 million it cannot settle in kwacha. Parliament's health committee chairperson put it on the record in September 2026: "The kwacha is available, but we need dollars" (health committee and CMST figures, reported November 2026). The budget counted the patients; the foreign-exchange counter did not.
  • The fuel correction was distributed downward. Non-food inflation — transport, housing, the imported goods the poor buy — stood at 31.5% in September 2026 even as headline inflation fell to 19.4% (National Statistical Office figures, reported November 2026). The correction's costs are itemised in the street record: 20-litre daily rationing, overnight queues, black-market petrol at more than double the global average price, and at least six deaths in fuel explosions this year documented by the Nation newspaper (Al Jazeera, 20 November 2026). The next lean season is already counted: about 2.6 million people projected in Crisis or worse to March 2027 (IPC projection, 2026).

Why: The counting machinery of this government genuinely reaches the whole map: a disaster declared for all 28 districts within three weeks, a fund allocated to every constituency of every party, a school policy with no regional gate. The lived distribution leaves named groups outside the count — refugees imprisoned for leaving an overcrowded camp, patients facing shelves at 40% stocked, an unanswered regional question over the input map, and the poor paying for macroeconomic honesty in minibus fares. Both are the record. MIXED.

Helped: Secondary-school households in every region; the roughly 4.0 million people counted into disaster planning in his first month; constituencies of every party receiving the development fund; the 1.9 million who exited crisis-level food insecurity after the 2026 harvest. Hurt: Dzaleka's roughly 49,000 residents — six of them imprisoned in June 2026 for leaving without a permit — as food aid is rationed to the most vulnerable 35,000; public-hospital patients, as availability fell to 40% nationally and 18% in central hospitals; minibus commuters, market traders and small businesses carrying 31.5% non-food inflation and the overnight queue.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Manifesto pledges and public statements vs. the record since 4 October 2025, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Free secondary education — the headline promise, delivered. The DPP manifesto, launched 3–4 August 2025, promised free secondary education and the abolition of fees attached to primary education including national examination fees. The policy took effect from the January 2026 school term; it is credited by the National Advocacy Platform's independent first-year assessment and acknowledged even in reviews sharply critical of the government on other files (DPP manifesto launch reporting, August 2025; NAP, 8 October 2026).
  • The K5 billion constituency fund — rolled out. Promised in August 2025 at K5 billion per constituency per year, the Reformed Constituency Development Fund is reported by the government as delivered, and the DPP restated delivery to its members in November 2026 (Malawi Freedom Network, November 2026). Full independent verification of disbursement across all 229 constituencies is not in the record assembled here; the rollout and the figure are documented, and the claim is graded as credibly in motion rather than fully proven.
  • The fertiliser price — kept to the kwacha, missed in the season. The farm input subsidy was revived at K10,000 per bag for 1.1 million households, against an open-market price near K180,000 per bag. The price — the promise's letter — held. The timing — its substance — did not: when the programme launched in November 2025 only about 30,000 tonnes were in the country, and the 2026/27 budget then cut the allocation by more than half, much of it to pay for fertiliser already imported (Panafricanvisions first-year review, 7 November 2026). The no-good-choice clause covers part of this: at Malawi's reserve levels, importing a full programme on time may not have been purchasable at any price, and that constraint is recorded, not charged. What the clause does not cover is the calendar — a planting input launched as planting began is an administrative failure, not a fate.
  • Fiscal consolidation — in motion as written. The manifesto promised consolidation savings rising from about K500 billion in 2026. The documented first year shows the fiscal deficit narrowing to an estimated 8.8% of GDP in 2025/26 (World Bank estimate; the fiscal year began under his predecessor and the inheritance is noted), domestic revenue beating its first-quarter 2026/27 target, and IMF staff recording that "the authorities have been implementing strong reforms to tighten fiscal discipline" with a new Extended Credit Facility in advanced design (IMF, 6 October 2026; Panafricanvisions, November 2026). Carrying out the elected platform is recorded here as word-keeping under the mandate rule, not graded as policy agreement.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "The Cabinet shall be very lean" — broken against his own number. He is on record after the election promising a cabinet of fewer than 20 ministers. The full cabinet named on 30 October 2025 numbered 24; the reshuffle of 5 January 2026 expanded it to 28, where it remains (Cabinet of Malawi records; MediaGov, January 2026). Supportive assessments praise the cabinet as lean relative to his predecessor's; the comparison he invited was his own figure, and 28 is not fewer than 20.
  • Fuel — the availability word unmet at grading. The campaign's core was the "four Fs": food, fuel, fertiliser, forex. Thirteen months in, the documented condition of fuel in November 2026 is overnight queues, 20-litre daily rationing, station violence, and black-market prices at more than double the global average (Al Jazeera, 20 November 2026). The price rises themselves are graded on Test 3 under the no-good-choice clause and are not double-counted here. What is graded here is the word: fuel that Malawians can actually obtain was promised, and at grading it is not yet delivered.
  • Forex stabilisation — receding. DPP policy documents promised a stable and predictable exchange rate within six months of taking office. At thirteen months, the IMF records continued foreign-exchange pressure and a wide gap between official and parallel rates, and no programme has yet been approved (IMF, 6 October 2026; Further Africa, 8 October 2026).
  • The assurance to his predecessor — contradicted so far by the pattern it denied. On 19 January 2026 he assured Chakwera personally that arrests are based on clear evidence and that the era of political arrests has ended. In April the Leader of the Opposition was arrested, and in May warrant-sought a second time (Test 1). Verdicts may yet vindicate the assurance case by case; as a word over time, it currently stands against the direction of his own government's arrests.
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: Chakwera's Tonse government (2020–2025) was elected on promises of jobs, cheap fertiliser and an anti-corruption drive. Its completed record includes the arrest of its own anti-corruption director by police in December 2022 and an interdiction the High Court declared illegal; an ACB left headless from May 2024; its vice-president Saulos Chilima arrested by the ACB in 2022, freed by discontinuance in 2024, and killed in the Chikangawa forest plane crash of 10 June 2024; and an IMF programme that lapsed uncompleted in May 2025. It also includes the ending: a prompt public concession on 24 September 2025 and a peaceful handover of the seat. Mutharika was elected substantially on the wreckage of that government's word. His own word is graded above on its own record, and the comparison flatters neither man completely.
  • Dated and checkable: on 31 October 2026 he committed to an end to blackouts by the end of December 2026 and asked the country for a year of patience. Both are recorded here so the record can grade them later; neither can be graded now.

Why: The two largest social promises of the campaign — free secondary education and the constituency fund — were kept or credibly rolled out, and the fertiliser price was kept in the letter. Against that stand a cabinet that broke his own stated number, a fuel word whose substance (availability) is unmet, a six-month forex word now thirteen months old, and a personal assurance on arrests that the arrest pattern contradicts. The kept promises are the kind that reach every household; the broken ones are the kind that reach every queue. MIXED.

Helped: Households freed of secondary-school fees in every region; the 1.1 million farm households reached at the K10,000 price where bags arrived in time; constituencies receiving development funding; future taxpayers, if the consolidation path and the IMF programme hold. Hurt: Households that planned around "fuel available" through a year of queues and rationing; taxpayers carrying a 28-member cabinet under an austerity programme that promised fewer than 20; the opposition figures arrested after a presidential assurance that political arrests had ended.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Peter Mutharika's second presidency is thirteen months old, and its shape is a study in which referees a returning ruler respects. The ones he did not appoint — his predecessor's chief justice, a parliament where he holds no majority, an electoral commission staffed by the other side, a press that prints his failures daily — he has left alone, and they have repaid the restraint by functioning: bail granted to his opponents, remand caps enforced, committees biting in public, a budget passed by negotiation. The ones aimed at him personally he has handled differently. The anti-corruption bureau has spent his entire tenure under acting leadership he extends at will, while its files and the police's handcuffs run toward the opposition — nine senior figures arrested, including the Leader of the Opposition twice, against a discontinued case for the deputy minister whose file was closed by a prosecutor who had been his lawyer. That is the file's one clear failure, and it is the first test because it is the first thing power does. On the wobble, his record holds the series' most striking submission — the man who complied with the annulment of his own re-election in 2020 — beside its most striking absence: twenty-five days privately abroad during converging crises, a vice-president who never addressed the nation, and, three weeks after his return, the country's churches publicly demanding a live address that has not come. On counting, his machinery reaches the whole map — all 28 districts under disaster declaration, every constituency funded, every child schooled free — while the lived distribution leaves the refugees of Dzaleka imprisoned for walking out of an overcrowded camp and hospital patients facing shelves the budget paid for and the forex counter emptied. On his word, the social promises were kept and the economic ones were not, and the file's honesty about that split is helped by his own habit of making dated, checkable commitments — the December blackout deadline now on the record will grade him again whether he speaks or not.

The four crossings, named: 1. Kept his word and it helped: free secondary education — promised in August 2025, delivered in January 2026, reaching households of every party and region; and the K10,000 fertiliser price, kept to the kwacha for the households the bags reached. 2. Kept his word and it still hurt: the anti-corruption drive against the previous government was a campaign promise, and it has been pursued as promised — and its one-directional pursuit is the same pattern that fails Test 1. The keeping earns the Test 5 credit; the direction is recorded in full on Test 1 and laundered nowhere. Beside it: the commitment to automatic fuel pricing, kept as told to markets and the IMF, and paid for at the pump by the poor (Test 4's ledger). 3. Went against his word and it helped: the campaign's fuel word was availability and affordability. He broke the affordability half deliberately, letting prices rise some 90% in six months — and the breaking is what the IMF credits with restoring market function and what kept priced supply flowing at all. The break is recorded on Test 5; the help is recorded here in full. 4. Broke his word and it hurt: the "very lean" cabinet — fewer than 20 promised, 28 delivered — under an austerity programme preached to everyone else; and the assurance of 19 January 2026 that the era of political arrests had ended, followed within four months by the arrest and re-arrest warrants of the Leader of the Opposition.
