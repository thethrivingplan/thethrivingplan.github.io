1 · The Café That Moves

Study the café. When you tap “I’ve got it,” the lights blink — and while they’re out, a few things quietly change. Tap every spot you believe changed.

Ready when you are.
Hint: don’t just look at objects — look at states. Open or shut? Full or empty? Lit or dark? On the wall or gone?
Why it works: your eyes take in a whole scene at once, but memory keeps only a sketch of it. Fixing three or four details on purpose — “clock, plant, sign” — gives the sketch anchors, and changes near an anchor jump out.

2 · The Tea Tray

An old parlour game, played for over a century: a tray of objects is carried in, you study it, it’s covered — and you say what was on it. Here the cover is a button.

Ready when you are.
The tray arrives when you tap start.
Hint: group the tray as you look — “the three round things, the two red things, the odd one by the spoon.” Groups are easier to hold than a list.
Why it works: memory holds chunks, not items. Five loose objects strain it; three little groups slide in easily. Grouping on purpose is the whole trick — spies, waiters, and nurses all use it.

3 · Names at the Party

You’ve just arrived. Meet everybody, faces and names together. Then the music starts, the names come off — and you’ll be asked who was who.

Ready when you are.
The guests arrive when you tap start.
Hint: hook each name to something you can see — “Rosa wears the rose-red scarf.” A silly hook beats a careful one; silly sticks.
Why it works: a bare name is slippery, but a name tied to a picture has two handles instead of one. You’re not memorising harder — you’re giving memory a second way back in.

4 · Codes & Sequences

Every row below follows a rule — in numbers, in shapes, or in little tunes you can play. Work out the rule, then tap what comes next.

Ready when you are.

Your first code arrives when you tap start.

Hint: say the gaps out loud — “plus 3, plus 3…” When the gaps themselves change, the rule lives in the gaps.
Why it works: a sequence is a story told in shorthand. Finding the rule is finding the story — once you can say it in words (“each step doubles, then rests”), the next step says itself.

5 · One Stranger in the Crowd

A busy street, everybody dressed to a pattern — except one. Tap the figure who breaks it. Fewer wild taps wins: each wrong tap costs a point.

Ready when you are.

The crowd gathers when you tap start.

Hint: don’t inspect faces one by one. Let your eyes drift over the whole crowd — the odd one usually tugs at the corner of your eye.
Why it works: your visual system flags “not like the others” before you can say why. Scanning loosely uses that flag; staring hard at one face at a time switches it off.

The Debrief — what you were actually practising

The disguise was the design: the face was play, the skills are real, and the naming comes after — because a skill you discover in yourself sticks better than one you were lectured about. Here is the shelf, named plainly.

The Café → Change-spotting

Noticing what altered between two looks at the same scene. The core of “something’s off here” — used by inspectors, editors, nurses on a ward round, and anyone who walks back into a room and feels it differ.

The Tea Tray → Observation memory

Holding a seen arrangement in mind after it’s gone. This is the classic Kim’s Game, named after Kipling’s novel Kim, where it trains a young intelligence agent — the documented parlour shape of a real training exercise.

The Party → Face-and-name recall

Binding a name to a face so it survives a crowd. The friendliest form of an operative skill — and, not coincidentally, the one that makes hosts, teachers, and organisers beloved.

Codes → Pattern-finding

Reading a run of things and inferring the rule that made it — in numbers, shapes, and sounds alike. The same move, three costumes.

The Crowd → Spotting the break

Fast visual search for the one element that violates the pattern of the rest — the anomaly sense behind proofreading, quality checks, and “wait, that one’s wrong.”

Tap to open — the honest note about transfer

These games genuinely train attention and memory inside the game: you will get measurably better at the café, the tray, and the rest, and the strategies (grouping, hooking a name to a face, saying the rule in words) are real techniques that carry into similar real tasks. What no honest page can promise is broad transfer: getting sharp at these games does not by itself make memory stronger everywhere, and research on “brain training” finds transfer is partial and task-shaped. Treat your scores as what they are — proof your attention is trainable, and a good time had training it — not as a certificate of a generally upgraded mind.

Tap to open — where these shapes come from

The tray game is Kim’s Game, described in Rudyard Kipling’s Kim (1901) and used since in scout, military, and observation training. Change-spotting pictures are a staple of observation exercises and “spot the difference” publishing. Face-name hooks are the standard advice of memory teachers. Nothing here is copied from any one source — the shapes are old, shared, and in the open, borrowed with thanks and expanded a little, which is how these things have always travelled.
