The Ruler's Test — Vladimir Putin, President of Russia

Subject: Vladimir Vladimirovich Putin — President of the Russian Federation (2000–2008; 2012–present), with the 2008–2012 premiership under President Dmitry Medvedev graded as part of the same continuous tenure, because executive control demonstrably remained with him through the "castling" period. The seat graded is the presidency, the office where executive power in Russia actually sits: command of the government, the security services, and the armed forces as commander-in-chief. He is graded on the full documented record of the tenure, with the record verified current through November 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Re-run: this dossier replaces the original Putin grading (FAILS / FAILS / MIXED / FAILS / FAILS) under the method as refined on 24 November 2026; the original file is kept, and this re-run states where the new parameters moved evidence between shelves. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Indictments are indictments, not convictions. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one. The Test grades the holding, never the platform. Carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Marks are lost only for HOW: a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). The no-good-choice clause: where the record shows a situation with no good choice — or none that appeared to be at the time — the dossier grades it as the best possible choice available in the moment and does not hold it against him; hindsight options do not count as options refused. What remains gradable is whether he built the trap himself earlier, whether he was honest about it while in it, and whether he took the least-harm door actually open. This dossier applies the clause explicitly where it was considered, including where it does not apply.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • In 2000 Putin publicly pledged "equidistance" of oligarchs from power, and the curb fell on politically active tycoons of more than one camp — media magnates Boris Berezovsky and Vladimir Gusinsky were driven into exile in 2000–2001, before the Yukos case (record summarized in U.S. Senate Resolution 65, 17 Feb 2011, and contemporary reporting).
  • The 2001 flat income tax of 13% and the 2000–2002 tax reforms applied generally across the economy and sharply raised compliance and state revenue (World Bank, A Decade of Declining Earnings Inequality in the Russian Federation, Policy Research Working Paper 7392).
  • The war's fiscal burden has likewise been raised by generally applicable law, not by bills aimed at named persons: the corporate profit tax rose to 25% in 2025 and VAT from 20% to 22% from 2026, applying across the economy (Moscow Times reporting, cited in CSS Eurasia research summary, November 2026).
  • The European Court of Human Rights, in its 2011 judgment on Khodorkovsky's first trial, found his arrest unlawful and his detention conditions degrading, but found insufficient evidence to conclude the prosecution was politically motivated (ECHR judgment 2011, as debated in UK Parliament Hansard, 23 Jul 2013) — a genuine limit on the strongest version of the selective-justice claim.

Evidence against:

  • The U.S. Senate formally resolved that the Khodorkovsky/Lebedev convictions were "a politically motivated case of selective arrest and prosecution that flagrantly undermines the rule of law," noting Yukos's assets were expropriated to a state company while Putin publicly called Khodorkovsky a "thief" who must "sit in jail" days before the 2010 verdict (U.S. Senate Resolution 65, 17 Feb 2011).
  • No comparably wealthy Putin loyalist has faced a Yukos-style prosecution; oligarchs who stayed out of politics (e.g., Roman Abramovich, Alisher Usmanov, Oleg Deripaska) retained their fortunes through the same tax practices era (widely documented; contrast drawn in U.S. State Department Human Rights Report 2005, cited in House Resolution 327, 23 Jun 2011).
  • The 15–17 March 2024 presidential election was held, per the European Parliament, "without any political competition, in a severely restricted environment of systemic and grave repression," with reports of ballot stuffing and falsified protocols; Russia declined to invite OSCE/ODIHR observers for the second consecutive national election, contrary to its OSCE commitments (European Parliament resolution, 25 Apr 2024; ODIHR/OSCE PA statement, 29 Jan 2024). Under the mandate rule, the platform he was elected on is not graded here; the environment in which the election itself was conducted is a HOW fact, and it is graded.
  • The March 2022 war-censorship laws (up to 15 years for "false information" about the armed forces) have been applied to critics of the war while pro-war ultranationalists publishing critical material avoided prosecution — i.e., the same law bends by viewpoint (Human Rights Watch, 7 Mar 2022; application pattern documented in the laws' enforcement record, 2022–2026).
  • The "foreign agents" framework, introduced 2012 and expanded in 2022 to require no foreign funding at all, is applied to independent media, NGOs and individuals critical of the Kremlin; no pro-government equivalent bears it (Committee to Protect Journalists, 6 Nov 2026).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the second law is not an episode but the operating pattern across the whole tenure: prosecution, censorship and "foreign agent" status fall by loyalty and viewpoint, while the generally applicable laws on the FOR shelf — the flat tax, the war tax rises — govern money, not power. The mandate rule does not move this grade: nothing on the against shelf is the platform he was elected on; all of it is HOW the holding is done.

Helped: Loyalist business figures who kept their fortunes by staying out of politics (Abramovich, Usmanov, Deripaska and their class); state companies that absorbed expropriated assets (Rosneft in the Yukos case); pro-government media and commentators, who publish under no equivalent legal exposure. Hurt: Mikhail Khodorkovsky and Platon Lebedev (imprisoned); Boris Berezovsky and Vladimir Gusinsky (exiled); independent media outlets, NGOs and individual journalists and activists designated "foreign agents" or prosecuted under the war-censorship laws; Russian voters in 2024, whose election was conducted without political competition.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS (domestic members clearly; external allies more mixed — see both shelves)

Evidence for:

  • Chechnya under Ramzan Kadyrov operates with extraordinary internal autonomy — its own security forces, social rules and patronage — inside the federation, the widest regional exception in Russia (widely documented; Kadyrov publicly positioned as a loyalist commander during the June 2023 Wagner crisis, AP/Army Times, 24 Jun 2023).
  • Formal allies have defied Moscow without military punishment: Kazakhstan refused to recognize the 2022 annexations and ruled out CSTO participation in Ukraine, and Armenia froze its CSTO participation in February 2024 and stopped paying dues — and neither was coerced back by force (EPIS Think Tank, Two Paths Away from Moscow, Sep 2026; Baku Research Institute, 2024).
  • Belarus's Alexander Lukashenko kept formal sovereignty for two decades, resisted full Union State absorption, and in June 2023 acted as broker — not subordinate — in ending the Wagner mutiny (RFE/RL, 26 Jun 2023).
  • Direct gubernatorial elections, abolished in 2004, were formally reinstated in 2012 (record in PolSci Dialogues, Vol. 2 No. 2, 2025).
  • One referee has kept a genuinely independent voice on the record through 2026: the Central Bank under Elvira Nabiullina publishes forecasts that contradict the government's optimism — on 23 October 2026 it held its key rate at 14%, raised its 2026 inflation forecast to 7.1–7.6% and put 2026 growth at 0.5–1.0% with a budget deficit of 3.2% of GDP — and on 12 November 2026 Nabiullina told the State Duma openly that productive capacity had been lost to attacks (Bank of Russia decisions and statements, Oct–Nov 2026; Interfax reporting, 12 Nov 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On 13 September 2004, days after the Beslan school siege, Putin announced the abolition of direct gubernatorial elections in all regions, replacing them with presidential appointment; the law was signed 12 December 2004, and the Federation Council had already been restructured (2000) to remove governors' automatic seats (Brookings, Governing Russia: Putin's Federal Dilemmas, 2005).
  • Regional autonomy was systematically harmonized away: regional laws and constitutions reviewed for compliance, asymmetric power-sharing treaties (e.g., Tatarstan's) eliminated from practice, and seven presidential "plenipotentiaries" layered over the regions from 2000 as the center's overseers (Brookings, 2005; CSS ETH Zurich, Russian Analytical Digest No. 43, 2008). The 2012 "restoration" of elections retained a Kremlin-controlled "municipal filter" on candidates.
  • Loyalty is personal, not institutional: Yevgeny Prigozhin, a favored insider for a decade, led the June 2023 mutiny after months of publicly pleading against the defense leadership; the rebellion's grievances were never adjudicated, charges were dropped under the Lukashenko deal on 27 June 2023, and Prigozhin died in a plane crash exactly two months later, on 23 August 2023 — Western officials alleged it was an assassination (ALLEGATION; the Kremlin denied involvement and attributed the crash to an onboard explosion under investigation) (Wikipedia record of the Wagner rebellion, citing AP; AP via Army Times, 24 Jun 2023).
  • Belarus's room to maneuver has narrowed sharply since 2020: post-election dependence on Moscow deepened into hosting Russian forces for the 2022 invasion launch and accepting deployment of Russian tactical nuclear weapons in 2023 (Reuters, Jun 2023; EPIS, Sep 2026).
  • The central bank's autonomy is real but held on a leash the record has already shown: Nabiullina reportedly tried to resign after the invasion and was prevented from doing so — the referee keeps her whistle because she is not permitted to leave the field (Bloomberg reporting, 2022, cited in subsequent analyses; cross-referenced in Test 3).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the grip is structural and documented at the source: elected regional power abolished by his own announcement in 2004, referees filtered before they may compete, and loyalty enforced personally up to the destruction of a favored insider who stepped out of line. The FOR shelf is genuine — allied states have defied him without invasion, Chechnya spins almost freely, and the central bank still publishes unwelcome numbers — but each exception runs on his sufferance, and sufferance is the grip wearing a looser glove. The mandate rule does not apply here: none of this is platform; all of it is HOW the seat is held.

Helped: Ramzan Kadyrov and the Chechen patronage network (autonomy in exchange for loyalty); the presidential administration's appointees in the regions; Lukashenko, whose regime's survival since 2020 has been underwritten by Moscow. Hurt: Russia's regional voters, who lost direct election of their governors in 2004 and received back only a filtered version; Tatarstan and the other republics whose negotiated autonomy was harmonized away; Yevgeny Prigozhin (dead, in circumstances Western officials allege were assassination — ALLEGATION, denied by the Kremlin); Belarusian sovereignty in substance, narrowed into hosting the 2022 invasion launch and Russian nuclear weapons.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades the other option: when another option became available, did he at least consider it openly? Considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • After the Kyiv offensive failed, Russia withdrew from northern Ukraine (April 2022) and Putin appointed the invasion's first overall theater commander, Gen. Alexander Dvornikov — an implicit admission the original structure had failed (ISW, Russian Offensive Campaign Assessment, 30 Apr 2023).
  • After Ukraine's Kharkiv counteroffensive (6–11 Sep 2022), Putin overcame his documented reluctance and ordered partial mobilization on 21 September 2022, and aims were quietly downsized from the maximalist opening plan to a Donbas-focused attrition war (ISW, 26 Feb 2023; Dara Massicot testimony, U.S. Senate Armed Services Committee, 28 Feb 2023).
  • In November 2022 Gen. Sergey Surovikin was permitted to execute an orderly withdrawal from Kherson's right bank rather than sacrifice the garrison — a real retreat, publicly announced (National Security Journal, 2025).
  • On the economy he kept and empowered technocrats: Central Bank Governor Elvira Nabiullina's capital controls and rate policy stabilized the ruble; Russia's GDP grew ~3.6% in 2023 and ~4.1–4.3% in 2024 after a ~1.2% contraction in 2022, beating early collapse forecasts (IMF figures, 2023–2024; SSDP analysis, 2024). In December 2024 Putin publicly acknowledged inflation and overheating as real problems (Bank of Russia reporting, Dec 2024), and the acknowledgment has continued on the record: the central bank's October–November 2026 statements openly forecast near-stagnation (0.5–1.0% growth in 2026), raised the inflation forecast to 7.1–7.6%, and named capacity lost to Ukrainian strikes on refineries as a cause — bad numbers, published by his own referees, not suppressed (Bank of Russia, 23 Oct 2026; Interfax, 12 Nov 2026).
  • The negotiating door has been used, not only brandished: at the trilateral US–Russia–Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi on 10–11 November 2026, the sides agreed in principle to a large-scale prisoner exchange (described by President Zelenskyy as 1,000 to 1,200 prisoners on each side), and US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Putin in Moscow on 12 November 2026 (RFE/RL reporting via OilPrice, 11 Nov 2026; Atlantic Council experts' assessment, 11 Nov 2026).
  • The other option, considered and declined with stated reasons — recorded here as NOT against-evidence under the method: on 19 November 2026, in Shenzhen, Putin publicly declined talks on a proposed limited ceasefire covering energy infrastructure and Black Sea shipping, stating his reason openly — Ukrainian strikes on the Crimean Bridge — and the refusal was announced in public, not buried (CBS News and Guardian reporting, 19 Nov 2026). The option became available; he looked at it; he declined it and said why. What the refusal cost is recorded in the ledger below and in Test 4; the considering itself is not a demerit.

Evidence against:

  • The feedback channels themselves were criminalized at the war's start: the 4 March 2022 laws Putin signed impose up to 15 years for reporting that contradicts the official line, forced independent media to close or flee, and banned calling the war a war (Human Rights Watch, 7 Mar 2022; UN experts' statement via OHCHR, 11–12 Mar 2022).
  • Facing the September 2022 Kharkiv rout, Putin doubled down politically even while adjusting militarily — declaring the annexation of four Ukrainian oblasts on 30 September 2022, including territory Russia did not control (National Security Journal, 2025).
  • The commanders most blamed for the failures, Shoigu and Gerasimov, were kept in place through the June 2023 mutiny that demanded their removal; Putin publicly backed Shoigu days after the mutiny rather than answer its stated grievance (AP, 26–27 Jun 2023). (Shoigu was eventually moved to the Security Council secretary post in May 2024.)
  • Nabiullina reportedly tried to resign after the invasion and was prevented from doing so — the system's adjustment capacity depends on experts it will not release (Bloomberg reporting, 2022, cited in subsequent analyses).
  • Putin has never publicly acknowledged that the February 2022 plan — designed in a small circle on assumptions Massicot's Senate testimony calls "faulty" (that Kyiv would fall in days and Ukrainians would not strongly resist) — was misconceived (U.S. Senate Armed Services Committee testimony, 28 Feb 2023).
  • The Abu Dhabi opening was narrowed by his own side within a day: after US envoys reported on 12 November 2026 that Putin had agreed to European participation in the next round, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov walked it back on 13 November, saying Moscow would decide only once it understood Europe's intentions, and Russia's negotiator Vladimir Medinsky stated that territorial exchanges were out of the question (Reuters reporting via contemporaneous summit coverage, 12–13 Nov 2026). The option of a wider table became available; the answer, after one day, was a refusal to look at it on the stated terms.
  • On the war's largest feedback of all — his own state's death registry — the system's answer through November 2026 was concealment: Russia publishes no count of its war dead (losses classified since 2015), and the scale became public only when leaked Rosstat records were analyzed by exiled Russian outlets (Meduza, Mediazona, The Insider, 23 Nov 2026; see Test 4). A wobble answered by classifying the instrument panel is recorded here as well as in Test 4.

The no-good-choice clause — considered and not applied: The clause was considered for the February 2022 invasion decision and for the war's continuation since. It does not apply. The record shows other options existed and were visible at the time — negotiations were running in February 2022, no invasion deadline bound him, and the Abu Dhabi and limited-ceasefire files in November 2026 show doors still opening — and the situation he now manages (sanctions, attrition, a strained war economy) is a situation whose origin the record traces to his own earlier choices. Under the clause's mechanics, a ruler who built the trap is graded on the building; the clause shields only the genuinely trapped. The Shenzhen limited-ceasefire refusal is therefore graded above as a considered decline, not under this clause.

Why: The grade lands on MIXED because the adjustment record is real and the openness record is not. Militarily and economically he has repeatedly absorbed failure and changed course — withdrawals executed and announced, mobilization ordered against his own reluctance, technocrats empowered and their bad numbers published, a prisoner-exchange track worked in November 2026. But the adjustment runs through channels he keeps criminalized: the press that would report the wobble faces 15 years, the founding error of the war has never been owned, the wider negotiating table was refused within a day, and the state's own death count stayed classified until it leaked. Under the new parameters, the Shenzhen refusal moved off the against shelf — declining an option with stated reasons is his right under the ethic; the grade holds at MIXED on the refusal-to-look items that remain.

Helped: Russian soldiers of the Kherson garrison, withdrawn in order in November 2022 rather than sacrificed; prisoners of war on both sides covered by the November 2026 Abu Dhabi exchange agreement in principle; Russian households and businesses served by the ruble's stabilization under Nabiullina's policy in 2022–2024. Hurt: Independent Russian journalists and media outlets (closed, exiled, or prosecuted under the March 2022 laws); Ukrainian civilians facing another winter of grid strikes after the limited energy ceasefire was declined at Shenzhen on 19 November 2026; European governments, kept outside the negotiating room by the 13 November 2026 walk-back; the Russian public, whose state's own registry of its war dead reached it only through a leak.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does his accounting include the whole flock — minorities, opponents, neighboring peoples, his own soldiers — or are some counted out of the family?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The first decade delivered broadly shared gains: poverty fell from ~30% of the population in 2000 to ~11% by 2012–2014, real disposable income rose ~160% (2000–2012), and GDP per capita nearly doubled — gains that reached pensioners and poor regions, not only Moscow (World Bank Working Paper 7392; World Bank data via European Parliament Research Service briefing, Apr 2022).
  • War spending now channels large sums to poor regions: regions with the highest per-capita military deaths (Tuva, Buryatia, Altai, Chechnya, Adygea) also saw household deposits grow 30–50%, reflecting contract bonuses and death payments to soldiers' families (Bank of Finland Institute for Emerging Economies analysis, reported by Re:Russia, 2023–2024) — the state does pay, heavily, for the flock it sends.
  • Russia's official self-description under Putin is multi-ethnic and multi-confessional, and ethnic republics retain formal statehood, languages and (in Chechnya's case) wide internal latitude (see Test 2, evidence for).
  • The November 2026 Abu Dhabi agreement in principle on a large prisoner exchange — 1,000 to 1,200 prisoners on each side, per President Zelenskyy — counts captured soldiers of both armies as people to be brought home, and his government agreed to it (RFE/RL reporting, 11 Nov 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The war's death burden is radically unequal: BBC Russian Service/Mediazona name-verified data show confirmed deaths per 10,000 working-age men highest in Tuva (48.6) and Buryatia (36.7) versus Moscow (1) and St. Petersburg (2.5) — a 15-to-25-fold gap between the poorest minority republics and the capitals (Bessudnov, Demographic Research Vol. 48, 2023, using BBC/Mediazona data).
  • The count itself has now been documented at a scale the original grading could only approach through the named list. As of 6 November 2026 the BBC Russian/Mediazona volunteer project had verified 285,881 named Russian military dead. On 23 November 2026, Meduza, Mediazona and The Insider published analyses of leaked Rosstat death records — which the outlets state they authenticated — putting registered Russian military deaths at roughly 472,000 to 499,000 through late September 2026, and a modeled total above 700,000 once the missing are included (Meduza/Mediazona and The Insider analyses, 23 Nov 2026; Le Monde, 24 Nov 2026). The figures are contested in method and the Kremlin has published no count of its own to set against them; what is not contested is that the state's official accounting to the families remains zero published names, the losses having been classified by decree since 2015 (Mediazona/BBC, 2022–2026).
  • Crimean Tatars, Crimea's indigenous people, have been counted out since 2014: their representative body, the Mejlis, was banned as "extremist" on 26 April 2016, Tatar media were closed, and members face arbitrary searches, arrests and prosecution — documented as "increasing persecution" by the UN human rights office (OHCHR press briefing, 17 May 2016; UN General Assembly draft resolution A/C.3/72/L.42, 31 Oct 2017).
  • On 17 March 2023 the International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for Putin — an INDICTMENT, not a conviction — finding reasonable grounds to believe he bears responsibility for the war crime of unlawful deportation and transfer of Ukrainian children to Russia (ICC press release, 17 Mar 2023; the Kremlin rejects the ICC's jurisdiction and denies wrongdoing).
  • Alexei Navalny, the leading domestic opponent, was imprisoned on charges international observers called politically motivated and died in an Arctic penal colony on 16 February 2024. Russian authorities said natural causes (arrhythmia). In February 2026 the UK, France, Germany, Sweden and the Netherlands stated laboratory analysis "conclusively confirmed" the toxin epibatidine in samples from his body and held Russia responsible (ALLEGATION by five governments; the Kremlin "resolutely rejects" it). U.S. intelligence agencies assessed Putin likely did not personally order the death (joint statement of five European governments, Feb 2026, via Reuters/AP; U.S. intelligence assessment reported 2024).
  • The war economy's civilian costs are now counted in the state's own statistics: in January–September 2026, 208,500 organizations ceased operating in Russia against 109,000 newly registered, a near two-to-one closure ratio analysts attribute to rising costs and the 14% key rate; gasoline prices rose 21% and diesel 13.4% in 2026 as Ukrainian strikes disabled refineries, and utility tariffs rose ~13.4% (Rosstat data via Moscow Times/Izvestia reporting, November 2026; Bank of Russia statement, Oct 2026).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the counting-out is categorical, not incidental: an indigenous people banned as a body, the leading opponent dead in custody, a neighboring people's children the subject of an ICC indictment, and his own soldiers dying at rates 15 to 25 times higher in the poor minority republics than in the capitals — while the state publishes no count of them at all, a silence broken only when its own registry leaked in November 2026. The FOR shelf is real — a decade of broadly shared rising incomes, heavy payments to soldiers' families, a prisoner exchange agreed — but payments to the families of the counted-out do not count them back in. Under the mandate rule, the war's continuation is his platform and is not graded here; the distribution of its deaths, and who is missing from the count, is HOW — and it is graded.

Helped: Pensioners and poor households of the 2000s, whose incomes rose broadly; soldiers' families in Tuva, Buryatia, Altai, Chechnya and Adygea receiving contract bonuses and death payments that raised regional deposits 30–50%; prisoners covered by the November 2026 exchange agreement in principle. Hurt: The war dead of Tuva, Buryatia and the other poor republics, borne at 15-to-25 times the capitals' rate, and their families — at least 285,881 named dead by 6 November 2026, with registered deaths documented at roughly 472,000–499,000; Crimean Tatars, whose representative body stands banned; Ukrainian children deported to Russia (ICC indictment, contested by the Kremlin); Alexei Navalny (dead in custody, responsibility alleged by five governments and denied by the Kremlin); Ukrainian civilians under grid strikes through the winter of 2026–2027; Russian small businesses, closing at nearly twice the rate they open in 2026.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Track record of agreements and statements: promises made vs. kept. Under the mandate rule, carrying out the platform he was elected on is FOR-shelf evidence here; the Test grades the keeping, and the hurt any kept promise did is recorded in the ledger, not erased by the credit.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The platform, carried out — mandate-rule credit. Putin has repeatedly run on continuity, sovereignty and the prosecution of the war's stated aims, and he has governed on exactly that: the war continued on his stated terms, the annexed territories are administered as Russian, and the social-payment machinery of the war economy (contract bonuses, death payments, pensions) has been funded year after year, including through the generally applicable tax rises of 2025–2026 (see Test 1). Whatever the content is graded to be elsewhere in this dossier, on this axis the record is that he did what he said he would do.
  • January 2022: when Kazakhstan's President Tokayev invoked CSTO Article 4 during unrest, the Russian-led force deployed within days, guarded infrastructure, and withdrew on schedule by 19 January 2022 — an alliance commitment answered and then exited as promised (EPIS Think Tank, Sep 2026; Diplomatic Courier, 2023).
  • On Minsk, Moscow's stated case is not empty: Ukraine never implemented Minsk II's political provisions (special status, elections law), and former German Chancellor Angela Merkel said in December 2022 that Minsk had been "an attempt to give Ukraine time" — a remark Putin cites as proof the other side negotiated in bad faith (Die Zeit interview, Dec 2022; Russian government statements, 2022).
  • In the 2000s Russia ran budget surpluses every year 2000–2007 and paid down state debt ahead of schedule, meeting its financial obligations to creditors early (World Bank-cited fiscal record, Economy of Russia data, 2000–2008).
  • New START, the final chapter: after suspending participation in February 2023 (against shelf), Putin in September 2025 offered to keep observing the treaty's central limits voluntarily for one year past its expiry if the United States did the same. The United States did not accept, preferring a new agreement including China; the treaty expired on 5 February 2026, the first time since 1972 that no binding limits constrain the two largest arsenals (contemporaneous reporting, Sep 2025–Feb 2026; The Munich Eye, 6 Feb 2026). On 19 November 2026 at Shenzhen, Putin joined Presidents Trump and Xi in a joint declaration that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought, pledging dialogue on nuclear risk reduction — a declaration with no numbers, timetable or verification (Shenzhen trilateral declaration, 19 Nov 2026). The offer to keep the limits was his; the lapse is shared, and this shelf records both halves.
  • The 1994 Budapest Memorandum was signed by Boris Yeltsin, not Putin personally; Putin's stated position (4 Mar 2014) is that a revolution had created a "new state" in Ukraine toward which Russia had made no commitments — his argument, on the record, for why the obligation lapsed.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Whatever its signatory, the Budapest Memorandum was the Russian state's pledge to respect Ukraine's existing borders and refrain from force; the US, UK, Canada, France, Germany, Italy and Japan formally stated the 2014 annexation of Crimea breached it, and the 2022 full-scale invasion breached it again (Harvard Belfer Center, Budapest Memorandum at 25, 2019; U.S. House Resolution 758, 4 Dec 2014).
  • From November 2021 to 20 February 2022, Russian officials "repeatedly denied that Russia had plans to invade Ukraine" — Lavrov said Russia was "not going to start a war" — while the invasion force assembled; the full-scale invasion began 24 February 2022 (Prelude to the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine, documentary record; House of Commons Library Research Briefing, 1 May 2025).
  • In his 24 February 2022 address Putin stated Russia had no plans to occupy Ukrainian territory; on 30 September 2022 he signed the annexation of four Ukrainian oblasts (Kremlin address, 24 Feb 2022; annexation record, Sep 2022).
  • On 22 February 2022 Putin declared the Minsk agreements — which Russia had signed and spent seven years demanding Ukraine implement — no longer existed, ending them by announcement the day he recognized the DNR/LNR (House of Commons Library Research Briefing, 1 May 2025).
  • Term limits: the 1993 constitution barred more than two consecutive presidential terms. Putin observed its letter in 2008 by moving to the premiership while retaining executive control through the Medvedev presidency (2008–2012 "castling"), then in 2020 the Tereshkova amendment "zeroed" his prior terms — approved by a compliant Constitutional Court within days — making him eligible to remain president until 2036 (Verfassungsblog, 12 Mar 2020; term-limits record, 2020 amendments to Article 81).
  • In February 2023 Putin suspended Russia's participation in New START, the last remaining U.S.–Russia nuclear arms-control treaty, which Russia had ratified and observed since 2011 (Reuters, 21 Feb 2023). The September 2025 offer to observe its limits voluntarily (FOR shelf) mitigates the ending; it does not un-suspend 2023.
  • The alliance-keeping shelf has a hole in it: Armenia, a CSTO member, invoked Article 4 after Azerbaijani strikes on its own territory in September 2022 and received only a monitoring mission; it froze its CSTO participation in February 2024, citing the broken guarantee (EPIS Think Tank, Sep 2026).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the broken words are the load-bearing ones: a state's border pledge set aside by a legal theory invented for the occasion, televised denials of an invasion being assembled, "no plans to occupy" followed within seven months by annexation decrees, and a constitutional term limit zeroed out by amendment so the same man could stay. The new parameters genuinely strengthened the FOR shelf — the mandate rule credits him with governing on the platform he ran on, and the New START extension offer was a real offer the other side declined — and the grade still does not move, because the mandate rule credits the keeping of a platform; it does not launder the breaking of specific pledges, and the specific pledges broken here were made to named counterparties on dated occasions. Word-keeping and outcome are graded on separate axes in the Summary; neither is permitted to soften the other.

Helped: Kazakhstan's government in January 2022, whose Article 4 call was answered and exited on schedule; Russia's creditors in the 2000s, paid early; his own electorate on the narrow axis of predictability — voters who supported the war's continuation got the war's continuation; arms-control constituencies, briefly, by the September 2025 offer to keep New START's limits. Hurt: Ukraine, whose borders were pledged in 1994 and crossed in 2014 and 2022; Ukrainians who heard "no plans to occupy" in February 2022 and annexation in September; Armenia, whose Article 4 call in September 2022 was answered with observers; Russian citizens whose constitution's term limit was zeroed in 2020; both nuclear arsenals' neighbors — the whole world, on the risk axis — by the 2023 suspension that began New START's end.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Test 1 — One law: FAILS. Why: the law bends by loyalty and viewpoint — selective prosecution at the top (Khodorkovsky), censorship and "foreign agent" law applied by position on the war, an election conducted without competition — while the laws that do apply to everyone govern money, not power.
