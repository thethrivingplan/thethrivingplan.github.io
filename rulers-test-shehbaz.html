The Ruler's Test — Shehbaz Sharif, Prime Minister of Pakistan

Subject: Muhammad Shehbaz Sharif — Prime Minister of Pakistan since 4 March 2024; previously Prime Minister from April 2022 to August 2023, and three times Chief Minister of Punjab. He leads a PML-N–PPP coalition government formed after the general election of 8 February 2024. He remains in office at grading: the Prime Minister's Office was issuing statements in his name on 24 November 2026, and on 19 November 2026 he addressed the American Business Council in Islamabad as prime minister. His elder brother, Nawaz Sharif, leads the PML-N. Pakistan's civil-military record is the standing context of this file: the army chief, Asim Munir, was promoted to Field Marshal in 2025 and, under the 27th Constitutional Amendment of November 2025, also holds the new office of Chief of Defence Forces. The tests below grade the seat's own documented acts within that context — including the acts by which the seat enlarged, or declined to enlarge, its civilian counterparts' room to move. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts ruled against the prosecution of his principal opponent, repeatedly, while Shehbaz held office. Imran Khan's cipher conviction (10 years, January 2024) was overturned and he was acquitted by the Islamabad High Court on 3 June 2024; his iddat conviction (7 years, February 2024) was annulled on 13 July 2024; the sentence in the January 2024 gifts reference (14 years) was suspended by the IHC in April 2024; and he was granted bail in the later gifts case in November 2024. Each of these orders issued from courts of the state Shehbaz governs (Islamabad High Court and district judiciary records, 2024).
  • The accountability-law amendments his first government enacted — whatever their origin (see against) — were in the end general in application and were upheld as legislation parliament was entitled to pass: restoring them on 6 September 2024, a five-member Supreme Court bench under Chief Justice Qazi Faez Isa held unanimously that the petitioner had "failed to establish that the amendments were unconstitutional" and that judges "cannot be the gatekeepers of Parliament." Khan himself then sought acquittal in the £190 million case under those same amendments, and his party's chairman argued publicly that two of Khan's cases "should be over" because of them (Supreme Court judgment, 6 September 2024; contemporaneous reporting).
  • The flagship privatization of his term was conducted as an open, televised auction with competing bids: on 23 December 2025 a consortium led by Arif Habib Corporation won 75% of Pakistan International Airlines with a bid of Rs135 billion against a Rs100 billion reserve price, outbidding a Lucky Cement–led consortium in a live second round (Privatisation Commission and APP reporting, 23 December 2025). The outcome of that auction is contested on other grounds (Tests 2 and 5); the process itself was public and competitive.
  • The corruption cases that keep Khan in prison were decided by courts, in trials whose appeals are moving through the ordinary appellate courts: convicted in the £190 million (Al-Qadir Trust) case on 17 January 2025 (14 years; his wife Bushra Bibi, 7 years), his appeals were being heard by the Islamabad High Court in November 2026, which on a November 2026 hearing date ordered jail authorities to give him at least an hour with his lead counsel before the next hearing (accountability court judgment, 17 January 2025; IHC proceedings reported November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • His own government's accountability law was rewritten around its own defendants. The amendments to the National Accountability Ordinance enacted by his PML-N–led PDM government in 2022–23 restricted NAB to cases of Rs500 million or more, exempted federal cabinet decisions from investigation, and removed regulatory bodies from its domain; the BBC-cited estimate at the time was that cases involving roughly Rs1.2 trillion left the bureau's purview, and among the cases closed or returned were corruption references against Shehbaz himself, his brother Nawaz Sharif, and President Asif Ali Zardari. A Supreme Court bench struck the amendments down on 15 September 2023 (2–1) as unconstitutional; his government appealed, and after their restoration in September 2024 Shehbaz and his son Hamza themselves applied for the Ramzan Sugar Mills reference against them to be transferred out of the accountability court under the amended law (Supreme Court judgments of 15 September 2023 and 6 September 2024; accountability-court proceedings, September–October 2024). The law was general in form; it was written, defended, and first used by the people it relieved.
  • The 27th Constitutional Amendment, passed by his coalition in November 2025, wrote named categories of office-holder out of the criminal law's reach: lifetime immunity from criminal proceedings and arrest for the President and for five-star officers (Field Marshal, Marshal of the Air Force, Admiral of the Fleet), and immunity for the prime minister during tenure. UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk recorded that the amendment "also establishes lifetime immunity from criminal proceedings and arrest for the President, Field Marshall, Marshall of the Air Force and Admiral of the Fleet," alongside its judicial changes (OHCHR statement, November 2025). A law that exempts the seat's own coalition president and the army chief the seat promoted is not one law for all.
  • The election that seated his government carries a documented administrative record, not merely an opponent's complaint. The Commonwealth Observer Group's final report (released in autumn 2025, more than 18 months after the poll) found that voters' "fundamental political rights" were restricted, that the election-day shutdown of mobile services "reduced the transparency of the process," and that it had surveyed documentation appearing to show that "in some cases, copies of Forms 45 held by party agents differed from those used in the tabulation of results that later appeared on the ECP website, with vote totals and turnout figures altered." The nonpartisan Free and Fair Election Network (FAFEN) documented violations at more than two-thirds of polling sites of a kind that could have enabled outcome changes. The PTI's claim that its mandate was stolen is an ALLEGATION the party supports with its own Form 45 collection; the discrepancies themselves are documented by observers. The Election Commission and the government maintain the results were lawful and that PTI lost its election symbol through its own failure to hold lawful intra-party elections (Commonwealth Observer Group final report, 2025; FAFEN reporting, February 2024; ECP statements, including its public rebuttal of a retired judge's criticism, 4 November 2026).
  • The composition of parliament was then changed by review litigation his coalition brought. On 12 July 2024 a Supreme Court majority (8–5) held PTI eligible for the reserved seats for women and minorities. His coalition parties and the Election Commission petitioned for review; on 27 June 2025 the Constitutional Bench created by his 26th Amendment overturned that judgment 7–3, and roughly 77 reserved seats were redistributed to the ruling coalition — restoring the two-thirds majority in the National Assembly that constitutional amendments, including the 27th, require (Supreme Court orders of 12 July 2024 and 27 June 2025; International IDEA democracy tracker, June 2025).
  • The prosecutions of his opponent show a documented pattern of conviction followed by appellate collapse for the cases brought before this term, and fresh convictions within it: of the four convictions obtained against Khan in the weeks before the February 2024 election, three had been overturned, annulled, or suspended on appeal by mid-2024 (above). Within this term, Khan and Bushra Bibi were convicted in the £190 million case (17 January 2025) and in a further gifts case concerning a Bulgari jewellery set received from the Saudi crown prince in 2021 (17 years each, 20 December 2025, by a special court sitting in Adiala Jail). PTI's position that the cases are politically motivated is an ALLEGATION as to motive; the appellate record of the earlier cases is fact, and the trials' venue — inside the jail where the accused is held — is documented.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, media, the Election Commission, provincial governments, and the military's civilian counterparts keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Individual courts continued to rule against the state in the highest-profile case in the country (Test 1): acquittals and bails for Khan in 2024, a Supreme Court order in February 2026 — after a report placed before it recorded Khan saying his right eye was working at only 15 per cent — directing that he be given access to his personal physicians and telephone calls with his sons, and an IHC order in November 2026 compelling jail authorities to admit his counsel (court records, 2024–2026; counsel's account of the couple's confinement conditions, reported November 2026, is the defence's account and is labeled as such).
  • The one province governed by his opponents was never taken from them. Khyber Pakhtunkhwa has been governed by PTI continuously through his term, through open confrontation with the centre; no governor's rule was imposed, and the province's chief ministership changed in October 2025 by PTI's own internal decision, not by federal intervention (provincial record, 2024–2026).
  • Critical private media continued to publish throughout his term, including the Dawn editorials criticizing his flagship privatization and the televised and print coverage of every controversy in this dossier. The social platform X, blocked in Pakistan from February 2024, was restored in May 2025 during the India conflict and remained accessible at grading (see against for the block itself).

Evidence against:

  • The judiciary was restructured twice in thirteen months by his coalition. The 26th Amendment (20–21 October 2024) ended the seniority rule for Chief Justice, gave a parliamentary committee the pick from among the three senior-most judges, reconstituted the Judicial Commission of Pakistan so that judges no longer hold a majority, removed the Supreme Court's suo motu power, and created constitutional benches chosen under the new commission. The UN Special Rapporteur on the independence of judges had urged review before passage (letter of 14 October 2024); High Commissioner Türk said the amendments were "adopted hastily, without broad consultation and debate" and would "seriously undermine the independence of the judiciary" (OHCHR, 22 October 2024). The 27th Amendment (signed 13 November 2025) then removed constitutional jurisdiction from the Supreme Court altogether and vested it in a new Federal Constitutional Court whose first chief justice and first judges were, in Türk's words, "already assigned by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister." Within hours of its signing, the senior puisne judge, Justice Syed Mansoor Ali Shah, and Justice Athar Minallah resigned from the Supreme Court; Shah's thirteen-page letter called the amendment "a grave assault on the Constitution of Pakistan" that "dismantles the Supreme Court of Pakistan, subjugates the judiciary to executive control," and Minallah wrote that the Constitution he had sworn to uphold "is no more" (resignation letters, 13 November 2025). The government's stated rationale — that a constitutional court fulfilled the 2006 Charter of Democracy, would free ordinary courts from constitutional cases consuming judicial time, and would curb a court that had itself overreached — is on the record and is noted by its critics as well (Law Minister Azam Nazeer Tarar, Senate, 8 November 2025). In November 2026 the International Commission of Jurists published its assessment of the two amendments together: they "have led to systemic judicial capture by other branches of the State," leaving the higher judiciary without "the institutional guarantees of independence required under international law" (ICJ, Judicial Capture, November 2026).
  • The restructured bench's first great institutional act was to restore military trials of civilians. A five-member Supreme Court bench had held such trials unconstitutional on 23 October 2023. His government appealed; on 7 May 2025 the Constitutional Bench formed under the 26th Amendment allowed the appeals 5–2, restored the Army Act provisions, and military courts' convictions of 105 civilians for the 9 May 2023 riots (sentences of two to ten years, pronounced December 2024) stood. The majority directed parliament to legislate an independent right of appeal to the high courts within 45 days (detailed judgment, 22 September 2025). Amnesty International, marking the ruling's first anniversary in May 2026, described the injustice of civilian military trials as "ongoing" and said the 2025 decision "has fundamentally undermined the right to a fair trial and the right to liberty in Pakistan" (Supreme Court judgments of 23 October 2023, 7 May 2025 and 22 September 2025; Amnesty International, 7 May 2026).
  • Speech law was tightened against the documented opposition of the profession it most affects. The PECA (Amendment) Act of January 2025 created Section 26A — up to three years' imprisonment and a Rs2 million fine for intentionally disseminating information the speaker "knows or has reason to believe to be false" and likely to cause "fear, panic or disorder" — and replaced the FIA's cybercrime wing with a new National Cyber Crime Investigation Agency. It was passed by the National Assembly on 23 January 2025 and the Senate on 28 January, amid a walkout by journalists from the press gallery and nationwide protests by the Pakistan Federal Union of Journalists; the Senate opposition leader said on the floor that the bill's purpose was "to target a specific political party," and the government's reply was that the law targeted social media, not journalists, and "can be improved" (parliamentary record and PPF reporting, January 2025). Cases followed: journalist Waheed Murad was picked up by unidentified men on 28 March 2025 and then charged under PECA (Global Voices/Advox documentation, 2025). The platform X remained blocked from February 2024 until May 2025, and mobile internet was shut down nationwide on election day, 8 February 2024 (Commonwealth Observer Group report, 2025).
  • The military's place inside civilian government was formalized, not merely tolerated. The Special Investment Facilitation Council — created under his first government in June 2023, with the army chief on its apex committee and an army "national coordinator" — was given statutory powers by amendments to the Board of Investment Act under which it may "summon" and "direct" regulatory institutions and recommend project-specific relaxations of the regulatory framework; the Economic Advisory Group warned that this conflicts with the council's own members' commercial interests and erodes contracting institutions, and the Financial Times reported military officials steering projects from canals to energy contracts (EAG statement; FT reporting, January 2025). The 27th Amendment then constitutionalized the command side: the office of Chief of Defence Forces, held by the army chief concurrently, a National Strategic Command whose head the prime minister appoints "on the recommendation" of the army chief, and lifetime rank, privileges and immunity for five-star officers. Shehbaz's own description of the arrangement, on 19 November 2026, was that stabilization rested on "an unprecedented partnership" between the political leadership and the military, crediting "Chief of Defence Forces Field Marshal Asim Munir" by name (address to the American Business Council, 19 November 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the IMF, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • On the gravest external shock of his term, the adjustment was fast and it held. After the 22 April 2025 Pahalgam attack killed 26 civilians, his government publicly offered to join a "neutral, transparent" international investigation — an offer India rejected. India struck nine sites in Pakistan and Pakistan-administered Kashmir on 7 May (Operation Sindoor); Pakistan retaliated, including its 10 May Operation Bunyan-un-Marsoos against Indian air bases; and on 10 May his government accepted the ceasefire announced that day, which has held since. Both governments claim victory; the claims of aircraft losses are each side's own — Pakistan claims five or more Indian jets downed, India acknowledges losses without confirming the number — and are recorded here as claims, not findings (International Crisis Group, Asia Briefing N°185, 17 September 2025; contemporaneous reporting).
  • On the economy, he absorbed the IMF's conditions rather than denying the crisis, across four successive reviews: the 37-month, $7 billion Extended Fund Facility approved in September 2024 reached staff-level agreement on its fourth review on 7 October 2026, with the Fund recording that "the authorities have successfully navigated the impact of the Middle East conflict, and strong policies have helped preserve macroeconomic stability" (IMF Press Release 26/326, 7 October 2026). When the Fund objected to his government's 2026 fuel-relief scheme (Rs75 billion, announced as a three-month measure) as broad and costly, the government accepted at that same review that the scheme should be phased out promptly — the adjustment is written into the staff-level agreement (IMF PR 26/326 and Express Tribune reporting, October 2026).
  • Individual judicial corrections of his government's cases were complied with, not defied: Khan's 2024 acquittals and bails took effect, the February 2026 Supreme Court directions on Khan's medical access and family calls were implemented (he was produced at the Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences on 20 August 2026 and declared medically fit), and the November 2026 IHC counsel-access order was obeyed (court records and reporting, 2024–2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the judiciary, every pushback was answered by going further. Bar associations, the UN Special Rapporteur, and the UN High Commissioner objected to the 26th Amendment in October 2024; the government's answer, thirteen months later, was the 27th — which removed constitutional jurisdiction from the Supreme Court entirely. When two of the court's senior judges resigned in protest on 13 November 2025, no review, pause, or consultation followed; and when the International Commission of Jurists in November 2026 called for the two amendments to be reviewed "with wide public consultation," the record before this dossier shows no such review undertaken (OHCHR statements, October 2024 and November 2025; ICJ, November 2026).
  • On the November 2024 protest, the answer was denial without inquiry. PTI's "Final Call" march reached Islamabad on 24–26 November 2024 demanding Khan's release and the reversal of the 26th Amendment; the army was deployed in the capital with shoot-on-sight orders reported for the protection of installations, and a night operation on 26–27 November cleared D-Chowk. The death toll is CONTESTED and unresolved: PTI states 12–20 of its workers were killed; the government states that no protester was killed by security-force fire and that five to six security personnel were killed by protesters; independent compilations put total deaths at roughly 10–18 including security personnel. Hospital sources speaking anonymously to The Guardian reported that records of the dead and injured were confiscated, and Al Jazeera published names of deceased protesters provided by their families; the interior minister dismissed the fatality claims as "propaganda" and challenged PTI to produce names. Some 4,185 people were arrested. No independent inquiry was conceded (Amnesty International and HRCP statements, November 2024; Guardian and Al Jazeera reporting, November–December 2024; compiled casualty records).
  • On the election itself, no adjustment was offered at all: against FAFEN's documentation and the Commonwealth Observer Group's findings (Test 1), the government's position throughout has been that the February 2024 election was free and fair and that its disputes belong to election tribunals — tribunals whose slow pace of decision has itself been a standing documented criticism by the HRCP and the legal community through 2025–26.
  • On Balochistan, the wobble was answered with the prosecutor. The Baloch Yakjehti Committee's demands — produce the disappeared, account for the dead — were pressed through 2024–25, including after the 11 March 2025 Jaffar Express hijacking by the Balochistan Liberation Army (an attack BYC had no documented part in). On 21 March 2025 police fired on protesters in Quetta demanding the release of a detained BYC member, killing three; on 22 March BYC's leader, Dr. Mahrang Baloch, was arrested at the sit-in that followed. The state's director-general of military public relations publicly called her a terrorist proxy in May 2025 — a characterization BYC rejects and which is recorded here as the state's position, not a finding. She was convicted on 22 June 2026 (see Test 4). No commission of inquiry into the disappearances themselves, of the kind BYC and the PTM demand, was conceded.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include PTI voters, Baloch and Pashtun citizens, Afghan refugees, the poor, religious minorities — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The social floor was raised, on the IMF's own record: health and education spending rose from 2.2 per cent of GDP in FY24 to 2.5 per cent in FY26, with a commitment to 2.8 per cent in FY27, and the Fund's October 2026 statement lists "prioritizing social spending" among the program's results; the government also reports Rs27 billion disbursed in direct digital cash transfers in 2026 and the dissolution of the Public Works Department in favor of direct transfers (IMF PR 26/326, 7 October 2026; prime minister's address, 19 November 2026).
  • Within the refugee operation (see against), a documented line was held in practice: registered Proof of Registration cardholders accounted for only about 1 per cent of arrests and 1 per cent of deportations in UNHCR/IOM's November 2026 reporting, most PoR returns being processed through UNHCR's Voluntary Repatriation Centres — the registered were, in the main, not the ones seized (UNHCR/IOM joint updates, October–November 2026).
  • His opponents' voters kept their province: PTI's Khyber Pakhtunkhwa government, elected on the same February 2024 vote, has governed throughout (Test 2), and PTI sits in the National Assembly as the principal opposition bloc.

Evidence against:

  • PTI's voters are the largest single counted-out group. PTI-backed independents won the largest bloc of National Assembly seats in February 2024 while their party was denied its election symbol and its leader sat in prison; their reserved seats were transferred to the governing coalition in June 2025 (Test 1); their November 2024 march on the capital was dispersed in the night operation described in Test 3, with the death toll still officially unresolved; and their leader remains in Adiala Jail under the £190 million and December 2025 gifts convictions, with his appeals pending and his lawyers reporting — as the defence's account, contested by the authorities — near-24-hour solitary confinement (records cited in Tests 1–3; IHC proceedings, November 2026).
  • Baloch citizens are counted by a commission that traces people and prosecutes no one. The Commission of Inquiry on Enforced Disappearances had received 10,806 cases by February 2026 — 2,924 of them from Balochistan — and had "disposed of" 9,280, recording 4,875 persons returned home and 308 found dead; the UN Committee Against Torture observed in June 2026 that despite more than 9,000 disposals, "not a single official was ordered to be prosecuted" (COIED monthly report, February 2026, as compiled in OHCHR submissions; UN Committee Against Torture, June 2026). The BYC's own documentation — an advocacy source, labeled as such — records 752 new disappearances in Balochistan in the first half of 2025 alone. The movement's leader, Dr. Mahrang Baloch, a physician named to TIME100 Next and BBC 100 Women in 2024, was sentenced on 22 June 2026 by an Anti-Terrorism Court in Quetta to two terms of life imprisonment over the killing of a Frontier Corps soldier at the July 2024 Gwadar gathering; Front Line Defenders and the Human Rights Foundation document that her hearings were held inside prison with her participation by video link and characterize the charges as politically motivated — characterizations the state disputes, maintaining that BYC is a proxy of armed groups (Front Line Defenders case record, June 2026; Edelstam Foundation, 2026; ISPR statements, May 2025).
  • Pashtun civic organization was proscribed outright. On 6 October 2024 the federal government listed the Pashtun Tahafuz Movement — a movement whose stated demands are the production of missing persons before courts, a truth commission on extrajudicial killings, and mine clearance — as a proscribed organization under the Anti-Terrorism Act, days before its planned national jirga; Amnesty International and the HRCP called for the ban's reversal, citing PTM's record of peaceful advocacy. The Peshawar High Court dismissed the challenge to the ban in early 2026, directing the movement to the government's own review committee. Amnesty urgent actions of April and November 2026 document PTM activists forcibly disappeared — two taken after leaving a government-organized convening in Peshawar on 12 November 2025, another missing since 18 May 2026 (Interior Ministry notification, 6 October 2024; PHC proceedings, 2026; Amnesty International Urgent Actions 44/26 and 116/26).
  • Afghan refugees were moved from tolerated presence to mass removal under his government. The Illegal Foreigners Repatriation Plan (announced under the 2023 caretaker government) was extended by his government to Afghan Citizen Card holders from 1 April 2025 and to Proof of Registration holders after their cards expired on 30 June 2025. UNHCR and IOM count 2,758,958 returns from September 2023 to 7 November 2026, of which roughly 279,000 were deportations; 86,644 Afghans were arrested or detained between January and mid-October 2026 alone; and among undocumented and ACC-holding returnees surveyed, 95 per cent cited fear of arrest as a reason for leaving. UNHCR has urged Pakistan to exempt Afghans with international protection needs from involuntary return. The government's position — that it is repatriating illegally resident foreigners who impose security and economic burdens — is on the record and is the stated basis of the policy (UNHCR/IOM joint updates, October–November 2026).
  • The poor paid for the stabilization, on figures his own government published. The World Bank's Poverty & Equity Brief (October 2026) records the national poverty rate rising from 22 per cent in 2018-19 to 29 per cent in 2024-25 — "the sharpest reversal since the 1990s" — with real consumption falling for the bottom 70 per cent of households and the Gini index reaching 32.7, its highest in over 25 years. His planning minister, Ahsan Iqbal, released the survey and conceded that stabilization policies under the IMF program — subsidy withdrawal, devaluation, energy-price adjustment — contributed to the increase (World Bank, October 2026; Planning Ministry release reported February 2026).
  • Religious minorities remained exposed to the mob and the blasphemy file: HRCP's annual reporting through his term documents continuing mob violence on blasphemy allegations — including the May–June 2024 Sargodha case, in which a Christian man beaten by a mob died of his injuries — with arrests of alleged attackers following the violence rather than protection preceding it (HRCP reporting, 2024–2025). His government's public position is protective — most recently a prime-ministerial message of 16 November 2026 affirming commitment to interfaith harmony — and is recorded here as its stated position against the documented outcomes.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record since March 2024, and his 2022–23 term where it bears on the tests, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • IMF stabilization — the central commitment of March 2024, delivered as stated. On taking office he said a new IMF program was unavoidable; the $7 billion EFF was approved in September 2024 and has passed four reviews to staff-level agreement (7 October 2026), with reserves rebuilt to about $21.5 billion and the Fund attesting macroeconomic stability preserved (IMF records, 2024–2026). The inflation trajectory he inherited at its worst — 38 per cent at the May 2023 peak of his first term — fell to a 4.5 per cent average in FY25 (World Bank/IMF data).
  • PIA privatization — promised on taking office, delivered. "Upon assuming office, I had promised the nation that steps would be taken for the privatization of state-owned enterprises; this process of PIA's privatization is a very important milestone in this regard" (prime minister's statement, 23 December 2025, on the Rs135 billion auction). The promise was kept in the letter; the terms on which it was kept — only Rs10.125 billion of the price to the treasury, some Rs650 billion of PIA debt retained by the state, 15-year tax exemptions for the buyers, and the military-run Fauji Foundation's fertilizer company inside the winning consortium, whose chairman took the PIA board chair at the June 2026 handover — are documented and contested, with Dawn's editorials and opposition figures calling the transaction a transfer "from one arm of the state to another." Those terms are graded as a counting/favor question under Tests 1 and 2; here, the word itself was performed.
  • The civil-military partnership — kept, because it was the promise. His government's operative commitment, stated openly and repeated on 19 November 2026, was government in "unprecedented partnership" with the military establishment. By that stated standard the word was kept in full: SIFC empowered, the army chief promoted to Field Marshal, the CDF office created, and the partnership publicly credited for economic stabilization. What that partnership cost the other tests is graded there, not here.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Single-digit inflation and 4 per cent growth — receding at grading. Inflation, having averaged 4.5 per cent in FY25, peaked again in May 2026 and stood at about 10.3 per cent in September 2026; the IMF's October 2026 World Economic Outlook raised its FY27 inflation forecast to 9.9 per cent against the government's 8.2 per cent target, and held FY27 growth at 3.5 per cent against the government's 4 per cent — a gap the finance minister publicly disputed ("I am still quite clear that we should clock north of 4%") while accepting the program that contains the Fund's figures (IMF, October 2026; Reuters interview with Finance Minister Muhammad Aurangzeb, October 2026). FY26 growth is estimated by the Fund at 3.6 per cent.
  • An economy that stands on its own — not on the documented path. His first term's rhetoric of ending dependence on lenders has been succeeded by a second, larger IMF program running to October 2027 and a public record, from inside his own cabinet, that concedes the method: Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal's 2026 memorandum to the prime minister, reported by The Express Tribune, states that Pakistan "was achieving fiscal stabilisation largely by cutting development spending and borrowing more, rather than widening the revenue base and raising exports." Poverty at 29 per cent (Test 4) is the household-level entry on the same ledger.
  • The Charter of Democracy — invoked for the 27th Amendment, kept only in part. The government's stated justification for the Federal Constitutional Court was that it fulfilled the 2006 Charter of Democracy, signed by his party and the PPP. The Charter did promise a constitutional court; it also promised that judicial appointments would be made through a commission structured to keep them out of executive hands. The enacted design — first FCC judges assigned by the President on the prime minister's advice, per the UN High Commissioner's summary — keeps the Charter's institution while reversing its safeguard (Charter of Democracy, 2006; Law Minister's Senate speech, 8 November 2025; OHCHR, November 2025). The characterization of this as fulfillment is the government's; the discrepancy is documented.
  • The first-term word, for the pattern. In April 2022 he promised relief to households in terms that became famous — that he would sell his own clothes to bring down the price of flour. Within his first term flour prices rose sharply amid the 2022–23 crisis and inflation reached 38 per cent (May 2023); the stabilization that eventually brought inflation down was the work of the caretaker period's IMF Stand-By Arrangement and his second term's EFF (Tests 3 and 5, above). The promise is recorded because his second-term economic word has been markedly more careful — and markedly better kept — than his first.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Shehbaz Sharif has governed Pakistan for two and a half years as the civilian face of an openly declared civil-military partnership, and the record shows the partnership's price list. The law was bent around his own house first — an accountability statute rewritten by a government whose leaders were its defendants, then a constitutional amendment placing the president, the prime minister, and five-star officers beyond prosecution — and the courts were restructured twice in thirteen months until, in the International Commission of Jurists' November 2026 finding, they no longer hold the institutional guarantees of independence; the two senior judges who said so from inside resigned within hours of the second amendment's signing. The flock is counted with named exceptions: the largest opposition party's voters, whose mandate, seats, march, and leader were each in turn administratively, judicially, and physically contained; the Baloch, whose missing are traced by a commission that has never produced a prosecution and whose best-known advocate now holds two life sentences; the Pashtuns, whose rights movement is proscribed; nearly three million Afghans returned across the border, most under documented fear of arrest; and a poverty rate his own planning minister published at 29 per cent. Against that record stand real entries on the other shelves: he took the IMF's medicine in the open and the program holds; he faced down a four-day war with India and accepted the ceasefire that ended it; his opponents kept their province, his critics kept their newspapers, and the courts — even restructured — still acquitted his rival, bailed him, and ordered his jailers to admit his lawyers and his doctors. The wobble grade is mixed because the adjustment is genuine where the pressure is economic or external, and absent where the pressure is constitutional. But the tests measure the seat, and on the evidence assembled here the seat gives by partnership more than by law, grips the courts and the airwaves more than it holds them, and counts the whole country except the parts of it that would not vote for the arrangement.
