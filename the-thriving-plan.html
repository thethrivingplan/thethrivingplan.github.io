Money is a symbol. It is a token everybody agrees to treat as standing for value — a bill, a coin, a number in an account.

The thing itself is worth almost nothing on its own — paper with a picture on it, a bit of metal, digits on a screen. All of its worth is the agreement.

What money does is three jobs:

  • The go-between for trading — I give the symbol, I get the goods, and no direct swap is needed.
  • The counting stick — how we say what something is “worth” and compare one thing with another.
  • The jar — worth stored now, to spend later.

Because the symbol only works while everybody keeps agreeing to it, the symbol can end up standing between people and the real things — the food, the homes, the care — that already exist. The real things are real. The symbol is the agreement. That is why this plan can talk about a world with no money: nothing real disappears; only the go-between is retired.

And that token is what gets held over people — standing between a person and the real things as leverage. Retire the go-between and there is nothing left to hold over anybody. Money is just the most common thing held over people; the plan is a world with no holding things over people — a world where nothing is held over anybody.

Leverage is holding what somebody needs, so their choices stop being fully theirs. Money is only the most common handle. Anything a person needs can be held over them.

We need to stop letting the bad people have it. And the plan does not do that by handing the handle to nicer holders — it removes the handle itself. A handle doesn’t check who picks it up; that is why the plan takes handles away entirely.

Money is the handle every system is gripped by — the thing held over the people inside it, so their choices stop being fully theirs. Take the handle away and a system has to run on what is actually in front of it: the record, the work, and the need. That is step one. Everything after it — most of this page — is step two: rebuilding the same systems, run the new way.

Start with the legal system. Four places where money — not the facts of a case — decides what happens to a person:

  • Bail — freedom priced before any verdict. Most people in America's local jails have not been convicted of anything: only about 120,000 of the people in jail on a given day are serving a sentence; the rest are awaiting trial, many because they cannot post bail (Prison Policy Initiative, 2026). The median felony bail is $10,000 (Bureau of Justice Statistics, 2009) — eight months' income for the typical person held, whose median income was about $15,600 a year for men and $11,100 for women (Prison Policy Initiative, 2016). Waiting is not neutral: in a study of over 420,000 defendants in Philadelphia and Miami-Dade, being held before trial raised the chance of conviction by about 14 percentage points — mostly through guilty pleas — and cut formal employment years later, with no net effect on future crime (Dobbie, Goldin & Yang, American Economic Review, 2018). Bail's own stated purpose is only to bring people back to court — the American Bar Association: bail “is not supposed to be used as punishment.” Two states have taken the money out of that decision, and their whole record stands: New Jersey (2017) cut its pretrial jail population 43.9% in three years on its judiciary's own count — while appearance rates slipped slightly, new charges among people released rose slightly, and racial disparity did not move. Illinois abolished cash bail outright (2023) — the first state to do so; its university evaluators found far fewer people detained and smaller jails in year one, a rebound in year two, and no verdict on crime possible yet, while sheriffs say the law ties judges' hands on repeat and violent offenders. None of that rescues the priced door: the question was never whether release decisions matter — it is whether they should turn on a bank balance.
  • Fines and fees — courts fed by the people in front of them. In 2015, the US Department of Justice found Ferguson, Missouri's enforcement “shaped by the City's focus on revenue rather than by public safety needs”: the city's 2015 budget expected $3.09 million of its $13.26 million general fund from fines and fees, and the report quotes its finance director urging the police to ramp up ticket writing to fill the gap. The same report corrects the famous figure itself — warrants went to about 9,007 people in 2013, covering 32,975 offenses, not 32,975 warrants. Nationally, at least $27.6 billion in court debt was owed on the Fines and Fees Justice Center's 2021 count — a floor, since only 25 states could report a total at all. Unpaid, the debt becomes the next punishment: over 11 million driver's licenses stood suspended for unpaid fines and fees in 2019 (Free to Drive). And the machine does not even pay for itself — the Brennan Center found counties studied in Texas and New Mexico spending over 41 cents of every dollar raised on hearings and jail alone, and one New Mexico county spending at least $1.17 to collect a dollar. The distinction the research itself draws: a fine is a punishment, meant to deter; a fee is a charge, meant to raise revenue. Pieces of step one are already done: Ohio became the 26th state to end or cut debt-based license suspensions in 2025, and San Francisco eliminated its local criminal-justice fees in 2018, waived some $33 million owed by 21,000 people, and reports that its revenue did not fall.
  • Civil forfeiture — property accused; the owner pays to argue. A civil forfeiture case is brought against the property itself, and no conviction of its owner is required. Counting government records from 45 states, the District of Columbia, and the federal Justice and Treasury departments, the Institute for Justice found at least $68.8 billion forfeited between 2000 and 2019 — and states plainly that the true figure is higher, because many jurisdictions do not report. The burden runs backwards from a criminal trial: in 29 states and under federal law, the owner must prove their own innocence to recover property; only 13 states and D.C. put the burden on the government, whose standard in most places is just “more likely than not.” The typical seizure is small — a median cash forfeiture of $1,276 across the 21 states with data — while a lawyer for even a simple fight costs at least $3,000, so in the four states that track it, owners sought their property back in 22% of cases or fewer. The Department of Justice gives the practice its purpose in its own manual — to “deter, disrupt, and dismantle criminal enterprises,” cartels among them. Its own Inspector General found in 2017 that the DEA had seized over $4 billion in cash in a decade, the vast majority of it forfeited with no criminal proceeding at all, and that the Department does not systematically measure whether its seizures advance investigations. New Mexico ran step one here in 2015: forfeiture only after a criminal conviction, proceeds to the state's general fund instead of the seizing agency — and the Institute's study of the years after found no rise in crime attributable to the change (the Institute's own analysis, labeled as such).
  • Counsel — a right that runs on funding. Since Gideon v. Wainwright (1963), the Constitution has required states to give a lawyer to criminal defendants too poor to hire one — extended, in Argersinger (1972), to any case that actually ends in imprisonment. What that right delivers depends on what gets funded. The old national guideline let one public defender carry 150 felonies or 400 misdemeanors a year; the 2023 national workload study (RAND, the National Center for State Courts, and the American Bar Association) measured what reasonably effective defense takes — 248 hours for a murder case, 99 for a high-severity felony — and called the 1973 standards “outdated, not empirically based, and inadequate.” Where funding runs out, the right thins to paper: Oregon's judiciary counted a record 3,875 people waiting without a lawyer on September 1, 2024, and in 2016 the New Orleans public defender's office began refusing serious felony cases and waitlisting defendants. In civil cases there is no general right at all — in eviction courts, on the National Coalition for a Civil Right to Counsel's current count, about 4% of tenants have a lawyer, against 84% of landlords. New York City created that right for eviction cases in 2017, first in the nation: 89% of fully represented tenants kept their homes in 2024 on the city's reporting — while the share of tenants actually getting a lawyer fell to 42%, because a right without funding is a door with nobody home. The counterpoint stands at the same size: Michigan rebuilt its public defense from 2013, more than doubling funding to $319 million by 2024 under standards experts call a national model — the gap is a funding choice, not a law of nature.

The same handle, everywhere else. Housing: 15.8 million homes stand vacant while 745,652 people were counted homeless on one night — the honest on-market ratio is about 6.6 homes to 1 (Part Three) — and money is the gate between them. Food: 29% of the US food supply goes unsold or uneaten (Part Three) in the same towns where people go hungry; the gate is the price. Care: raising children and tending elders is already done unpaid — US women's unpaid care alone is valued at $1.48 trillion a year (Part Two) — the work was never the token's creation. And the essentials themselves are run by about a fifth of the workforce (Part Two): in system after system, the real thing exists and the people to run it exist. The handle is what decides who may reach it.

The honest limit, at full size. Money is the most common handle — it is not the only one. Status can hold a door shut; an office can; force can, with no dollar moving anywhere. Removing money removes the money-handle; it does not by itself remove those — which is why the leverage statement, two bubbles up, names the root as leverage, not currency. Nor is every court dollar corrupt: records must be kept, buildings kept up, people paid — some of what fees recover is honest cost, and the quarrel is not with bookkeeping. It is with money as the gate and the incentive: freedom priced before a verdict, courts fed by their own fines, property kept unless its owner can afford to argue, a right that arrives only when it is funded. Step one removes the gate. The rest — the record, the work, the need, and the handles still to name — is step two's honest work, and this page does not pretend otherwise.

Removal is step one. Rebuilding is step two. This plan is step two, written down — so step one has somewhere to land.

Hospitals, all over the world — what stops on day one. The bill. Insurance approval standing as a gate between a diagnosis and its treatment. Medical debt — the $220 billion of it worked in Part Eleven — loses the thing it attaches to. Nobody is sorted by ability to pay before they are sorted by need. The care itself changes nothing that morning: same wards, same staff, same medicines on the shelves.

The work that disappears is the payment layer. US hospitals put 25.3% of their spending into administration, against 12.4% in Canada (Health Affairs, 2014) — and the researchers' own reading is that about 12 points of that is the logistics every system carries (procuring, scheduling, coordinating); the American excess above the floor is largely the added work of billing patient by patient. Across the whole US system, billing-and-insurance work alone ran to about $471 billion in 2012 (Jiwani and colleagues, 2014), and administration of every kind took 34.2% of US health spending in 2017 against 17.0% in Canada (Annals of Internal Medicine, 2020) — each study counts its own basket, and the Sources say which. These are real jobs done by real people; under this plan they are the first hands freed for the work that is actually short — the wards themselves.

What does not change is everything money never made. Beds are physical: 12.6 hospital beds per 1,000 people in Korea, 2.8 in the United States, 1.0 in Mexico, under 1 in Niger (OECD, 2025; World Bank) — removing money does not move a bed across a border by itself, and the OECD itself names staffing, not beds, as the tighter constraint. The World Health Organization projects a shortage of 11 million health workers by 2030, and training a doctor typically takes around ten years (OECD): skill cannot be summoned by declaration. Nearly 2 billion people have no access to basic medicines (World Health Organization, 2017) — the factories, the supply chains, and the roads are logistics, not price; fixing them is step two's work, stated as such. And the rich-country/poor-country gap does not close on day one: nurse density runs more than ten times higher in high-income countries than in low-income ones (World Health Organization). Removal changes who may walk through the door. It does not furnish the building.

The same test, briefly, for schools — and for everything else. Schools: the buildings, the books, and the teachers exist; what falls is the fee, the priced district line, the loan. What does not fall is the teacher shortage — UNESCO counts 44 million more teachers needed worldwide by 2030, most of them to replace teachers who leave. For the rest, Parts Two and Three already ran the census: the essentials are produced by a minority of the workforce and the surplus already exists. Everywhere, the pattern is the same pair: access changes on day one; capacity changes at the speed of training and building.

And if AI and robots are utilized — utilized honestly. Machines already carry real work: 4.66 million industrial robots were in operation worldwide in 2024, 542,000 of them installed that year (International Federation of Robotics), and Amazon states it has deployed a million robots across its warehouses. Software already reads images beside doctors — the US Food and Drug Administration has authorized over 1,600 AI-enabled medical devices (as of September 2026), framed by the FDA itself as supporting clinical decisions: support, not replacement — the best-known surgical robot is worked by a surgeon's own hands at a console, and no fully autonomous surgical system is in routine use. What machines do not carry today is hands-on care: nursing and elder care remain human work — the Bureau of Labor Statistics projects care to be the fastest-growing work in the country — and care robots remain pilots, not staff. Measured across the economy (McKinsey Global Institute), under 5% of occupations could be fully automated with demonstrated technology, while most occupations hold tasks machines can carry. So the honest line: robots multiply the people who can do the job — they haul, sort, schedule, scan, and remember. They do not replace the need for the people; they do not grow food or staff a ward today. In this plan, AI is the helper layer — offered, never forced — carrying the paperwork so hands can do the care.

So the change, right now, is this: the gate opens, and the wasted work stops — access and the billing layer change the day the handle is removed. What does not change is physics and people: beds, medicines, trained hands, time. The limits were never price. They were always inventory — and inventory can be counted, shared, and grown. That counting is step two, and it is most of this page.

Nobody does work they hate for free. People only do it because the bills make them. The hated job is not held by interest or by calling — it is held by the rent, the utilities, the food bill: by everything that stops if the pay stops.

In the average US jurisdiction, the cheapest homes are assessed at over twice the ratio of the most expensive homes — so their owners pay roughly double the effective property tax rate.                 Part one

                The two famous numbers — and what they actually measure

                Whenever this idea comes up, two numbers get quoted as if they were the answer. They are real research — but neither one is about giving up money, and blending them is an error. Here is each one, scoped precisely.

                ~25% committed minority
                Share of a small group that had to hold a new convention before everyone flipped

                The Centola experiment

                Centola, Becker, Brackbill & Baronchelli, Science 360, 2018

                • What it wasAbout 200 paid online volunteers, in groups of ~20, coordinating on a name for a pictured face. Once a name settled, researchers inserted confederates who always used a different one.
                • What it foundBelow a 25% committed minority, only ~6% of the rest ever switched. At 25–31%, the new name reached 72–100% adoption in every such trial. (Trials ranged 15–35%; the theoretical critical point was ~24.3%.)
                • What it doesn't proveSwitching a name costs nothing. Renouncing money means forgoing rent, medicine, fuel and tax compliance. The authors themselves note the threshold shifts with how entrenched a behaviour is — and no replication establishes 25% as a universal constant. Earlier models predicted ~10%; some observational work suggested up to ~40%.
                3.5% peak participation
                The bar is drawn at 3.5% of a population — small share, enormous absolute number

                The Chenoweth "rule"

                Chenoweth & Stephan, NAVCO dataset; Why Civil Resistance Works, 2011

                • What it was323 protest campaigns worldwide, 1900–2006, each aiming at the maximum: overthrowing a government or winning territorial liberation. Nonviolent campaigns succeeded about twice as often as violent ones (~53% vs ~26%).
                • What it foundEvery campaign whose peak event drew active, sustained participation of at least 3.5% of the population succeeded. Chenoweth calls it a "rule of thumb," not an "iron law" — and most successful campaigns won below 3.5%.
                • What it doesn't proveIt is about crowds at protest peaks inside organised campaigns — not about 3.5% of people holding an opinion or joining a scheme. Critics note the dataset omits failed uprisings that never got recorded (Anisin), that Bahrain (2011) and Brunei topped 3.5% and still failed (Seifman), and that the data concern toppling autocratic governments, not changing an economic system inside a democracy (Matthews). Success rates have also declined since ~2010.

                At US scale, for orientation only: 3.5% of ~340 million people is ~11.9 million people; 25% is ~85 million. Neither figure says anything about whether those people include the farmers, nurses, grid operators and truck drivers a money-free circuit would need. A protest-participation share and an essential-worker share are different populations.

Any honest plan counts carers first, not last.

                Part three

                The surplus reality check

                The hopeful half of the idea is true: the US throws away staggering amounts of what it already produces. The honest half: surplus is scattered, perishable, and often in the wrong place — which is why "there's plenty" and "everyone eats" are different statements.

                Food: 29% uneaten — still nearly a third

                29% of the US food supply — 70 million tons, worth $380 billion — went unsold or uneaten in 2024 (ReFED, 2026 report). That is down 2.2% from 2023, the first meaningful drop since the pandemic. USDA ERS counted 31% lost at retail + consumer level in 2010 data; its standing headline range is 30–40%.

                Superseded, printed as history, never blended: the 2023 ReFED report put the 2021 figure at 38% — 91 million tons, $444 billion — before the series and its methods were revised; the 2025 report sits between, at 31% / 73.9M tons / $382B (2023 data). In the 2021 data, under 2% of the surplus was donated and about half was generated in households. Recovering any of it is itself essential work.

                Retail & warehouse space: tighter than you'd think

                Shopping-centre vacancy sat at 5.3–5.4% in 2024 — a ~20-year low (Cushman & Wakefield) — roughly 230 million sq ft vacant out of a 4.3+ billion sq ft inventory. Industrial/warehouse vacancy ran ~6.6% (CBRE, 2024).

                There is no large idle retail glut nationally. Vacant commercial space is market-priced and dispersed — not a free commons waiting to be claimed.

                Homes: the ratio is real, the catch is real too

                15,812,000 vacant housing units (Census, Q3 2026) against 745,652 people counted homeless on one night (HUD, Jan 2025). Gross, that's ~21 empty units per homeless person — but only 4,930,000 units were actually on the market (3,866,000 for rent + 1,064,000 for sale only), which makes the honest ratio ~6.6 to 1, with units often in the wrong place or condition. This page prints the honest ratio, not the meme's ~21:1.

                HUD point-in-time counts are widely regarded as undercounts — one night, literal homelessness only. No new national HUD total has superseded the Jan 2025 figure.

                This plan’s way — who benefits the most

                The people who benefit most are the people the current way reaches last — and that is most of the world. The bottom half of adults, 2.8 billion people, who today split 8% of the income. The 831 million living on under $3 a day, and the 1.12 billion in slums and informal settlements, for whom food, a roof and care stop being things that have to be bought before they can be had. Nearly half of humanity lives on under $8.30 a day; under this plan, nobody’s food, housing or care waits on what they can pay, so the gain is largest exactly where the lack is deepest. The fewer handles a person’s life is currently held by, the more this changes for them — and the people held hardest today gain the most.

                This plan’s way — who benefits the least

                The people who benefit least are the people the current way already reaches first — the top tenth of adults, and above them the top 1%. Not because they are shut out: the food, the housing, the care and the work they choose are all still there for them, the same as for everyone. They benefit least because they lack the least today — there is the least missing from their lives for the plan to hand back. What they gain is real but smaller: a world where nobody around them is desperate, and where what they have is no longer something they have to defend. Measured against what everyone else gains, it is the smallest share of the benefit — because they started with the largest share of everything else.

                And who gets hurt by this plan’s way? In plain words: nobody loses anything real. Nobody loses their home, their belongings, their work, or their standing as a person. The only thing anybody loses is the handle itself — the power to hold what other people need over them: the rent charged because the alternative is the street, the interest charged because the alternative is going without, the wage squeezed because the alternative is hunger. That power is not a thing a person needs to live; it is a grip on other people’s needs, and losing a grip is not an injury. What does not go away: consequences. With money gone, everybody is judged on their actions — a person who harms still answers for the harm, on the record, exactly as the thought process at the top of this page lays out. The plan removes the handle. It does not remove accountability, and it does not take anything real from anyone — including the people who hold the most today.

                The money machine itself — and the honest gaps

                The work of running money for its own sake is smaller than it looks: 52.2 million people work in finance and insurance worldwide — 1.6% of all jobs on Earth (ILO). Those are people, not villains: their skills — counting, checking, coordinating, keeping records people can trust — are exactly the skills a money-free circuit runs on. It is the gate their work currently guards that the plan removes, not the workers. How much wider the penumbra runs — debt collection, money-compliance, cash handling, financial marketing — no sourced global total exists, and this page will not invent one.

                One blank, printed at full size: about 1.2 billion people rent the home they live in (2010s academic estimate, Kemp 2016) — but nobody on Earth credibly counts the world’s landlords. No UN, World Bank, ILO or academic source publishes a worldwide landlord total; rental ownership is mostly small-scale and unregistered in much of the world, which is likely why. So this section names who pays rent, and does not pretend to a count of who collects it. Any version of this argument that hands you a precise global landlord number is handing you a number nobody measured.

                What's been tried — and what the record shows

                This is not a new idea, and the attempts left evidence. The pattern across a century of them: every real case either stayed small, stayed partly inside money, or was shut down / run by a state. Each card names what worked and what broke it. Standing cases re-checked 2 November 2026 (weekly deep sweep) — the Twin Oaks correction and the four newest cards below landed in that pass.

                Twin Oaks, Virginia ~85 adults + 15 children · 1967–today · status corrected 2 Nov 2026, from its own site

                What worked — No internal money at all. A labour-credit system (38.5 hrs/week on the community's own current homepage; older accounts say 42 hrs/week — printed as found, not blended; every kind of work counting equal) made fairness explicit and solved the free-rider problem. Grows much of its own food; collective health provision.

                The limit — Its cash-earning businesses today are tofu, book indexing and work with the Southern Exposure Seed Exchange. The long-time hammock business was lost to a wildfire on 20 Mar 2024 (~$1M damages); a separate electrical fire in late Jan 2026 burned a residence housing 10 members — no one hurt, rooms found for everyone, "not in immediate financial peril" (its own words). The older ~$1M/yr hammock-and-tofu income figure (Broadview, 2023) is retired as current — no verified current total income was found, so none is printed. It has stayed at ~100 people, by design, for five decades.

                Sardex, Sardinia 3,500 businesses / €70M a year (RIPESS Europe, Oct 2025) · 2009–today

                What worked — Business-to-business mutual credit with no euros changed hands kept firms trading through a credit crunch. Professional brokers plus conservative credit limits suppressed free-riding and hoarding; a peer-reviewed network study counted 1,477 businesses and 48,170 transactions (Iosifidis et al., Nature Human Behaviour, 2018).

                The limit — Deliberately not money-free: a complement to the euro. Wages, taxes, imports and energy are still paid in euros, and running it is itself a paid business. Its size is printed as a range, because the accounts differ: 3,500 businesses / €70M a year (RIPESS Europe, Oct 2025); other recent accounts give 4,000+ members / €40–50M a year. A claim of 10,000 Sardinian businesses circulates — unverified, and not adopted here. Re-checked 2 Nov 2026: still operating; no new count found.

                Wörgl stamp scrip, Austria Town of ~4,500–5,000 · Jul 1932 – Sep 1933

                What worked — A local currency with a 1%-per-month stamp fee circulated fast, funded public works (roads, a bridge, a water system), and was accepted for local taxes — some paid in advance.

                What broke it — Legal suppression. The Austrian National Bank asserted its money-issuing monopoly and banned it on 1 Sept 1933; the town lost in court. (The unemployment improvement is quoted as −16% in some retellings and −25% in others, against +19% nationally — contemporary data are thin, so treat the exact figure as contested. Hundreds of US Depression-era stamp-scrip issues likewise ended after federal action in 1933.)

                Ithaca HOURS, New York 1991–2010s · no longer circulating

                What worked — A local currency anchored at $10 = one hour of work, with millions in dollar-equivalent trade over two decades and a share of new issue granted to community groups. Peak size is inconsistently reported — from ~300 businesses / 1,600 participants in an early account to 500–900 businesses in later retellings.

                What broke it — Key-person dependence and the spend-onward limit. Use declined after founder Paul Glover — the full-time networker — left Ithaca; paper scrip lost out to cards; and businesses accumulated HOURS they couldn't pass on, because suppliers, taxes and utilities demanded dollars.

                Time banks, US Typically 50–500 members · since 1995

                What worked — An hour is an hour, whoever gives it — which dignifies care work. Good for rides, yard work, companionship, minor repairs. The largest examples reach ~1,500 members (Hour Exchange Portland).

                The limit — Services only: no food production, medicine or capital goods. Scarce specialists (plumbers, doctors) rarely offer hours at equal credit; coordination itself needs coordinators; and dormancy is the modal outcome in evaluations. Hours are not accepted for tax, rent or utilities.

                COVID mutual aid networks, US 50 groups (Mar 2020) → 1,000+ (May 2021)

                What worked — Explosive, voluntary, money-light help: food and medicine delivery, rent funds, neighbour matching through simple forms (Town Hall Project / Mutual Aid Hub counts; 851 groups analysed in one platform study).

                What broke it — Attrition. Peer-reviewed studies (2021–22) document the same sequence everywhere: vaccines arrived, volunteers returned to paid work, drivers and fundraising dried up, burnout spread, and groups scaled back. They redistributed existing money and food brilliantly — but built few sources of their own, so the sharing shrank.

                Emergency Response Rooms, Sudan 737 neighbourhood rooms · all 18 states · Apr 2023–today

                What worked — Federated mutual aid at national scale: 737 neighbourhood base rooms, coordinated through district and state councils, run by ~26,000 unpaid volunteers in all 18 Sudanese states. Since the war began in April 2023 they have sustained millions — communal kitchens, healthcare, water repairs, evacuations, civilian protection — where the state has collapsed and international aid often cannot reach (Right Livelihood laureate profile, 2025). The recognition is fresh: the Balzan Prize for Humanity, Peace and Fraternity among Peoples was announced for the ERRs on 7 September 2026. And the funding behind them is growing — the UN Sudan Humanitarian Fund's ERR/mutual-aid programme grew from $15 million in 2024 to $25 million in 2025–26 (Refugees International, 22 Oct 2026). The wider response plan stood at 43.4% funded as of 17 Oct 2026 — $1.25 billion received against $2.87 billion in requirements, 20.4 million people targeted (Sudan HNRP 2026 tracking) — beside September's 41% figure; 33.7 million people in need in 2026 stands — a funding percentage moving is not the need shrinking.

                The limit — Not money-free. The labour is volunteered; the supplies are not conjured — they run on diaspora and donor cash: the cash border at war scale. The same Refugees International report notes that only ~6% of that fund reached Sudanese actors directly in 2025 — most of the money still passes through intermediaries first. A separate count of "more than 2,100 ERR groups" (Social Change Nest, Oct 2026) counts a different unit than the 737 federated base rooms and is not blended with it; a reported Nobel Peace Prize nomination for the ERRs is a nomination, not a prize, and is not printed as one. And the cost is counted in people: volunteers have been violently targeted, with nearly 160 killed (Right Livelihood). This case proves federated mutual aid can carry essentials for millions under state collapse. It does not prove a money-free economy — both halves are the finding.

                Community land trusts, US 308 trusts · model since 1969

                What worked — Land is held permanently in trust, homes leased for 99 years, and resale formulas keep them affordable across generations, with very low foreclosure rates (Lincoln Institute of Land Policy count, Jan 2024: 308 CLTs in 48 states + DC + Puerto Rico).

                The limit — Scale and the cash border: roughly 35,000 homes in 55 years against a 149.8-million-unit housing stock, and trusts still need grants, mortgages and tax payments in dollars to acquire land. They decommodify land tenure — not money itself. The count itself is a range: 308 in the Jan 2024 census, ~350 by the movement's own 2025 estimate (John Emmeus Davis — an estimate, not a census; link unverified).

                Mondragón cooperatives, Spain 71,415 workers · 1956–today

                What worked — Worker ownership at full industrial scale: €11.322 billion in sales and €618.8 million in aggregate profits (2025 annual report; 2024: €11.213 billion, 70,085 workers), solidarity funds and worker redeployment that cushioned recessions, capped pay ratios.

                The limit — Not a money-free case at all — wages, prices and trade in euros throughout; listing it as one is a category error. And the market still bites: flagship Fagor went bankrupt in Oct–Nov 2013 after debt-fuelled expansion and recession, with suppliers demanding cash on delivery — though inter-cooperation redeployed most of its ~1,800 Basque member-workers.

                Wartime rationing, Britain Whole population ~48M · 1940–1954

                What worked — Acceptance, driven by fairness. The Wartime Social Survey (early 1942) found universal approval in principle, with "fair shares — nobody has more than oneself" the main positive motive; the nutrition of the poor actually improved.

                The limit — Not voluntary and not money-free: coupons plus money, state coercion, criminal penalties — and a black market throughout. It proves people accept equal, guaranteed allocation in an emergency under a trusted administrator. It does not prove allocation runs itself without one.

                Cuba's Special Period gardens Havana · 1991–today

                What worked — When imports collapsed, Havana converted 35,000 hectares to urban agriculture (25,000 allotments by 1995). Calorie intake recovered from ~1,000–1,500/day (1993, FAO estimate) to ~2,600/day by ~2008; officials put city-grown produce at over 50% of Havana's fruit and vegetables (some researchers say ~90% of fresh produce — the spread is definitional).

                The limit — A catastrophe, not a template: the average Cuban lost ~30 lb in three years. It was a state programme — state land, state targets, rationing (since 1962) and food imports running throughout. Gardens supplied vegetables, not calories overall; grains, oils, fuel and medicine stayed import-dependent.

                River Run Farm, Massachusetts Working farm held by a land trust · purchased Jun 2025 · added 2 Nov 2026

                What happened — Berkshire Community Land Trust bought River Run Farm, Great Barrington, from philanthropist Jane Iredale in June 2025 for $1.65 million — 83.5 acres in the final account (The Berkshire Edge, 2 Nov 2026; the trust's own newsletter gives the closing as 30 Jun 2025 and says it now holds over 100 acres of farmland in trust). Iredale had bought the land first, in 2022, and held it while the trust raised the money — a bridge holder carrying the land at no speculation. Off the Shelf Farm, run since 2018 on scattered handshake leases, holds a 98-year lease; the farmers own the buildings and build equity through improvements, taxes are incorporated and paid by the land trust, and the secure lease unlocked a $497,519 Massachusetts food-security infrastructure grant.

                The limit — One farm, one town — a securing, not a scale change, and no figure elsewhere on this page is superseded by it. The numbers differ by source and date and are printed that way, never blended: earlier trust accounts describe 79 acres; campaign figures ran $1.25M / $1.6M against the completed $1.65M. It took a philanthropist bridge buyer, community fundraising and public money to close, some anticipated government funding did not come through, the farm still sells its food in dollars, and the lease is low-cost rather than free. A door opened wider — for a working farm — not the circuit itself.

                Carré Laval, Québec A whole neighbourhood placed in a social utility trust · announced 5 Oct 2026 · added 2 Nov 2026

                What happened — The City of Laval announced the upcoming constitution of a fiducie d'utilité sociale (social utility trust) for Carré Laval — the first time in Québec a city has entrusted a complete neighbourhood's development to such a trust (Ville de Laval, 5 Oct 2026). Filed under its real name: the headline that carried it calls it a "community land trust," but the legal form is Québec's social utility trust, related to and distinct from the classic ground-lease trust. The city owns more than 85% of the site's land; the trust, constituted in perpetuity, will progressively acquire the lands to remove them from real-estate speculation. The site is printed as the sources give it, never blended: 40 hectares (~99 acres, Gouvernement du Québec, 2020); 42 hectares in 2024 reporting — with a potential of more than 3,500 housing units around a 22-hectare park, and $10 million in provincial decontamination aid granted in 2019.

                The limit — This is an announcement of a trust to be constituted, not a constituted trust and not a built neighbourhood — no home stands on it yet; the first real-estate project is projected for 2027, with a $910,000 startup envelope for 2026. Development remains entirely inside money. The permanence cuts both ways and is printed, not hidden: per the municipal summary as reported, the deed could not be modified after signing without a court's approval, nor ended before its 50th anniversary and then only at 25-year intervals — a lock that protects the mission also binds future residents who never chose it, in visible tension with this plan's rule that exit stays as open as entrance. The process was contested on the record: opposition councillors voted against the recommendation, saying they had too little time to examine it (Yalla Magazine, 8 Oct 2026 — stated opposition, printed as such). A door opened wider, at neighbourhood scale — not the circuit itself.

                The ROAD to Housing Act, US A federal definition for land trusts · law 11 Jul 2026 · added 2 Nov 2026

                What happened — The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act (Public Law 119-101) became law on 11 Jul 2026. Its Section 501 codifies a broad federal definition of a community land trust — nonprofit, state or local-government holder; permanent affordability as a primary purpose; monitoring; a ground lease, deed covenant or similar measure keeping housing affordable for not less than 30 years; preemptive purchase options — and names shared-equity models as HOME-qualifying (Shelterforce, 4 Sep 2026, quoting the enacted text). The breadth is the point: only 30% of CLTs have the classic tripartite board (Grounded Solutions Network survey). Section 210 adds a FY 2027–2031 HUD pilot of conversion grants able to fund CLTs and housing cooperatives — a pilot, printed as such.

                The limit — The Act provides no funding itself (HOME was $1.25 billion last year, against $1.8 billion in FY 2010 — about $2.75 billion in 2026 dollars, per Shelterforce), and HUD must still issue program notices to operationalise it. A definition is eligibility certainty, not purchase money — the cash border in Part eight stands untouched.

                District heating, Denmark Heat at national scale on a cost-only price · added 2 Nov 2026

                What the record shows — An essential system that already runs at national scale on a cost-only price, owned by the people it heats. Scale, printed as the sources give it, never blended: 66% of households connected (DBDH, on Statistics Denmark 2023 figures — 352 member companies supplying 99% of district heating, 2M households, 3.7M people); ~69% in 2023 (Danish District Heating Association via RAP, 2025); 63% of the heating market (DBDH). Companies are owned by their consumers directly (co-operatives — connection makes you a voting member) or indirectly (municipal companies); municipalities plan and zone under the Heat Supply Act (1979) and guarantee loans. The price rule: the non-profit principle of necessary costs — price may not exceed necessary costs, surpluses return as lower prices, prices are reported to the Danish Utility Regulator twice a year. Systems run from village scale (~100 consumers) to the Copenhagen pool (98% of the city's buildings).

                The limit — Still entirely inside money: krone bills, market fuel and equipment, guarantee debt. Biomass sustainability is contested, and prices vary widely by company — cost-only is not cost-equal. Sharpest for this plan: zoning can oblige owners to connect and remain connected, paying at least fixed tariffs — in direct tension with the plan's never-forced rule, named here, not hidden; a voluntary analogue is unproven at this scale. Gap, printed plainly: no verified sector-wide workforce headcount was found — Denmark's version of the Part two census remains an open research item.

                Part six

                Who owns the pipes, wires and towers

                One more question the plan has to face: the essentials don't run on workers alone — they run on infrastructure somebody owns. Towers, grids, water plants, trucks and depots. So: what about the companies that hold access to utilities — cell towers, electricity, sanitation, goods delivery? Look at each system and the same shape appears every time: a small, nameable workforce; a few paper owners; and — in several of these systems — the not-for-profit form already running at full scale today.

                Electricity — already public for 55 million people

                601,300 people run every US utility (Part two). The owners vary — but roughly 2,000 electric utilities are community-owned and not-for-profit, and together they serve more than 55 million Americans (American Public Power Association). Los Angeles, Seattle, Austin and Nashville all run on public power. The community form isn't a proposal here; it's Tuesday.

                Counting note: the 55 million stands on the APPA / EIA-861 2022 basis. APPA's current page describes the same sector as serving more than 49 million people. Different bases, different dates — a definitional difference, printed side by side in Parts six and fourteen and never blended or averaged.

                Cell towers — four landlords, and the carriers rent

                The US has ≈158,500 purpose-built cell towers (Wireless Infrastructure Association). Four landlords hold about three-quarters of them: American Tower ~42,000, Crown Castle ~40,000, Vertical Bridge ~18,000 and SBA Communications ~17,400. The phone companies whose names are on the bill mostly don't own the towers — AT&T, Verizon and T-Mobile rent space on them. The operators and the owners are different people. And the real gate isn't the steel: it's the FCC spectrum licence that says who may transmit at all.

                Sanitation & water — small crews, much of it already municipal

                Waste management and remediation employs ~526,000 people (BLS, NAICS 562) — refuse collectors alone number 94,170 (BLS, 2025) — and a large share of collection is already done by city public-works crews; a handful of national firms dominate only the private slice. Water and sewage systems sit inside the 601,300 utility workers counted in Part two, and most are municipally owned already.

                Goods delivery — the biggest network is already public

                Moving goods is the 6.6 million transportation & warehousing jobs from Part two. Private giants — UPS, FedEx, Amazon — sit on top, but the largest single delivery network in the country, the US Postal Service, is already a public service reaching every address. Same shape again: many workers, few owners, one giant public incumbent.

                Available anywhere there is a signal — if they unlock it and allow it

                What follows is the plan's proposal — what should happen — stated as a proposal, not as a fact about today. The fact underneath it is plain, and it is the one this part has just shown: the networks already exist. The towers are standing (≈158,500 of them in the US, above), the cables are laid, the signal already reaches nearly everywhere people live. Access to them is a gate — and somebody holds the key to that gate.

                So the ask is a single act, and it could happen right off the bat: the carriers and providers would only have to unlock it and allow it. If they did, this plan — and anything like it — could be available for free anywhere there is internet or a cell tower, riding free alongside any cell service or internet service, the way this page already rides free on whatever connection opened it. Anybody should be able to use any cell tower, and nobody should have to pay for it: the structures are already there — they just have to let it be free.

                Printed honestly: today that gate charges money and demands permission — the spectrum licence above, tower rents, subscriptions. Unlock-and-allow is a choice the gatekeepers would have to make; nobody on this page can make it for them. That is why the plan names it early, as Step 2 of the step-by-step in Part nine — not as a hope tacked on at the end, but as one of the first moves, because a plan people cannot reach cannot spread by choice.

                What this means for the plan: the owners are not the obstacle they look like. The people who climb the towers, run the plants and drive the trucks are workers — they're already in the essential core in Part two, and Step 1 recruits operators, not owners, by trade. What ownership adds is a border, like the cash border in Part eight: rents, franchise fees and licences (spectrum above all) that demand dollars and permission. The answer is the same posture as the cash fund — pay the border where you must — plus the move the record already proves: grow the community layer underneath, the way public power already serves 55 million people, until the landlords are renting to the community instead of the other way round.

                Part seven

                The roadblocks — the pattern, not any one case

                Seven obstacles recur across a century of attempts, in country after country. Any serious plan has to answer all seven — the step-by-step in Part nine is built to do exactly that.

                1

                Legal suppression

                States defend the money monopoly. Wörgl was banned by Austria's National Bank after 14 months; US stamp scrip ended with federal action in 1933. The most successful local experiment on record was ended by a courtroom, not by failure.

                2

                Taxes demand cash

                Every case, same wall: taxes are assessed and payable in state currency — including tax on barter itself. The money-free circuit generates a dollar tax bill in proportion to its own activity (Part eight).

                3

                Supply chains for medicine, fuel and parts

                Fagor's suppliers demanding cash on delivery; Cuba's dependence on imported grains, oil and medicine; Twin Oaks' cash-earning businesses; Sardex members' euro costs. Somebody upstream almost always still wants money.

                4

                Free-rider strain

                Solved only where accounting was explicit — Twin Oaks' labour credits, Sardex's credit limits and brokers. The systems that lasted kept score; none ran on unstructured generosity alone.

                5

                Scale limits of trust and coordination

                Time banks stall at hundreds of members; reciprocal trust is built face to face and thins as numbers grow. Coordination is real work, and somebody has to do it.

                6

                Key-person decay and attention loss

                Ithaca HOURS declined when its founder left. COVID mutual aid shrank when volunteers returned to paid work. Enthusiasm is a fuel that runs out; structure is what remains.

                7

                The landlords and the licences

                The infrastructure itself — towers, grids, depots, routes — is owned by a few firms (Part six: four landlords hold ~three-quarters of US cell towers), and access runs on permission: spectrum licences, franchises, leases. A border that demands dollars, like the tax border in Part eight. The answer is in the record: the operators are workers, already counted in the core — recruit them, not the owners; community ownership is a proven form (public power serves 55 million people today); so the circuit pays this border where it must and grows its own layer underneath where it can.

                Part nine

                The plan, step by step — simplest form

                How to get there, built only from what the record above actually shows. Each step says how it works and why it works, and names the evidence it stands on. None of it requires everybody at once — it starts wherever a group of people chooses to start, and it grows by working, not by waiting for a threshold.

                1

                Recruit by role, not by headcount

                The first people a thriving circuit needs are the ones who run the essentials: growers, drivers, nurses and carers, water and power crews, repairers. A city of 400,000 needs ~37,600 of them in the narrow core alone — including essentially all of its hospital, water, grocery-logistics and trucking staff.

                How it worksCount the jobs first, the crowd second. Map who does what in your town, and invite the essential crews in by name and trade.
                Why it worksThe famous percentages count warm bodies; essentials run on specific skills. Composition is the binding constraint — Part four's arithmetic.
                2

                Unlock it and allow it — open the networks that are already built

                Right near the front, because reach comes before everything else: this plan could be available anywhere there is internet or a cell tower. The structures are already there — the towers are standing, the cables are laid, the signal already reaches nearly everywhere people live. All the carriers and providers who hold the gates would have to do is unlock it and allow it: let this ride free alongside any cell service or internet service, and let anybody use any cell tower without paying for it.

                How it worksThe gatekeepers of Part six — carriers, providers, tower landlords and the spectrum licensers — open access instead of metering it. No new network has to be built first; the ask is permission, not construction.
                Why it worksA plan people cannot reach cannot spread by choice. The infrastructure already exists; access is a gate somebody holds — and a gate can be opened by the people holding it.

                Stated as what it is — a proposal: today those gates charge money and demand permission (Parts six and eight), and nobody here can open them on the gatekeepers' behalf. The step is early because the decision is simple, even if making it happen is not: they just have to let it be free.

                3

                Keep score, kindly

                Every hour given is counted; every balance is visible; credit has clear, agreed limits. An hour of childcare and an hour of plumbing both register — which is how care work stops being invisible.

                How it worksLabour credits (Twin Oaks) or mutual credit with limits and brokers (Sardex) — simple ledgers everyone can see.
                Why it worksIt's the only free-rider solution the record shows working for decades. Keeping score isn't the opposite of generosity — it's what protects generosity from burning out.
                4

                Build the cash edge on purpose, from day one

                The community sells something outward — produce, crafts, services — and holds a shared cash fund that pays the taxes, insurance and licence fees the law demands in dollars: roughly $8,000–9,200 a year per homeowner, and barter itself is taxed.

                How it worksOne foot in the cash economy, deliberately — Twin Oaks has done exactly this since 1967; its current cash-earning businesses are tofu, book indexing and seed-exchange work (its long-time hammock business was lost to a 2024 wildfire; no verified current income total exists, so none is printed).
                Why it worksThe cash border is law, not attitude. Planning for it is the difference between a community and a countdown to a tax lien.
                5

                Start where the surplus already is

                29% of all food grown is never eaten — 70 million tons a year (ReFED 2026, 2024 data). Millions of homes sit empty while 745,652 people were counted homeless on a single night (HUD, Jan 2025). The first work of the circuit is matching what already exists to who already needs it.

                How it worksFood recovery runs, shared tools and vehicles, spare rooms matched to people — logistics, done by the transport and care crews from Step 1.
                Why it worksThe surplus is real but scattered and perishable, so matching it is genuine work with genuine payoff — the fastest visible proof that the circuit feeds people.
                6

                Be a node, not an island

                Link neighbourhoods into a city and regional circuit big enough to support a hospital, a pharmacy supply chain, fuel and parts. A 1,000-person unit shares food and care beautifully — it cannot make insulin.

                How it worksFederate early: local circles for trust and daily needs, wider rings for the specialised goods only scale can make.
                Why it worksSurvival-level self-sufficiency is modelled at ~100–500 people; modern essentials need millions. Pretending otherwise is how communities end up choosing between purity and medicine.
                7

                Stay legal — and expect the legal fight

                Licences kept current, taxes paid from the cash fund, books open to anyone who asks. And no illusions: Wörgl worked, and was still shut down by the central bank in 14 months.

                How it worksDesign so nothing depends on issuing money the state claims as its monopoly — credits inside a community are a greyer, safer country than printed currency.
                Why it worksLegal suppression is the roadblock that ended the most successful experiment on record. Surviving it is a design requirement, not an afterthought.
                8

                Plan for the dip

                Rotate the heavy roles. Recruit continuously, not just at the exciting start. Rest people before they burn out, and write down how everything works so no single person is load-bearing.

                How it worksSuccession and rest are scheduled like any other essential job — because they are one.
                Why it worksMutual aid grew from 50 groups to over 1,000 — then shrank when volunteers had to return to paid work, and Ithaca HOURS faded with its founder. Attrition, not opposition, is what usually ends these things.

                And the "everybody at once" question, answered straight: the record contains no case of a whole society switching at once — and the cases that came closest (rationing, Cuba) were state-run emergencies, not free choices. What the record does contain is communities that started small, kept score, paid their cash border, and lasted decades. The thriving spreads the way Twin Oaks and Sardex spread: by working, visibly, where people can see it — one freely convinced person at a time.

                Part ten

                What AI could do to make the switch easier

                The plan in Part nine runs on coordination: knowing who does what, matching surplus to need, keeping score fairly, and teaching each step as it is taken. That is exactly the kind of work an AI assistant is built for. So here is the honest version of what AI like this could contribute — for anyone who wants it, and only for them. Never forced. Nobody has to join, nobody has to use an AI to join, and leaving stays as open as arriving. The AI is a tool people pick up, not a door anybody is pushed through.

                The rule that governs everything below: AI suggests, people decide. Every use here is opt-in — a person or a community chooses it, sees what it is doing, and can switch it off or walk away at any point, the same way Step 8 says no single person should ever be load-bearing. If a use of AI ever needs pressure to work, that use has failed the plan's own test, and the honest answer is to drop it, not to push harder.

                1

                Map the circuit before anyone commits

                Step 1 says count the jobs first, the crowd second. An AI can do that counting fast: take a town's real workforce — who grows, drives, nurses, repairs, keeps water and power running — and show, role by role, what a money-free circuit there would actually need and where the gaps are, the same arithmetic Part four does by hand, done for any place that asks.

                How it worksPublic workforce and census data go in; a clear map of essential roles, coverage and holes comes out, in plain language.
                Why it worksPeople can only volunteer for a plan they can see. A map turns "someday, somehow" into named jobs a real person can choose — or decline.
                2

                Match surplus to need, while it's still good

                Part three's problem is logistics, not scarcity: a third or more of food is grown and never eaten because it is scattered and perishable. An AI is good at exactly that matching — this much produce, expiring then, needed there, a driver free now — and at the same matching for spare rooms, shared tools, vehicles and skills.

                How it worksOffers and needs are listed by the people who hold them; the AI pairs them, routes them, and reminds before things spoil.
                Why it worksMatching is genuine work, and it never sleeps. Automating the pairing leaves the human work — growing, cooking, driving, caring — to humans, where it belongs.
                3

                Keep the score, visibly and fairly

                Step 3's lesson from Twin Oaks and Sardex is that generosity lasts when the accounting is explicit. An AI can keep that ledger tirelessly: hours given and received, balances anyone can inspect, credit limits applied the same way to everyone — including care work, counted first rather than last, so it stops being invisible.

                How it worksA shared, readable record — every entry checkable by the people in it, every rule the community agreed written next to the numbers.
                Why it worksIt answers Roadblock 4 (free-rider strain) without a boss: fairness people can verify is what lets them keep being generous.
                4

                Coordinate across nodes, so small stays viable

                Step 6 says a neighbourhood survives as a node in a bigger circuit, not as an island. Coordination between circles — who has a surplus of what, which town needs a specialist this week, when the shared truck runs — is the work that thins out as groups grow (Roadblock 5). An AI can carry that traffic: scheduling, translation between groups, and a shared picture of the whole circuit that no single coordinator has to hold in their head.

                How it worksEach circle keeps its own decisions; the AI only moves information between them — requests, offers, timings — in everyone's own language.
                Why it worksTrust is built face to face and stays local; information is what needs to travel. Letting a machine carry the information protects the trust instead of replacing it.
                5

                Teach every step as it's taken

                Every step in this plan arrives with its How It Works and its Why It Works, so nobody is just following orders. An AI can do that at the scale of one person and one second: answer "what do I do right now?" with the next doable step, explain how and why it works, in the asker's own language and at their own pace — bookkeeping for the cash fund, licence requirements for a clinic, the first season of a community garden.

                How it worksAsk, get the step and the reasoning together, do it, ask the next one. The doing teaches the step.
                Why it worksThe goal was never permanent guidance — it's people who no longer need a guide. Teaching that rides along with the work is how the scaffolding comes away.
                6

                Watch the borders so they never ambush anyone

                The cash border (Part eight) and the licence border (Part six) are law, with deadlines: taxes, insurance, spectrum and franchise fees, inspections. An AI is a tireless calendar — tracking what is owed, when, and in what currency; keeping the books open; flagging a deadline months before it becomes a lien — so the community's one foot in the cash economy never trips it. Part eleven adds one more border of exactly this kind: even forgiven debt can arrive with a dollar tax bill attached.

                How it worksThe fund's obligations live in one visible list the AI maintains and nags about; humans make every payment decision.
                Why it worksWörgl was ended by a courtroom, not by failure. Staying legal is a design requirement (Step 7), and vigilance is cheap for a machine and expensive for a tired volunteer.
                7

                Rehearse the switch before living it

                Before anyone changes how they eat, work or pay a bill, an AI can run the plan against the numbers: what happens at this size, with these roles missing, in a bad season, when a founder leaves? The honest gaps in Part fifteen — the contested figures, the unknown minimum sizes — can be tested as ranges instead of wishes, so a community chooses its risks with its eyes open.

                How it worksThe Part four arithmetic, rerun for any town and any assumption, with every assumption labelled as one.
                Why it worksA switch people rehearse is a switch people chose. The plan spreads by working visibly; a rehearsal is the cheapest visible proof there is.
                8

                Write it all down, so nobody is load-bearing

                Ithaca HOURS faded when its founder left; mutual aid thinned when its volunteers burned out (Roadblock 6). An AI can hold the community's memory: how everything works, written down as it is learned, searchable by anyone, in plain words — rotas, supplier arrangements, what failed last winter and why — so knowledge belongs to the circuit, not to whoever happens to remember it.

                Finding things is a tool under this same umbrella, offered free: The Doorway — ask it anything, and it works offline.

                How it worksDecisions and know-how are recorded as they happen; the AI organises and retrieves them, and anyone can read or correct the record.
                Why it worksEnthusiasm is a fuel that runs out; structure is what remains. A memory that outlives its founders is structure.

                What AI cannot do — stated as plainly as the rest of this page: it cannot grow food, drive a truck, nurse a patient or fix a pump; the essential work stays human, which is the whole point of Part two. It cannot make anyone's choice for them, and any version of this that used AI to nudge, pressure or enrol people would contradict the plan it claims to serve — a switch people didn't choose isn't freedom. And it can be wrong: its maps, matches and forecasts need the same checking this page gives every other number, with the sources shown and the hedges kept. Used inside those limits — freely chosen, openly checked, easy to leave — AI lowers the cost of coordinating until the coordination mostly runs itself, and the people are left with the part that was always theirs: the work, and each other.

                Part eleven

                If debts were erased — by choice

                Part eight left one door shut: existing debts don't pause because a debtor renounced money — default means foreclosure, garnishment, exclusion. So open the same door from the other side. What if the debts were erased — by the people holding them, freely? Landlords letting arrears go, lenders cancelling balances, neighbours tearing up IOUs, strangers buying up other people's debt in order to abolish it. All of it choice-based, in both directions: the creditor chooses to let go, the debtor chooses what to do with the freedom — join the circuit, pay it forward, or simply breathe. A forgiveness nobody chose isn't forgiveness; it's seizure with better branding, and this plan doesn't run on that.

                The pile: $18.8 trillion

                US household debt stood at $18.8 trillion at the end of 2025: mortgages $13.17 trillion, auto loans $1.67 trillion, student loans $1.66 trillion, credit cards $1.28 trillion (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, Household Debt & Credit, Q4 2025). Medical debt sits partly outside those totals — Americans owed at least $220 billion in medical bills (Peterson-KFF analysis, Feb 2024 — the figure holds, corroborated; its primary page is link-unverified, flagged in the Sources), with 14 million adults owing more than $1,000 of it.

                Scale matters for honesty: erasing "debt" in general means erasing claims of very different kinds — a mortgage is a house, held by bond investors; a medical bill is a hospital's receivable; an IOU between neighbours is a relationship.

                Erasure already happens — chosen, at scale

                The Rolling Jubilee (Strike Debt, from 2012) raised roughly $700,000 in donations and used about $400,000 of it to buy distressed debt on the secondary market — where it trades for pennies on the dollar — abolishing $13.5 million of medical debt for ~2,700 people, plus $1.2 million of other personal debt; later tallies put its total abolished above $30 million. Undue Medical Debt (founded 2014 by two former debt-collection executives) industrialised the same move: it reports roughly $100 of medical debt abolished per $1 donated, and by its own count more than $49 billion relieved for over 31 million people (charity's figures, via Wikipedia, Oct 2026 — self-reported, so read them as its claim, not an audit).

                Nobody was forced in either case: donors chose to give, sellers chose to sell, and the debtors' only part was opening a letter that said the debt was gone.

                The ancient precedent — and its difference

                Debt erasure is older than coinage. In Bronze Age Mesopotamia, new rulers proclaimed "clean slates" — crop debts annulled, bondservants freed, land returned — and the biblical Jubilee (Leviticus 25) carried the same reset into law: debts cancelled and land restored in the fiftieth year. Those resets worked often enough that societies repeated them for millennia.

                But note the mechanism: they were decrees. A king or a law imposed them on creditors who had not chosen them. The version on this page keeps the reset and removes the decree — slower, patchier, and the only kind this plan allows.

                1

                Forgive what you yourself hold

                The simplest form needs no system at all: a landlord cancels arrears, a lender writes off a loan, a business voids an invoice, a family member releases a family debt. The holder of the claim is the one person who can erase it by a pure act of choice — and people do, constantly, at kitchen-table scale. What changes in this scenario is only the scale and the openness: doing it deliberately, and saying so, so others can see it's allowed.

                How it worksOne signature, one letter, one ledger line struck — by the creditor, nobody else.
                Why it worksIt is the one form of erasure with no intermediary and no coercion anywhere in it. It also frees the debtor's future income immediately — no garnishment, no lien, no escrow needed before they can join anything.
                2

                Buy strangers' debt — and abolish it

                Debts the original creditor has given up on are sold in bundles for a fraction of face value, normally to collectors who then pursue the full amount. The Rolling Jubilee and Undue Medical Debt run the same purchase in reverse: buy the bundle, cancel every debt in it, notify the debtors. Because the discount is so deep, small chosen gifts retire large chosen-by-nobody burdens — the medical-debt version turns on the order of $1 into $100 of relief.

                How it worksDonations or a community's cash fund (Step 4) buy distressed portfolios at market price; the buyer, now the creditor, forgives them all.
                Why it worksEvery link is voluntary — the seller sold, the donor gave, the buyer forgave. It plugs straight into the plan: a circuit's cash edge could retire its own members' outside medical and collection debts first.
                3

                Cancel what the circle owes itself

                Inside a community, debts are often a web, not a line: A owes B, B owes C, C owes A. By mutual agreement the web can be netted — offsetting claims cancelled against each other until only real, remaining balances survive, or none. This is erasure by unanimous consent of everyone in the web, and it is quietly what the Part nine ledger does going forward: hours given and received inside the circuit are a record, not a trap, and a community can choose to zero its own books at an agreed moment — its own small jubilee, decreed by nobody but its members.

                How it worksEveryone holding and owing inside the web agrees the offsets; the ledger shows the cancellation the same way it showed the debts.
                Why it worksConsent is total by construction — a single "no" keeps that person's claim alive. Nothing is taken; balances people no longer want to hold over each other simply stop existing.
                4

                Use the legal doors that already stand open

                Choice-based erasure doesn't have to invent its machinery. A debtor can choose to file bankruptcy and receive a court discharge; a creditor can choose a negotiated settlement for less than face; lenders can choose forbearance, modification or outright release. These are existing, lawful doors — anyone anywhere can walk through the ones their jurisdiction offers, which is this plan's whole method applied to debt: legally open a closed door.

                How it worksNothing new is built; the debtor or creditor uses discharge, settlement or release procedures that already exist in law.
                Why it worksIt survives Step 7's test — staying legal — and it needs no threshold of adopters. One filing, one settlement, one release at a time.

                Keeping the record of it straight is what Any Legal Case — The Record is for — free of charge.

                The honest costs — erasure is a door, not a wand: (1) It cancels a claim, not a need. A forgiven medical bill doesn't treat the next illness; a cleared mortgage doesn't heat the house. Erasure clears the past so the circuit in Part nine can carry the future — it does not replace it. (2) The creditor's loss is real. Behind an institution stand depositors, pension funds and bondholders — much mortgage debt has no single owner who can choose, because it was bundled and sold to thousands of investors; the most erasable debts are the ones a nameable holder still holds. (3) The tax sting: in the US, cancelled debt of $600 or more is generally reported on Form 1099-C and treated as taxable income to the debtor — with exclusions for bankruptcy and insolvency (IRS) — so a chosen forgiveness can hand the freed person a dollar tax bill: the cash border of Part eight, arriving by the back door. (4) If forgiveness ever became expected rather than chosen, lenders would price it in — tighter credit, higher rates — which is one more reason the choice has to stay real: a gift that can be demanded stops being given.

                Both directions, same honesty: every number and scenario on this page runs in either direction — growth and decline — with the core tenet intact. Forward, the evidence is the tipping figures in Part one and the cases that lasted in Part five. Backward, it is the same record read the other way: mutual aid shrinking from 1,000+ groups as volunteers returned to paid work, Ithaca HOURS going dormant, time banks lapsing, communities staying small on purpose — and, in the fullest version, people freely choosing the token again: leaving the circuit, taking wages, charging money, with no penalty and no locked door behind them. Exit stays as open as entrance in every scenario, and "never forced" cuts both ways — nobody is forced in, and nobody is forced to stay. Any scenario that requires force to reach its ending — a jubilee by decree, a circuit that can't be left — is struck from this plan, not softened.

                Part twelve

                A chosen clean slate — no flood, just expansion

                Everything on this page runs on one mechanism, and it has a name: FREE CHOSEN EXPANSION. Free — no paywall, no fee, no cost of entry. Chosen — every step voluntary, every exit open, never forced. Expansion — growth by addition, no flood, no destruction. The old resets were imagined as a great flood: wipe the world to wipe the slate. This one wipes nothing. It is a clean slate people choose for themselves, built entirely from everything we already have available to us — the surplus of Part three, the essential workers of Part two, the community forms already running at scale in Part six, and the legal doors already standing open in Part eleven.

                Free

                No paywall, no fee, no cost of entry. The plan costs nothing to read, copy or retell, and joining a circuit asks for a person's work, not their money — exactly the terms the lasting cases in Part five already run on.

                Chosen

                Every step voluntary, every exit open, never forced. People enter one at a time, by their own decision, and can leave the same way, with no penalty and no locked door behind them — the same rule that governs the AI of Part ten and the debt erasure of Part eleven governs the whole.

                Expansion

                Growth by addition, no flood, no destruction. The circuit grows alongside what already exists — paying its cash border, using the systems already built — and spreads by addition: one person, one role, one community at a time, until thriving is simply the bigger thing.

                Part thirteen

                The arts in the circuit — post it and let it happen

                Music is the clearest test of the whole idea, because its toll-booths are the most visible. Today a song travels a permission chain — aggregator, platform, royalty collection — each link taking its cut for letting the work move. In a working circuit that chain is dead weight. The simplest distribution is the honest one: post it, freely, and let it travel — the same relay everything else rides. Nothing about the work gets poorer. What changes is where the credit lives.

                Credit lives in the record

                Credit doesn't disappear when the toll-booths do — it moves. Instead of travelling with a payment, credit travels with the act itself: the record says who wrote it, who played it, who recorded it, the way liner notes always did. The work carries its makers' names wherever it goes, and no booth stands between the work and the listener.

                Tags are claimed, never imposed

                A tag on a work sits there to be claimed by whoever wants it — a collaborator, a venue, a scene — and imposed on nobody. It is the mirror of the standing rule: never tag anyone without their yes. Here the yes runs the other way: the tag waits, open, and each person decides for themselves whether to pick it up. Opt-in from both directions.

                Origin is a fact, not a grade

                The record also states how a thing was made — human, AI, or the blend — plain on its face, the way "recorded live" is a fact on an album. Not as a good thing and not as a bad thing: as information people deserve to have. The moment an origin mark becomes a gold star or a scarlet letter, it stops being the record and becomes a toll-booth again.

                And the line that settles it: a person at a desk with a program, mixing and mastering, is already riding machine intelligence the whole session — the tuning, the mastering chain, the noise cleanup, half the tools in the rack. There has not been a purely "human" recording in years; there is one spectrum of hands and machines, and everybody is somewhere on it. So the origin mark can't be a verdict — there would be nobody left unmarked to hand verdicts down. It is simply credits: engineer, mixer, mastering, machine — all in the same small print, none of it a charge. The work speaks from there.

                Part fourteen

                How money-models actually make their money

                Before deciding what to say no to, it helps to see how the machines on the other side actually work. Every business model — honest, extractive or outright fraud — answers one question: where does the payout come from? From a product someone outside the system wanted? From a spread, a rent, a pooled risk? Or from the next person through the door? Work through the shapes below and a pattern emerges: the models that serve create value for someone who isn't paying to be inside the model. The models that extract — and the frauds that collapse — feed on the participants themselves.

                First, the frauds — pyramid and Ponzi

                Pyramid schemes Payouts = recruits' money

                The mechanism — You pay to join, and your income comes from recruiting people below you, whose joining fees and purchases flow up the levels. There may be a product on the shelf, but it is decoration: the money that pays you entered through the wallets of newer participants, not from customers outside.

                Why it appears to work — At the start the base is tiny and inflow is greater than outflow, so the first levels genuinely get paid — in real money, on time. Those real payouts are the advertising. Every paid early joiner is proof, shown to the next round, that "it works."

                Why collapse is guaranteed — The maths, not morale. If each person must recruit 6 more, level 10 alone needs over 60 million people and level 13 needs more people than Earth holds. Growth must be exponential; the population is finite. When recruiting slows — and it must — the bottom levels, always the large majority, have nobody left to pay them. A pyramid doesn't fail when people lose faith. It fails when it runs out of people, which was true on day one. Try it in the calculator below.

                The record — This is not theory in court. In FTC v. BurnLounge, the Ninth Circuit (2014) held a music-store MLM was an illegal pyramid because its cash rewards were tied to recruitment rather than sales to ultimate users — upholding the Koscot test and a $16.2 million redress order. The border is policed case by case, which is why honest negatives matter: in 2016 Herbalife paid $200 million and was forced to restructure so rewards track verifiable retail sales — and the FTC still stopped short of calling it a pyramid scheme. And the earnings reality underneath the pitches: the FTC's September 2024 staff analysis of 70 MLM income disclosures found the vast majority of participants received $1,000 a year or less — before expenses — and in at least 17 of the companies reviewed, most participants received nothing at all.

                Ponzi schemes Payouts = new investors' money

                The mechanism — One operator in the middle promises investment returns — high, steady, low-risk. There is little or no real investing. The "returns" paid to earlier investors are simply money collected from newer ones, exactly as the SEC defines it: "an investment fraud that pays existing investors with funds collected from new investors." Statements show profits that were never earned.

                Why it appears to work — Unlike a pyramid, victims don't recruit — they just watch balances grow and early withdrawals get honoured promptly. That smoothness is the tell the SEC lists first among its red flags: high returns with little or no risk, and returns that stay oddly consistent whatever the market does. Each honoured withdrawal recruits the next investor better than any pitch could.

                Why collapse is guaranteed — The scheme books promises faster than real money arrives: owed returns compound, real earnings are near zero, so survival needs a constant, growing flow of new money. The moment inflow slows, or many investors ask to cash out at once, there is nothing underneath — redemptions exceed deposits and it ends in a day, after looking solid for years.

                The record — Madoff — The largest known case shows both numbers at once. Client statements showed about $64.8 billion — paper balances, most of it profits that never existed. The cash actually lost was far smaller and is still stated only as a range (roughly $17–19 billion depending on the accounting method), so this page prints the paper total and the recoveries as the hard numbers: the SIPA trustee recovered $14.72 billion, and the Justice Department's Madoff Victim Fund made its final distribution on 30 December 2024, bringing its total to $4.3 billion paid to 40,930 victims in 127 countries. Note the two different deaths, too: a pyramid dies of saturation — it runs out of recruits. A Ponzi dies of redemptions — it can look solvent for decades until withdrawals arrive faster than new money.

                Pyramid, Ponzi, or legitimate MLM? The distinctions

                • Who recruits? In a pyramid, the participants must recruit — recruiting is the business. In a Ponzi, nobody recruits; one operator collects from passive investors. In a legitimate multi-level marketing business, participants can earn by selling, whether or not they recruit.
                • Where does the money come from? Pyramid: fees and purchases of the recruits themselves. Ponzi: deposits of newer investors. Legitimate MLM: retail sales to ultimate users — people who buy the product because they want the product.
                • The legal test. The FTC's standard, from its 1975 Koscot decision, still used today: a plan is a pyramid when participants pay for (1) the right to sell a product and (2) the right to rewards for recruiting others that are unrelated to sales to ultimate users. A real product on the shelf does not save a plan whose rewards run on recruitment — regulators judge how it operates in practice, including "inventory loading," where recruits buy stock mainly to qualify for bonuses. BurnLounge (9th Cir. 2014) refined the point: rewards do not have to be completely unrelated to product sales — if recruiting is what the rewards primarily pay for, the plan fails the test.
                • What kills each? A pyramid dies of arithmetic — it runs out of recruits. A Ponzi dies of flow — new money slows or withdrawals spike. A legitimate business can shrink, stagnate or fail like any business, but nothing in its design requires endless exponential growth to pay yesterday's promises.

                Second, the bank — the model underneath all the others

                1

                Deposits in, loans out — and the spread between

                The core trade is simple to state: a bank pays depositors a lower rate of interest and charges borrowers a higher one, and keeps the difference — the spread. The spread is not pure profit: out of it come the real costs of screening borrowers, running payments, holding branches and systems, and absorbing the loans that never get repaid. On top of the spread sit fees — accounts, cards, transfers, penalties — which need no lending at all. The spread, measured across the whole industry: the FDIC's net interest margin for US banks was 3.28% in the fourth quarter of 2024 — the gap between what earning assets yield and what funding costs, before those costs, losses and fees are counted. (An industry-wide total for fee income was not pulled in this pass — see Part fifteen — so no fee-vs-spread split is printed as a number here.)

                2

                Lending creates deposits — the part most people get backwards

                The textbook picture — banks gather deposits, then lend them out — is not how it works. The Bank of England states it plainly in its own explainer: when a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a deposit that did not exist before; repayment deletes it again. Banks create money by lending, limited not by a pile of deposits but by capital rules, by whether borrowers look likely to repay, and by needing enough central-bank reserves to settle payments to other banks. That is why the money supply grows and shrinks with lending itself — and why a credit boom inflates the money in the system, not just moves it around.

                3

                The built-in fragility: mismatched maturities

                A bank's deposits can be withdrawn today; its loans come back over years. That gap — borrowing short, lending long — is the service (your savings stay spendable while a mortgage lasts decades) and also the weakness. If too many depositors ask at once, no bank, however honestly run, holds enough cash on hand: that is a bank run. The modern speed record: on 9 March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank's customers withdrew $42 billion in a single day — about a quarter of its deposits — with roughly $100 billion more in requests queued for the next day, according to the Federal Reserve's inspector-general review; regulators closed the bank on 10 March. Runs are why deposit insurance and central-bank lending exist — the system knows its own shape needs a backstop. The other failure modes follow the same shape: loans sour in a downturn all at once, the spread that looked like earnings turns out to have been underpriced risk, and fees quietly become the most reliable income precisely because they carry no risk at all.

                Where it servesPayments that clear, savings that stay liquid, risk screened and carried by an institution instead of a neighbour — real work, worth a real spread.
                Where it turns extractiveWhen the spread and fees grow past the cost and risk they cover — penalty fees aimed at the poorest accounts, complexity that hides the true price, and risk whose losses land on the public backstop while the gains stayed private.

                Third, the typical shapes — how each one makes money, why it works, where it turns

                ModelHow it makes moneyWhy it works — and where it fails or turns extractive
                Retail markupthe oldest shape there is

                Buys or makes a thing for less than it sells it for; the margin covers premises, staff, spoilage and risk. At the largest scale on Earth: Walmart's gross margin was 24.1% in its fiscal 2025 — roughly 24 cents of every sales dollar left to cover everything else.

                Works because the value is visible — you can compare the price with the thing in your hand. Turns when the seller controls the comparison itself: monopoly shelves, hidden fees at the till, or margins on necessities where the buyer has no walk-away power.

                Subscriptionpay every month, use any time

                A recurring fee for continuing access — software, streaming, a gym, a club. Income is predictable, so the business can plan and invest. The proof of scale: Netflix took in $39.0 billion in 2024 from about 302 million paid memberships.

                Works when the service keeps being worth it month after month — retention is earned. Turns when the model starts farming forgetfulness: hard cancellations, silent renewals, and pricing that depends on subscribers not using what they pay for.

                Marketplace commissiontake a cut for matching

                Brings buyers and sellers together — rides, rooms, crafts, apps — and keeps a percentage of each transaction for the matching, trust and payment plumbing. Airbnb's standard take is now a single 15.5% host fee on the booking subtotal; its older split fee (~3% from the host, ~14% from the guest) came to roughly the same total.

                Works because finding each other was the hard part, and the platform genuinely solves it. Turns once both sides are locked in: the cut climbs, the platform competes with its own sellers, and the matchmaker becomes a toll-booth on a road it no longer maintains.

                Freemiumfree tier, paid tier

                The basic version is free; a minority pay for more capacity, features or convenience, and their fees carry the free majority.

                Works when the free tier is honestly usable and the paid tier is honestly better — sampling at zero risk. Turns when the free tier is deliberately hobbled or becomes the product in the advertising sense — see below — and "free" users discover they were the inventory.

                Advertising / attentionthe audience is what's sold

                The service is free to users because advertisers pay for access to the users' attention and data. Two customers, one product — and the user isn't the paying one. The scale: Google advertising brought Alphabet $264.59 billion in 2024 — over three-quarters of everything the company took in (Form 10-K).

                Works — genuinely — for funding things nobody would pay for directly. Turns structurally: the incentive is to maximise time and emotional arousal, not the user's good, because attention is the crop. Of all the honest models, this is the one whose interests point most reliably away from its users.

                Razor-and-bladescheap handle, dear blades

                Sell the durable platform cheap — printer, console, coffee machine — and earn the real margin on the consumables only it can use. Honest limit: companies do not disclose handle-vs-blade margins in their filings, so no verified margin figure exists to print here — this row is mechanism only, on purpose.

                Works when the total cost stays fair and visible: a cheap machine with reasonably priced refills is an honest bundle. Turns when lock-in is the point — chipped cartridges, blocked third-party refills — and the cheap handle was bait for a captive aftermarket.

                Franchiserent out a proven recipe

                The brand owner licenses its name, system and supply chain to local operators for an upfront fee plus a continuing royalty on their sales.

                Works because both sides bring something real — a tested recipe and brand trust from one side, local capital and labour from the other. Turns when royalties are charged on sales, not profit, so the franchisor eats even in the franchisee's losing months, and when territories are oversold to collect fees — sliding toward the pyramid shape: income from recruiting operators rather than from customers eating.

                Lending / interestthe bank model, everywhere

                Money now, more money back later: the interest is rent on money plus a price for the risk it never returns. Mortgages, cards, payday loans — same shape, different prices.

                Works when it moves a purchase a person can truly carry — a home, a tool — from someday to today, at a price they understood. Turns as the price climbs and the clarity falls: at the extreme end, the business performs best when the borrower almost can't pay — rolling balances, fees on fees — which is rent on desperation, not on money.

                Insurance risk-poolingmany pay in, few claim

                Collect premiums from many people, invest the float, pay the claims of the unlucky few, keep the difference between premiums in and claims plus costs out.

                Works — beautifully, in principle — because it is mutual aid with arithmetic: a catastrophe one family can't survive becomes a small shared cost. Turns when profit grows by not paying: exclusions in fine print, claims friction as strategy, and pricing people out of the risks they most need pooled.

                Cooperative / mutualowned by the people in it

                Same trades as above — retail, banking, insurance, power — but the surplus belongs to the members: workers, customers or policyholders. Mondragón (Part five) and mutual insurers and credit unions run this shape at full scale — US credit unions alone: 4,572 federally insured institutions, 140.4 million members, $2.31 trillion in assets (NCUA, Q1 2024). The bank model above, owned by its depositors.

                Works because the extraction question dissolves — there is no outside owner for the surplus to leak to, so prices, wages and reserves get argued out among the people affected. Fails the ordinary ways: slow decisions, hard capital-raising, and the market still biting from outside (Fagor, Part five). Its failures are business failures, not design frauds.

                Public ownershipthe state as the owner

                It mostly doesn't aim to: services are funded by taxation and run at cost or below — the postal service, public power (Part six), roads, water. On the headcount, both published bases are shown rather than blended: the 55 million figure stands on the APPA / EIA-861 2022 basis, while APPA's current public-power page describes the sector as serving more than 49 million people — the difference is basis and date, and Part six carries the 55 million figure on the 2022 basis, with the other beside it.

                Works where the thing is a natural monopoly or a universal need, and "profit" would just be a tax collected by a company instead. Fails by politics rather than arithmetic: underinvestment when budgets are squeezed, and no price signal to show what things cost. The honest comparison is never "public vs perfect" — it is public vs the private version of the same pipes.

                The test this page applies to every shape — including its own plan: ask where the payout comes from. If it comes from value delivered to someone outside the payment chain — a customer who wanted the thing, a borrower who carried the loan, a risk genuinely pooled — the model can run forever without needing the world to grow. If it comes from the participants themselves — their fees, their attention, their desperation, their recruits — then growth is not its success, it is its fuel, and the only questions are how fast it burns and who is standing at the bottom when it stops. The circuit in Part nine is built to fail this test's bad side on purpose: no joining fees, no recruitment rewards, no level paid by the level below — hours counted for work done, and that is all.

                Part sixteen

                What if all of this happened at once?

                This is the last part because it depends on all the others. Every part before this one tests a single thread on its own — the essential core, the surplus, the score-keeping, the cash edge, the unlocked networks, the erased debts, the coordination. This part asks the question the whole page has been building toward: what happens when every mechanism in the plan fires together, at the same time, instead of one at a time?

                The honest method, stated up front: combine only the earlier parts' own figures and findings, exactly as they stand there with their sources; name where the mechanisms compound — each one making another stronger — and where they collide — each one making another harder. No combined figure is invented: nobody has researched the all-at-once picture as a single case, so no new number for it is printed here.

                Where it compounds

                The core is in, and it can finally coordinate. The narrow essential core — 18.8% of jobs, about 32 million people (Part two) — stops being the bottleneck it is in every one-at-a-time run the moment the federation of Part nine and the coordination of Part ten are running with it: the same crews, no longer stranded in separate, unconnected attempts.

                The surplus starts moving. 29% of the food supply going uneaten (Part three) is a logistics problem; with drivers in, score kept kindly, surplus matched from day one, and the networks unlocked and free to use (Parts six and nine), the matching stops depending on who can pay for the connection.

                The borders shrink together. Debts erased by choice (Part eleven) lower the cash each household must still find, and a chosen clean slate (Part twelve) means new circuits start without the old drag — so the cash edge of Part nine has less border left to defend.

                Proof stops being the bottleneck. Each lasting case in Part five worked in isolation and stayed small. Working visibly together — the arts posted freely (Part thirteen) travelling on unlocked networks — is the compound the one-at-a-time record never got to show.

                Where it collides

                The cash border does not vanish. Even with debts erased, taxes, insurance and fees still demand roughly $8,000–9,200 a year per mortgage-free homeowner (Part eight) — and forgiven debt can itself arrive with a dollar tax bill (Part eleven). All at once does not repeal that arithmetic.

                Visibility invites the legal fight. The most successful single experiment on record, Wörgl, was shut down by a courtroom, not by failure (Parts five and seven). Everything firing together is harder to ignore — Step 7's "stay legal" stops being a precaution and becomes the front line.

                The weakest role still binds. The Test Lab's lesson survives the combination: a circuit is only as strong as its weakest essential role. All at once raises every role together — it does not let any one of them be skipped.

                One gate is not in the circuit's hands. Unlock-and-allow (Step 2) is a decision for the carriers and providers who hold the keys. The plan can ask for it early and plainly; it cannot grant it to itself.

                Status — still being figured out, printed as such: the combined picture above is a synthesis of this page's own parts, and the interactions themselves are unverified. Which compounds dominate, which collisions bite first, and what the all-at-once version does to the numbers in Parts two and four have not yet been researched as a combined case — that work is still ahead, and until it is done, this part prints no combined figure and claims no combined verdict. What is verified is each thread on its own, in its own part, with its sources. (This is a different question from the one Part nine's closing note answers: the record contains no case of a whole society switching at once. This part is about the plan's own mechanisms firing together — a question the plan can keep working, in the open, exactly the way this page works everything else.)

                The compass

                How to move around this page

                The round button in the lane at the right is a compass — a clean white face in a thin slate ring, in the exact slate of the resignations page’s compass, with a “Navigate” pill underneath it. Its face is kept elegant and simple: one fine inner ring, four plain tick marks, and a single two-tone needle — no N, E, S or W letters on it at all. It owns that lane at every scroll position, so it can never cover a card, a chart, or a line of text. On a phone it rests halfway down that lane, where your thumb sits while you hold and scroll. Tap it and it blooms into a dial. Every point on the dial is named by the job it does — plain place names and jobs, no compass letters to memorise.

                • Short answer takes you to the Surface — the short answer just below the top. Next part and Previous part walk you one part forward or back, landing on that part's closed door — whether to open it is your call. Sources takes you to the appendix.
                • Top of page and Bottom of page sit under the dial, and in Go to: Top of page goes to the very top, above the free banner, and Bottom of page goes to the very end, at the footer. Neither one is the short answer — that has its own point, above.
                • The centre of the dial names your last place — tap it and the compass simply closes: you're done, and you stay where you are. Previous part is how you go back.
                • The ? point is Why: it explains the place you're standing in by six questions — WHO · WHAT · WHEN · WHERE · WHY · HOW.
                • The ≡ point is Go to: every place on this page, listed — the Test Lab, all sixteen parts, and the sources. Nothing in the Go to list leaves this page.
                • Other pages sits under the dial, beneath Top and Bottom, and opens the Go to panel at its Other pages section: one panel, arranged the way the resignations page arranges it — “Go to — on this page” first, then Other pages below it, holding the doors off this page. The Test Runs, the Doorway, the Poster Board, Any Legal Case — The Record, Go For It?, and this page’s three deep dives. Nothing appears in both lists: a destination is either on this page, in Go to, or off it, under Other pages.
                • The Key point opens the legend — every symbol on the dial, its name, and what it means.
                • The ▶ point goes straight to the Test Lab, where the plan can be run — and broken — in play.
                • The dial stays open when you tap a point — it never folds away on its own. Land, look, and tap another point if you are not where you want to be; it closes only when you tap the compass button again. The Go to list is the exception: picking a place there closes the list as the place opens, so it never sits on top of where you landed. The centre is the other exception: tapping it just closes the compass — you're done, and you stay where you are; Previous part is how you go back.
                • Parts remember where you left them. Close a part partway through — with its floating Close, its bottom Close, or its door — and reopening its door takes you back to the spot you closed from, not the top.

                1. Download it while things are normal. Save the file on one good device — a phone, tablet, or computer — before you ever need it offline. That device is now the host.
                2. When there’s no internet, turn on that device’s hotspot. Look in Settings for “Hotspot” or “Personal Hotspot.” A hotspot makes a small local network by itself. Other devices nearby can join it. They use its name and password. They do not need the internet to connect to each other. But the hotspot alone does not hand over the file. It only connects the devices. The next step is how the file travels.
                3. Get the file onto the other devices — pick either way:
                  • Send it directly, device to device — the simplest route. Once devices can see each other (or even without the hotspot), send the one file by Bluetooth, Quick Share (Android), AirDrop (Apple), or a USB cable. Every device that receives it ends up with its own full copy — no connection needed ever again on that device.
                  • Or serve it locally from the host — an option, not a promise on every phone. Some devices have a local sharing or file-server app: running on the host, it gives a local address (a string of numbers the app shows you). Others join the host’s hotspot, open that address in their browser, and read or download the page from the host. If your device has no such app, use the direct send above — it does the same job.
                4. Keep the host alive. Sharing drains a battery faster. Plug the host into whatever power you have — a power bank, a car charger, solar, even a hand crank — and let the other devices save their own copies while it lasts.
