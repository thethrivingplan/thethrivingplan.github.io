The Ruler's Test — Friedrich Merz (Germany)

Subject: Friedrich Merz, Chancellor of Germany (in office since 6 May 2025), leader of the CDU since January 2022; previously Bundestag member (1994–2009, 2021– ), CDU/CSU parliamentary leader, and chairman of the supervisory board of BlackRock Asset Management Deutschland (2016–2020). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Both shelves are shown for every test: the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION; contested items are described with who disputes them. Tenure note: His chancellorship is roughly 18 months old. Where the in-office record is thin, this dossier draws on his long prior career and says so; where even that cannot fairly settle a test, the grade reflects the short record rather than a guess.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Does he apply the same rules to allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders, with no favorites at the source — in migration law, fiscal rules, and enforcement?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His signature fiscal change was made by changing the rule itself, in the open: rather than shifting money between funds off-budget (the maneuver the Federal Constitutional Court struck down against the previous government in November 2023), Merz amended the Basic Law's debt brake with the required two-thirds majorities — Bundestag 512–206 on 18 March 2025, Bundesrat approval 21 March 2025 — creating the €500 billion infrastructure fund and the defence exemption as general constitutional rules, not discretionary favors (Bundestag/Bundesrat records, March 2025; NPR, 18 March 2025).
  • The amended fiscal rules distribute room by formula, not by loyalty: the federal states' permitted structural deficit was doubled to 0.35% of GDP for all Länder alike, and the defence exemption is a standing rule (spending above 1% of GDP), not a case-by-case dispensation (Oxford Economics research briefing, 5 March 2025).
  • The border regime is formally uniform: since 7 May 2025, federal police have been instructed to turn back undocumented arrivals, including asylum seekers, at all land borders regardless of nationality; the Interior Ministry reports turnbacks and a roughly 20% rise in deportations (19,538 in January–October 2025) applied across nationalities (Federal Interior Ministry figures via InfoMigrants/Handelsblatt, 2025; Reuters, 2 June 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The uniform border rule was maintained after a court found its application unlawful: on 2 June 2025 the Berlin Administrative Court ruled that rejecting three Somali asylum seekers without the EU's Dublin procedure was unlawful. Interior Minister Dobrindt responded that the ruling applied only to that case — "We will continue with the pushbacks" — and Merz said the ruling might limit but would not stop the policy. His own coalition partner's justice minister, Stefanie Hubig (SPD), publicly said the court's decision must be followed (Berlin Administrative Court ruling, 2 June 2025; Reuters, 2–3 June 2025; InfoMigrants, 2 June 2025).
  • The fiscal rule change was passed by the outgoing, already-voted-out Bundestag in its final sitting (18 March 2025) rather than by the parliament voters had just elected — a timing chosen because the new chamber could not supply the two-thirds majority. The Federal Constitutional Court declined to stop the vote, but the legitimacy of binding a successor parliament this way remains CONTESTED: supporters cite the security emergency; opponents, including the AfD and Left parties that filed complaints, call it a circumvention of the electorate (Bundestag record, 18 March 2025; Northbridge Institute analysis, March 2025).
  • Relief under his own tax promises was distributed selectively: the electricity-tax cut his coalition agreement promised "for all" was delivered to industry, commerce and agriculture, while households — the group with the least lobbying weight — were left out in 2025 and were still unfunded in the 2027 budget approved in November 2026 (Coalition agreement, 5 May 2025; Clean Energy Wire, November 2026, citing Der Spiegel).
  • His prior career supplies the standing conflict-of-interest question, which remains an ALLEGATION of proximity rather than a documented favor: as BlackRock Germany's supervisory-board chairman (2016–2020) he held meetings with federal ministers that the watchdog LobbyControl criticized as door-opening; no documented instance of his government directing a benefit to BlackRock appears in the record searched (LobbyControl, cited in DW/Guardian coverage; Britannica biography).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the Länder, the Bundestag and his own coalition partner, the courts and the Bundesbank, and EU partners keep their own autonomy — or does he centralize and punish independence?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The institutions demonstrably still bite. His own coalition's dissenters defeated him in the first chancellor ballot on 6 May 2025 — 310 votes against the 316 required, the first first-ballot failure in Federal Republic history — and he took office hours later on a second ballot (325 votes) rather than by any pressure mechanism; the secret ballot that embarrassed him was never challenged (Bundestag record, 6 May 2025; AP, 6 May 2025).
  • His junior partner blocks him in the open and survives: SPD Finance Minister Lars Klingbeil's ministry repeatedly declined to fund the household electricity-tax cut Merz publicly promised (Der Spiegel via Clean Energy Wire, November 2026), and SPD leaders publicly threatened "fierce opposition" to his pension statements without sanction (Reuters, 21 April 2026).
  • When younger CDU MPs revolted against the pension package in late 2025, Merz climbed down and accepted a commission compromise rather than forcing the vote or disciplining the rebels (Reuters, 5 May 2026). In July 2026 a breakthrough package adopted the pension commission's recommendations instead of his original design (Torch Media Africa summary of the July 2026 agreement).
  • The Länder are partners, not prefectures: the March 2025 package required and received Bundesrat consent and doubled the states' own borrowing room; after the September 2026 eastern election disasters, all eight CDU state premiers publicly backed Merz voluntarily — support he had to be given, not commanded (Bundesrat record, 21 March 2025; NRB, 22 September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • His governing style is documented as small-circle and advice-resistant: an Atlantic Council assessment of his first year (May 2026) found he "keeps no close circle of advisers and is not much given to taking advice," with a Chancellery that "struggled to coordinate policy" and a mishandled Constitutional Court appointment process in 2025 (the coalition's judge candidate, Frauke Brosius-Gersdorf, withdrew in August 2025 after a CDU backbench revolt the Chancellery failed to manage) (Atlantic Council, May 2026).
  • Personnel power has been used abruptly: in the 29 July 2026 reshuffle, Transport Minister Patrick Schnieder was informed of his dismissal while on holiday, his designated successor withdrew hours before the announcement leaving the ministry briefly vacant, and a state secretary learned of his removal from the press before being told — conduct CDU MPs from Schnieder's home state protested by cancelling a meeting with Merz (Wikipedia, "2026 Merz government crisis," compiling German reporting, July 2026; FutureUAE analysis, 14 August 2026).
  • Toward EU partners the grip shows as unilateralism: the May 2025 border rejections were imposed despite Poland's objections — Prime Minister Tusk said he had "warned the German side back in March" and that Warsaw's patience was "wearing out," and Poland imposed its own retaliatory checks from 7 July 2025; most other Schengen neighbors likewise reintroduced controls facing Germany (Tusk statements, 1 July 2025; European Parliament question P-002767/2025, 8 July 2025).
  • Public ultimata substitute for coalition process: Merz repeatedly set his SPD partner deadlines in speeches (the promised "autumn of reforms," public pension red lines), a pattern both partners' lawmakers blame for the coalition's public feuding through 2026 (Reuters, 21 April 2026 and 5 May 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When parliament, courts, voters, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • After the Saxony-Anhalt catastrophe of 6 September 2026 (AfD 43.8%, CDU 17.2%, its worst result in the state since reunification), Merz faced the result personally and on camera: "This is the worst election defeat the CDU has suffered in years — indeed, in decades"; "We are all deeply shocked"; he conceded "I know that I personally was a constant topic in this election campaign" and promised consequences and better communication rather than blaming voters or the count (CNN, 7 September 2026; Le Monde, 8 September 2026).
  • He has conceded specific failures before being forced: in October 2025, after heavy CDU state-election losses, he acknowledged the unkept electricity-tax promise had opened "a gap in our credibility" and committed budget leeway to closing it (Clean Energy Wire, November 2026, recounting his October 2025 statement).
  • On reform pace generally, he publicly accepted the criticism that his promised reform effort "has been slower than initially communicated" after the 2026 growth forecast was cut (Merz statement reported February 2026).
  • On the pension revolt, adjustment took institutional form: rather than whipping his own young MPs into line, he accepted the commission route and the July 2026 compromise built on its recommendations (Reuters, 5 May 2026; July 2026 package reports).

Evidence against:

  • The defining wobble of his rise, he doubled down on: after the 29 January 2025 migration motion passed only with AfD votes (348–345) — breaking the taboo his own predecessor Angela Merkel then publicly called "wrong," citing his November 2024 assurance that nothing should pass with AfD support — Merz's defense was that "a correct decision doesn't become wrong because the wrong people agree." Mass protests followed; two days later his binding migration bill failed anyway, with dissenters from his own camp (Bundestag record, 29–31 January 2025; Merkel statement, 30 January 2025, via AP).
  • On the June 2025 court ruling against his border rejections, the adjustment was cosmetic: the government promised fuller legal justifications while continuing the identical practice, with Merz framing the judgment as a limit on "scope for maneuver," not a correction (Reuters, 3 June 2025).
  • On the October 2025 "Stadtbild" (cityscape) remark — that migration remained "this problem in our cityscape" — asked to clarify, he doubled down before he explained: "I have nothing to take back, on the contrary, I underline it again… ask your daughters what I may have meant." Clarification (that he meant people without legal status or work who break rules) came only days later, after protests and criticism from his own party (Potsdam press conference, 14 October 2025; German Policy/ZDF Politbarometer coverage, October 2025).
  • After September 2026 he coupled contrition with refusal to change course: "My determination to continue on the path of reform is unbroken" and "Giving up is not an option for me," while resisting his own coalition partner's calls to soften the pension and health measures voters had just punished (Reuters and NRB, 22 September 2026; The Guardian via EuropeSays, September 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Who is inside his count — asylum seekers and migrants, citizens of migration background, the poor, eastern Germans — and who is outside it?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The asylum system itself still stands and still grants protection: Germany processed roughly 113,000 first-time asylum applications in 2025 — the lowest in over a decade, but a functioning legal channel, not an abolished one — and the Interior Ministry states the goal as reducing irregular migration "while maintaining access to asylum for people who meet legal requirements" (Federal Interior Ministry figures, January 2026).
  • Legal labor migration is affirmatively inside his count: the government fast-tracked skilled-worker visas (nurses, engineers, IT) even while tightening asylum, and during the Stadtbild row Merz clarified that "Germany and the European Union will continue to depend on immigration" — a clarification 63% of respondents told ZDF's Politbarometer they considered correct (Interior Ministry reporting, 2025; ZDF Politbarometer via German Policy, October 2025).
  • Ukrainian refugees kept temporary protection, and after the September 2026 AfD victory Merz publicly assured "vulnerable minorities" that Germany would "stick to its values and constitution" even if the AfD took state power (Merz statement, 7 September 2026, via The Guardian/EuropeSays).
  • His defenders' integration case, stated fairly: the coalition's position is that order is the precondition of welcome — that uncontrolled arrivals overwhelmed municipalities (Dobrindt: "the country's ability to integrate had simply reached a breaking point") and that a state that cannot enforce its own decisions cannot sustain public consent for any migration at all (Dobrindt statement, July 2025, via AIDA/ECRE country report).

Evidence against:

  • A whole legal category was counted out of family life: the Act in force since 24 July 2025 suspended family reunification for two years for all beneficiaries of subsidiary protection — some 351,400 people in Germany, predominantly Syrians who fled the Assad regime — separating spouses and minor children by statute rather than by individual case (Act on the Suspension of Family Reunification, 24 July 2025; AIDA/ECRE country report, updated June 2026).
  • People Germany had already promised to admit were offered money to disappear: the Afghan admission programmes were paused in May 2025, leaving roughly 2,100 Afghans — many of them former local staff and their families with conditional acceptance pledges — stranded in Pakistan; in November 2025 the Interior Ministry wrote to around 650 of the most vulnerable offering payments of up to roughly €14,250 for a family of four to renounce their admission pledges and return to Taliban-ruled Afghanistan or a third country (AIDA/ECRE country report, 2026, documenting BMI letters of November 2025).
  • Asylum seekers arriving overland are outside the count in practice: rejections at the border without the Dublin procedure continued after the Berlin Administrative Court held them unlawful (2 June 2025), and deportations were extended to Afghanistan — 81 convicted men flown to Kabul on 18 July 2025, with Taliban contacts since regularized — and prepared for Syria (Reuters, 2 June 2025; InfoMigrants, 2025; BMI deportation figures).
  • Citizens of migration background are rhetorically marked as the visible problem: the October 2025 "Stadtbild" remark and its "ask your daughters" defense were condemned as stigmatizing by the SPD, the Greens, and CDU figures including MEP Dennis Radtke and Berlin mayor Kai Wegner ("you cannot pin that on nationality"), and drew street protests under the slogan "We are the cityscape." The remark sits in a documented pattern from his prior career: in January 2023 he described boys from immigrant families on ZDF as "kleine Paschas" ("little pashas") who disrespect female teachers — condemned at the time as a stigmatizing generalization about Muslim children (Potsdam press conference, 14 October 2025; ZDF "Markus Lanz," 10 January 2023, widely reported).
  • The poor are counted mainly as a cost line: the coalition's conversion of Bürgergeld into a tighter "Neue Grundsicherung" with stricter sanctions, and the 2026–27 rounds of postponed tax relief and shelved levies, drew sustained criticism that welfare recipients absorb the adjustment while the promised household relief waits (American-German Institute, "150 Days of the Merz Government," October 2025; Reuters, November 2026 budget reporting).
  • Eastern Germans' verdict on being counted is itself evidence: in September 2026 the AfD took 43.8% in Saxony-Anhalt and 38.2% in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, where the CDU collapsed to 4.9% and lost every seat; nationally, an INSA poll for Bild found only 14% considered Merz the right chancellor. Electoral results are not proof of exclusion, but a governing party's near-erasure across the east is the measurable form of a constituency concluding it is outside the count (State election results, 6 and 20 September 2026; INSA/Bild, September 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Promises made vs. promises kept — the fiscal pledges, the migration pledges, and the long record of stated positions.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The migration pledges — the centerpiece of his campaign — were substantially kept, fast: he promised in January 2025 to order permanent border controls and turn back "all attempts at illegal entry without exception" from day one; the rejection directive issued on 7 May 2025, his first full day in office, and the measurable results followed — first-time asylum applications roughly halved in 2025 (about 113,000, from about 229,000 in 2024) and deportations rose about 20% (Merz statement, January 2025, via AP; Federal Interior Ministry figures, 2025–26).
  • The defence promise was kept at scale: having campaigned on rebuilding the Bundeswehr, he drove the constitutional financing through, backed the June 2025 NATO Hague commitment (5% of GDP on defence and security by 2035), and set Germany on a path to 3.5% core defence spending by 2029 — more than doubling the defence budget toward €150 billion (NATO summit record, June 2025; NATO Secretary General Rutte, Berlin, 9 July 2025).
  • The firewall pledge, in its formal sense, has held: despite the January 2025 vote, he has formed no coalition or governing arrangement with the AfD at any level, repeated the refusal after the September 2026 eastern results, and campaigned against the party throughout (Merz statements, 2025–26).
  • On one earlier controversy he did correct himself in words: after calling Ukrainian refugees "social tourists" in September 2022, he apologized the following day — a small but documented instance of a stated position withdrawn rather than defended (Bild TV remark and apology, September 2022, widely reported).

Evidence against:

  • The fiscal word — the pledge he had been loudest about for years — was reversed within days of the election. The CDU/CSU campaign programme committed to keeping the constitutional debt brake; Merz, its most prominent defender, then negotiated the brake's loosening in the first days of coalition talks and passed it through the outgoing parliament on 18 March 2025, before taking office. NPR's contemporaneous account calls it plainly "an about-turn for Merz, whose party had spoken out against running up new debt before the election." His defense — that the Trump administration's February 2025 turn on Ukraine changed the facts — is on the record; so is the sequence (CDU/CSU election programme, February 2025; Bundestag vote, 18 March 2025; NPR, 18 March 2025).
  • The household electricity-tax cut has now been promised three times and kept zero: written into the coalition agreement for 2025, limited that year to industry; acknowledged by Merz himself in October 2025 as a credibility gap; promised again by him personally for 2027 ("We will reach agreement tomorrow," November 2026) — while his own finance ministry stated the roughly €5.4 billion was not in the approved 2027 budget and no funding had been found (Coalition agreement, May 2025; Der Spiegel via Clean Energy Wire, November 2026; tagesschau budget-committee report, 13 November 2026).
  • His November 2024 assurance — cited back to him by Angela Merkel — that no measure should be carried by AfD votes before the election was abandoned by his own choice on 29 January 2025, the episode at the root of the word-keeping case against him (Merkel statement, 30 January 2025, via AP).
  • His oldest measurable pledge inverted completely: in 2018 Merz promised that under his leadership the AfD's support would be halved. Under his chancellorship the AfD instead doubled its Saxony-Anhalt vote, leads national polls at roughly 28–29%, and the Union polls near 20% (Le Monde, 8 September 2026, recalling the 2018 pledge; INSA/Bild, September 2026).
  • Promised household income-tax relief has been repeatedly postponed (most recently in the November 2026 budget talks, alongside a shelved plastics levy and a postponed sugar tax), and by his first anniversary 73% of Germans doubted his economic competence — the field on which his word was his candidacy (Reuters, 5 May 2026, citing Handelsblatt survey; Reuters, November 2026).

Overall Summary

Eighteen months in, the pattern is a chancellor whose word is reliable exactly where it is hardest — migration enforcement and defence financing were promised in detail and delivered on schedule — and unreliable where it costs his own side something: the debt brake he championed for years was loosened within days of the election, and the household electricity-tax cut has been promised three times without being funded once. The institutions around him plainly keep their spin — courts rule against him, his partner vetoes him, his own MPs defeated him on his first ballot — but he meets their pushback with doubling down as often as with adjustment, and his count, as legislated, excludes named groups: subsidiary-protection families, pledged Afghan admissions, and asylum seekers turned back against a court's reading of the law. Grades: One Law — MIXED. Holds Without Gripping — MIXED. Answers the Wobble — MIXED. Counts Everyone — FAILS. Keeps His Word — FAILS. The record is still early — much of it is one crisis year, and several of these grades could move within a single legislative term — but the direction of each grade is set by documents, votes, and his own statements, not by his historic unpopularity (14% in INSA's September 2026 poll), which this dossier treats as context, never as evidence.
