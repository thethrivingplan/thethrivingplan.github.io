The Ruler's Test — João Lourenço, President of Angola

Subject: João Manuel Gonçalves Lourenço — President of Angola since 26 September 2017. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: under Angola's 2010 constitution the president is both head of state and head of government — the office of prime minister was abolished in 2010 — and he commands the executive, the budget, and the armed forces. Lourenço, a former general and defence minister, was the chosen successor of José Eduardo dos Santos (president 1979–2017) and took office in Angola's first electoral presidential transition. He was re-elected after the 24 August 2022 general election, in which the MPLA won 51.17% of the vote to UNITA's 43.95% — the closest result in the country's history, which cost the MPLA its two-thirds parliamentary majority; UNITA rejected the outcome and the Constitutional Court upheld it, and Lourenço was sworn in for his second term on 26 September 2022 (contemporaneous reporting; Constitutional Court ruling, September 2022). The 2010 constitution limits presidents to two five-year terms, and on 10 November 2025 Lourenço publicly affirmed that he would not seek a third, describing the limit as inviolable (ANGOP; CNN International interview). His tenure status at grading requires one distinction the coverage often blurs: on 5 November 2026 the MPLA Central Committee endorsed him as sole candidate for the party presidency at the congress of 9–10 December 2026 — a party office the constitutional term limit does not bar him from holding, and one the MPLA's late-2024 statute changes expressly allow to be held separately from the presidential candidacy (MPLA announcement, 5 November 2026; BGA analysis, 2025). Some outlets frame the endorsement as a path to a third presidential term, which would require a constitutional amendment; that framing is contested, and both readings are recorded under Test 5. He is graded on a full nine years in office — the file is not thin. His predecessor dos Santos appears as context where the record Lourenço inherited, or campaigned against, bears directly on a test. Vice President Esperança da Costa (since 15 September 2022) would serve out the term in the event of a vacancy. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption campaign he launched on taking office in 2017 reached the apex of the previous order — people no Angolan court had ever touched. José Filomeno dos Santos, the former president's son and former head of the sovereign wealth fund, was convicted in August 2020 in the $500 million transfer case, alongside a former central bank governor (Supreme Court, August 2020). Isabel dos Santos, the former president's daughter and former Sonangol chair, faces multiple criminal charges in Angola, her assets were frozen, and in late 2024 the United Kingdom sanctioned her under its Global Anti-Corruption regime, accusing her of embezzling more than £350 million from state enterprises — charges she denies (UK Foreign Office designation, November 2024; ICIJ "Luanda Leaks," January 2020). Businessman Carlos Manuel de São Vicente was convicted in March 2022 and sentenced to nine years (Reuters, March 2022), and former communications minister Manuel Rabelais was convicted in April 2021 (AFP, April 2021).
  • The campaign was still reaching upward in his second term. In March 2025 the trial opened at the Supreme Court of generals Manuel Hélder Vieira Dias "Kopelipa" (former head of the security services) and Leopoldino Fragoso do Nascimento "Dino" — the two most powerful fixers of the dos Santos era — on charges including embezzlement, fraud, criminal association, and money laundering in the China International Fund reconstruction schemes (Lusa, 10 March 2025; ANGOP, 2025). The charges are ALLEGATIONs until a court rules; the fact that the men are in the dock at all is the one-law evidence.
  • The state's own recovery machinery produced figures no predecessor attempted: the national prosecutor reported in 2022 more than 3,000 active corruption and financial-crime cases and claimed some $20 billion in illicitly acquired assets recovered or identified — a government figure, recorded as a claim (Office of the Attorney General via US Congressional Research Service, October 2024).
  • The outcome measure moved further in his first five years than in any comparable period: Angola's Corruption Perceptions Index score rose from 19 of 100 in 2017 (167th of 180) to 33 in 2022 (116th), its best-ever standing (Transparency International, CPI 2017–2022).

Evidence against:

  • The campaign's map and the map of the old regime are the same map. The US Congressional Research Service records that "many of the highest-profile targets" have been relatives and associates of dos Santos, alongside "allegedly selective prosecutions or other favoritism toward some of his own political associates and relatives" (CRS, October 2024). Chatham House's Alex Vines stated the structural point early: "there is a danger that this reform process is only selective, targeting only the family and friends of the dos Santos, when there are clearly also people in Lourenço's entourage who should be prosecuted" (Chatham House, via contemporaneous reporting, 2020). Nine years on, no documented prosecution of a figure from Lourenço's own circle appears in the record assembled here.
  • The generals' trial itself documents the line. The 223-page indictment names former Sonangol chairman and former vice-president Manuel Vicente repeatedly, yet Vicente is not a defendant, a suspect, or — as defence counsel noted publicly — even a listed declarant in the case; the defence calls the proceedings a "misunderstanding" (Lusa, 10 March and 30 June 2025). The man at the centre of the dos Santos-era oil state remains outside the process that is judging his subordinates.
  • The flagship conviction did not survive the courts — which is recorded here as a one-law fact in both directions (see Test 2): on 4 April 2024 the Constitutional Court voided Filomeno dos Santos's conviction as unconstitutional, finding breaches of legality, adversarial proceedings, and defence rights (Constitutional Court ruling, 4 April 2024). A presidential pardon followed in December 2024, effective 1 January 2025, which he publicly declined as unnecessary given the annulment; some secondary accounts describe a Supreme Court reinstatement of the conviction before the pardon, and the sequence is recorded here as contested between those accounts (Presidential Decree, December 2024; contemporaneous reporting).
  • The public does not experience one law. Afrobarometer's 2024 survey found 54% of Angolans saying corruption increased in the preceding year — a 21-point rise since 2019 — and 63% believing ordinary people risk retaliation for reporting it (Afrobarometer, AD906, 2024). The CPI score has stalled and slipped since its 2022 peak: 33 in 2022–2023, 32 in 2024 and 2025, still 120th–121st of 180 (Transparency International, CPI 2024–2025). Isabel dos Santos's own position — that the cases against her are a political vendetta — is recorded as her position; the charges against her remain ALLEGATIONs, never tried in an Angolan court.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Constitutional Court ruled against the executive's signature project, in public, and the ruling stood. Its 4 April 2024 judgment voided the conviction of the former president's son — the emblematic verdict of Lourenço's anti-corruption campaign — on fair-trial grounds, and the government neither defied it nor removed the judges; the presidency answered with a pardon, a power the constitution gives it, rather than with pressure on the court (Constitutional Court ruling, 4 April 2024; ANGOP, April 2024). A court that can annul the president's flagship case is not wholly gripped, and this shelf records that at full strength.
  • Private and critical media continue to operate and to publish against him: investigative outlet Maka Angola, the independent press (Expansão, Novo Jornal), and foreign correspondents cover the corruption cases, the protests, and the succession openly. Angola's World Press Freedom Index position improved from 123rd in 2015 to 100th of 180 in 2025, a rise the EU delegation in Luanda publicly acknowledged as real progress (RSF, 2025; EU Delegation to Angola, 2025).
  • The 2022 election was run, lost ground in, and certified through the formal institutions: the MPLA's two-thirds majority disappeared, UNITA's legal challenge was heard and decided by the Constitutional Court, and the result — however disputed — was not altered by decree (Constitutional Court, September 2022).

Evidence against:

  • The state took the country's main private television voices into its own hands, through the prosecutor, and has not given them back. In July 2020 the Attorney General's asset-recovery service placed Grupo Medianova — TV Zimbo (Angola's first private TV channel), Rádio Mais, and the newspaper O País — under state guardianship as assets associated with figures of the previous administration; in August 2020 TV Palanca, Rádio Global, and a production agency were transferred to state ownership the same way (State Media Monitor, 2026; CPJ, 2020). Five years on, the outlets remain state-controlled, their reprivatization still "under discussion" as of June 2025, and the state-aligned media sector absorbed Kz 238.8 billion in state support in 2020–2024 (IGAPE sectoral report, September 2025). The asset-recovery rationale is the government's stated basis; the effect is that the broadcasters most Angolans watch are held by the seat this test grades.
  • The electoral referee's leadership was renewed through a process the opposition documented as rigged, and seated over its walkout. In February 2025 the Superior Council of the Judiciary declared CNE president Manuel Pereira da Silva the winner of the competition for his own job with 91 points, against 48 and 33 for the other candidates. UNITA's parliamentary group stated the competition's regulation "is riddled with illegalities" and "seek[s] to privilege a certain candidate," filed constitutional challenges to both the competition and the CNE's composition, and walked out of the inauguration; the Constitutional Court dismissed UNITA's challenge to the CNE composition (ruling 994/2025), and the inauguration proceeded with the merits of the competition challenge still, on UNITA's account, unjudged (Africa-Press/UNITA and MPLA statements, 2025; ANGOP, 2025). The MPLA's position — that the choice "results from law" — is recorded; the man who will referee the 2027 election holds his seat through a process half the political country regards as arranged.
  • The legal toolkit for gripping the street and the press was expanded in his second term. In August–September 2024 Lourenço signed the Law on Vandalism of Public Goods and Services, carrying prison sentences of up to 25 years where protest involves damage, and a National Security Law granting broad surveillance and media-interference powers without prior court approval — both characterized by Freedom House and Angolan civil society as instruments of repression (Freedom House, 2025; BTI, 2026; CRS, October 2024).
  • Journalists covering pushback are handled as part of the pushback: police detained and beat journalists covering the October 2020 protests in Luanda, holding some for more than 50 hours without charge (CPJ, 27 October 2020), and journalists were among those arrested in the July 2025 unrest (CIVICUS, 2025). The prosecution service that seized the media groups is headed by an attorney general the president appoints; so are the judges of the courts that would review him. The Constitutional Court's 2024 ruling shows the grip is not total; the record shows where it closes.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The voters' sharpest pushback was accepted. In August 2022 the MPLA recorded its worst result since independence — 51.17%, the loss of its two-thirds majority, and defeat in Luanda itself — and Lourenço took his second term on that count, governed with the reduced majority, and did not annul, rerun, or administratively reverse the result (National Electoral Commission results, 2022; Constitutional Court, September 2022). In a region where incumbents have answered such counts by changing them, acceptance is the test's core evidence.
  • Organized labour's pushback was answered with negotiation and a signed concession. Facing in 2024 the first general strike movement in decades, the government negotiated with the union federations and signed the Triennial Agreement of May 2024: a 25% civil-service salary adjustment from January 2025 and a new national minimum wage — which took effect in September 2024 at 70,000 kwanzas, more than double the previous 32,181 — with annual negotiations thereafter (Triennial Agreement, May 2024; Lusa, February 2025). The 25% rise was delivered two months late, in March 2025, but backdated to January, with the delay announced openly by the Secretary of State for Labour (Jornal de Angola, January 2025).
  • He answered history's wobble in the open. On 26 May 2021 Lourenço delivered a public apology on behalf of the Angolan state for the summary executions that followed 27 May 1977 — an event the state had refused to name for 44 years — calling the reaction "disproportionate" and recognizing that many of the executed were innocent; the victims' commission (CIVICOP, created 2019) continued its work, and the apology was praised publicly even by UNITA's president (Presidential address, 26 May 2021, via ANGOP). In December 2024 he used the pardon power across the lines: the 50th-anniversary pardon, effective 1 January 2025, freed 51 prisoners including his predecessor's son and the four activists imprisoned since 2023 for organizing a protest (Presidential Decree, December 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The street's largest pushback was answered with live ammunition first and explanation second. The 1 July 2025 removal of diesel subsidies raised prices 33% and transport fares up to 50%; after youth protests on 12 July were dispersed with tear gas and rubber bullets, the taxi drivers' strike of 28–30 July 2025 became nationwide unrest. The presidency's own figures record 22 dead, 197 injured, and 1,214 arrested, with the army deployed in the capital; CIVICUS and rights organizations put the toll at at least 30 killed and more than 1,500 arrested, with security forces firing live ammunition (Presidency statement, 30 July 2025; CIVICUS Lens, 2025; International Crisis Group, July 2025). The government defended the subsidy cut as fiscally necessary, condemned the violence, attributed the unrest in part to manipulation and criminal infiltration — Lourenço had previously described protest over fuel prices as a pretext to destabilize the government — and promised investigations into alleged abuses; the UN called for an independent probe (Presidency statements, July 2025; UN, August 2025). The policy was not adjusted; the count of the dead was published by the same office that ordered the deployment.
  • Parliament's one attempt to call him to account was closed procedurally, without the merits being heard. UNITA's October 2023 impeachment initiative — the first against a sitting Angolan president, a near-100-page motion signed by all 90 of its deputies — was voted down 123 to 1 abstention on 14 October 2023 after the MPLA insisted on a public rather than secret ballot and UNITA walked out; UNITA's parliamentary leader alleged the ruling party threatened to "fingerprint the votes" of deputies — an ALLEGATION the MPLA denies, calling the motion legally baseless (National Assembly, 14 October 2023; contemporaneous reporting by AFP and regional outlets). Whatever the motion's merits, the wobble was answered by majority arithmetic, not by argument.
  • The judiciary's wobble in his own flagship case (Test 2) was answered within his powers — a pardon — but the pardon was extended to a man whose conviction the Constitutional Court had already voided, which the recipient himself publicly noted in declining it; the exchange records a presidency answering a court's correction by reasserting its own clemency over the same file (Presidential Decree, December 2024; contemporaneous reporting).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's voters, Cabinda, the poor, the victims of the old conflicts, and the protesters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The victims of the state's own history were counted, by name and by policy, for the first time. The 2019 creation of CIVICOP and the May 2021 state apology covered the victims of all political conflicts from independence to 2002 — including the MPLA's own 27 May 1977 executions and, as Lourenço's speech itself listed, victims of UNITA's internal killings; the remains of UNITA founder Jonas Savimbi had earlier been exhumed and returned to his family for burial in 2019, and in May 2026 the state declared national mourning around the return of hundreds of sets of remains to families (Presidential address, 26 May 2021; CIVICOP reporting, 2019–2026). UNITA's president praised the apology publicly — a counting acknowledged by the counted.
  • Clemency crossed the country's dividing lines: the January 2025 pardon freed both the former president's son and four young activists jailed for a protest the government had prosecuted (Presidential Decree, December 2024).
  • The wage floor was raised for the poorest formal workers: the national minimum wage more than doubled in September 2024 (32,181 to 70,000 kwanzas), the civil-service salary adjustment of 25% followed in 2025, and the Kwenda cash-transfer programme for poor households was expanded with World Bank support during his tenure (Presidential decree, September 2024; Triennial Agreement, May 2024; World Bank programme reporting).

Evidence against:

  • The arithmetic of the oil state still excludes most of the counted. Roughly 31% of Angola's ~37 million people live in poverty on the national measure, nearly half live on less than US$3.65 a day, and youth unemployment stands at 56.4% — after nine years of a presidency elected on diversification and jobs (World Bank, 2025; ARIES Angola Strategic Intelligence Report, May 2026; CIVICUS, 2025). Angola remains one of the world's most unequal oil economies: the wealth is documented, and so is who does not reach it.
  • In July 2025 the poor were counted as a security problem. The unrest began among minibus taxi drivers and the passengers who depend on them — CIVICUS calculates the post-hike daily commute in Luanda at US$4.36 against daily earnings of US$3.65 for over half of Angolans — and the response killed at least 22 people on the government's own count and arrested more than 1,200, including union leaders and journalists (Presidency statement, 30 July 2025; CIVICUS Lens, 2025). A 15-year-old boy was left paralyzed after being shot by police, a case publicized by Angolan rights activists (Wikipedia summary of contemporaneous reporting, 2025; Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch called for independent investigations).
  • Cabinda remains counted at a discount. Human Rights Watch reported in May 2023 that the human rights situation in the oil-rich enclave "has remained poor since President Lourenço took office in 2017," with activists arrested for exercising fundamental rights and police targeting those who speak against the government (Human Rights Watch, May–August 2023, via contemporaneous reporting). The province that produces much of the oil has no elected local government to answer to — like every Angolan municipality (Test 5).
  • The 44% who voted against him in 2022 are governed through referees they did not choose and cannot remove: a CNE leadership seated over their walkout (Test 2), an impeachment channel closed by majority vote (Test 3), and state-held broadcasters as the dominant source of news (Test 2). They are counted at the ballot box — the 2022 count genuinely cost the MPLA — and discounted in the institutions between elections.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign and program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 26 September 2017, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The term limit — his largest word, kept so far, at the moment it was cheapest to break. In the 2021 constitutional revision, Lourenço's party held the qualified majority needed to amend the constitution, and the two-term limit was left untouched; he has stated publicly that had there been a purpose to remain in power, "this could have been done" (CNN International interview, via ANGOP). On 10 November 2025 he affirmed he would not run in 2027 and called the two-term limit inviolable (ANGOP, November 2025). The word is kept to date and becomes fully testable in August 2027.
  • The anti-corruption campaign — happened, at the promised scale. The 2017 pledge to fight corruption produced the prosecutions, asset seizures, and recoveries detailed under Test 1, an IMF Extended Fund Facility programme (2018–2021) completed on schedule, and a CPI gain of 14 points in five years (Transparency International; IMF programme reviews). Whatever its selectivity, the campaign was not a slogan.
  • The reconciliation word — performed. The 2019–2021 commitments on the victims of the political conflicts produced CIVICOP, the exhumation and return of remains including Savimbi's, and the 2021 apology — each delivered as announced (Test 4).
  • The wage agreement — substantially performed, late. The May 2024 Triennial Agreement's first steps were delivered: the minimum-wage clause on time (September 2024) and the 25% civil-service adjustment two months late but backdated (Lusa, 2025).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Local elections — the clearest broken word of the tenure. The autarquias were written into the 2010 constitution, promised by the MPLA for 2020, and re-promised through both of Lourenço's campaigns; nine years into his presidency, Angola has never held a local election, and no date stands. The government's stated grounds — insufficient infrastructure and unfinished legislation — alternate by year; the 2025 administrative redivision, which more than doubled the number of municipalities, supplies the reason for the next postponement (Friends of Angola/DW interview, 2025; Tjarks analysis, 2025). The opposition's reading — that the MPLA is "afraid of losing power" locally after losing Luanda in 2022 — is recorded as its position; the fact underneath it, fifteen years of postponement, is not contested.
  • Jobs and diversification — the direction is real, the destination recedes. The 2017 campaign's signature pledges — 500,000 jobs and an economy freed from oil — remain the standing shorthand in Angolan public debate for the unkept word. Government job-creation claims are contested by unions and analysts; the measurable endpoints at grading are youth unemployment of 56.4% and an economy in which oil still provides the overwhelming share of exports and state revenue (World Bank, 2025; ARIES, May 2026). The Lobito Corridor and non-oil investment are in motion; the promise was transformation, and the record is dependence, somewhat loosened.
  • The salary accord — falling behind its own schedule. The May 2024 agreement set a path unions understood as a cumulative 100% civil-service adjustment by 2027. Delivered: 25% in 2025 and 10% at the start of 2026; in October 2026 the government proposed a further 10% for 2027 — a cumulative ~51% — which the union federations rejected as a betrayal of the accord: "What we are demanding is what was agreed with the government in 2024… the government, as an honourable party, has an obligation to honour it" (Expansão, 30 October 2026). The government's position — fiscal sustainability, inflation offsets, and a broader count of delivered benefits — is recorded; on the unions' arithmetic, half the word will be missing when the agreement expires.
  • The third-term word — kept in the letter, shadowed in the structure. Lourenço's November 2025 affirmation that he will not seek a third presidential term stands unretracted, and the constitution backs it. Against it: in May 2026 he announced he would seek another term as MPLA president; in the weeks before the congress his potential rivals "dropped away or were removed," leaving him unopposed, and on 5 November 2026 the Central Committee endorsed him as sole candidate (MPLA, November 2026; contemporaneous reporting). Party leadership is not the presidency — the MPLA's 2024 statutes allow the two to be held by different people, and the party must still select a 2027 candidate — but some coverage (TRT Afrika via QNC News, November 2026) reads the endorsement as positioning for a third term requiring constitutional change, a reading the opposition shares and the MPLA contests. The record grades the word as kept so far and names the file that will settle it: the 2027 candidate list.
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: dos Santos left office in 2017 having never faced an election he could lose, with the term-limit question answered by retirement on his own schedule; the seat Lourenço holds was made answerable — two terms, a real count in 2022 — partly by the constitution his predecessor's party wrote and partly by his own choices within it. The contrast is the context in which his word is graded.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

João Lourenço is the successor who prosecuted his patron's house: the anti-corruption campaign that defines his presidency put a former president's son in prison, his daughter under sanction and charge, his generals in the dock, and billions of claimed dollars back on the state's books — and it stopped, visibly and consistently, at the edge of Lourenço's own circle, leaving the dos Santos era's central oil figure named in an indictment but never charged. The referees tell the same two-sided story in a darker register: a Constitutional Court independent enough to void his flagship conviction, beside an electoral commission whose chief was reseated over the opposition's walkout, the two main private television groups absorbed into state hands by the prosecutor and never returned, and 2024 laws arming the executive against protest and press. He accepted the worst election result his party has ever recorded and negotiated with the first general strike in decades; he also answered the July 2025 street with live ammunition and a published toll of 22 dead. He apologized for 1977 when no predecessor would name it, doubled the minimum wage, and pardoned across the lines; he has never held the local elections his constitution and his campaigns promised, and nearly half his country still lives under $3.65 a day. The grades are MIXED around a single FAIL because the file is nine years long and genuinely divided — and because the largest item on it is still open: he holds the word he gave on the two-term limit, in a party that has just made him its unopposed leader again. The files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: the MPLA's 2027 candidate list and whether the constitution is touched, whether Manuel Vicente is ever charged, whether the seized broadcasters are reprivatized, whether the CNE that referees 2027 earns the loser's consent, and whether the autarquias are ever called.
