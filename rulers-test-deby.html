The Ruler's Test — Mahamat Idriss Déby, President of Chad

Subject: Mahamat Idriss Déby Itno — President of Chad. Premise verified November 2026: he still holds the executive seat; no succession has occurred. He took power on 20 April 2021, at the head of a Transitional Military Council (CMT), hours after his father, President Idriss Déby Itno — in power since 1990 and killed in clashes with the rebel Front for Change and Concord in Chad (FACT) a day after provisional results projected his sixth term — died; the constitution then in force pointed succession to the President of the National Assembly, and instead the CMT suspended the constitution and dissolved the government and National Assembly (US Senate resolution text, 2022; CSIS, 2022). He led the transition (CMT 2021–2022, then transitional president from October 2022), won the presidential election of 6 May 2024 with 61.03% of the vote on official turnout of 75.78%, confirmed by the Constitutional Council on 16 May 2024, and was sworn in on 23 May 2024 for a five-year term (ANGE provisional results via Human Rights Watch, May 2024; UN national-authorities briefing, October 2024). He is graded on the whole holding since April 2021, weighted to the elected term since May 2024. Two later changes to that mandate are part of the record, not corrections to the premise: in December 2024 he was elevated to Marshal of Chad, and constitutional amendments approved in September 2025 and promulgated on 8 October 2025 extended the presidential term from five to seven years and removed term limits entirely, applying from the next election (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026; State Media Monitor, 2026). His prime minister since 23 May 2024 is Allamaye Halina. Chad's presidency is the executive seat — head of state, head of the armed forces, and the office that appoints the government — so the presidency, not the premiership, is graded. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure in itself; the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The transition's opening moves included opponents at the table. The Doha peace agreement of August 2022 brought many armed groups into the Inclusive and Sovereign National Dialogue (DNIS), and after the dialogue former opposition leader Saleh Kebzabo of the UNDR was appointed prime minister of the transitional unity government in October 2022 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2022; NSA analysis, 2022). In January 2024 Déby appointed his principal electoral rival, Succès Masra of Les Transformateurs, as prime minister — Masra served January–May 2024, through the election he contested against Déby (Human Rights Watch, August 2026).
  • The electoral machinery did, in at least one documented instance, rule against the ruling party's own: in November 2024 ANGE rejected the candidacy of the MPS secretary-general, Mahamat Zen Bada, and five other MPS officials for the legislative elections, citing a previous forgery conviction (ANGE provisional list via parliamentary-election record, November 2024).
  • Clemency has been applied across the line, late: the November 2023 amnesty law covered the events of 20 October 2022 on all sides, and on 14 August 2026 Déby pardoned Masra and eight leaders of the opposition GCAP coalition, who were released on 15 August 2026 (Reuters, 14 August 2026; Human Rights Watch, 21 August 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The founding file of the elected term is an accountability file that was closed by law rather than by verdict. On 20 October 2022 — "Black Thursday" — security forces fired live ammunition on protests against the extension of the transition. The counts are printed separately and never blended: the government said at least 50 killed and more than 300 injured (Prime Minister Kebzabo, press conference, 20 October 2022); Chad's National Human Rights Commission (CNDH) reported 128 killed and 518 injured (CNDH report, February 2023, via Human Rights Watch); the World Organisation Against Torture (OMCT) and the Chadian League for Human Rights reported more than 218 killed, more than 1,300 arbitrarily detained, and at least 40 disappeared (OMCT/LTDH, April 2023). Hundreds of detainees were transferred to Koro Toro prison, some dying en route (Human Rights Watch, October 2023). Three years on, no criminal investigation had been opened, and the November 2023 amnesty law shields those responsible for the abuses from accountability (OMCT, October 2025; Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026). The law that protects the forces who fired is not the law applied to the people fired on.
  • The killing of an opponent drew no investigation at all. On 28 February 2024, weeks before the presidential election, security forces killed opposition leader Yaya Dillo, president of the Socialist Party Without Borders, during a raid on his party's headquarters in N'Djamena; more than a year later the authorities had not clarified the circumstances, and the death was still uninvestigated in 2025 (Human Rights Watch, August 2025; World Report 2026). Twenty-four of Dillo's relatives arrested at the time were held at Koro Toro and released only in December 2024; his party's subsequent head, Robert Gam, was released in June 2025 after eight months' detention without charge (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026).
  • The same pattern — no inquiry — covers the state's own side's violence: celebratory gunfire by security forces after the May 2024 election victory killed at least 11 people, including children, with no meaningful inquiry, prosecutions, or compensation documented through 2025 (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026).
  • The elected term's central prosecution was of the runner-up. Masra, who won 18.53% in May 2024, was arrested on 16 May 2025 over alleged links to the Mandakao intercommunal killings of 14 May 2025 (government figure: at least 41 killed), tried alongside 74 co-defendants, pleaded not guilty, and on 9 August 2025 was convicted of spreading racist and xenophobic messages and complicity in murder and sentenced to 20 years and a 1 billion CFA franc (~USD 1.8 million) fine (Human Rights Watch, 12 August 2025). Human Rights Watch assessed the charges as politically motivated — recorded here as that organisation's assessment, contested by the Chadian court that convicted and by the Supreme Court, which upheld the conviction in May 2026. The August 2026 pardon freed Masra but left the conviction and the fine standing, so the legal disability on the main opponent remains while the prison door opened (Human Rights Watch, 21 August 2026). Whether Masra incited violence is an ALLEGATION the court answered and monitors dispute; what is documented is the asymmetry: the opponent's alleged incitement reached a 20-year sentence in under three months, while Black Thursday, Dillo's killing, and the celebratory-gunfire deaths have produced no investigation in years.

Why: One law means the same process reaches the state's own forces and the state's opponents. Here the documented process ran fast and hard in one direction — against protesters, a killed opponent's party, and the election runner-up — and did not run at all in the other: an amnesty instead of an investigation for October 2022, silence on Dillo, silence on the celebratory gunfire. The inclusive appointments and the 2026 pardons are real, and they sit on the FOR shelf; they are acts of discretion by the seat, not a law that binds the seat, and discretion that can pardon is the opposite of one law for all.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — security-force members shielded by the 2023 amnesty; MPS insiders who faced no Black Thursday accountability; Déby himself, whose principal rival was removed from politics for fifteen months. Hurt — the families of those killed on 20 October 2022 (128 by the national commission's count; more than 218 by OMCT/LTDH's), the Koro Toro detainees, Yaya Dillo's family and party, Succès Masra and his 74 co-defendants, and the families of the at least 11 people killed by celebratory gunfire in 2024.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the legislature keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The transition did produce the institutions it promised, in form: a constitutional referendum on 17 December 2023 (official result: 85.90% yes, 14.10% no, turnout 62.8%, validated by the Supreme Court on 28 December 2023), a presidential election in May 2024, the first legislative elections since 2011 on 29 December 2024, and Chad's first-ever Senate elections in February 2025, completing the bicameral parliament the 2023 constitution created (Supreme Court validation via WNN Africa, December 2023; AFP, March 2025).
  • The Constitutional Council did hear and rule on challenges rather than refusing them: it rejected Masra's appeal against the 2024 presidential result on 16 May 2024, rejected the Bloc Fédéral's annulment appeal against the referendum, and in the Senate tally reallocated two seats initially assigned to the MPS to allied parties following appeals (Crisis Group, May 2024; Constitutional Council via AFP, March 2025).
  • Critical monitors still publish: Human Rights Watch, CIVICUS, RSF, and Chadian rights leagues issue detailed reports naming the government's conduct, and this dossier is built from them.

Evidence against:

  • The clearest grip in the series: the term limit itself was removed. In September 2025 the National Assembly — in which the MPS holds 124 of 188 seats — overwhelmingly approved amendments extending the presidential term from five to seven years and removing limits on re-election; the amended constitution was promulgated on 8 October 2025, "effectively enabling Déby to remain in power indefinitely, subject to holding elections every seven years" (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026). A further September 2025 proposal, to amend Article 77 so the president may lead a political party, is documented as sparking intense debate (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). The referee that was supposed to end his tenure was rewritten by a legislature his party dominates, two years into the term the voters granted under the old limit.
  • The legislature's composition was itself produced under boycott and control claims. The MPS won 124 of 188 National Assembly seats (45.18% of the vote; turnout 51.56–52.38% in conflicting official-adjacent counts, printed separately) and 43 of 46 elected Senate seats, with Déby appointing the final 23 senators; Les Transformateurs and allied opposition parties boycotted both votes, calling the results pre-fabricated — an interested party's characterisation, recorded as such, but the boycott itself is documented fact (ANGE results, January 2025; AFP, March 2025). Under Chad's winner-takes-all multi-member rules a party over 50% in a constituency takes every seat, which converted a 45% vote share into two-thirds of the Assembly.
  • The electoral referee's independence is contested by the disputants who must use it. Opposition groups boycotted the 2023 referendum saying the junta controlled the process, called its figures not credible (GCAP president Max Kemkoye: "a second coup d'état"), and boycotted the December 2024 elections over a "corrupt" electoral register and lack of free-and-fair guarantees (Reuters, December 2023; parliamentary-election record, October 2024). International NGOs described the May 2024 presidential election as "not free nor credible" (AFP, March 2025). These are assessments by named disputants, not findings of this dossier; what is documented is that every referee body in the chain — CONOREC, ANGE, the Constitutional Council — was constituted during a transition Déby headed.
  • The courts' autonomy is contradicted by the Masra sequence and the GCAP dissolution: the Supreme Court dissolved the opposition GCAP coalition in April 2026, its leaders were sentenced in May 2026 to eight years for plotting insurrection, and the same court upheld Masra's 20-year sentence in May 2026 — after which only a presidential pardon, an act of the executive, could free any of them (Human Rights Watch, August 2026; New African, 2026).
  • The media referee is intimidated on a documented pattern. In March 2025 three journalists were arrested accused of spying for Russia, among them staff of the newspaper Le Pays; RSF's 27 August 2025 report documented a persistent campaign of surveillance and intimidation of critical journalists over the preceding 18 months — covert following by men in civilian or military attire in unmarked vehicles — driving some into exile, hiding, or out of the profession (Le Monde via Maghrebi, April 2025; RSF via CIVICUS Monitor, August 2025). In September 2025 the authorities revoked the citizenship of an activist and a journalist (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025).

Why: Holding without gripping means the referees can say no and make it stick. In Chad the referees were all built during his transition, the legislature they produced removed the limit on his tenure, the courts dissolved the opposition coalition and upheld the runner-up's 20-year sentence, and journalists work under documented surveillance. The FOR shelf shows institutions existing and occasionally ruling against his party at the margins; existence is not autonomy, and the one decision that mattered most — how long he may stay — was taken in his favour by his own majority.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Déby, whose tenure is now legally unbounded; the MPS, holding 124 of 188 Assembly seats and 43 of 46 elected Senate seats. Hurt — voters whose 2024 grant of a five-year, limited mandate was rewritten mid-term; opposition parties dissolved, boycotting, or led from prison; journalists surveilled, arrested, exiled, or stripped of citizenship; and the GCAP leaders sentenced in May 2026.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, the ruler at least considered it openly.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The France file is the strongest documented case in this dossier of a ruler reversing himself openly when another option became available. At his 23 May 2024 inauguration Déby promised to end arrangements with states that treated Chad as a colony; on 28 November 2024 Chad terminated the defence cooperation agreement with France, and the last French forces left by 31 January 2025 (government statements via Military Africa, December 2024; AFP chronology, 2025). Then, when a renegotiated partnership became available, he considered it in the open rather than treating the rupture as irreversible doctrine: he met President Macron at the Élysée on 29 January 2026, the two presidents announced a "revitalised partnership" prioritising economic cooperation, a defence declaration of intent was signed in Paris on 13 October 2026, and French aircraft made a joint-exercise stopover in N'Djamena on 19 November 2026 — a new arrangement on stated new terms, not a quiet restoration of the old bases (Élysée joint communiqué, January 2026; defence-ministers' declaration, October 2026; African Press Agency reporting, November 2026).
  • Pushback over the political prisoners was eventually answered with an adjustment, not only with denial: the 14 August 2026 pardons of Masra and the eight GCAP leaders followed the Supreme Court's May 2026 upholding of the sentences and sustained international criticism, and Masra on release called for reconciliation (Reuters, August 2026; The Nation Africa, August 2026).
  • The Sudan war's spillover was answered with a stated, reversible measure: on 23 February 2026 Chad closed its eastern border with Sudan indefinitely, citing repeated incursions by Sudanese armed groups after fighting at al-Tina, while stating that limited humanitarian exemptions could still be granted with prior approval (reporting of the government decision, February 2026).
  • The no-good-choice clause is applied here, by its mechanics, to the succession moment of April 2021 — and only to that moment. The options that actually existed when Idriss Déby was killed in battle, with FACT rebels advancing, were: (a) follow the constitution and hand interim power to the President of the National Assembly, amid an active rebel offensive and an army whose command was the regime's own; (b) a military council holding the capital and the army together while a transition was organised. Each carried real harm — constitutional rupture in the first case's likely collapse, constitutional rupture by design in the second. The choice made, (b), is graded as a choice inside a trap Déby did not build: the personalised, military-centred state was his father's construction over 31 years. What remains gradable, and is graded in Tests 2 and 5, is what he did inside the trap afterwards — extending it, making himself eligible, and removing the limits — and whether he was honest about it; the record there is that the extensions were announced openly, as decisions, not concealed.

Evidence against:

  • The street's first and largest pushback was answered with live fire and then with an amnesty that ended the conversation. The 20 October 2022 protests were against a wobble-response already made: the DNIS, boycotted by Wakit Tama, Les Transformateurs, the main rebel groups FACT and CCMSR, and the Catholic Church, had resolved to extend the transition by two years and make transition leaders eligible to run (NSA analysis, 2022; CIVICUS Monitor, 2022). The documented response to the protests was the crackdown graded in Test 1, a curfew, and bans on opposition parties including Wakit Tama (government statements, October 2022). No investigation, no revision of the extension, no published reconsideration followed in the three years documented by OMCT (October 2025).
  • Voters' and parties' pushback at each subsequent ballot — referendum boycott, election challenges, legislative boycott — was answered by proceeding on the announced figures each time, with the challenges rejected by bodies constituted under the transition (see Test 2). Proceeding is not adjustment; the one structural demand of the "no" campaign, a federal rather than unitary state — a preference a 2023 poll by the Network of Chadian Journalists and Reporters put at 71% of Chadians — was not taken up, and the constitution kept the unitary form (referendum record, 2023).
  • The 2025 constitutional revision was pushed through at the moment of maximum pushback capacity — after Masra's August 2025 sentencing had removed the main opposition voice — and approved "allowing for little debate or opposition" (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026). When the wobble came from inside his own coalition's parliament, the answer was to use its majority, not to open the question.

Why: Two different rulers appear in this file. Abroad, Déby adjusted in public, twice, in opposite directions, and said why each time — the France reversal is exactly what considering another option openly looks like. At home, every pushback that touched his tenure — the street in 2022, the boycotts, the federalism demand — was met by force, by proceeding, or by using the majority the pushback was protesting. The pardons of August 2026 are a genuine late adjustment on the prisoners file, and they keep this grade at MIXED rather than FAILS; they do not reach the tenure file, where no adjustment has ever been documented.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Chad's bargaining position with France, renegotiated from a position of stated sovereignty; the nine pardoned opposition figures and their families (August 2026); border communities given a stated, if severe, security rationale in February 2026. Hurt — the October 2022 protesters, whose demand was never revisited; federalist voters, whose documented majority preference was set aside without a second look; Sudanese refugees and traders cut off by the February 2026 border closure, humanitarian exemptions notwithstanding.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the south and the north, the protesters, the displaced, the refugees, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest counting-in of the term is the refugee file, and it is documented at scale. Chad kept its border open to Sudanese fleeing the war that began in April 2023 for nearly three years: by December 2025 Chad hosted 1.5 million refugees in total, of whom Sudanese numbered about 1.3 million, with 100% of asylum-seekers able to access asylum procedures in 2025 and prima facie recognition for new arrivals (UNHCR Annual Results Report 2025, Chad, May 2026; WFP/UNHCR joint statement, April 2026). One in three people in eastern Chad is now a refugee, and the state continued receiving them while itself one of the world's poorest countries (UNHCR/WFP, April 2026).
  • The elected government's development frame is written for the whole map: the National Development Plan "Chad Connexion 2030," validated by the Council of Ministers on 29 May 2025 and launched in Abu Dhabi on 10 November 2025, comprises 268 projects and reforms at about USD 30 billion, with stated ambitions to raise GDP by 60% and lift 2.5 million Chadians out of poverty by 2030, and a World Bank water-security grant of USD 160 million approved on 18 June 2026 is directed at refugee-hosting and vulnerable provinces, with about 1 million beneficiaries, over half from host and refugee communities (Council of Ministers validation via Capmad, 2025; World Bank press release, 18 June 2026). The IMF's four-year Extended Credit Facility of about USD 630 million, agreed at staff level on 22 May 2025, is anchored on the same plan with social-protection measures (IMF, May 2025).
  • Inclusion at the table, when it happened, crossed the country's deepest line: a southerner and Christian, Masra, was made prime minister in January 2024, and before him Kebzabo, a southern opposition veteran, held the post through the transition's hardest year.

Evidence against:

  • The south's protesters were counted as a security problem, and the south's deadliest file of the elected term was answered by prosecuting a southern opposition leader rather than by any documented protection programme. Intercommunal herder–farmer violence persisted through 2025 in the south and east: at least 41 killed at Mandakao on 14 May 2025 (government figure) and at least 17 killed, including women and children, at Orégomel in June 2025 (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026). The documented state response that reached a verdict was Masra's (Test 1); no comparable documented accountability for the killings themselves appears in the record assembled here.
  • The poverty count moved against the poor during the elected term. Extreme poverty ($2.15/day) rose from 33.9% in 2023 to 36.5% in 2024 — some 688,000 additional people — and was projected to keep rising to 38.4% in 2025 and 45.4% in 2026 (World Bank figures via ARIES Consult strategic report, April 2026). These are projections and estimates, printed as such; the direction, on the sourced figures, is documented, and it falls on a population already about 44.8% poor at the national line (2022).
  • The refugee welcome outran the resources, and the shortfall is counted in rations: by April 2026 WFP and UNHCR could assist only four of every ten refugees, had halved food assistance for most, faced a USD 428 million shortfall, and reported 80,000 families without shelter and more than 243,000 people stranded in exposed border areas (WFP/UNHCR joint statement via Reuters, 9 April 2026). The funding failure is substantially donors', and is recorded as such; the February 2026 border closure (Test 3) is the point at which the counting-in stopped, and it was Chad's decision.
  • Elite composition is contested on ethnic lines. Crisis Group's July–December 2026 outlook records Déby promoting Zaghawa officers to secure the loyalty of the officer corps amid the Sudan spillover, and analysts describe Zaghawa dominance of appointments — recorded here as analyst assessment and ALLEGATION-level characterisation, not as a documented appointments audit, because none appears in the record assembled here (Crisis Group, On the Horizon, August 2026; ARIES Consult, April 2026). No documented counterweight — a published, regionally balanced appointments record — appears either.

Why: The refugee file is a genuine, large, sustained counting-in, maintained for years at real cost to a poor state, and the development plan is written in universal terms. Against it: the southern farming communities whose deadliest years brought prosecution of their best-known political voice rather than documented protection, a poverty rate rising through the elected term, and a border that closed in February 2026. Both shelves are heavy; neither cancels the other, and the grade is MIXED because the record is.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — about 1.3 million Sudanese refugees granted asylum and prima facie recognition, and eastern host communities reached by the 2026 World Bank water programme. Hurt — farming communities in Logone Occidental and Mayo-Kebbi Ouest, where at least 41 and at least 17 people were killed in 2025 with no documented accountability for the killings; the roughly 688,000 people who fell into extreme poverty in 2024; refugees whose rations were halved and the 243,000 left in exposed border areas; and those turned back after the February 2026 closure.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Transition pledges and public statements vs. the record since 20 April 2021, weighted to the elected term since 23 May 2024.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Elections were, in the end, held — every one of them. The transition that began in April 2021 delivered the December 2023 referendum, the May 2024 presidential election, the December 2024 legislative elections (the first since 2011), and the February 2025 Senate elections. Chad was the first of the Sahel's military-led states to complete a full electoral cycle (Reuters, January 2025). The dates slipped (below); the events occurred.
  • The France promise — kept as written. His 23 May 2024 inauguration pledge to end colonial-style arrangements was carried out within the year: the defence agreement terminated 28 November 2024, French forces gone by 31 January 2025 (see Test 3). This is crossing 1 in full: the word was kept, and on the documented record it helped the constituency that had demanded it — civil-society and opposition groups had publicly pressed for the French departure.
  • The development programme — launched as promised, unproven as delivered. The president's political programme of 12 flagship projects and 100 actions was folded into Chad Connexion 2030, validated May 2025, launched November 2025 with USD 20.5 billion in announced pledges against the USD 30 billion sought, and made the mandatory frame for the 2027 budget (Ministry of Finance circular, 2026; Afrikactus, October 2026). Announced pledges are not disbursements — the plan's own framework distinguishes them — so this is recorded as in motion, not kept.
  • The reconciliation word of 2026 — partially kept. The August 2026 pardons were issued, the presidency said, on the attorneys' request, and all nine men were released (presidency statement via Reuters, 14 August 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The founding promise — broken twice over. In April 2021 the CMT promised a transition of 18 months to free and democratic elections, and Déby pledged not to run for the presidency; the African Union's Peace and Security Council stated on 14 May 2021 that no extension beyond the 18 months ending October 2022 would be acceptable and called on transition leaders not to contest the elections they organised (AU PSC via US Senate resolution text, 2022; AFP, 2022). The record: the DNIS extended the transition by a further two years in October 2022 and made transition leaders eligible; the presidential election was held in May 2024, 37 months after the takeover; and Déby ran, and won. This is crossing 4 in full: the word was broken, and the hurt is documented in Test 1 — the protests against the extension were the ones fired on during Black Thursday.
  • The voters' mandate was rewritten mid-term. The May 2024 electorate voted under a constitution setting a five-year term; the September–October 2025 amendments extended terms to seven years and removed limits altogether. Whatever the amendments' legality under the Assembly's procedure, the promise the 2024 ballot embodied — a bounded mandate — was replaced by an unbounded one without returning to the voters (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026).
  • The justice word — receding. No investigation into Black Thursday, the Dillo killing, or the 2024 celebratory-gunfire deaths had been opened by late 2025, and the November 2023 amnesty law forecloses the first of them; reparations for victims of the Hissène Habré era, begun partially in 2024, made little progress in 2025 (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026; OMCT, October 2025). Crossing 4 again: commitments to accountability were made by the transition he headed, and the outcome for the victims' families is documented hurt.
  • The pardon that kept the conviction. The August 2026 pardon freed Masra but expressly did not waive the civil penalties, and the conviction stands — HRW's position is that it must be vacated for Masra to participate in politics (presidency statement via Reuters, August 2026; Human Rights Watch, 21 August 2026). A reconciliation that leaves the reconciled man a convict with a USD 1.8 million fine is recorded as crossing 3 in reverse form: the release helped, and is credited above; the word it breaks is the fuller reconciliation the release was presented as, and that shortfall is recorded here rather than in the ledger of the release itself. The two axes are kept separate throughout this test: the France file is kept-word-and-helped (crossing 1); no documented case of kept-word-and-hurt (crossing 2) at presidential level appears in the record assembled here, and none is invented; the border closure of February 2026 is a kept security word whose hurt falls on refugees, recorded in Tests 3 and 4 — it is the closest approach to crossing 2 and is named as such, as an approach, not a clean instance.

Why: Test 5 grades the word that founded the holding, and that word — 18 months, elections, and not standing in them — was broken on every element, in the open, over three years, with the breaking enforced by the crackdown graded in Test 1. The elected term adds a second broken bound: the five-year limited mandate of May 2024 became, by his own majority's amendment, a seven-year unlimited one. Against that stand real kept words — the electoral cycle completed, the France pledge executed, a development plan launched — and they are credited in full on the FOR shelf. They do not outweigh the founding file, because every later promise he made was made from a seat he held by breaking the first one.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Déby himself, converted from transitional military ruler to elected president with an unlimited mandate; the MPS, entrenched across both chambers; constituencies that wanted the French departure and got it. Hurt — the Chadian voters of 2021's promise, who waited 37 months for an election its organiser won; the October 2022 protesters, hurt at the moment the break was announced; Masra, freed but still convicted; and future Chadian electorates, whose term limit — the one promise a constitution makes to people not yet voting — no longer exists.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: FAILS.

Mahamat Idriss Déby is graded on a holding that began, in April 2021, inside a trap he did not build — a battlefield succession in a personalised military state, with rebels advancing — and the dossier applies the no-good-choice clause to that moment alone. Everything after it was choice. One law FAILS because accountability ran in one direction only: a 20-year sentence in under three months for the election runner-up, and no investigation in years for Black Thursday (128 killed by the national commission's count; more than 218 by OMCT/LTDH's), for Yaya Dillo's killing, or for the 11 killed by his own forces' celebratory fire — with an amnesty law closing the largest file; the hurt falls on victims' families, detainees, and the opposition, the help on the shielded forces and the seat itself. Without gripping FAILS because the referees were all constituted under his transition and the decisive one — the term limit — was abolished by his party's two-thirds Assembly majority in October 2025, while courts dissolved the opposition coalition and journalists worked under documented surveillance. The wobble is MIXED because abroad he adjusted openly and twice — ending the French defence pact, then considering the renegotiated partnership in public when it became available, and pardoning the political prisoners in August 2026 — while at home every pushback touching his tenure was met by force or by proceeding. Counts everyone is MIXED because the counting-in was real and enormous — some 1.3 million Sudanese refugees received, asylum access at 100% in 2025 — and so was the counting-out: southern communities whose 2025 dead brought no documented accountability, extreme poverty rising by some 688,000 people in a year, and a border closed in February 2026. His word FAILS because the founding promises — 18 months, and not to run — were broken on every element, and the bounded mandate the voters granted in May 2024 was rewritten into an unbounded one; the kept words on the other shelf — the completed electoral cycle, the France pledge, the launched development plan — were all delivered from the seat the broken word secured. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether Masra's conviction is vacated or only pardoned, whether any Black Thursday or Dillo investigation is ever opened, whether Chad Connexion 2030's pledges become disbursements, and whether the seven-year unlimited mandate is ever again submitted to a contest the referees do not owe him.
