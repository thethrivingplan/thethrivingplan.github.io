The Three-Body Test — Yes, No, Otherwise

Three stars under real Newtonian gravity. Which star gets ejected first? These pages publish three computed runs that answer that question — a generic three, a figure-eight control, and a generic four — with the method fixed in advance, the energy drift printed as the honesty check, and the full initial conditions below so anyone can re-run them.

Honest label — read this first

Real computed runs, done November 24, 2026. This is a simulation: pure Newtonian gravity integrated numerically, not footage of real stars. Nothing here is drawn by hand or tidied after the fact. Initial conditions, timesteps, and drift figures are printed in full, failed attempts are named where they happened, and chaotic runs are flagged as chaotic. Where a result depends on the exact numbers, the page says so.

    1 · The Question

    Which star gets ejected first?

    These runs answer a TikTok simulation by satisfactman (@satisfactman), titled in spirit: “Three stars. Real Newtonian gravity. Which star gets ejected first?” Three equal stars, honest gravity, no script — the only question is who leaves.

    In his video, the answer was the cyan star — the one that started at the lower right. After 83 counted slingshots, cyan was thrown out, and pink + orange remained behind as a bound pair.

    Watch satisfactman's video ↗

    The runs below use our own integrator and our own starting numbers — a separate test of the same question, not a copy of his. One resemblance is noted where it occurs, and labeled as a resemblance, not a replication.

    2 · The Method

    The boxes were fixed before running

    Nothing was tuned after seeing an outcome. The rules below were set first, and every run is reported against them — including the runs that failed.

    • Real Newtonian gravityPure Newtonian gravity, no softening, no tricks. G = 1, every star has mass 1, distances of order 1.
    • The integratorLeapfrog (kick–drift–kick). Runs A and B use a fixed timestep; Run C uses the same leapfrog with an adaptive timestep — the honesty notes explain why.
    • Equal massesEvery star weighs the same. No star starts privileged; whatever happens is arrangement, not favoritism.
    • What “ejected” meansA star is more than 20 distance units from the barycentre of the others, its energy relative to them is positive (unbound), and it is still receding.
    • Energy drift is the honesty checkTotal energy must not drift. Every run prints its drift. A run whose energy blows up is a broken run, not a result — and broken runs are named, below.
    • Outcomes in plain languageWho left, when, how fast; who stayed, how tightly bound, with what period. No outcome is upgraded or softened.
    Chaos flag — applies where marked

    Runs A and C are chaotic systems. In a chaotic run, which star leaves first can depend on the exact numbers; the shape of the ending is the robust result. Each run below carries its own flag rather than hiding behind this one.

    3 · Run A — Generic Three

    The three that threw a star out

    A rough triangle, small tangential velocities, centre-of-mass frame. Timestep dt = 0.0002. This is the video's own test, run with our numbers.

    Star 0 — the one that started at the right-hand point — was ejected at t = 7.36. Early. The triple did not dance for long: it had one hard encounter and threw a star.

    t = 7.36Ejection time, Star 0
    v∞ = 3.43Asymptotic departure speed (3.46 as it crossed the 20-unit line)
    a = 0.091Semi-major axis of the Stars 1 + 2 binary left behind
    0.122Orbital period of that hard binary
    0.025%Energy drift, final (worst moment 0.057% — inside the 0.1% bar)

    Stars 1 and 2 stayed together as a hard, tight binary. The ejected star's speed and the pair's tightness are the same event seen from two sides — the pair fell deep into its own well, and the kick that bound it is the kick that threw Star 0 out.

    A resemblance, labeled as one: in satisfactman's video the ejected star also started at the right-hand side, and the other two also remained as the pair. Different setups, different numbers — not a replication.

    Chaos note: Run A is chaotic, though its outcome — Star 0 out at t ≈ 7.4, hard pair left behind — was identical at two different fixed timesteps.

    4 · Run B — Figure-Eight, the Control

    The three that does NOT eject

    The Chenciner–Montgomery figure-eight: three equal stars chasing each other down one shared figure-eight track, each taking the same path in turn. Standard published initial conditions. Timestep dt = 0.0005, three full periods (period ≈ 6.3259, total t ≈ 18.98).

    It held. Three full periods, no ejection, no pair forming, nobody left behind.

    3Full periods completed
    0.00068Maximum deviation of any star from the shared track
    0.00001%Energy drift, worst moment — the integrator is not inventing anything here

    This run is the control, and it matters: three does not automatically mean chaos and ejection. A three can be a perfect, repeating weave when the arrangement is exactly balanced. The generic three falls apart; the balanced three holds. The difference is the arrangement, not the number.

    5 · Run C — Generic Four

    Two ejections — and an exchanged pair

    Two loose pairs on a slow mutual approach: Pair A (Stars 0, 1) around (−1.8, 0); Pair B (Stars 2, 3) around (1.8, 0.3), turning the opposite way — shifted to the centre-of-mass frame. Adaptive leapfrog: dt = 0.002 × the closest pair's dynamical time (η = 0.002, capped at dt = 0.01); 728,596 steps.

    t = 72.57Star 3 ejected first — gently, departure speed settling to 0.52
    t = 110.14Star 1 ejected second — even more gently, asymptotic speed 0.16, barely unbound
    a = 0.256Semi-major axis of the surviving pair, Stars 0 + 2
    0.577Orbital period of the surviving pair
    0.052%Energy drift, final (worst moment 0.121% — a brief excursion during the single closest encounter, stated plainly rather than rounded away)

    The pair that survived was Stars 0 and 2 — one from each original pair. An exchange, not a survival of either starting pair.

    So the four did not end as two pairs, and not as a triple-plus-one. It ended as one pair and two singles, and the pair that survived was made of strangers.

    Chaos flag — Run C

    Run C is chaotic, and its details are sensitive. The same initial conditions integrated at η = 0.005 instead of 0.002 gave the same kind of ending — two ejections and one exchanged bound pair — but with a different individual star leaving first (Star 2 at t = 78, then Star 1 at t = 129; final pair 0–3). Who leaves first depends on the exact numbers. The shape of the resolution is the robust result.

    Discarded attempts, on the record: fixed-step attempts at Run C failed honestly before this run. A collapsing four-body system throws encounters too close for a fixed step, and the integrator answered with energy errors of thousands of percent — broken runs, discarded. That is why Run C uses the adaptive step. The drift figures above are the ones from the run reported.

    6 · The Reading — the house reading, labeled as such

    What could this mean?

    What follows is a reading, not a result. The results end above; this is the Earth Treatment applied to them — one house's way of seeing the shape the runs made. It claims nothing the numbers didn't do.

    Two binds. Yes and no, the solved pair: two-body gravity is the one case with a closed answer — clean ellipses, each star the other's whole world, forever. The pair holds because each is the other's counter.

    Three is otherwise. There is no general closed answer for three, and an unbalanced three does what Run A did: it resolves into a pair + free. Yes and no bind tighter for having shed the otherwise — the pair's orbit literally shrinks — and the otherwise carries the difference away, unbound, on its own road. Not destroyed. Not punished. Otherwise.

    The figure-eight is the three as a weave. Run B is the standing counterexample, filed beside the claim rather than under it: the same three, balanced exactly, holds forever — all three on one shared road, trading places, nobody paired and nobody exiled. Three does not mean ejection. Arrangement decides.

    Four resolved as pair + two free — with an exchanged pair. Completeness was not tidiness. The four shed an otherwise and an opposite-to-the-otherwise, and the pair that survived was woven across the original boundaries: one from each starting pair, bound to a stranger.

    HYPOTHESIS CONFIRMED. ONCE.

    Exactly that claim, and no bigger. Once — by a run whose numbers anyone can re-run, with its counterexample standing on the same page.
