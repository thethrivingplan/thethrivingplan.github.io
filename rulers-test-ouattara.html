The Ruler's Test — Alassane Ouattara, President of Côte d'Ivoire

Subject: Alassane Dramane Ouattara — President of the Republic of Côte d'Ivoire, in office since April–May 2011. The seat graded is the presidency, which in Côte d'Ivoire's system is the executive seat: head of state and head of government in substance, commander of the armed forces, holder of the pardon power, and appointer of the prime minister, the president of the Constitutional Council, and the senior judiciary and security leadership. Ouattara, born 1 January 1942, is an economist — former governor of the Central Bank of West African States (BCEAO), former deputy managing director of the IMF, and prime minister under Félix Houphouët-Boigny (1990–1993). He won the 28 November 2010 run-off as certified by the electoral commission and the United Nations (54.1%); his predecessor Laurent Gbagbo refused to concede, the Constitutional Council of that day annulled votes in Ouattara's northern strongholds and proclaimed Gbagbo the winner, and the ensuing crisis killed about 3,000 people (UN count) before Gbagbo's arrest in April 2011. That crisis is context here, not graded conduct. Ouattara was re-elected in 2015 (about 84%), again in 2020 (94.27%, opposition boycott), and again on 25 October 2025 (89.77%, turnout officially 50.10%), and was sworn in for a fourth term on 8 December 2025. He is graded on the full tenure since 2011, weighted to the third and fourth terms, where the contested record sits. The premise of the brief is verified: he still holds the seat in November 2026. Two premise corrections are recorded where they belong in the evidence: the instrument that kept Gbagbo off the 2025 ballot was a presidential pardon (6 August 2022) that erased the sentence but not the conviction — the amnesty route, which would have erased both, was never taken — and the first-instance exclusion of Tidjane Thiam was made by an Abidjan court on 22 April 2025, with the Constitutional Council's role following from it. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The standing ethic of the Test is printed here because it governs every grade below: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule also governs: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one — carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done: a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier names the options that actually existed, why each carried real harm, and grades the choice made inside the constraint — while still grading whether he built the trap earlier, whether he was honest inside it, and whether he took the least-harm door actually open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The laws that removed his rivals were real laws, applied by courts through stated legal reasoning, not decrees of exclusion. Gbagbo's bar rests on an in-absentia conviction (January 2018, 20 years) for the taking of BCEAO funds during the 2010–11 crisis — a taking that is itself documented, whatever one thinks of a trial held in the defendant's absence while he was in ICC custody. Thiam's removal rests on Article 48 of the 1961 Nationality Code, a text that predates Ouattara's presidency by half a century. Affi N'Guessan's 2025 rejection was on sponsorship-signature grounds, a rule applied to dozens of minor candidates as well: the Constitutional Council validated 5 of 60 candidacy files in 2025 and 4 of 44 in 2020, and most of the rejected were unknowns, not heavyweights (Constitutional Council decisions, 14 September 2020 and 8 September 2025; International IDEA democracy tracker, April 2025).
  • The opposition was never banned as such. Simone Gbagbo — the former first lady, convicted in 2015 and amnestied by Ouattara in August 2018 as part of an amnesty covering some 800 people — was validated as a presidential candidate in 2025 and took 2.42%. The PDCI contested the December 2025 legislative elections and won 32 of 255 seats. In 2020 the Council validated Bédié and Affi N'Guessan, the two leading opposition candidates who had filed compliant files (Constitutional Council, September 2020; CEI results, December 2025).
  • One senior figure from his own side of the 2011 war was prosecuted to the maximum: Amadé Ouérémi, a militia commander who fought for the pro-Ouattara forces, was sentenced to life imprisonment in April 2021 for his role in the March 2011 Duékoué massacre. It stands as the single major domestic conviction of a commander from the winning side (contemporaneous AFP/Reuters reporting, April 2021).
  • His clemency instruments were used toward opponents, repeatedly: the 2018 amnesty (~800 people, including Simone Gbagbo), Gbagbo's passport and freedom to return (2021), and the 6 August 2022 presidential pardon of Gbagbo himself, with his bank accounts unfrozen and his presidential annuity restored, announced "in the interests of strengthening social cohesion" (presidential address, 6 August 2022).
  • The economic rules of his tenure were general in form and application: the Conseil du Café-Cacao's guaranteed farmgate price (at least 60% of the reference price by the system's own rule) is paid to every registered producer regardless of politics; the IMF-supported fiscal program, the mining and petroleum codes, and the tax-base broadening of 2023–2026 fell on the whole economy, his own camp's business networks included (World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, 2026; IMF Country Report 26/171, June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The pattern of who the law eliminates is one-directional, and it is total. Every heavyweight who could have contested his third and fourth terms was removed by a legal instrument produced or activated during his presidency: Gbagbo (in-absentia conviction, January 2018, while in ICC custody in The Hague, for a crisis-era funds seizure — his eligibility then held in place by the choice of pardon over amnesty, below); Guillaume Soro, his own former prime minister and Assembly speaker turned rival (20 years in absentia, April 2020, after a one-day trial; life imprisonment in absentia, June 2021; his movement GPS dissolved by the same judgment and his assets confiscated); Charles Blé Goudé (in-absentia conviction, 2019; struck from the roll); and Thiam (below). No figure of Ouattara's own camp has faced any comparable judicial elimination from political life in fifteen years (Constitutional Council decisions, 2020 and 2025; court reporting, 2019–2025).
  • The Thiam instrument was a dormant law woken for one man. Article 48 of the 1961 Nationality Code — automatic loss of Ivorian nationality on acquiring another — had, in Thiam's words, "never been applied to anybody"; it was applied to him weeks after the PDCI named him its candidate, on a complaint brought by private petitioners, after the electoral commission itself had dismissed ten similar complaints as ill-founded on 16 April 2025. The Abidjan Court of First Instance held on 22 April 2025 that he lost his nationality in 1987 when he acquired French citizenship, that his February–March 2025 renunciation of French nationality came too late to restore his place on a roll he had joined in 2022, and that the decision could not be appealed. His lawyers' argument that he was French by birth — which would take him outside Article 48 — was rejected for insufficient proof (court reporting and counsel statements, April 2025; International IDEA, April 2025).
  • The pardon-over-amnesty choice is the cleanest single exhibit. Ouattara had two instruments for Gbagbo in 2022: amnesty, which under Ivorian law erases the conviction and its civil consequences, and pardon (grâce), which erases only the sentence. He chose the pardon, publicly, as a reconciliation gesture — and its legal effect, whether intended or not, was that Gbagbo remained a convicted man stripped of civil rights and therefore off the electoral roll for 2025. Gbagbo's party and counsel state the choice was deliberate; the presidency has never explained why the lesser instrument was chosen if full reconciliation was the aim. The African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights had ordered in September 2020 that all obstacles to Gbagbo's registration be removed; the order was not implemented, and Gbagbo's further challenge was dismissed by that court in June 2025 on the record as it stood (African Court orders, September 2020; case reporting, June 2025).
  • The 2011 account was settled by one law for the losers and another for the winners. The ICC indicted only the Gbagbo camp; domestic prosecutions of the crisis years fell overwhelmingly on the same side; and human-rights investigators documented for years that crimes by pro-Ouattara forces — including at Duékoué, where hundreds were killed in March 2011 — went almost entirely unprosecuted, Ouérémi's 2021 conviction the exception that proves the shape (Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International reporting on Côte d'Ivoire, 2011–2021). Simone Gbagbo's 2017 acquittal by an Ivorian assize court on crimes-against-humanity charges is recorded on the other side of this shelf's ledger: the courts were capable of ruling against the prosecution's interest in a Gbagbo case, once, in a trial whose conduct the ICC's outstanding warrant against her (never executed; she was not transferred) left contested.
  • The eligibility law that protected him was of his own making (developed under Test 5): the 2016 constitution's Article 55 — "elected for five years… re-elected only once" — says nothing about terms served before its adoption, and the reading that reset his count to zero was validated by a Constitutional Council whose president is a presidential appointee, in 2020 and again by implication in 2025. A rule a ruler writes, whose ambiguity only he benefits from, and whose referee he appoints, is not one law for all at the source, even when every step is formally legal (Constitution of 2016, Art. 55; Constitutional Council, September 2020).

Why the grade lands here: The FOR shelf is genuine — real statutes, real courts, real clemency, a general economic rulebook, and one life sentence for a commander of his own side. But Test 1 grades the source, and at the source the record runs one way: across fifteen years, every legal instrument that decided who may compete for his seat fell on a rival, the one dormant article was woken for exactly one registrant, and the one discretionary choice that would have restored his oldest rival (amnesty) was declined in favor of the instrument that left the bar standing (pardon). No comparable instrument has ever fallen on his own side. That is a second law in operation, whatever the formal legality of each step. Helped: Alassane Ouattara and the RHDP (an uncontested field in 2020 and 2025); Simone Gbagbo and the ~800 amnestied in 2018; cocoa producers and taxpayers under general economic rules; Gbagbo personally, in part — freed from his sentence, his accounts and annuity restored. Hurt: Laurent Gbagbo, Tidjane Thiam, Guillaume Soro, and Charles Blé Goudé (each eliminated from electoral politics by conviction or roll removal); the voters of the PPA-CI, PDCI, and GPS, whose chosen candidates the law removed before any vote; victims of pro-Ouattara forces in 2011, whose cases were, Ouérémi's aside, never prosecuted.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The press operates, and by the standard regional measure it has gained ground: Côte d'Ivoire rose to 54th of 180 in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index (score 66.27, up ten places from 2025), with a plural print and online landscape, opposition newspapers publishing, and the 2017 press law keeping most press offences civil and protecting sources (RSF Index 2026, via State Media Monitor country file).
  • Election days themselves were administered without the count being overturned: AU and ECOWAS observer missions reported the 25 October 2025 vote proceeded peacefully, the Constitutional Council recorded that no candidate lodged a complaint against the results, and the announced figures (89.77%) were confirmed on 4 November 2025 without alteration (AU/ECOWAS mission statements, October 2025; Constitutional Council, 4 November 2025).
  • A state institution documented against the government's own line in 2025: the National Human Rights Council (CNDH), a public body, published its own count of six deaths linked to the October 2025 protests between 11 and 21 October — a figure published while the government was presenting the crackdown as order-keeping (CNDH count, reported October 2025).
  • The central bank is not his to grip: the BCEAO is a regional (WAEMU) institution, and the IMF's June 2026 completion of the final EFF/ECF/RSF reviews — with the debt-distress assessment improved from moderate to low risk — records a macroeconomic framework run through regional and multilateral referees rather than presidential discretion (IMF, 24 June 2026).
  • The 2026 dissolution of the CEI was publicly justified as a response to the opposition's own long-standing criticism of the commission's independence — the African Court had ordered CEI reform as far back as 2016 — and the ratification of the dissolving ordinance was sent to the National Assembly, whose committee approved it on 17 November 2026 with a plenary vote scheduled for 10 December 2026 (Reuters, 6 May 2026; National Assembly committee reporting, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The referee of his eligibility is his appointee, twice over. The Constitutional Council — whose president is appointed by the President of the Republic — validated the reset reading of Article 55 that made his third term lawful (14 September 2020, rejecting 40 of 44 candidacy files, including Gbagbo's and Soro's), and under its current president, Chantal Nanaba Camara, appointed by Ouattara in 2023, validated five of sixty files in 2025 and confirmed his fourth term. Whatever the legal merits, the body that decides whether the president may run, and which of his rivals may run against him, sits by presidential appointment (Constitutional Council decisions, 2020 and 2025).
  • He publicly pronounced a court's sentence before the court did. In October 2020, with Soro's state-security trial still ahead, Ouattara told Jeune Afrique of his former prime minister: "For him it will be life in prison." In June 2021 the Abidjan court of assizes imposed exactly life imprisonment, dissolved Soro's movement, and confiscated the defendants' assets; an appeal court upheld the sentence in 2022. A president announcing the verdict in advance is the grip made audible, whether or not any judge took an instruction (Jeune Afrique interview, October 2020; court reporting, 23 June 2021).
  • When a court above his own ruled against him, he removed the court. On 22 April 2020 the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights ordered Côte d'Ivoire to suspend the arrest warrant against Soro; on 28 April 2020 an Abidjan court sentenced Soro in absentia anyway, and the same day the government, in cabinet, withdrew Côte d'Ivoire's declaration allowing individuals and NGOs to petition the African Court at all — denouncing its "serious and intolerable" actions. The court that had also ordered (2016) the reform of the CEI and (September 2020) the restoration of Gbagbo to the roll was thereby placed beyond his citizens' reach (African Court provisional measures, 22 April 2020; government communiqué, 28–29 April 2020; Opinio Juris and CGLJ analyses, May 2020).
  • The electoral commission's life and death both ran through the executive. The CEI's composition was contested by the opposition throughout his tenure and its 2025 refusal to revise the electoral roll — demanded by the opposition after Thiam's and Gbagbo's removals — was final. Then, on 6 May 2026, after the commission had delivered the 2025 results, the Council of Ministers dissolved it by ordinance (Ordinance 2026-265), with its replacement left to be designed by the government, no structure announced, and a liquidator appointed by interministerial decree in September 2026. SBM Intelligence reports the outgoing CEI chairman was redeployed to head the Council of State, the body that now adjudicates electoral disputes — reported here as SBM's account, contested by no official denial on the record assembled. Dissolving a referee after the match, and designing its successor inside the executive, is centralization even when the dissolved referee was genuinely distrusted (Reuters, 6 May 2026; SBM Intelligence, 2026; ordinance ratification reporting, November 2026).
  • Assembly itself was switched off at the decisive moment of 2025. On 2 October the National Security Council — chaired by the president — announced that gatherings protesting the Constitutional Council's decisions would be met with "all necessary measures"; a 10 October decree banned protests in Abidjan; and interministerial decree 0755 of 17 October 2025 banned meetings and public demonstrations by political parties nationwide for two months, exempting only the five validated candidates' own gatherings — which is to say, the two largest opposition parties were forbidden to assemble for the entire campaign and its aftermath (National Security Council statement, 2 October 2025; decrees of 10 and 17 October 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2025).
  • The prosecution's choice of chamber tells its own story: opposition figures arrested in the 2025 cycle were processed through the Anti-Terrorism Chamber of the Abidjan court — six PDCI officials were held for some nine months before release on 20 July 2026 — and protesters were sentenced in batches to three years for "disturbance of public order" within days of arrest, in some documented cases without legal representation (Amnesty International, March 2026; case reporting, July 2026).
  • A grave press-freedom file stands open against the state. RSF's investigation holds that in July 2025 the Beninese investigative journalist Hugues Comlan Sossoukpè — a political refugee in Togo since 2021 — was seized in Abidjan and handed to Benin on 10 July 2025 without legal process, and that a second journalist, Ali Moumouni, was arrested as his collaborator; RSF filed the case against Côte d'Ivoire and Benin at the ECOWAS Court of Justice in April 2026. This is recorded as an ALLEGATION supported by RSF's documented investigation; the ECOWAS Court has not ruled, and the Ivorian authorities' account is not on the record assembled here (RSF complaint and investigation, April 2026).

Why the grade lands here: Some referees genuinely stand outside his reach — the regional central bank, the IMF program, a press corps that improved its index ranking, a state rights council that published its own protest death count. But the referees who decide the one question that matters to the seat — who may run for it — are gripped at every joint: the validating council is his appointment, the electoral commission was dissolved by his cabinet with its successor undesigned, the continental court was exited the week it ruled for his rival, his own voice announced a defendant's life sentence before trial, and the right to assemble against all of it was banned for the campaign's duration. Test 2 grades the referees; the decisive ones do not hold independently. Helped: Alassane Ouattara and the RHDP (every eligibility question of 2020 and 2025 resolved in their favor; a successor electoral body to be designed by the incumbent government); the five validated 2025 candidates, who campaigned with assembly rights denied to the excluded parties. Hurt: Guillaume Soro (life sentence announced in advance by the head of state); Ivorian citizens generally, who lost direct access to the African Court in April 2020; the excluded parties' supporters, banned from meeting for two months of 2025; the six PDCI officials held nine months; journalists, if the Sossoukpè account is borne out — graded as allegation, not finding.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The 2020–2022 cycle contains a real, documented adjustment, made in the open. After an election season that killed 85 people on the government's own count, Ouattara met Bédié face to face on 11 November 2020; the opposition's National Transition Council stand-off ended without a parallel government; Pascal Affi N'Guessan, arrested on 7 November 2020 on charges including murder and terrorism (ALLEGATIONs, never tried), was provisionally released on 30 December 2020; a formal political dialogue ran through 2021; and the March 2021 legislative elections were held peacefully with full opposition participation, the RHDP taking about 58% of seats and the opposition entering parliament in force. He then received Gbagbo at the palace on 27 July 2021 — the two men's first meeting since the war — spoke of restoring trust, pardoned him in August 2022, and sat with both predecessors in July 2022 to discuss a shared withdrawal from politics (meeting and dialogue reporting, November 2020–July 2022; CRS country report, 2023).
  • The cocoa collapse of 2026 was answered with an open adjustment and a least-harm door, and the no-good-choice mechanics are recorded here in full. The options that actually existed when the world price fell through the guaranteed 2,800 FCFA/kg: (a) hold the price — exporters were already refusing or unable to pay it, beans were piling up unsold, and the CCC's forward-sale system faced insolvency, leaving farmers with paper prices and no buyers; (b) cut the price to the mechanism's level — the strict formula would have given 947 FCFA/kg, halving-plus farm incomes in a sector supporting some five million people; (c) cut partway and socialize the loss on stock already harvested at the promised price. He took (c): the mid-crop price was set at 1,200 FCFA/kg on 4 March 2026 — above the formula's 947 — and the state bought back 100,000 tonnes of unsold beans at the full 2,800, an operation completed in early August 2026, after more than 1.5 million tonnes had already been paid at the record price. The constraint is recorded, not counted against him; what remains gradable — the timing of the record price, announced by him personally on 1 October 2025, three weeks before the presidential election — is graded under Tests 4 and 5, not here (Conseil du Café-Cacao and agriculture-ministry announcements, October 2025–September 2026; sector reporting, 2026).
  • The 2026 CEI dissolution, whatever its Test 2 cost, was framed and partly functions as an answer to a decade of pushback about the commission — including an African Court order from 2016 and a fifteen-party opposition demand of September 2024 — and the replacement question was at least opened rather than denied (Reuters, 6 May 2026; SBM Intelligence analysis, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The adjustments come after the contested point is settled, never while it is live. In 2020 the sequence was: candidacy announced over mass protest (August), protests banned and machete attacks on demonstrators documented by Amnesty International with police standing by (August–October), the election held under boycott (31 October) — and only then, with the third term secured, the meeting, the dialogue, the releases. In 2025 the sequence repeated without the second half: the pushback (the 9 August 2025 Abidjan march by tens of thousands demanding roll revision and the reinstatement of excluded leaders — a march that was, to the record's credit, allowed to happen peacefully) was answered by the CEI's refusal ("impossible" — a revision would take seven months), the October protest bans, 1,658 arrests on the official count, and no dialogue before or after the vote (Amnesty International, 2020 and 2025–26; CEI statement, 2025; CIVICUS, 2025).
  • The other-option rule, applied: when another option became available, the record shows refusal to look, three times at the decisive joints. (i) In 2022 he himself floated the option of a joint retirement of the three old rivals; when Bédié died still in harness (1 August 2023) and Gbagbo did not withdraw, the option of retiring anyway — the thing he had said in 2020 he wanted, a transfer to a younger generation — was not taken up but inverted into a fourth-term candidacy. (ii) The amnesty option for Gbagbo was available from 2021 onward and was declined in favor of the pardon, without stated reasons (Test 1). (iii) The African Court's orders of April and September 2020 were options to de-escalate the Soro and Gbagbo files through a referee both sides had accepted in 2013; the answer was to exit the referee's jurisdiction within the week. Considering an option and declining it with stated reasons would not be against-evidence; in each case the record shows either silence on reasons or the removal of the forum (African Court orders, 2020; government communiqué, April 2020; Reuters reporting of the July 2022 meeting).
  • The street's pushback under his government has twice been met with the security answer first: 2020 (85 dead on the government's count, including at least 16 in the Dabou clashes of 19–21 October 2020 on HRW's documentation) and 2025 (11 dead on the National Security Council's count; the CNDH's separate count of six protest deaths between 11 and 21 October stands unreconciled beside it; hundreds sentenced to three-year terms within days). In neither cycle is there a documented public accounting by the president of the security forces' own conduct — the wobble is managed, not answered (government and CNDH counts, kept separate as the sources give them; HRW, 2020).
  • The 2023–2026 northern security wobble — jihadist spillover from Burkina Faso and Mali along the northern border — was answered with deployment and silence in roughly equal measure; it is recorded here as an open file rather than evidence either way, the public record of deliberation being thin.

Why the grade lands here: The FOR shelf is not nothing: the 2020–22 de-escalation was real, cost him something with his own hardliners, and produced the calmest legislative election of his tenure; the 2026 cocoa decision took the least-harm door inside a genuine trap and said so openly. But the pattern across two full cycles is that pushback is answered only once it can no longer change the outcome — dialogue after the term is secured, a commission dissolved after it has delivered — and while the question is live, the answer is bans, arrests, and exited jurisdictions. That is neither the open adjustment the test rewards nor pure denial; it is adjustment on a delay that always runs in his favor. MIXED. Helped: The country as a whole in 2021–22 (a peaceful legislative election, a functioning dialogue, Gbagbo home and pardoned); cocoa farmers holding unsold stock in 2026 (100,000 tonnes bought back at the full record price); political prisoners of the 2020 cycle released into the dialogue period, Affi N'Guessan among them. Hurt: Protesters of August–November 2020 and October 2025, whose pushback was met with force and prosecution before any hearing; the opposition electorate of 2025, whose central demand (a revised roll, their candidates restored) was refused without a forum; cocoa farmers as a class in 2026, whose price still fell 57% — recorded under the clause as the least-harm door, not as a demerit here.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's voters, the regions, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The economic accounting is broad, sustained, and independently verified. Growth averaged about 6.5% a year over the past decade and ran at 6.3% in 2025; the fiscal deficit was brought to the WAEMU ceiling of 3.0% of GDP in 2025; GDP per capita reached about USD 3,014 (2025). Poverty on the World Bank's lower-middle-income line fell from 41.7% (2021) to 36.0% (2025), and on the national line from 55.4% in 2011 to 37.5% in 2021 — a fall of nearly eighteen points across his tenure, slower than the growth rate but real and in the right direction (World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, 2026; IMF, June 2026).
  • The cocoa price — the single largest income lever for the rural poor — was set at levels that transferred world-market gains to farmers at scale: 1,800 FCFA/kg for the 2024/25 main crop, 2,200 for its mid-crop, and a record 2,800 from 1 October 2025, with more than 1.5 million tonnes in fact paid at the record price before the market turned. The producing regions that receive this income are not his political base alone; the cocoa belt spans the south and west, including the Gbagbo heartlands (Conseil du Café-Cacao price announcements, 2024–2025; sector reporting, 2026).
  • The reconciliation gestures counted the other side's leader as a citizen again: Gbagbo received at the palace (27 July 2021), his annuity paid, his accounts unfrozen, his sentence pardoned (2022); Simone Gbagbo amnestied (2018) and later validated as a candidate (2025); Blé Goudé permitted to return (November 2022). The December 2025 inauguration address promised a president "of all citizens, without exception," and congratulated the defeated candidates by name (inaugural address reporting, 8 December 2025).
  • Public investment was spread on the map — roads, bridges, power and water programs under successive National Development Plans, with the 2026–30 plan (USD 206 billion) targeting full national drinking-water and electricity coverage — and the state's salary, pension, and service obligations were maintained through the COVID and commodity shocks without the arrears crises that marked earlier decades (World Bank and IMF program documents, 2023–2026).

Evidence against:

  • At the decisive moment of political counting, the opposition's electorate was counted out before a single ballot. The candidates of the PPA-CI (Gbagbo), the PDCI (Thiam), and the GPS (Soro) — the three formations that between them represent the largest non-RHDP voting blocs — were all removed by the instruments detailed in Test 1. The official turnout of 50.10% means half the registered electorate did not vote in a five-candidate race from which their candidates were absent; in 2020 the same structure produced 94.27% on a boycott. A count that begins by striking the other side's names is not counting everyone, however clean the tally of the remainder — and the AU/ECOWAS finding that election day was peaceful answers a different question (CEI results, 2020 and 2025; Constitutional Council confirmations).
  • The dead of the two cycles fall on one side of the map, and the counts are kept separate here as the sources give them. 2020: the government spokesman announced 85 killed and 484 injured between August and November 2020 (34 before the vote, 20 on election day, 31 after) — the government's own count, which blamed the opposition's civil-disobedience campaign; Human Rights Watch documented more than 50 killed in the post-election violence it investigated, including at least 16 in Dabou. 2025: the National Security Council announced 11 deaths (including a gendarme), 71 injured, and 1,658 arrests; the CNDH separately recorded six deaths linked to protests between 11 and 21 October. The figures are not blended; what is common to both cycles is that the dead and the arrested were overwhelmingly drawn from the opposition's street (government statements, November 2020 and October 2025; HRW, 2020; CNDH, 2025).
  • The 2025 arrests swept wider than the protests. Amnesty International documented, five months on, detainees held solely for having been near a protest — including a street vendor in late pregnancy arrested on 11 October 2025 while selling juice near a demonstration and sentenced on 16 October to three years — and others convicted in trials without lawyers. People who were not even protesters were counted as offenders (Amnesty International, March 2026).
  • The growth's own accountants record its limits: informality at 82% of employment, formal employment at 25%, job quality lagging, and the World Bank's judgment that growth "has not been sufficiently inclusive," with poverty falling far more slowly than output. The cocoa cut of March 2026 (2,800 → 1,200 FCFA/kg, held for the 2026/27 main crop on 1 September 2026) halved the headline income of the producing countryside — graded under the no-good-choice clause in Test 3 as a trap not of his making in its market half, but recorded here in the ledger because the hurt is real and lands on the rural poor (World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, 2026; CCC announcements, 2026).

Why the grade lands here: Two different countings run through the tenure. The economic counting is genuinely national — growth, poverty reduction, infrastructure, and a farmgate price that paid the other side's regions the same as his own — and it is verified by referees he does not control. The political counting is the opposite: at both decisive elections, the opposing electorate's chosen names were removed before the count, half the register stayed home in 2025, and the cycles' dead and detained came from the side that was counted out. A ruler who counts everyone in the budget and counts out his opponents' voters at the ballot lands exactly in the middle of this test. MIXED. Helped: Cocoa producers nationwide (record prices actually paid on 1.5 million-plus tonnes); the poor as a class (poverty down on both measures across the tenure); Gbagbo and his household (annuity, accounts, freedom); public employees and pensioners paid without arrears crises; investors and the WAEMU framework (macro stability). Hurt: The PPA-CI, PDCI, and GPS electorates (their candidates struck before the vote in 2025; the GPS electorate's since 2020); the dead and injured of 2020 (85 and 484 on the government's count; HRW's separate documentation) and of 2025 (11 dead, 1,658 arrested on the official count; CNDH's separate six); bystander detainees of October 2025; cocoa farmers' incomes in 2026 under the price cut.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 2011, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The development program — the platform he actually campaigned on — was delivered at scale, three elections running. In 2015, 2020, and 2025 he ran on stability, growth, and infrastructure, and the record is the one graded in Test 4: a decade of ~6.5% average growth, poverty down on both measures, the deficit at the WAEMU ceiling, the IMF program completed through its final reviews in June 2026 with the debt-distress rating improved to low risk, and a USD 206 billion National Development Plan 2026–30 launched on schedule. Under the mandate rule, carrying out the elected platform is FOR-shelf evidence here, and his is substantial (IMF, June 2026; World Bank, 2026).
  • The reconciliation words were substantially performed. He said Gbagbo and Blé Goudé were free to return (April 2021) — they returned. He met Gbagbo (July 2021), pardoned him (August 2022), unfroze his accounts, and paid his annuity arrears. He congratulated his 2025 opponents by name at his inauguration and repeated the promise to be president of all Ivorians (2021–2025, as dated in Tests 1 and 4).
  • The cocoa word was kept while it cost money. The 2,800 FCFA/kg price he announced personally on 1 October 2025 was in fact paid on more than 1.5 million tonnes, and when the market collapsed the state bought back 100,000 tonnes of stranded stock at that same price rather than at the new one — a promise honored past the point where honoring it was cheap (CCC and sector reporting, 2025–2026).
  • The 2020 reversal was owned in public, at the time, in terms. He did not pretend the March statement had not been made; he named the circumstance (his successor's death), called the candidacy "a real sacrifice" and a response to "force majeure," and submitted the eligibility question to the Constitutional Council. Openness about a broken word does not unbreak it, but the record distinguishes it from denial (address of 6 August 2020).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The retirement word — broken twice, the second time without even a force majeure. Through his second term he repeatedly promised to retire in 2020. On 5 March 2020, before both houses of parliament, he made it formal and specific: "I have decided not to be a candidate in the presidential election of 31 October 2020," pledging to "transfer power to a younger generation"; the RHDP designated Prime Minister Amadou Gon Coulibaly a week later. Gon Coulibaly died on 8 July 2020. On 6 August 2020 Ouattara announced his own candidacy. The no-good-choice clause is applied here by its mechanics, because the moment was a real trap: the options that actually existed in July 2020 were (a) run himself — breaking the March word and igniting the term-limit fight; (b) elevate an unprepared substitute weeks before the vote, against a mobilized Bédié candidacy; (c) postpone the election, itself unconstitutional and crisis-laden. Each carried real harm, and the death that created the trap was not of his making. The clause therefore records the constraint and does not count the bare fact of running in 2020 against him. What remains gradable, and is graded, is the trap's origin and the sequel: the trap existed because succession had been concentrated in a single designated man with a known, serious cardiac history, with no bench built behind him across a decade in power; the legal door for the reversal existed because his own 2016 constitution had been written with a resettable count and he had kept that reading in reserve — in March 2020 itself he noted the constitution would permit him to run even as he renounced doing so; and the "sacrifice" was renewed in 2025, when no successor had died, no emergency obtained, and the only circumstance was that the joint-retirement understanding he had floated in 2022 had not been taken up by Gbagbo. A sacrifice renewed on schedule is a tenure (parliamentary address, 5 March 2020; RHDP designation, March 2020; address of 6 August 2020; candidacy address, 29 July 2025).
  • The 2025 justification named the break and overrode it. In his 29 July 2025 address he did not claim his earlier words had been misunderstood; he argued, in substance, that duty stood above them — the constitution "allows me to run for another term and my health permits it," amid "unprecedented security, economic, and monetary challenges," and the term would be one of "generational transmission." A ruler may believe that; the Test grades the word against the record, and the word — transfer to a younger generation, stated in 2020, repeated in the 2022 three-presidents setting, implied by his own designation of a successor in 2020 — was not kept (address of 29 July 2025; Reuters reporting, July 2022).
  • The succession word is now on the record and unperformed. At his 8 December 2025 inauguration he promised: "This term will be one of generational transmission; it will be, for our nation and me, an act of responsibility and political maturity." Eleven months into the term, no successor has been named, no transmission mechanism announced; the vice-presidency (Tiémoko Meyliet Koné, since 2022) and the premiership (Robert Beugré Mambé, since 2023) stand as offices, not as a designated handover, and the one succession he did designate, in 2020, ended with his own return. The promise is recorded here as an open file — it has four years to run and may yet be kept; it is graded as unperformed to date, not as broken (inaugural address, 8 December 2025; government composition reporting, 2026).
  • Smaller words on the same pattern: the electoral roll was not revised in 2025 although the electoral calendar provided for revision — the CEI's refusal rested on time, and the time existed because the exclusions that made revision urgent were litigated through the first half of the year; the new electoral body promised in May 2026 ("a new mechanism" to restore trust) remained undesigned at grading, six months on, with the dissolved commission in liquidation and its ratification bill still before the Assembly (CEI statement, 2025; Reuters, May 2026; Assembly reporting, November 2026).

Why the grade lands here: The two axes are graded independently, as the method requires, and they point in opposite directions — which is exactly why the crossings matter. On the program axis his word was kept at a scale few in this series match: what he told voters he would build, he largely built. On the tenure axis — the words about his own limits, which are the words this test exists for — the record is a formal parliamentary promise to leave (March 2020), a reversal excused by a death (August 2020, clause applied), and then a second renewal (2025) excused by nothing but duty and health, against rivals his own legal order had already removed. The crossings that occurred: kept his word and it helped — the development program, delivered; kept his word and it still hurt — his pledge to uphold the 2016 constitutional order as he read it, kept faithfully, whose keeping produced the reset eligibility and the framework within which rivals were excluded; broke his word and it hurt — the retirement promise, whose 2020 breach was followed by 85 deaths on the government's own count in the ensuing crisis, and whose 2025 renewal required the October bans and arrests to carry; broke his word and it helped — claimed by the RHDP (that the 2020 reversal prevented a succession vacuum and worse violence) and disputed by the opposition (which holds the reversal caused the crisis); the dossier records the claim and the dispute and does not settle it, the counterfactual being unprovable either way. A word about other people's money, kept; a word about his own departure, broken twice — the grade follows the second axis because the first is already credited in full on this shelf. Helped: Voters who backed the development platform in 2015, 2020, and 2025 (the program was delivered); cocoa farmers paid at the promised record price, including the 100,000-tonne buyback; Gbagbo, whose return and pardon were promised and performed. Hurt: The younger generation of Ivorian politics — including his own party's — to whom transfer was promised in 2020 and again as "transmission" in 2025, and who remain, at grading, without a named successor or a date; the opposition electorate, who heard the 2020 promise and its breach; the dead of the 2020 crisis that the breach ignited (85 on the government's count).

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: FAILS.

Alassane Ouattara is the strongest economic performer in this series to receive failing grades, and the dossier's two halves do not cancel — they describe one method of rule applied to two different objects. Where the object was the economy, the holding was open-handed: a general rulebook, referees he does not control (the BCEAO, the IMF, the WAEMU ceiling), a farmgate price paid to friend and foe regions alike, poverty down eighteen points on the national line across fifteen years, and a least-harm door taken inside the 2026 cocoa trap. Where the object was his own tenure, the holding closed: a constitution written with a resettable count, a validating council of his appointment, a continental court exited in the week it ruled for a rival, a life sentence for that rival announced in his own voice before the court spoke, every heavyweight challenger removed by conviction in absentia or by a nationality article dormant since 1961, and — when the lesser clemency would have left the bar standing — the lesser clemency chosen. The ledgers, test by test: One law (FAILS) — the law eliminated only rivals; helped: the RHDP and the amnestied; hurt: Gbagbo, Thiam, Soro, Blé Goudé, their electorates, and the unprosecuted dead of his own side's 2011 crimes. Without gripping (FAILS) — the eligibility referees are gripped at every joint, though the bank, the Fund, and the press stand outside the grip; helped: the incumbent; hurt: Soro, citizens' access to the African Court, the banned parties of October 2025. The wobble (MIXED) — real adjustment exists (the 2020–22 dialogue cycle, the cocoa buyback) but only ever after the contested point is settled in his favor; helped: the country in 2021–22, farmers holding stock in 2026; hurt: the protesters of both cycles, answered with force while their questions were live. Counts everyone (MIXED) — everyone in the budget, the opposition's voters out of the ballot; helped: producers, the poor as a class, the reconciled; hurt: the excluded electorates and the two cycles' dead (85 in 2020 on the government's count, HRW documenting more than 50 separately; 11 in 2025 on the official count, the CNDH's six recorded separately, 1,658 arrested). His word (FAILS) — the program word kept at scale; the departure word broken in 2020 under a genuine trap the clause records, then broken again in 2025 under no trap at all; the transmission word of December 2025 stands open and unperformed. The crossings stand as named in Test 5: kept-and-helped (the program), kept-and-hurt (the constitutional order as he reads it), broke-and-hurt (the retirement promise and the 2020 crisis), broke-and-helped claimed by his camp and disputed by the opposition, unsettled here. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the generational transmission promised on 8 December 2025 is performed — a named successor, a mechanism, a date — before 2030; whether the electoral body that replaces the CEI is designed with the opposition or merely for it; and whether the amnesty never granted to Gbagbo, or some equivalent restoration, ever converts the pardon from an instrument that freed the man and kept the bar into the reconciliation it was announced as.
