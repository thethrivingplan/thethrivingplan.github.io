The Ruler's Test — Daniel Ortega, Co-President of Nicaragua

Subject: Daniel Ortega Saavedra — Co-President of Nicaragua since 18 February 2025, serving alongside his wife, Rosario Murillo, who was elevated from vice president to co-president by the constitutional reform that entered into force that day. Ortega has held the presidency continuously since 10 January 2007; he previously led Nicaragua from 1979 to 1990, first as coordinator of the Sandinista junta and then as elected president (1985–1990), and lost the 1990 election to Violeta Barrios de Chamorro, handing over power peacefully. He returned to office by winning the November 2006 election with roughly 38% of the vote under rules — a 35% first-round threshold — created by his party's 2000 pact with former president Arnoldo Alemán. He is 81 years old in November 2026, appears in public infrequently, and in July 2026 declared that Nicaragua would hold no more elections. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The repressive statutes were written in general terms and have in fact been applied beyond the political opposition: the Foreign Agents Law (2020), the "Sovereignty Law" (Law 1055, December 2020), and the NGO-registration regime were enforced against Catholic charities, evangelical ministries (including the US-based Mountain Gateway church, whose Nicaraguan pastors were imprisoned in December 2023 on money-laundering charges the church denies), medical NGOs, and universities alike — the closure machinery did not spare religious or charitable organizations with no party alignment (Amnesty International, The State of the World's Human Rights: Nicaragua 2024; Reuters, 5 September 2024).
  • In the regime's later years the machinery also turned inward: the UN Group of Human Rights Experts on Nicaragua (GHREN) documented in September 2026 that targets of detention and enforced disappearance had expanded to include former state and military officials and former Sandinista figures — including the death in custody of Humberto Ortega, the president's own brother and former army chief, which the GHREN attributed to the government (GHREN report, Anatomy of State Cruelty, 21 September 2026; UNifeed, 28 October 2026).
  • Prisoner releases were at times extended through formal instruments rather than pure discretion: the June 2019 Amnesty Law freed several hundred detained protesters, and the February 2023 and September 2024 releases were executed through court orders (see Test 3).

Evidence against:

  • The law's sharpest instruments exist only for one side. Law 1055 (December 2020) defines "traitors to the homeland" in terms that were then used to prosecute opposition figures; no member of the governing circle has faced it. Between 2 June and 24 July 2021, seven presidential pre-candidates — Cristiana Chamorro, Arturo Cruz, Félix Maradiaga, Juan Sebastián Chamorro, Miguel Mora, Medardo Mairena, and Noel Vidaurre — were arrested and thereby prevented from standing in the November 2021 election, while Ortega's own candidacy proceeded (OHCHR, A/HRC/49/23, 24 February 2022). The Supreme Electoral Council (CSE), whose magistrates are aligned with the ruling party, dissolved three opposition parties in the same cycle, including Citizens for Liberty on 6 August 2021, hours after a rival party's complaint.
  • The February 2023 denationalizations were legislated retroactively around a single act of clemency: on 9 February 2023, 222 released prisoners were flown to Washington; the same day the National Assembly passed Law 1145, and a constitutional amendment followed, stripping them of nationality as "traitors." A week later 94 more people — most of them already in exile and none of them tried in the proceeding — were stripped of nationality and their property ordered confiscated (Congressional Research Service, Nicaragua: In Brief, R48294; OHCHR oral update, 9 December 2024). By December 2024 the UN Deputy High Commissioner put the total officially denationalized at at least 546; the GHREN's court-order count for February 2023–September 2024 is 452, and it believes the real number higher (OHCHR, December 2024; GHREN, September 2025).
  • The 2018 killings were never investigated against the state's own side. The government recognized 197 deaths as of 25 July 2018 (OHCHR report, August 2018); the IACHR documented at least 355 killed between April and September 2018 and the GIEI — the expert group the government itself admitted under the 2018 dialogue agreement — concluded that crimes against humanity had been committed. The government's position, stated at the National Dialogue on 23 May 2018 by Foreign Minister Denis Moncada and maintained since, is that the events were a failed, US-financed coup attempt, not social protest; on that premise, prosecutions ran against protesters (terrorism and organized-crime charges) and not against police or pro-government armed groups (IACHR, Gross Human Rights Violations in the Context of Social Protests in Nicaragua, June 2018; GIEI report, December 2018; OHCHR, August 2018).
  • Confiscation followed the same asymmetry: the property of the 222, of the 94, and of the 135 people expelled to Guatemala in September 2024 was seized by court order, while no comparable process has touched assets accumulated by the ruling family and its circle — whose administration of Venezuelan ALBA funds (an estimated US$3.6 billion disbursed by 2017, averaging roughly US$470 million a year in 2008–2014) was conducted off-budget through Albanisa, a private Venezuelan–Nicaraguan joint venture whose Nicaraguan side was run by the FSLN's treasurer, with no public accounting (Cruz, 2018, as compiled in subsequent analyses; Ortega government statements defending the arrangement as cooperation outside state channels).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, media, the church, universities, and civil society keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The honest for-shelf here is thin, and what exists belongs mostly to the first decade. From 2007 to roughly 2017, independent media operated, opposition parties held Assembly seats and municipalities, the private-sector umbrella COSEP negotiated policy with the government in a standing tripartite arrangement, and the 2011 and 2016 elections — however criticized — were held on schedule with opposition candidates on the ballot. Even after 2018, the formal shell of separate institutions was preserved until the 2025 reform rewrote it.
  • Within the current order, the September 2026 GHREN report noted that the co-presidents rule through institutions rather than around them: laws, court orders, and Assembly votes are the instruments used — the grip is exercised in legal form (GHREN, September 2026). That is a fact about method, recorded here because the test measures autonomy, and autonomy is what the record below addresses.

Evidence against:

  • The judiciary and electoral authority were captured in sequence, on the record: a contested 2009 Supreme Court ruling — issued by Sandinista magistrates sitting with substitutes after opposition magistrates declined to attend — voided the constitutional ban on consecutive re-election for Ortega specifically; the January 2014 constitutional reform then abolished term limits altogether and concentrated appointment powers; and by 2021 the CSE was annulling parties and the courts were sentencing the arrested pre-candidates in proceedings the OHCHR found arbitrary (OHCHR, A/HRC/49/23; BBC reporting on the 2014 reform). The GHREN's October 2026 summary states the endpoint plainly: under the 2025 constitution "the legislature and the judiciary are no longer even independent branches of government, but are organs coordinated by the two co-presidents" (UNifeed, 28 October 2026).
  • Civil society was abolished as an independent sphere. Between April 2018 and August 2024 the government cancelled the legal status of more than 5,000 NGOs; by late 2026 the count exceeded 5,500–5,600, close to 80% of those that existed — human-rights groups, medical charities, religious organizations, the Nicaraguan Red Cross (dissolved May 2023, accused of "attacks on peace and stability" during the 2018 protests), and the Missionaries of Charity (expelled 2022) among them (Congressional Research Service, R48294; Amnesty International, 2024; IPS/Global Issues, August 2026).
  • Universities were confiscated. Some 37 universities have been closed since 2021, culminating on 15–16 August 2023 in the seizure of the Jesuit-run Central American University (UCA) — founded 1960, a hub of the 2018 protests — after a court declared it a "center of terrorism," an accusation the university and the Jesuit order called unfounded; its buildings, accounts, and assets were taken and the Jesuits' legal status cancelled days later (Associated Press / Religion News Service, 17 August 2023; US House letter to Secretary Blinken, 13 October 2023).
  • Independent media were eliminated inside the country. Confidencial's newsroom was raided and occupied on 13 December 2018 and raided again on 20 May 2021; it operates from exile in Costa Rica. 100% Noticias was raided on 21 December 2018 and its director and news director imprisoned. La Prensa — the country's oldest daily — endured a 75-week customs blockade of its newsprint (2018–2020), printed its last paper edition on 12 August 2021, and had its headquarters raided by riot police the next day and later confiscated; its publisher was imprisoned and its staff exiled. Confidencial counts at least 61 media outlets closed or confiscated across the Ortega years and more than 280 journalists in exile (an opposition outlet's tally, labeled as such); Amnesty International's count is at least 276 journalists forced to flee between 2018 and 2024 (Confidencial, The War on Journalism in Nicaragua; Knight Center LatAm Journalism Review; Amnesty International, 2024).
  • The Catholic Church — in a country roughly half Catholic — was treated as an enemy institution: more than 1,000 religious processions banned during Lent and Easter seasons; Caritas closed; Catholic radio stations shut; priests arrested, expelled, or exiled in waves (18 clergy, including Bishop Rolando Álvarez, expelled to the Vatican in January 2024); the Vatican's embassy closed in 2023 after Managua proposed suspending relations. A researcher cited in the US State Department's 2023 International Religious Freedom Report documented 667 acts of aggression against the Catholic Church from April 2018 to August 2023 (Congressional Research Service, R48294; US State Department, 2023).
  • The February 2025 constitutional reform converted grip into text: it created the co-presidency, extended terms from five to six years, subordinated the other branches to presidential "coordination," and placed the police and army formally under the co-presidents. On 19 July 2026 Ortega declared Nicaragua would hold "never, never, never" more elections, and on 1 September 2026 the Assembly approved in first reading a further reform extending terms to seven years, postponing the next election to 2028, and barring anyone designated a "traitor" from office — second-reading ratification pending in January 2027 (CSIS analysis, 2026; Human Rights Watch statement, 21 September 2026; Global Centre for the Responsibility to Protect, country page, updated November 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest adjustment on the record is real and undisputed: having lost the February 1990 election, Ortega accepted the result and handed power to Violeta Chamorro, then spent sixteen years in opposition contesting elections he lost (1996, 2001) without attempting to seize power. The seat he now holds was regained at the ballot box in 2006.
  • In April 2018 he reversed the trigger policy within days: the social-security (INSS) reform that sparked the protests was annulled on 22 April 2018, five days after the protests began (2018 protests chronology; IACHR reporting).
  • He entered the National Dialogue in May 2018 with the Catholic bishops as mediators, admitted the IACHR and the GIEI, and signed the March 2019 negotiation agreement with the Civic Alliance committing to release political prisoners and reform the electoral system; the June 2019 Amnesty Law then freed several hundred detainees under ICRC supervision (National Dialogue record, 2018–2019; NSCAG statement, February 2023, a pro-government source, for the government's own count of 336 beneficiaries by end-2019).
  • Twice he traded prisoners for relations with Washington: the 9 February 2023 release of 222 prisoners to the United States (Secretary of State Blinken called it "a constructive step"), and the 5 September 2024 release of 135 to Guatemala after months of US negotiation (Reuters, 5 September 2024). Ortega's own account — that the releases were a sovereign Nicaraguan proposal, not a negotiation, and that those freed were convicted coup participants, not political prisoners — is the government's position and is printed here as such (Ortega statement via Tortilla con Sal, 14 February 2023).

Evidence against:

  • Every post-2018 adjustment was tactical, bounded, and followed by deeper closure. The INSS withdrawal came after nearly thirty people were already dead; the protests' actual demands (justice, electoral reform, his resignation) were answered with "Operation Cleanup" in June–July 2018, in which police and pro-government armed groups dismantled the roadblocks by force (IACHR, 2018). The dialogue collapsed; the March 2019 agreement's electoral-reform commitments were not implemented, a failure the government's own negotiating record does not dispute in substance (Orozco testimony, Tom Lantos Human Rights Commission, July 2021).
  • Pushback from monitors was answered with expulsion, not adjustment: the OHCHR team was expelled in August–September 2018 after its report (Ortega called the agency an "instrument of terror and death"); the GIEI and the IACHR's monitoring mechanism were expelled in December 2018, days before the GIEI's report was presented — it was released in Washington instead. Nicaragua then withdrew from the OAS (process completed November 2023), withdrew from the UN Human Rights Council and other multilateral bodies in 2025, refused all cooperation with the GHREN, and left the Central American Parliament in October 2026 (OHCHR, 2018; HRW statement, September 2026; Parlacen withdrawal reporting, October 2026). The GHREN's findings — reasonable grounds to believe crimes against humanity were committed as "a deliberate and well-orchestrated State policy carried out through defined chains of command from the presidency" — are rejected by the government as politically motivated and US-directed; that rejection is its stated position (GHREN, February 2025; government statements as reported).
  • The February 2023 and September 2024 releases freed the prisoners and simultaneously punished them further: exile, denationalization, and confiscation (Test 1) — an adjustment in form that extended the sanction in substance.
  • The terminal wobble answer came in 2026: facing the scheduled end of his term, Ortega did not adjust to the electoral calendar but abolished it — the July 2026 "no more elections" declaration and the September 2026 seven-year-term reform (Test 2). On the evidence, the pattern across two decades is consistent: concession where concession costs nothing structural (a decree withdrawn, prisoners traded), refusal wherever the pushback touches the hold on power itself.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the 2018 protesters and their families, the exiles, Indigenous and Afro-descendant communities on the Caribbean coast, and the church — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The material inclusion of the first decade is documented by sources independent of the government. World Bank and national survey figures (INIDE/EMNV, as compiled by the World Bank) show general poverty falling from 48.3% in 2005 to 24.9% in 2016, and extreme poverty from 17.5% to 6.9%, during average growth of about 5% a year in 2010–2017. Free public health care and education were restored by decree in January 2007; the Zero Hunger program distributed productive assets to rural families with women heads of household prioritized; electricity coverage rose from roughly 54% in 2006 to above 99% by the government's figures, broadly consistent with the expansion independent observers describe; and Nicaragua's road network is rated among the best in Central America (World Bank country data; Alliance for Global Justice compilation of official and multilateral figures; Nicaragua Solidarity Coalition — a pro-government source — for the 99.41% coverage figure, labeled as such).
  • The programs were formally universal: subsidies for electricity, water, and transport, the school-feeding and roofing programs, and the restored public services were not conditioned on party membership in their stated design, whatever the documented role of the party-run Councils of Citizen Power in administering local distribution (2007 program records; Baltodano critique, 2008, for the CPC administration point).

Evidence against:

  • The 2018 protesters were counted out in the most literal accounting there is: the dead. IACHR documented at least 355 killed April–September 2018; the government's recognized figure at the time was 197; the GIEI concluded the killings formed part of crimes against humanity; more than 2,000 were injured and over 2,000 arbitrarily detained across the period to February 2023 on the IACHR's count (IACHR, 2018 and 2023 annual report; OHCHR, 2018). Families of the dead — organized as the Mothers of April — have been surveilled, harassed, and barred from public commemoration; protests and marches have been effectively prohibited since September 2018, when Ortega declared political demonstrations illegal.
  • The exiles are counted out of the nation itself. More than 935,000 people — roughly one in eight Nicaraguans — have been driven into exile since 2018 (IPS/Global Issues, August 2026); UNHCR counted 356,201 Nicaraguan asylum seekers abroad at the end of 2024. At least 452 people were stripped of nationality by court order (GHREN, March 2026), hundreds more by the broader official count; 318 nationals were documented as denied entry to their own country between June 2018 and August 2025; and the GHREN's March 2026 report describes a transnational surveillance network — army, police, and migration authorities — monitoring and intimidating exiles, whose relatives inside Nicaragua face retaliation. In June 2025 a retired army major and prominent exiled critic, Roberto Samcam, was assassinated at his home in Costa Rica; the killing is documented, its authorship is not adjudicated, and it is recorded here as context for the exiles' security, not as a finding against the government (GHREN, March 2026; IPS, August 2026).
  • Indigenous and Afro-descendant communities on the Caribbean coast were left unprotected on their own titled land. Law 445 (2003) demarcated Indigenous territories covering about 31% of the country, but the final "clearing" stage was never completed, and armed settlers (colonos) invaded Miskito and Mayangna territories for logging, cattle, and mining: CEJUDHCAN documented some 40 Indigenous people killed, 47 injured, 44 kidnapped, and 4 disappeared in land-invasion cases from 2015 to early 2020; a January 2020 attack on the Mayangna community of Alal killed four; an August 2021 attack killed twelve (nine Miskito, three Mayangna); attacks and displacements in Alal and neighboring territories were still being reported in October–November 2026. The OHCHR urged prompt, independent investigation in February 2020; impunity is the documented norm, and the government's position — that it protects the communities and that the violence is a land conflict among private parties — is printed as its position (OHCHR press briefing, 7 February 2020; Associated Press, 25 August 2021; Artículo 66 via The Overlooked, November 2026). The Indigenous political movement was also decapitated: Brooklyn Rivera, leader of the YATAMA party, was detained in September 2023 and, per the GHREN's September 2026 report, died in custody, with responsibility attributed to the government; YATAMA's legal status had already been cancelled by the CSE ahead of the 2023 regional elections.
  • The church was counted as an enemy (Test 2), and political prisoners remained as the residue of every release: the Association for Memory and Justice for Nicaragua documented 72 political prisoners as of 23 October 2026, 33 of them in enforced disappearance; the GHREN verified at least 161 enforced disappearances since 2018 and at least eight deaths in custody of people held for political reasons (Association for Memory and Justice, October 2026; GHREN, September 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record, with dates — the 2006 "reconciliation" platform and the ALBA-era promises against the two decades since.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The social platform of 2006 was substantially delivered in the first decade. Ortega campaigned in 2006 on fighting poverty and hunger, restoring free health care and education, microcredit and support for small producers, and jobs. In office: school and clinic fees were abolished in January 2007; Zero Hunger and Zero Usury were launched; poverty fell by roughly half on World Bank-compiled figures between 2005 and 2016; electricity coverage roughly doubled; and the IMF program he negotiated in 2007 was completed (Test 4 sources; 2006–2007 program records).
  • The reassurances to business were kept for a decade. He promised in 2006 to respect private property, keep CAFTA, and maintain macroeconomic stability — and did so through the tripartite alliance with COSEP until that alliance collapsed in 2018, a continuity even his critics' accounts record (Upside Down World, January 2007; Orozco testimony, 2021).
  • The ALBA relationship was entered openly and operated as announced — Nicaragua joined ALBA as his first official act on 10 January 2007, and the Venezuelan cooperation (Petrocaribe terms: half the oil bill financed over 23 years at 2%) flowed for a decade on roughly the stated terms, funding the social programs above. That the funds were kept off-budget and unaccountable is graded under Test 1; that the partnership happened as promised is the fact for this shelf.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or reversed):

  • "National Reconciliation and National Unity" — the name of his government and the core promise of 2006 — is the record's largest broken word. The candidate who reconciled publicly with Cardinal Obando y Bravo, ran with a former Contra leader as his vice president, and promised a government of reconciliation now rules a country where the cardinal's church is persecuted, the opposition is imprisoned or exiled, and 452+ citizens have been declared traitors and stripped of their nationality (2006 campaign record; GHREN, 2026).
  • The constitutional word was rewritten each time it bound him. The constitution he swore to in 2007 barred consecutive re-election; a contested 2009 court ruling voided the ban for him; the 2014 reform abolished term limits; the 2025 reform made his wife co-president and lengthened terms; the September 2026 reform (first reading) lengthens them again and cancels the 2026 election. On 19 July 2026 he stated the endpoint himself: Nicaragua would hold "never, never, never" more elections (2014 and 2025 reform records; CSIS, 2026; HRW, September 2026).
  • The March 2019 negotiated commitments were not kept: the agreement signed with the Civic Alliance promised electoral reform and the release of all political prisoners; the releases partially occurred under the Amnesty Law, the electoral reform did not, and by 2021 the pre-candidates were in prison (agreement text, 29 March 2019; Orozco testimony, 2021).
  • Property rights, promised in 2006, ended in confiscation as policy: the assets of the denationalized — homes, pensions, businesses — were seized by court order from 2023 onward, and exiles' civil records have been erased, extinguishing pensions and legal identity; the former rector Ernesto Medina's testimony that his nationality, rights, and retirement pension were all taken is one documented instance among hundreds (Aula Abierta / UN interactive dialogue record, 2023; OHCHR, December 2024).
  • The succession signals complete the arc: the co-presidency formalizes Murillo's succession (she succeeds alone if he dies, under the 2025 text), and their son Laureano Ortega Murillo — an investment-promotion official with no elected office — acts as the regime's de facto foreign minister, signing the strategic agreements with China (including the July 2024 development-cooperation package) and Russia, and managing the family's business interests, as documented by CSIS and CRS. The 2021 switch of recognition from Taiwan to China and the free-trade agreement in force since 1 January 2024, and the deepened Russian security relationship (T-72 tanks 2016, GLONASS station and police-training center 2017, military cooperation pact signed September 2025, Russian advisers confirmed June 2026), were executed as state policy; whether they serve Nicaragua or the family's position is the contested question, and both characterizations are on the record (Congressional Research Service, R48294; CSIS, 2026).

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Daniel Ortega's file is two records in one tenure, and the tests catch both. The first decade delivered measurably on the material promises of 2006 — poverty halved on the World Bank's figures, electricity nearly universal, free clinics and schools restored — through an alliance with the private sector he had promised to respect, funded by a Venezuelan partnership he entered openly. The second record begins in April 2018 and has never stopped deepening: at least 355 protesters killed on the IACHR's documentation against a government that calls the episode a foreign coup attempt; every referee — courts, electoral council, universities, press, church, civil society — captured, closed, or expelled; 452 or more citizens stripped of their nationality; nearly a million people in exile; and a constitution rewritten five times in effect, ending, in his own words of July 2026, with no more elections at all. The UN's expert group finds reasonable grounds to believe crimes against humanity were committed as deliberate state policy from the presidency; the government rejects the finding as an imperial instrument; both positions are printed in this dossier at full strength, and the documented facts beneath them are not in serious dispute. The wobble and the word grade MIXED because the adjustments and the kept promises are real — he once handed over power he had lost, withdrew the decree that lit the fire in 2018, and kept his economic word for a decade. But the seat as it stands in November 2026 gives by favor and designation of traitors, grips every institution the test names, and counts out the protesters, the exiles, the coast's Indigenous peoples, and the church. The man who returned to power promising reconciliation has made himself, by law, irremovable — and has said so.
