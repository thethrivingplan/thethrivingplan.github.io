The Resume Builder

Never beg to work somewhere — and never lie to get in, either. A resume is a record of what you actually did, said in words the reader can find. Most big companies run every application through ATS first — applicant tracking software: the program that reads and ranks resumes before a person ever sees them. This builder writes your real record in the plain format that software can actually read, then checks it against a real job posting — honestly, without stuffing in a single word you didn't write.

The honest promise, up front

No builder can guarantee you a hire — anybody who promises that is selling something. What this one can do is make sure your real record gets read: by the software first, and by the person after it. 1 · Build your resume

Fill in what you've actually done. The preview below updates as you type, and your draft saves itself on this device as you go — see the note at the bottom of the form.

Your name & how to reach you

This goes at the top of the resume itself, in the body of the document — never in a page header or footer, because the screening software often can't read those.

Summary

One to three lines: what you do, how long you've done it, and the strongest true thing about your work. Not a wish — a record.

Work experience

One job at a time, most recent first. Under each job, write one thing you did per line — start with what you did, and add a number or a result wherever there's a true one: “Ran the register for 200+ customers a shift” beats “responsible for the register” every time. Duty-filler like “responsible for…” gets flagged under the box, gently.

Skills

The things you can actually do — tools, machines, programs, kinds of work. Separate them with commas or put one on each line. Only list what you could demonstrate or talk through in an interview.

Education

School, what you earned there, and the year. Still in school counts — say so plainly.

Certifications

Licenses and certificates you actually hold — one per line, with the year if you know it. These matter more than most people think: some application systems ask about required licenses as yes/no knockout questions (questions that end an application on the spot), so the real ones belong where they can be found.

3 · The honest match check

Paste a real job posting. The tool pulls the posting's own key phrases and shows you two lists: what's already in your resume, and what's in the posting but not in your resume. Then it asks the only honest question there is.

How this works — and what it will never do. Many screening systems match the posting's exact words and don't understand synonyms, so the honest move is to describe what you really did using the posting's own words for it — same work, their vocabulary. This tool will never insert a skill, title, or claim you didn't write. It won't stuff keywords either: stuffing (cramming a posting's words into a resume whether they're true or not) is now flagged by newer systems, and a claim you can't back up is a debt that comes due at the interview.

The tool reads the posting's repeated key phrases — titles, skills, tools, duties. It's a word-matcher, not a judge: a phrase can matter even if it appears once, and a repeated phrase can be boilerplate. You decide what counts.

    4 · Why it looks this plain

    The three layers, the way this page explains everything: what it is, what it means, and what it could mean for you.

    What it is

    A single-column document with standard headings — Summary, Work Experience, Skills, Education, Certifications — contact details in the body, no tables, no columns, no graphics, no text tucked into headers or footers.

    What it means

    Screening software reads a resume the way a form reads fields: top to bottom, one line at a time. Columns make it read across two jobs as if they were one. Tables scramble the order. Graphics and header/footer text often don't get read at all. Plain isn't a style choice — it's the shape the reader can parse.

    What it could mean for you

    Every job you ever held is already written down in you. In this format, all of it survives the trip — the software sees the same record the human sees, and nothing true about your work gets lost to a layout.

    One tap deeper — the plain-text version, and when to use each output

    Copy plain text is the safest output of the three: pasted into an application form or an email, it arrives exactly as written, with no hidden formatting. Print / Save as PDF prints only the resume — none of this page around it — for postings that ask for a file or for handing to a person. Download .txt gives you the same plain text as a file you can keep, send, or paste later. If a posting asks for a specific file type, follow the posting — the words matter more than the wrapper.

    5 · The record on screening software

    What is actually known about the software your resume meets first — every claim labeled with how solid it is, at the size the claim deserves.

    About 98% of Fortune 500 companies route applications through screening software before a human reads themVerified — as reported

    That's from Jobscan's Fortune 500 report (2025), which checked the application systems of the largest US companies directly. One honest note: Jobscan sells resume tools for this exact software, so it has an interest in the topic — but the underlying count is a checkable, company-by-company audit, and it's the best public count there is. The takeaway stands either way: at any large company, software is your first reader.

    “75% of resumes are auto-rejected by the software” — that famous claim is NOT VERIFIEDNot verified

    You will see this number everywhere, usually from someone selling a fix for it. It traces back to Preptel, a resume company that shut down in 2013 and never published any method behind the figure — no study, no sample, no data anyone can check. It has been repeated for over a decade because it scares people into buying things. Treat it the way you'd treat any claim with no record behind it: it isn't evidence, and it isn't a reason to panic — or to stuff a resume with words that aren't yours.

    Most systems rank and sort — they don't auto-reject your contentEstimate — small study

    A 2025 study of US recruiters by Enhancv (n=25 — a small sample, so hold it as a strong hint, not a census) found that most screening systems rank and sort applications for a human rather than throwing them out over wording. The rejections that really are automatic are mostly knockout questions: are you authorized to work here, do you hold the required license, can you work the required schedule. Those are yes/no gates, answered by you — so answer them honestly and exactly, and make sure any license they ask about also appears in your Certifications, spelled the way the posting spells it.

    Formatting is the silent killerEstimate — vendor-reported

    In vendor testing of resume-parsing software, a plain single-column document was read at about 93% accuracy, against about 86% for a two-column layout — and tables badly scramble what the software reads, mixing lines from different jobs together. These figures come from the companies that sell the software (interested parties, labeled as such), but every independent test points the same direction: the plainer the document, the more of you survives the read. That's why this builder makes exactly one layout.

    The software matches words, not meaningsVerified — as reported

    Many systems search for the posting's exact words and do not understand that “ran the register” and “cash handling” are the same work. So the honest move is not invention — it's translation: describe what you really did using the posting's own words for it, where those words are true. Same work, their vocabulary. The match check above exists to find exactly those spots.

    One resume per posting beats one resume for everywhereVerified — as reported

    Because the match is against that posting's words, a resume tailored to one posting — your real record, arranged and worded for that job — outperforms the same generic resume blasted at fifty postings. Tailoring is not lying: it's choosing which true things to put first and what to call them. Your draft saves on this device, so tailoring a copy for the next posting starts from your record, not from a blank page.
