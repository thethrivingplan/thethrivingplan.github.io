Lantern Road — Tower Defense

Walkers are coming down the lantern road to your gate. You build towers on the grass beside the road; the towers fire on their own; every walker stopped pays gold, and every walker that reaches the gate costs lives. Spend the gold on more towers and upgrades, and hold the road for 20 waves.

An original game in a documented form: tower defense traces to Rampart (1990) and grew up through the custom-map years and the desktop wave of the 2000s. The full lineage is at the bottom of this page. Everything here — name, towers, walkers, words — is our own.

The walkers

Mite — the common walker. 30 health, steady pace, costs 1 life.
Runner — 18 health but nearly twice as fast. Costs 1 life.
Brute — 120 health, slow, costs 2 lives.
Shellback — 430 health, very slow, costs 3 lives.
Every wave, every walker's health grows by 30% of its base over the wave before. That growth — not any single wave — is what ends most games.

Tap to open — how it works, in plain words

The road. Walkers enter at the lantern post and follow the dirt road to your gate. They never leave it, never fight back, and never speed up — except Runners, who are simply born quick.

Towers. Each tower watches a circle around itself (its range) and fires at the walker in that circle that has travelled furthest. Damage is per hit; rate is hits per second. Splash towers hurt every walker near the one they hit. A Frost Shard slows whatever it touches by 45% for a moment. A Coin Grove never fires — it pays gold while waves run, and it is how an economy-first game is played.

Upgrades. Every tower has three levels. Level 2 costs 80% of the build price and multiplies damage by 1.7; level 3 costs 130% and multiplies damage by 2.7. Range grows a little each level. Selling a tower returns 70% of everything you spent on it.

The economy. Stopped walkers pay bounties (Mite 4, Runner 5, Brute 9, Shellback 18). Finishing a wave pays a bonus of 12 gold plus the wave number. Gold unspent earns nothing — the only bank on this road is a Coin Grove.

Losing and winning. Each walker through the gate costs lives. At zero lives the game ends. Survive wave 20 and you win — unless Endless is on, in which case the road simply keeps coming.

Gestures. Tap does the thing. Hold any main control (or hover over it with a pointer) and it explains itself before it acts. On the board: tap to build and select; with a keyboard, use the arrow keys to move the marker and Enter to build or select.

Three honest ways to hold the road

There is no single right defence. These are the three plans experienced players argue about; each one wins games the others lose, and the Coach will name which one your board is drifting toward as you play.

1. Choke-point — pile onto the bends

Put your strongest towers where the road turns. A bend gives one tower two stretches of road to watch, so each shot is bought once and spent twice. Frost at a bend is worth two Frosts on a straight.

The trade: the straights between bends are thinly watched. Fast Runners can slip a gap your bends never see, and one crowded corner is one bad wave from being overrun.

2. Spread coverage — a tower for every stretch

Space towers so their ranges tile the whole road with no gaps. Nothing travels unwatched; Runners get no free road anywhere.

The trade: no spot is strong. Brutes and Shellbacks walk through a thin spread the way rain walks through a fence — everywhere at once, stopped nowhere. Spread defences usually need upgrading everywhere, which the gold rarely allows.

3. Economy-first — plant Coin Groves early

Buy one or two Coin Groves in the first waves, defend thin, and let the groves pay for a late wall of upgraded Mortars and Lanterns. A grove pays for itself in roughly eight waves and profits after.

The trade: the early waves are defended with pocket change. One leak-heavy wave at wave 4 can cost lives you never get back, and a grove built after wave 12 rarely earns its price before the game ends.

Honest numbers — measured, not asserted

Difficulty claims are easy to print and hard to mean, so these were measured. We ran a headless simulation of this game's exact shipped numbers — same tower costs, damage, ranges, walker health, wave lists, and gold — playing two fixed reference defences, and recorded where each game ended. The simulation is deterministic: the same defence on the same settings ends on the same wave every run.

The full reference defence builds sixteen towers in a fixed bend-first order and upgrades whenever spare gold allows, up to level 3. The light reference defence builds eight towers and upgrades only to level 2 — closer to a first-time player's board. Both build only between waves; you may also build mid-wave, so treat these as a floor for careful play, not a ceiling. One difference to know: in the simulation, shots land the instant they are fired; on this page, shots take a fraction of a second to arrive. Read the numbers as close readings, not prophecies.

Tap to open — lineage, and what is original here

The documented form. Tower defense — building stationary defences along a fixed route that waves of attackers must run — is a documented game form with a traceable line. Rampart (Atari, 1990) put build-and-defend phases around waves of attackers a generation before the name settled. The form grew up in the custom-map years: player-made maps for StarCraft and Warcraft III in the late 1990s and early 2000s turned "tower defense" into a named genre with its own conventions — a path, waves, buildable towers, an economy of kills. The desktop and browser wave of the 2000s, titles like Desktop Tower Defense (2007), carried it to everyone. This game is built on that documented form, the way a new chess variant is built on chess.

What is original. The name Lantern Road, both maps, all five towers (Dart Turret, Ember Lantern, Frost Shard, Hill Mortar, Coin Grove), all four walkers (Mite, Runner, Brute, Shellback), the artwork drawn on the board, and every word of text are original to this page. No other game's name, art, map, tower, enemy, or text is used. Where a mechanic is the genre's common property — a path, waves, ranges, bounties — it is used as the form itself, which belongs to everyone who plays it.

Why? → How? → Act. Why: the road works because a walker under fire longer is a walker stopped sooner — so bends, range, and time are the whole game. How: the three strategies above are three ways of buying that time. Act: build, watch what leaks, and let your own record — not anyone's say-so — grade the plan.
