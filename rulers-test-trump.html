The Ruler's Test — Donald J. Trump, President of the United States

Graded: November 24, 2026 · Scope: current term and prior record, on the documented public record as of November 2026.

How this grading works: Each of the five tests is graded only on the evidence listed under it — documented public record: court rulings, official records and data, reputable news reporting, and the subject's own statements, each cited by outlet and date. Both shelves are presented for every test: the strongest evidence that he passes, and the strongest evidence that he fails. Unproven charges are labeled ALLEGATION and are never stated as verdicts. Where the evidence itself is contested, the contest is named. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — Gives by one law

Does he apply the same rules and law to allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders, with no favorites at the source?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for

  • The prosecution of former National Security Adviser John Bolton — a prominent Trump critic — rested on an investigation that began before Trump returned to office and had career-prosecutor backing; Bolton pleaded guilty on June 26, 2026 to one count of unlawful retention of national-defense information, agreeing to a $2.25 million fine and forfeiture of his federal pension (Reuters, June 26, 2026; DOJ statement, June 26, 2026; sentencing per the court docket was moved to December 4, 2026 and was still pending at this grading).
  • In December 2025 he pardoned Rep. Henry Cuellar, a Democrat — clemency extended across party lines (noted with the context that Cuellar had been a critic of the prior administration) (reporting summarized in WUGA/NPR clemency coverage, Nov.–Dec. 2025).
  • His signature tariffs were imposed broadly — on allies and adversaries alike, not aimed only at opponents' countries (CNN, Feb. 20, 2026, describing "sweeping tariffs across the globe").

Evidence against

  • Former FBI Director James Comey was indicted Sept. 25, 2025 and New York Attorney General Letitia James on Oct. 9, 2025, by interim U.S. Attorney Lindsey Halligan, who was installed after career prosecutor Erik Siebert was pushed out amid pressure to bring charges (CNN, Nov. 24–25, 2025; AP, Nov. 25, 2025).
  • On Nov. 24, 2025, Judge Cameron McGowan Currie dismissed both indictments, ruling Halligan had been unlawfully appointed in violation of the Appointments Clause (Congress.gov, CRS Legal Sidebar LSB11380; CNN, Nov. 24, 2025).
  • When the Justice Department tried to re-indict James, grand juries in Norfolk (Dec. 4, 2025) and Alexandria (Dec. 11, 2025) both declined to indict (Wikipedia, "Prosecution of Letitia James," compiling case record; Fox News, Dec. 2025).
  • Comey was re-indicted on Apr. 28, 2026 in the Eastern District of North Carolina on two counts of threatening the president over a May 2025 Instagram photo of seashells arranged "8647" — ALLEGATION, unproven; his motions to dismiss for vindictive/selective prosecution and on First Amendment grounds were pending, with arraignment moved to January 2027 (case-status roundup, Oct. 25, 2026).
  • Clemency in the second term totals more than 1,600 pardons and commutations, concentrated on supporters, allies, and the connected: roughly 1,500 January 6 defendants pardoned on Jan. 20, 2025, including full pardon for Enrique Tarrio and commutations for seditious-conspiracy convictions (Jan. 20, 2025 proclamation; WUGA/NPR, Nov. 10, 2025).
  • George Santos's seven-year sentence was commuted on Oct. 17, 2025 after he had served under three months, wiping out his restitution obligation (NPR/Politico, Oct. 2025).
  • Changpeng Zhao of Binance was pardoned Oct. 21, 2025; Binance had business ties to the Trump family's World Liberty Financial venture, and Trump told 60 Minutes he did not know who Zhao is (Reuters, Oct. 2025).
  • In November 2025 he issued preemptive pardons to 2020-election figures including Rudy Giuliani, Mark Meadows, Sidney Powell, and John Eastman — none of whom faced federal charges at the time (Nov. 2025 pardon proclamations, widely reported).
  • In December 2025 he pardoned former Honduran president Juan Orlando Hernández, who was serving a 45-year federal sentence (Dec. 2025, widely reported).
  • As of Oct. 25, 2026, federal probes touching other named critics — John Brennan, Adam Schiff, Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell, Sen. Mark Kelly, Rep. Elissa Slotkin, and Rep. Eric Swalwell — remained unresolved, neither charged nor publicly cleared (case-status roundup, Oct. 25, 2026).

Test 2 — Holds without gripping

Do the members of his system — states, agencies, courts, allies, his own party — keep their own autonomy and spin, or does he punish independence and demand personal loyalty?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for

  • After the Supreme Court ruled against him in Trump v. Cook (June 29, 2026), the ruling stood: Fed Governor Lisa Cook remained in office under the process the Court required, rather than being removed by defiance (TechTimes, Oct. 1, 2026; Take Care Ledger, updated Nov. 22, 2026).
  • His appointee Kevin Warsh became Federal Reserve chair in May 2026, and the Warsh Fed raised interest rates on Sept. 16, 2026 — against Trump's publicly stated preference — with no removal following as of this grading (Take Care Ledger, updated Nov. 22, 2026).
  • His mid-decade redistricting push, pressed on Republican-led states starting with Texas, met resistance and largely failed in the 2026 results — the states and the process did not simply bend (NPR, Nov. 4, 2026).

Evidence against

  • On Jan. 24, 2025, at least 17 inspectors general were fired late at night without the statutory 30-day notice to Congress; on Sept. 24, 2025, Judge Ana C. Reyes ruled the firings violated the Inspector General Act, though she declined to order reinstatement (NPR, Jan. 25, 2025; Wikipedia, "2025 dismissals of U.S. inspectors general," compiling the ruling). Sen. Chuck Grassley (R) publicly objected to the missing notice.
  • Trump pressed Fed Chair Jerome Powell for rate cuts from April 2025 and spoke of removing him; on Aug. 25, 2025 he announced the removal of Governor Lisa Cook over a mortgage-fraud ALLEGATION referred by FHFA Director Bill Pulte — Cook was never charged in the record gathered (Take Care Ledger, updated Nov. 22, 2026).
  • On Jan. 9, 2026, the Justice Department served grand-jury subpoenas on the Federal Reserve threatening Powell with indictment over headquarters-renovation testimony; Powell's Jan. 11, 2026 statement called the move a pretext for pressure over interest rates; Judge James Boasberg quashed the subpoenas, finding their purpose was harassment and pressure; DOJ closed the Powell matter in April 2026; and the Fed's Inspector General reported in September 2026 that the renovation project was mismanaged but there were no grounds to believe a crime occurred (Take Care Ledger, updated Nov. 22, 2026; CAFE Insider).
  • In Trump v. Cook (June 29, 2026), the Supreme Court ruled 5–4 that the Federal Reserve Act's "for cause" protection is valid and a president must give notice and a hearing before removing a Fed governor; the same day, in Trump v. Slaughter, the Court overruled Humphrey's Executor for other agencies, expanding presidential removal power (TechTimes, Oct. 1, 2026; court opinions, June 29, 2026).
  • Sen. Thom Tillis announced his retirement on June 29, 2025, one day after Trump threatened a primary challenge over Tillis's vote against advancing the tax bill (AP, June 29, 2025). Trump allies launched a super PAC to defeat Rep. Thomas Massie (AP, June 2025).
  • Attorney General Pam Bondi was fired Apr. 2, 2026; reporting cited Trump's frustration that the Justice Department "isn't doing enough to target his political opponents," alongside the Epstein-files handling; Todd Blanche, Trump's former personal defense lawyer, became acting attorney general (ABC News, reported via NY Post, Apr. 2026; NBC News, Apr. 2, 2026).
  • DHS Secretary Kristi Noem was fired in March 2026 and replaced by Sen. Markwayne Mullin (NBC News, Apr. 2, 2026).

Test 3 — Answers the wobble

When the people and institutions that steady him — courts, voters, allies, his own officials, the evidence itself — push back, does he adjust openly, or deny, attack, and claim he cannot be moved?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for

  • On Apr. 9, 2025, after a severe Treasury selloff (the 10-year yield rose from 3.87% to 4.47% in three days), Trump paused the "reciprocal" tariffs for 90 days and said on the record: "The bond market is very tricky, I was watching it… people were getting a little queasy" (TheStreet, Apr. 2025; ABC7/AP, Apr. 10, 2025).
  • After the Supreme Court struck down his emergency tariffs (Feb. 20, 2026), he reimposed tariffs under a different statute — Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, a 10% global tariff effective Feb. 24, 2026 — answering through another legal channel rather than defying the ruling (JD Supra legal bulletin, Feb. 2026).
  • After months of resisting and calling the push a Democratic distraction, he signed the Epstein Files Transparency Act on Nov. 19, 2025 (the House had passed it 427–1) (AP, Nov. 20, 2025; signing statement, govinfo DCPD-202501133).
  • On election night, Nov. 3, 2026, he told the New York Times he was "absolutely willing" to accept the midterm results, told CBS "congratulations to the Democrats," and said he would work with them (USA Today, Nov. 4, 2026; NPR, Nov. 2026).

Evidence against

  • On Mar. 18, 2025, Trump demanded Judge James Boasberg's impeachment ("should be IMPEACHED!!!") after deportation-flight rulings; Chief Justice John Roberts issued a rare public rebuke the same day, stating that "impeachment is not an appropriate response to disagreement concerning a judicial decision" (Bloomberg Law, Mar. 18, 2025; AP, Mar. 18, 2025).
  • Deportation flights to El Salvador continued on Mar. 15, 2025 despite Judge Boasberg's verbal order to turn them around; the judge convened a hearing on possible defiance of his order (AP, Mar. 2025).
  • In the case of Kilmar Abrego Garcia, the administration admitted in court that his March 2025 removal to El Salvador violated a 2019 withholding order; the Supreme Court ruled Apr. 10, 2025 (with no noted dissent) that the government must "facilitate" his return; Trump and President Bukele publicly claimed powerlessness to return him; he was returned June 6, 2025 — to face a Tennessee human-smuggling indictment (Britannica case summary; Bloomberg Law, June 2025).
  • On May 22, 2026, Judge Waverly Crenshaw dismissed those charges, finding the prosecution presumptively vindictive — "The objective evidence here shows that, absent Abrego's successful lawsuit challenging his removal to El Salvador, the Government would not have brought this prosecution" — and calling the evidence an "abuse of prosecuting power"; the Justice Department said it would appeal (WSJ, May 22, 2026; AP, May 22, 2026; CNN, May 22, 2026).
  • After the Supreme Court's Feb. 20, 2026 tariff ruling, Trump said he was "ashamed of certain members of the court, absolutely ashamed for not having the courage to do what's right for our country" — including two of his own appointees, who had joined the majority (CNN, Feb. 20, 2026).
  • Days after accepting the midterm results on election night, he posted on ~Nov. 9, 2026: "Does anybody really believe that this 'Election' was honest," questioning the results he had just accepted (CNN, Nov. 14, 2026).

Test 4 — Counts everyone

Does his accounting include the whole flock — opponents, minorities, the poor, foreigners, people who share their streets — or are some groups counted out of the family?

GRADE: FAILS

Note on the split in this record: the strongest passing evidence is concentrated in his first term; the strongest failing evidence is the governing record of the current term.

Evidence for

  • The First Step Act, signed December 2018 with bipartisan support (87–12 in the Senate): the Justice Department's June 2024 annual report counted 44,671 people released under its provisions, with a 9.7% recidivism rate against the Bureau of Prisons' ~45% baseline (DOJ/Bureau of Prisons First Step Act Annual Report, June 2024; Congressional Record, Dec. 16, 2025).
  • His December 2025 pardon of Rep. Henry Cuellar extended clemency to a member of the opposing party (WUGA/NPR clemency coverage, Nov.–Dec. 2025).

Evidence against

  • Executive Order 14160 (Jan. 20, 2025) sought to deny birthright citizenship to U.S.-born children of unlawfully or temporarily present parents. On June 30, 2026, the Supreme Court held the order unconstitutional in Trump v. Barbara, ruling that the Citizenship Clause guarantees citizenship to nearly all born on U.S. soil (Supreme Court slip opinion No. 25-365, supremecourt.gov, June 30, 2026).
  • His mass-deportation program's own totals are contested, and the contest is part of the record: DHS claimed 605,000–622,000 "deportations" in year one plus roughly 1.9 million "self-deportations," while independent tallies of ICE-effectuated removals run about 290,000–335,000, and a New York Times analysis put the combined first-year total near 500,000 (Factually.co synthesis of DHS/TRAC/ICE data, Jan. 2026). By all accounts, interior arrests and detention rose sharply — roughly 66,000 people detained by December 2025.

Test 5 — Keeps his word over time

Promises made vs. kept, positions held vs. reversed, across years.

GRADE: MIXED

Reference point: PolitiFact's promise tracker, as of Oct. 26, 2026, scores 75 second-term promises: 15 Kept, 3 Compromise, 5 Broken, 21 Stalled, 31 In the Works.

Evidence for (kept)

  • Border enforcement: FY2026 Border Patrol apprehensions totaled 98,708 — the lowest in history, roughly 95% below the Biden-era level (CBP/DHS preliminary figures, Oct. 16, 2026).
  • He promised pardons for January 6 defendants and issued them on his first day, Jan. 20, 2025 (2025 proclamation).
  • He promised sweeping tariffs and imposed them — the promise was kept as an act, whatever its legal fate (see below) (2025 executive orders).
  • "No tax on tips" was enacted in capped, deduction form in the July 4, 2025 tax law (widely reported, July 2025).

Evidence against (broken or reversed)

  • "End the Ukraine war in 24 hours": PolitiFact rates this Promise Broken; Trump later said he had been "being a little bit sarcastic." As of Nov. 9–11, 2026 the war continued, with U.S.-brokered trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi reporting "substantial disagreements" remaining (PolitiFact; CNN, Nov. 9, 2026).
  • Prices falling "starting on Day One": rated Stalled by PolitiFact; grocery prices were still up about 1.8% year over year in September 2026, and AAA's national average gas price was $4.2046 on Oct. 30, 2026, against $3.0377 a year earlier (AAA figures, Oct. 2026).
  • After stating in February and May 2025 that Medicaid would not be touched, he signed the July 4, 2025 law containing more than $900 billion in Medicaid reductions (2025 statements; July 4, 2025 law).
  • "Mexico will pay for the wall" (2016): the 2025 law instead provided $46.5 billion in U.S. taxpayer funding (July 2025 law).
  • His 2016 pledge to eliminate the national debt in eight years: the national debt passed $40 trillion in August 2026 (Treasury figures, Aug. 2026).
  • The "$2,000 tariff dividend" checks floated in November 2025: none had been sent as of late 2026 (no disbursement on record).
  • He campaigned as the peace candidate against new wars; on Feb. 28, 2026 he ordered Operation Epic Fury — joint U.S.–Israeli strikes on Iran in which Supreme Leader Khamenei was killed and Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz; a ceasefire followed on Apr. 7, 2026 and a memorandum on June 17, 2026, but U.S. strikes resumed July 7, 2026 with further strikes in September 2026; U.S. service-member deaths were reported during the conflict (a figure of 18 by September 2026 appeared in one news report and is not independently confirmed here) (Wikipedia timeline of the 2026 Iran war, compiling dated reporting; War Powers notification letter, July 10, 2026).
  • The legal basis of his signature tariffs was struck down: on Feb. 20, 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump that the IEEPA emergency tariffs were unlawful; about $134 billion had been collected as of a December 14 CBP filing, with refunds left to the lower courts (CNN, Feb. 20, 2026; Weil, Gotshal & Manges LLP client alert, Feb. 20, 2026).
  • The New York Fed found that U.S. importers paid 86–94% of the 2025 tariff costs, contradicting his January 2026 Wall Street Journal op-ed claim that the costs fell "overwhelmingly on foreign producers" (Federal Reserve Bank of New York study, reported 2026).

Overall

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word over time: MIXED.

The pattern the evidence draws is a ruler who answers some wobbles honestly — pausing tariffs when the bond market turned, signing the Epstein-files law, accepting an election on the night — and who kept several large promises outright, the border and the day-one pardons first among them. But on the tests that measure the source itself, the record runs the other way: prosecutions of named opponents that courts dismissed or grand juries refused, clemency concentrated on allies and supporters, a pressure campaign against the Federal Reserve that a judge found was harassment, watchdogs fired in violation of the statute, and a citizenship order the Supreme Court held unconstitutional. Where his word was tested against time, it split — kept on the border, tariffs, and pardons; broken on 24-hour peace, day-one prices, Medicaid, and the debt. These grades stand on the evidence listed above, and they change if the evidence does.
