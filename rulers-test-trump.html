The Ruler's Test — Donald J. Trump, President of the United States (RE-RUN under the Nov 24, 2026 method)

Subject: Donald J. Trump — President of the United States, second term from 20 January 2025. The seat graded is the presidency, where executive power sits. This dossier is a re-run of the original Trump grading (24 November 2026: FAILS / FAILS / MIXED / FAILS / MIXED) under the method addendum of 24 November 2026. The original dossier is the evidence base; every item in it that still stands is carried forward, and documented developments through late November 2026 are added where they land. Nothing was dropped for convenience. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The mandate rule, applied throughout: Governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one. The Test grades the holding, never the platform. Carrying out what he ran on — border enforcement, mass deportation, tariffs, January 6 pardons — is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Marks are lost only for HOW: a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4).

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution of former National Security Adviser John Bolton — a prominent Trump critic — rested on an investigation that began before Trump returned to office and had career-prosecutor backing; Bolton pleaded guilty on June 26, 2026 to one count of unlawful retention of national-defense information, agreeing to a $2.25 million fine and forfeiture of his federal pension (Reuters, June 26, 2026; DOJ statement, June 26, 2026; sentencing per the court docket was moved to December 4, 2026 and was still pending at this grading).
  • In December 2025 he pardoned Rep. Henry Cuellar, a Democrat — clemency extended across party lines (noted with the context that Cuellar had been a critic of the prior administration) (reporting summarized in WUGA/NPR clemency coverage, Nov.–Dec. 2025).
  • His signature tariffs were imposed broadly — on allies and adversaries alike, not aimed only at opponents' countries (CNN, Feb. 20, 2026, describing "sweeping tariffs across the globe").
  • On Nov. 19, 2026 he posthumously pardoned Pete Rose, the baseball hits leader convicted in 1990 of filing false tax returns — a clemency act for a figure with no political role in his coalition, extended after MLB had already restored Rose's Hall of Fame eligibility in May 2025 (USA Today, Nov. 19, 2026; CNN, Nov. 19, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On Nov. 24, 2025, Judge Cameron McGowan Currie dismissed both indictments, ruling Halligan had been unlawfully appointed in violation of the Appointments Clause (Congress.gov, CRS Legal Sidebar LSB11380; CNN, Nov. 24, 2025).
  • When the Justice Department tried to re-indict James, grand juries in Norfolk (Dec. 4, 2025) and Alexandria (Dec. 11, 2025) both declined to indict (Wikipedia, "Prosecution of Letitia James," compiling case record; Fox News, Dec. 2025).
  • Comey was re-indicted on Apr. 28, 2026 in the Eastern District of North Carolina on two counts of threatening the president over a May 2025 Instagram photo of seashells arranged "8647" — ALLEGATION, unproven; his motions to dismiss for vindictive/selective prosecution and on First Amendment grounds were pending, with arraignment moved to January 2027 (case-status roundup, Oct. 25, 2026).

Why this grade lands: The mandate rule was applied first: none of the platform — not the tariffs, not the deportation program, not the pardons he promised on the trail — is graded against him here. What remains is the HOW, and the HOW is a documented two-track record. Prosecutions of named opponents were brought by an unlawfully appointed prosecutor installed after a career prosecutor was pushed out, were dismissed by a judge, and were refused twice by grand juries when the department tried again. Clemency, the one legal instrument that is entirely his, flowed in the thousands to supporters, allies, donors' circles, and the connected, with cross-party exceptions (Cuellar, Rose) real but rare against the total. A law that reaches opponents through irregular process and lifts allies by signature is not one law. FAILS stands, on the same evidence as the original grading, with the platform expressly removed from the scale.

Helped: January 6 defendants and their families (roughly 1,500 pardons and commutations); named allies and supporters granted clemency, including Enrique Tarrio, George Santos, Changpeng Zhao, Rudy Giuliani, Mark Meadows, Sidney Powell, John Eastman, and Juan Orlando Hernández; Rep. Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • After the Supreme Court ruled against him in Trump v. Cook (June 29, 2026), the ruling stood: Fed Governor Lisa Cook remained in office under the process the Court required, rather than being removed by defiance (TechTimes, Oct. 1, 2026; Take Care Ledger, updated Nov. 22, 2026).
  • His appointee Kevin Warsh became Federal Reserve chair in May 2026, and the Warsh Fed raised interest rates on Sept. 16, 2026 — against Trump's publicly stated preference — and held in October, with no removal following as of this grading (Take Care Ledger, updated Nov. 22, 2026). On Nov. 20, 2026 Trump again publicly demanded rate cuts but stated his criticism was not aimed at Warsh, and Cook herself publicly argued on Nov. 20 that rate hikes were urgently needed — a sitting governor he tried to remove, speaking against his stated policy in her own voice, unpunished (BigGo Finance summary of Nov. 20, 2026 remarks; CME market data, Nov. 2026).
  • When the administration moved a second time against Cook in late 2026, it used the channel the Court had required — a formal "for cause" process with notice and a hearing, set for Dec. 14, 2026 — rather than a second summary removal (Take Care Ledger, updated Nov. 22, 2026; CNBC via Take Care Ledger). Cook declined to attend the White House hearing process, her counsel calling it stacked (New York Times reporting, Nov. 16–17, 2026) — the characterization is hers, recorded as such.

  • The courts also ruled for him through ordinary process, and those rulings stood the same way: on Nov. 23, 2026 a Second Circuit panel held that the district court had erred in blocking removal of his New York hush-money case to federal court and ordered a prompt ruling on transfer — a procedural win inside the system, not over it (AP via PBS NewsHour, Nov. 16–23, 2026; Second Circuit order, Nov. 23, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On Jan. 24, 2025, at least 17 inspectors general were fired late at night without the statutory 30-day notice to Congress; on Sept. 24, 2025, Judge Ana C.   • On Jan. 9, 2026, the Justice Department served grand-jury subpoenas on the Federal Reserve threatening Powell with indictment over headquarters-renovation testimony; Powell's Jan. 11, 2026 statement called the move a pretext for pressure over interest rates; Judge James Boasberg quashed the subpoenas, finding their purpose was harassment and pressure; DOJ closed the Powell matter in April 2026; and the Fed's Inspector General reported in September 2026 that the renovation project was mismanaged but there were no grounds to believe a crime occurred (Take Care Ledger, updated Nov. 22, 2026; CAFE Insider).

Why this grade lands: The new evidence genuinely strengthens the FOR shelf — the Cook ruling was obeyed, his own Fed chair raised rates against his stated preference and kept his job, and the second Cook attempt was routed through the Court's process instead of around it. But the grade turns on the pattern, and the pattern is documented at every referee the Test names: watchdogs fired in violation of the statute (a court's finding, not an accusation), a Fed chair threatened with indictment through subpoenas a judge quashed as harassment, an attorney general fired amid reported frustration that she was not targeting his opponents enough, and sitting legislators of his own party threatened with primaries for single votes. Obeying a ruling after losing it is the floor of this test, not the pass. FAILS stands.

Helped: Kevin Warsh, confirmed and left in place while voting against the president's stated preference; the Federal Reserve as an institution, whose "for cause" protection the Supreme Court affirmed in Trump v. Cook; Lisa Cook, still in her seat at grading. Hurt: The 17 inspectors general fired without statutory notice; Jerome Powell, subjected to grand-jury subpoenas a judge found were harassment and pressure; Pam Bondi and Kristi Noem, removed from cabinet office; Thom Tillis, retired under a primary threat; Thomas Massie, targeted by an allied super PAC; the independence of the Justice Department as documented in the Bondi firing's reported rationale.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades the other option: when another option became available, did he at least consider it openly? Considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not evidence against; refusing to look at it is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • On Apr. 9, 2025, after a severe Treasury selloff (the 10-year yield rose from 3.87% to 4.47% in three days), Trump paused the "reciprocal" tariffs for 90 days and said on the record: "The bond market is very tricky, I was watching it… people were getting a little queasy" (TheStreet, Apr. 2025; ABC7/AP, Apr. 10, 2025). No-good-choice clause, applied: by April 9 the options actually open were to hold the announced schedule into a Treasury spiral — raising the government's own borrowing costs and importers' costs together — or to pause a signature policy days after announcing it and absorb the political cost. Each carried real harm; the pause was the least-harm door actually open, taken openly with the reason stated. It is graded here as the best possible choice available in the moment and is not held against him on any test; the tariff policy itself is graded on Tests 1, 4, and 5, where it belongs.
  • After the Supreme Court struck down his emergency tariffs (Feb. 20, 2026), he reimposed tariffs under a different statute — Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, a 10% global tariff effective Feb. 24, 2026 — answering through another legal channel rather than defying the ruling (JD Supra legal bulletin, Feb. 2026). This is the other-option rule in its plainest form: another option became available, he looked at it, and took it.
  • After months of resisting and calling the push a Democratic distraction, he signed the Epstein Files Transparency Act on Nov. 19, 2025 (the House had passed it 427–1) (AP, Nov. 20, 2025; signing statement, govinfo DCPD-202501133).
  • On election night, Nov. 3, 2026, he told the New York Times he was "absolutely willing" to accept the midterm results, told CBS "congratulations to the Democrats," and said he would work with them (USA Today, Nov. 4, 2026; NPR, Nov. 2026). The results were a heavy loss — Democrats' victory was described across coverage as overwhelming, and it materially checked his program, including his reach at the Federal Reserve (Hindu BusinessLine, Nov. 13, 2026; WSJ, Nov. 2026).
  • On the Cook file, after the Supreme Court required notice and a hearing, the administration's second attempt used that process (Dec. 14, 2026 hearing) rather than repeating the summary removal — an adjustment in method, recorded here as it was on Test 2 (Take Care Ledger, updated Nov. 22, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On Mar. 18, 2025, Trump demanded Judge James Boasberg's impeachment ("should be IMPEACHED!!!") after deportation-flight rulings; Chief Justice John Roberts issued a rare public rebuke the same day, stating that "impeachment is not an appropriate response to disagreement concerning a judicial decision" (Bloomberg Law, Mar. 18, 2025; AP, Mar. 18, 2025).
  • Deportation flights to El Salvador continued on Mar. 15, 2025 despite Judge Boasberg's verbal order to turn them around; the judge convened a hearing on possible defiance of his order (AP, Mar. 2025).
  • In the case of Kilmar Abrego Garcia, the administration admitted in court that his March 2025 removal to El Salvador violated a 2019 withholding order; the Supreme Court ruled Apr. 10, 2025 (with no noted dissent) that the government must "facilitate" his return; Trump and President Bukele publicly claimed powerlessness to return him; he was returned June 6, 2025 — to face a Tennessee human-smuggling indictment (Britannica case summary; Bloomberg Law, June 2025).
  • On May 22, 2026, Judge Waverly Crenshaw dismissed those charges, finding the prosecution presumptively vindictive — "The objective evidence here shows that, absent Abrego's successful lawsuit challenging his removal to El Salvador, the Government would not have brought this prosecution" — and calling the evidence an "abuse of prosecuting power"; the Justice Department said it would appeal (WSJ, May 22, 2026; AP, May 22, 2026; CNN, May 22, 2026).
  • After the Supreme Court's Feb. 20, 2026 tariff ruling, Trump said he was "ashamed of certain members of the court, absolutely ashamed for not having the courage to do what's right for our country" — including two of his own appointees, who had joined the majority (CNN, Feb. 20, 2026).
  • Days after accepting the midterm results on election night, he posted on ~Nov. 9, 2026: "Does anybody really believe that this 'Election' was honest," questioning the results he had just accepted (CNN, Nov. 14, 2026). Under the other-option rule this is the refusal pattern in miniature: the option of standing by his own stated acceptance was available, stated reasons for reversing it were not given, and the acceptance was withdrawn by insinuation rather than reconsidered openly.

Why this grade lands: The record genuinely splits, and the new parameters sharpen rather than move the split. Where events pushed — the bond market, a lost tariff ruling, a 427–1 House vote, a lost midterm — he adjusted in the open more than once, and the April 2025 pause is expressly protected by the no-good-choice clause. Where courts pushed on the deportation program's edges, the answers were an impeachment demand against the judge, flights that continued against a verbal order, and a wrongful removal defended as powerlessness until the man was returned into a prosecution a judge later dismissed as vindictive. Adjustment on economics, defiance on the courts' core orders, and an election acceptance retracted within the week: MIXED stands.

Helped: Importers, markets, and borrowers, by the April 2025 tariff pause and the lawful Section 122 channel; Epstein-files survivors and the public record, by the signed Transparency Act; voters in the 2026 midterms, whose result was accepted on the night it was rendered. Hurt: Kilmar Abrego Garcia, removed in violation of a court order and prosecuted on charges a judge dismissed as presumptively vindictive; Judge James Boasberg, targeted with an impeachment demand for a ruling; the two Supreme Court appointees publicly shamed for joining a majority against him; public confidence in the 2026 count, undercut by the president's own post-acceptance questioning of it.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does his accounting include the whole flock — opponents, minorities, the poor, foreigners, people who share their streets — or are some groups counted out of the family?

GRADE: FAILS

Note on the split in this record: the strongest passing evidence is concentrated in his first term; the strongest failing evidence is the governing record of the current term.

Mandate note, stated before the shelves: the mass-deportation program is the platform he was elected on. Under the mandate rule, enforcing immigration law against people unlawfully present is not, by itself, a Test 4 failure, and it is not graded as one below. This grade turns on who the record shows counted out beyond that platform: citizens by birth, people under courts' protection, and the accuracy of the accounting itself.

Evidence for:

  • The First Step Act, signed December 2018 with bipartisan support (87–12 in the Senate): the Justice Department's June 2024 annual report counted 44,671 people released under its provisions, with a 9.7% recidivism rate against the Bureau of Prisons' ~45% baseline (DOJ/Bureau of Prisons First Step Act Annual Report, June 2024; Congressional Record, Dec. 16, 2025).
  • His December 2025 pardon of Rep. Henry Cuellar extended clemency to a member of the opposing party (WUGA/NPR clemency coverage, Nov.–Dec. 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Executive Order 14160 (Jan. 20, 2025) sought to deny birthright citizenship to U.S.-born children of unlawfully or temporarily present parents. On June 30, 2026, the Supreme Court held the order unconstitutional in Trump v. Barbara, ruling that the Citizenship Clause guarantees citizenship to nearly all born on U.S. soil (Supreme Court slip opinion No. 25-365, supremecourt.gov, June 30, 2026). This is the counting-out the mandate rule does not protect: the platform was deportation of the unlawfully present; the order reached past it to redefine, by executive signature, which U.S.-born children count as citizens at all.
  • The enforcement program's own totals are contested, and the contest is part of the record: DHS claimed 605,000–622,000 "deportations" in year one plus roughly 1.9 million "self-deportations," while independent tallies of ICE-effectuated removals ran about 290,000–335,000, and a New York Times analysis put the combined first-year total near 500,000 (Factually.co synthesis of DHS/TRAC/ICE data, Jan. 2026). For fiscal 2026, DHS announced on Nov. 12, 2026 a record 483,200 ICE deportations and more than 491,000 arrests — figures outside analysts noted were released without the breakdowns (including criminal-history splits) that would let the count be checked (DHS release via CBS News, Nov. 12, 2026; Hindu BusinessLine, Nov. 13, 2026). An accounting of people that cannot be verified counts the counters, not the counted.
  • The removal machinery reached people the courts had counted in: Kilmar Abrego Garcia was removed in admitted violation of a 2019 withholding order (full record on Test 3) — a man a court had expressly placed under protection, counted out by error the administration then defended for months (Britannica case summary; Bloomberg Law, June 2025).
  • Interior arrests and detention rose sharply through the program — roughly 66,000 people detained by December 2025, with detention capacity funded at $45 billion in the fiscal 2025 reconciliation law (Congressional Research Service summary of the FY2025 reconciliation law, 2025; figures as carried in the original grading, Jan. 2026).

Why this grade lands: The mandate rule was applied in full, and it removes the largest single item from the against shelf: deporting people unlawfully present, at record scale, is what he told the voters he would do, and the Test does not grade the platform. What survives that removal still fails the test. An executive order attempting to un-count U.S.-born children was held unconstitutional by the Supreme Court; a man under a court's withholding order was removed anyway and his return resisted; and the program's published numbers remain contested and, in their fiscal-2026 form, unverifiable in detail. Counting everyone means the count itself has to be honest and the protected stay protected. FAILS stands — narrowed by the mandate rule to the HOW, and failing on the HOW.

Helped: People released under the First Step Act (44,671 by the June 2024 report) and their families; Rep. Henry Cuellar; communities and voters who supported the elected enforcement platform, whose preference the program carried out (recorded here because the ledger names help where the record shows it, without grading the platform itself). Hurt: U.S.-born children of unlawfully or temporarily present parents, whose citizenship the order sought to deny until the Supreme Court held it unconstitutional; Kilmar Abrego Garcia, removed under a court's protection; the roughly 483,200 people deported in fiscal 2026 and the mixed-status families separated by the program's interior enforcement (the program's lawfulness as a platform is not the grade; the separations are the ledger); people in detention as capacity expanded to roughly 66,000 by December 2025.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME
