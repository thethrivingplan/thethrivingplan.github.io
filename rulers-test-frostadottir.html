The Ruler's Test — Kristrún Frostadóttir, Prime Minister of Iceland

Subject: Kristrún Mjöll Frostadóttir — Prime Minister of Iceland since 21 December 2024, graded on an ongoing tenure of just under two years. The premise in the original brief is confirmed: she still holds the seat in November 2026, and no government change, coalition collapse, or early election has occurred. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Iceland's president, Halla Tómasdóttir (in office since 1 August 2024), is a largely ceremonial head of state, while executive power — the government, the budget, and the administration — sits with the prime minister. Frostadóttir, born 12 May 1988, an economist who worked at Morgan Stanley and as chief economist of the Icelandic Chamber of Commerce, was elected to the Althing in 2021 and became leader of the Social Democratic Alliance (Samfylkingin) in 2022, unopposed. She led her party to first place in the snap election of 30 November 2024 — called after her predecessor Bjarni Benediktsson's left-right coalition collapsed in October 2024 — with 20.8% of the vote and 15 of 63 seats, and formed a three-party coalition with Viðreisn (Liberal Reform Party, 11 seats) and the People's Party (Flokkur fólksins, 10 seats): 36 of 63 seats, all three party leaders women, dubbed the "Valkyrie government" (Government of Iceland, 21 December 2024; contemporaneous reporting, December 2024). She is Iceland's youngest-ever prime minister, taking office at 36. The coalition is intact at grading, but thinner: in autumn 2026 three MPs left governing parliamentary groups — Guðmundur Ingi Kristinsson (People's Party) and Jón Gnarr (Viðreisn) sit as independents who still support the government, while Ingvar Þóroddsson (Viðreisn) crossed to the opposition Independence Party on 16–17 November 2026 — leaving the coalition parties with 33 seats against a majority line of 32 (Daily Northern / Morgunblaðið reporting, November 2026). The next parliamentary election is due by November 2028. Her predecessors appear only as context: Bjarni Benediktsson (prime minister April–December 2024) and, before him, Katrín Jakobsdóttir (2017–2024), who resigned to run, unsuccessfully, for the presidency. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic printed on the Test card governs this dossier: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one — carrying out what the coalition ran on (the fishing fee, the EU referendum, welfare reform) is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how it was done — a second law for her own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect her (Test 4).

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Her signature revenue measure fell on the country's most protected insiders, not on outsiders. The fishing-fee law of July 2025 corrected the calculation base for the fee quota-holders pay for access to a common resource: the government documented that the fish prices used in the old base had been underestimated, so the fee had not reflected actual catch value. The corrected fee roughly doubles what fishing fees bring the Treasury — some ISK 18–20 billion — while, on the government's figures, fishing companies keep 67% of profit and pay 33% to the owner of the stocks, the nation (Political Data Yearbook 2026, Cambridge; Reykjavík Grapevine, June–July 2025). The fee applies by formula to every registered vessel owner; no exemption for allied companies appears in the documented record. A Gallup poll in late July 2025 found 65% of voters supported the bill and 24% opposed it (Political Data Yearbook 2026).
  • The wider revenue search was aimed, on her own stated account, at concentrated sectors rather than at households alone: pressed in parliament in September 2026, Frostadóttir said the government was "seeking additional revenue from banks, the tourism sector and natural resources," including the fishing fee (Daily Northern / mbl.is, 14 September 2026). The cruise-ship infrastructure fee (ISK 2,500 per passenger per port day, in force 1 January 2025 under Act No. 140/2024) applies to every cruise operator by the same rule (Cruise Industry News, November 2025; Tourism and Hospitality, 2026).
  • The welfare law she delivered was universal in form: the new disability system of 1 September 2025 replaced three overlapping allowances with a single disability pension under one set of rules, and social-security pensions were tied to the wage index for all recipients — a change Frostadóttir defended on the ground that disability pensioners, who cannot strike, are thereby given "a place at the bargaining table" (Ísland.is, Social Insurance Administration, 2025; Daily Northern, November 2026).
  • The outcome measure has not deteriorated on her watch: Iceland scored 77 of 100 in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025 (tied 10th of 180), unchanged from 77 in 2024 and up from 72 in 2023 (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The tax burden's incidence is contested, and the contest comes from inside her own coalition's promises. In an unscheduled Althing session on 14 September 2026, Independence Party MP Guðlaugur Þór Þórðarson accused her of breaking a pre-election pledge not to raise taxes on ordinary working people, putting total tax increases at ISK 60 billion over two years and citing income-tax changes, child-benefit and interest-benefit changes, and fixed-amount charges (Daily Northern / mbl.is, 14 September 2026). The ISK 60 billion figure is an opposition figure, recorded as such, not an audited total. Frostadóttir did not dispute that revenue had risen; she answered that the increases fell on banks, tourism, and resources and that some charges (a kilometre charge) had been inherited from the previous government's plans at a higher level than her government proposed (same reporting). Two months later the incidence argument helped break her majority's discipline: Viðreisn MP Ingvar Þóroddsson, defecting to the Independence Party on 16 November 2026, stated that the government's tax increases ran counter to the promises Viðreisn had made before the election and that business voters who had backed the party felt let down (Daily Northern / Morgunblaðið, 17 November 2026). A revenue system whose own coalition partner calls it a betrayal of the terms on which its voters signed up is, at minimum, not experienced as one law by all who live under it.
  • The fishing fee's burden is geographically uneven in a way the formula does not answer. The opposition argued throughout the 2025 debate that the corrected fee would harm competitiveness and "seriously harm the fishing industry in small fishing communities in the countryside" (Political Data Yearbook 2026). The industry body Fisheries Iceland (SFS) claimed the law would "severely damage" the sector — an interested party's claim, recorded as a claim (Reykjavík Grapevine, June 2025). The law passed unchanged (Test 3), so the small-community incidence was neither exempted nor phased on the documented record; whether it materializes is an outcome question the record assembled here cannot yet settle.
  • The cruise fee shows the same pattern in miniature: Cruise Iceland data submitted to parliament's Economic Affairs and Trade Committee recorded advance cruise bookings through 2027 falling by over 50% in some ports, with rural port communities bearing the loss, and the fee described as substantially higher than comparable charges in neighbouring countries (Cruise Industry News / Iceland Monitor, November 2025). The government did amend the fee downward in 2025 with effect from 2026 (Tourism and Hospitality, 2026) — an adjustment credited in Test 3 — but the original rate was set, in January 2025, at a level its rural incidence had not priced in.

Why: One law, in this tenure, is mostly a story of formulas applied to everyone — the fishing fee, the cruise fee, the unified disability pension — and the strongest FOR evidence is that the heaviest single measure fell on quota-holders, the classic Icelandic insiders, with majority public support. It lands at MIXED, not HOLDS, because the tax file as a whole is genuinely contested on incidence: the opposition's ISK 60 billion figure, the defection of one of her own coalition's MPs over the tax rises, and the rural concentration of the fishing and cruise fees together mean the record cannot certify that the burden was felt as one law by allies, opponents, capital, and countryside alike. Helped: the Treasury and, through it, every user of the services the new revenue funds; wage-earners and pensioners whose benefits now move with the wage index; the public as owner of the fish stocks, whose share rose toward the government's stated 33%. Hurt: fishing companies and, on the opposition's and industry's documented claims, small fishing communities whose margins the doubled fee compresses; rural cruise ports, on Cruise Iceland's booking figures; taxpayers who, on the opposition's account, paid more despite a no-rise pledge — an account her own former coalition MP repeated as he left.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • The central bank has moved directly against her government's central promise, in public, and she has let it. The Central Bank of Iceland cut its key rate through 2025 as inflation fell (8.5% in December 2024 to 7.3% a year later, on Central Bank figures cited in the Political Data Yearbook 2026), then reversed in 2026, raising the rate three times by a cumulative 75 basis points to 8% (S&P Global Ratings, 7 September 2026). On 18 November 2026 its Monetary Policy Committee held the rate at 8% unanimously and published a Monetary Bulletin forecasting a Treasury deficit of about ISK 16.5 billion for 2027 — flatly contradicting her government's budget bill, which assumes a surplus of ISK 5 billion (RÚV reporting via Iceland This Morning, 19 November 2026). The government answered with argument, not sanction: the foreign minister told parliament "we will not waver from a balanced budget," and Frostadóttir said the bank's forecast rested on outdated assumptions and the gap would be closed without new revenue (Daily Northern, 18–20 November 2026). No governor was threatened, no statute changed, no appointment retaliated against on the documented record. Governor Ásgeir Jónsson gives interviews explaining his own reasoning, including criticism of the construction outlook (RÚV, 18 November 2026).
  • The courts ruled, the market shook, and the government worked around the ruling rather than at the court. On 14 October 2025 the Supreme Court held Íslandsbanki's discretionary variable-rate mortgage terms partly unlawful; banks suspended loan products and the Central Bank's Financial Stability Committee responded with its own borrower-based measures (Central Bank statement, 31 October 2025). The government's answer was to build, with the Central Bank, a transparent published benchmark for indexed loans — accepting the court's premise that discretionary pricing was the problem (Government housing package, November 2025). A later Supreme Court ruling (10 December 2025, Arion Bank) upheld indexed terms as lawful; both rulings stand as delivered (Arion Bank statements, October–December 2025).
  • The electoral referee ran a vote her side lost, and the loss stood. The 29 August 2026 EU referendum was administered by the National Electoral Commission under an OSCE/ODIHR needs-assessment mission (ODIHR NAM Report, July 2026); turnout was about 82.5%, the No side won 52.8% to 47.2%, and the government accepted the count the same weekend (RÚV / National Electoral Commission results, August 2026; Reuters, 30 August 2026). The 2024 election that seated her was likewise run independently: her predecessor's coalition collapsed, a snap election was held on schedule, and power transferred in three weeks.
  • Parliament checks her government, including from inside the referees' own committees. In November 2026 the Althing's Constitutional and Supervisory Committee voted unanimously — government and opposition members together — to open an inquiry into Finance Minister Daði Már Kristófersson's communications with the state-owned power company Landsvirkjun and whether he misled parliament over the departure of its chief executive (Daily Northern / mbl.is, 5 November 2026). The inquiry proceeds against her own finance minister with her coalition's votes; no move to block or staff it appears in the record.
  • The media referee's numbers improved on her watch: Iceland rose five places to 12th of 180 in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index, scoring 82.77 (RSF, 2026). Freedom House scored Iceland 95 of 100 in Freedom in the World 2025. Critical outlets — the Reykjavík Grapevine, Heimildin, DV — cover her government's defections, tax disputes, and the Landsvirkjun affair at length and unimpeded.

Evidence against:

  • The one hard grip in the record is parliamentary, and it is real. In July 2025, to end the opposition's 160-hour filibuster of the fishing-fee bill — the longest debate in Althing history — the majority invoked the Article 71 cloture clause of the Parliament Act, a provision unused for decades. The opposition charged that Speaker Þórunn Sveinbjarnardóttir had acted "as Speaker of the majority, not Speaker of the whole Alþingi," and warned the move would poison the term; the majority answered that it had used "the democratic option" against an absolute minority veto (Political Data Yearbook 2026; We Are Aquaculture / RÚV, July 2025). The clause is lawful and the bill it freed had 65% public support; but a speakership perceived by the whole opposition as the majority's instrument is a referee whose neutrality is, on that episode, contested — and the contest is recorded here with both disputants named.
  • The Landsvirkjun file is an unresolved ALLEGATION of ministerial grip on a state company, and it is graded as allegation, not finding. Former chief executive Hörður Arnarson, who left in autumn 2026 after 17 years, had written to the board on 15 June 2026 criticising increased contact between the Finance Ministry and the board outside annual meetings; a confidential email of 11 September 2026 described to the minister a serious breakdown of trust. In parliament on 19 October 2026 the minister described the departure as the chief executive's own decision and "the full story"; opposition MPs then produced ministry minutes showing nine meetings with the board without the chief executive present, and accuse the minister of misleading parliament and of political interference — accusations the minister rejects ("I fully stand by my answer. I reject the accusations that I was untruthful"), while conceding his earlier answers about board meetings "had not been adequate" (Daily Northern / mbl.is, October–November 2026). The unanimous parliamentary inquiry of 5 November 2026 is the referee now deciding the question; as of grading it has not reported. What can be said on the record is that the finance minister's contact with a state company's board was heavier than the arm's-length principle his own party campaigned on — the meetings are documented; their purpose is what is disputed.
  • An opposition question, recorded as a question and not an act: in September 2026 Guðlaugur Þór Þórðarson asked whether the government planned to abolish the Fiscal Council after not following its recommendations on the financial plan (Daily Northern, 14 September 2026). No abolition proposal appears in the documented record assembled here; the question is noted because the Test watches for exactly this shape, and the honest entry is that nothing came of it on the evidence available.

Why: The referees that define this test have all, on the documented record, bitten the hand that appointed or funds them — the central bank raising rates against her flagship promise and publishing a deficit forecast against her budget, the Supreme Court unsettling the mortgage market her housing policy depends on, the electoral commission certifying her referendum defeat, a parliamentary committee of her own majority opening an inquiry into her finance minister — and in every case the institution's decision stood and the government's response was argument. That is what holding without gripping looks like. The two shadows are named and weighed: the Article 71 cloture, a lawful but decades-dormant instrument whose use put the Speaker's neutrality in dispute, and the Landsvirkjun affair, an ALLEGATION under active inquiry whose meetings-fact is documented but whose interference question is undecided. Neither, on present evidence, shows an institution captured or punished; the grade is HOLDS, with the Landsvirkjun inquiry named as the file most likely to move it. Helped: the Central Bank, the courts, the electoral commission, and the press, whose autonomy was exercised in public and respected in outcome; voters, whose referendum No was certified and obeyed; parliament's oversight committee, which could move unanimously against a sitting finance minister. Hurt: the opposition's procedural position in July 2025, when a filibuster it regarded as a minority right was closed by a dormant clause; Hörður Arnarson, Landsvirkjun's chief executive of 17 years, whose departure under a cloud of disputed accounts is documented whatever the inquiry finds; public confidence in the Speaker's neutrality, contested on the record by the whole opposition.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The biggest wobble of the tenure — the voters rejecting her government's defining project — was answered with immediate, complete, public acceptance. On 30 August 2026, the day after the EU referendum, Frostadóttir stood with her foreign minister and said: "We respect these results… This government is not going forward with the accession talks this term… you can't have a referendum like this every one or two years… our promise was to ask, get a result, respect it. This government will continue with the EEA agreement, but we will not go further with accession talks" (Reuters Connect, 30 August 2026). She framed the 82.5% turnout itself as the achievement — "over 80% of the country say something in this election… it gives us a stronger mandate to make decisions on this matter" — and redirected policy to strengthening the EEA relationship with Norway and Liechtenstein (same source). No recount demand, no blame of the question, no early election, no attempt to re-run the issue through parliament: the government's own post-referendum step was to have parliament consider formally withdrawing the dormant EU application (Swiss Standpoint / Trend Compass reporting, 11 September 2026).
  • The referendum's timing was itself an adjustment to a wobble, openly reasoned. The coalition had promised the vote by the end of 2027; in March 2026 the government proposed bringing it forward to 29 August 2026, and did so in a changed world — US President Donald Trump's repeated 2026 statements about taking control of Greenland, Iceland's nearest large neighbour, had shifted the security debate, and analysts and the government alike treated earlier consultation as the response (ODIHR NAM Report, July 2026; KAS briefing, June 2026; contemporaneous reporting, March–August 2026). When another option became available — ask now, in the new circumstances, rather than on the old timetable — she considered it in the open and took it, with the reasons stated. The Althing approved the resolution on 28 May 2026, meeting the three-month legal deadline by a day (Government of Iceland; Parliamentary Resolution No. 14/157).
  • The cruise-fee wobble was answered with a rate cut, not denial. After Cruise Iceland's data on collapsing advance bookings reached parliament and rural ports protested the fee's incidence, an amendment adopted in 2025 reduced the rate with effect from 2026 (Tourism and Hospitality, 2026). The adjustment is partial and its effects are not yet documented, but the shape is the test's shape: evidence in, policy moved.
  • The Supreme Court's October 2025 mortgage ruling was answered by building the thing the court required — a transparent Central Bank benchmark for indexed loans — inside the November 2025 housing package, rather than by attacking the court or legislating around it (Government housing package, November 2025; Central Bank Financial Stability Committee statement, 31 October 2025).
  • The November 2026 budget wobble was answered in the open, on the numbers. When the Central Bank forecast a ISK 16.5 billion deficit against her budget's ISK 5 billion surplus, Frostadóttir did not dispute the bank's right to publish or its independence; she argued the forecast's vintage ("based on the budget the government presented in the autumn… the economic outlook had changed"), committed to closing the gap without new revenue measures, and named the mechanism — ministry proposals, reallocations, the budget committee's work at second reading (Daily Northern, 20 November 2026). Whether the gap closes is Test 5's file; the answering was public and specific.

Evidence against:

  • The fishing-fee wobble was answered by ending the conversation, not by adjusting. The opposition filibustered for 160 hours; the bill that finally passed on 14 July 2025 passed unchanged from the initial proposal, 34 votes to 23 (We Are Aquaculture / RÚV, July 2025). Months of pushback — from the opposition, from Fisheries Iceland, from rural communities — produced no amendment, no phase-in, no concession recorded in the final text; the adjustment instrument was the cloture clause. A government is entitled to pass its programme (the mandate rule), and 65% of voters backed the bill; but Test 3 grades the answering, and on this file the answer to sustained objection was procedural termination. When another option became available in the form of a negotiated softening, the documented record shows it was not taken up; no stated reasons for rejecting specific amendments appear in the record assembled here.
  • The inflation wobble of 2026 was answered with persistence in a plan whose conditions had changed. Inflation, 4.5% in December 2025, rose through 2026 to 5.9% by October; the Central Bank raised rates three times to 8%; GDP shrank in the second quarter of 2026 and was thought to have shrunk in the third; builders warned in November 2026 of a "very cold winter" in housing, with construction firms cutting staff as high rates froze new-home sales, and the Federation of Icelandic Industries' chair called the bank's last two decisions "very serious mistakes" risking a harder landing (RÚV via Iceland This Morning, 19 November 2026; S&P Global, September 2026). The no-good-choice clause applies to the inflation fight itself and is applied here by its mechanics: the options actually open in 2026 were to hold fiscal policy tight and accept the construction and growth pain, or to loosen fiscal policy and feed the inflation the IMF and the Central Bank both identified as driven by wage and housing pressures plus an external oil shock from the Middle East war (IMF Article IV concluding statement, 11 June 2026) — each carrying real harm, and hindsight options do not count. The constraint is recorded, not counted against her. What remains gradable is the honesty and the adjustment at the margins, and there the record is thinner: the government's public line through autumn 2026 was that the plan was working and the balanced-budget target stood, while its own central bank's forecast said otherwise on both growth and the deficit.
  • The coalition wobble of autumn 2026 — three MPs out of the governing groups, one to the opposition, support for the government down from 69% at the term's start to 50% in the post-referendum Gallup poll and 47% in the Gallup poll fieldworked 29 September–1 November 2026 (Daily Northern, September and November 2026) — has so far been answered with discipline language rather than policy movement: Viðreisn's remaining MPs called further defections "completely unacceptable" (Jón Gnarr, himself by then an independent supporter, November 2026), and no reshuffle, renegotiation of the coalition platform, or early-election consideration appears in the documented record. The wobble is weeks old at grading; the entry records an absence, not a refusal.

Why: Two wobbles define the tenure and they were answered in opposite ways. The referendum — a defeat she had personally campaigned on the Yes side of — drew the model answer: instant acceptance, a stated rule for the future ("not this term"), and a policy redirect to the EEA, all in public within 24 hours. The fishing fee — a victory her majority owned — drew the opposite: 160 hours of objection closed by cloture, the bill unchanged. Add the smaller adjustments (the cruise-fee reduction, the court-compliant mortgage benchmark, the argued-not-suppressed budget dispute) and the unadjusted coalition erosion, and the record is genuinely two-sided. The grade is MIXED. Helped: the 52.8% who voted No, whose answer was implemented as given; EEA partners and everyone invested in the existing European arrangement, which gained a government committed to strengthening it; cruise-dependent rural ports, partially, by the 2026 fee reduction; mortgage borrowers, by the transparent benchmark the court ruling produced. Hurt: the parliamentary opposition and rural fishing interests in July 2025, whose objections were heard for 160 hours and answered with none of them; construction workers and firms in late 2026, carrying the tight-policy landing the government chose not to soften; her own coalition's MPs, three of whom exited their groups in a single autumn.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the rural constituencies, the disabled, pensioners, immigrants and asylum seekers, renters and first-time buyers, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The disabled were counted in, structurally, in the tenure's largest welfare act. The disability system that took effect 1 September 2025 unified three allowances into one pension (full rate ISK 416,950 per month before tax, with home and age supplements on top), introduced the lifelong age-related supplement the People's Party had campaigned on, raised the income-exemption threshold, and — by tying social-security pensions to the wage index — gave recipients who cannot strike a permanent seat in the wage round (Ísland.is / Social Insurance Administration, 2025; Daily Northern, November 2026). Frostadóttir presented the package in November 2026 explicitly as improvements for disabled people spanning pensions, rights protections, housing, and health (Daily Northern, November 2026).
  • Child poverty was named from inside the government, with a number attached. Education and Children's Minister Inga Sæland — who moved from Social Affairs and Housing to Education in the 11 January 2026 reshuffle — called it "a national disgrace" that 10,500 children live below the low-income threshold, presented fourteen measures against child poverty, and put the child-benefit allocation at over ISK 21 billion for the coming year, while herself criticising that ISK 2–3 billion of it leaks to high-income households through shortcomings in the legislation (Daily Northern / mbl.is, October–November 2026). A government that publishes its own worst number and its own design flaw is counting, even where the count shames it.
  • The referendum counted the countryside against the capital, and the countryside won. Reykjavík North voted 57.5% Yes and Reykjavík South 54.5% Yes; the South constituency voted 60.5% No, the Northwest 62.9% No, and the Northeast 61.2% No — and the national result followed the rural majority, and was implemented (Euronews constituency figures via Northbridge Institute, August 2026). Whatever else the EU file was, it was not the capital counting the regions out.
  • Housing policy was aimed, in design, at the counted-out end of the market: the November 2025 package raised initial grants to non-profit housing associations, expanded shared-ownership loans (funding raised from ISK 4 billion to ISK 5.5 billion, targeted, on Frostadóttir's account, at young people), planned a 4,000-apartment neighbourhood at Úlfarsárdalur primarily for young people and first-time buyers, restricted short-term rentals (the Airbnb bill: a registered home plus one additional property), cut tax advantages for multiple-property owners and rental income, and let municipalities surcharge undeveloped urban plots (Government housing package via Construction.is, November 2025).
  • Immigrants are a fifth of the country and appear in the accounting as participants, not only as cases: the foreign-born were 21.5% of the population in 2025 (OECD International Migration Outlook 2026), the coalition platform promised Icelandic-language opportunities and integration support, and the Ministry of Social Affairs and Housing ran its Development Fund for Immigrant Issues grants through her tenure (Government of Iceland, September 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Asylum seekers were counted out further on her watch, by law, in steps. On 21 December 2025 parliament raised application fees for residence permits and citizenship (in force 1 January 2026). Amendments adopted 16 February 2026, in force 6 March 2026, abolished the 18-month rule — under which an applicant whose case the state had not decided within 18 months received a temporary residence permit — and discontinued renewals of permits granted on that basis, while adding revocation of international protection for serious criminal offences and a tolerated-stay permit (OECD International Migration Outlook 2026). On 9 June 2026 parliament created a specialised detention centre for third-country nationals in return procedures (Act No. 48/2026). Each step was in the coalition platform's direction — alignment with neighbouring countries, a "humane and efficient" system — and each step's cost falls on the same small group: the roughly 1,600 first asylum applicants of 2025 (down 9.9% on 2024), of whom Ukrainians were about 1,100, applicants from the West Bank and Gaza about 200, and Venezuelans about 100, with 36% of the 390 decisions taken in 2025 positive (OECD, 2026 — all figures as the OECD prints them, kept separate). The abolished rule had protected precisely the people the state's own slowness had left waiting; its abolition counts the waiting out.
  • The housing count is not closing. The inherited national framework called for about 4,000 apartments a year (35,000 over 2023–2032, 30% affordable, 5% social); documented construction ran at under 2,000 a year, with a cumulative need the labour movement's housing specialists put at over 40,000 (Reykjavík Grapevine reporting of HMS framework and specialist assessments). Those figures substantially predate or span her tenure and are printed as the gap she inherited and governs inside, not as her creation. What is hers is the November 2026 position: high rates freezing new-home sales, builders cutting staff, the Central Bank governor himself flagging a possible oversupply of finished units alongside the shortage of affordable ones — a market counting sellers and builders out at once (RÚV via Iceland This Morning, 19 November 2026). The Úlfarsárdalur 4,000 were, at grading, a plan in consultation (City of Reykjavík planning process, first half of 2026), not homes.
  • The tight-money year fell hardest on the households least able to carry it. Inflation of 5.9% (October 2026) and a policy rate of 8% raise mortgage and rent costs regressively; the IMF's June 2026 statement identifies wage and housing costs as the persistent drivers and projects inflation averaging 5.2% in 2026, not reaching the 2.5% target until early 2028 (IMF, 11 June 2026). Opposition MPs put the human version on the parliamentary record in November 2026 — households "struggling with unemployment, high interest rates and inflation" (Progressive Party MP Stefán Vagn Stefánsson, via Daily Northern, 18 November 2026) — an opposition framing, recorded as such, of a distribution the macroeconomic figures corroborate in direction.
  • The People's Party's own voters are drifting out of the count. The party whose base is pensioners and disability recipients polled 3.9% in the Gallup poll of 1 November 2026 (from 13.8% at the 2024 election), below the level at which it would reliably hold seats; its former minister Guðmundur Ingi Kristinsson left the party in April 2026 citing a breach of trust and sits as an independent (Gallup via PolitPro, November 2026; Daily Northern, September–November 2026). His account of his January 2026 exit from the cabinet — that party leader Inga Sæland pressed him to resign while he was recovering from heart surgery, which he says appalled him before he stepped down citing his health — is his ALLEGATION, recorded as his account; the reshuffle itself (Sæland to Education, Ragnar Þór Ingólfsson to Social Affairs and Housing, 11 January 2026) is documented fact.

Why: The counting splits by group, and the split is the grade. Disabled people, pensioners, and children in poverty were counted in with structural changes and honest numbers; the rural majority was counted decisively in the referendum; young buyers were counted in design. Asylum seekers were counted further out by three successive legal steps, each deliberate; renters and mortgage-holders absorbed a disinflation whose costs are regressive by construction; and the housing shortage — the file on which her government will ultimately be counted itself — stands roughly where she found it, with the building trade warning of a frozen winter. The grade is MIXED. Helped: disability pensioners and social-security recipients, whose unified pension now rises with wages; the rural constituencies whose No decided the referendum; children in low-income households, whose ISK 21 billion child-benefit allocation and fourteen-measure programme are on the record; non-profit housing associations and shared-ownership borrowers, funded ahead of the general market. Hurt: asylum applicants who lost the 18-month rule's protection against the state's own delays, and those facing the new detention centre; households carrying 8% money and 5.9% inflation; construction workers laid off in the late-2026 freeze; People's Party voters, whose party's poll collapse to 3.9% leaves their representation in doubt.

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Coalition platform and campaign pledges vs. the record since 21 December 2024, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The EU referendum — her most specific word, kept early and then obeyed against herself. The coalition agreement of 21 December 2024 promised a referendum on resuming EU accession negotiations "no later than 2027" (coalition platform; Grapevine, December 2024). The government brought it forward, the Althing resolved on 28 May 2026, and the vote was held on 29 August 2026 — more than a year ahead of the deadline (ODIHR NAM Report, July 2026; Government of Iceland, May 2026). The promise had never been to win it; it was to ask, and after the No she completed the word by binding her own government to the answer for the rest of the term (Reuters, 30 August 2026). Under the mandate rule this is FOR-shelf evidence at full strength: she carried out the platform the voters elected, including the part that defeated her.
  • The fishing fee — promised as fair share, delivered as written. The coalition announced at its formation that fishing companies would pay a corrected, fair fee for a common resource; the bill was presented in March 2025 and, after the filibuster, enacted on 14 July 2025 with the government's calculation intact — companies keeping 67%, the nation taking 33% (Political Data Yearbook 2026). The keeping hurt some of the kept-upon (Test 4); the keeping itself is documented.
  • The welfare word — delivered on schedule. The People's Party's platform items carried into the coalition agreement — the tax-free threshold rising toward ISK 60,000 for pensioners, a lifetime element for disability pensioners, pensions keeping pace — issued in the disability system of 1 September 2025 and the wage-indexation of social-security pensions (coalition priorities, December 2024; Ísland.is, 2025). The new system arrived on the date the 2025 budget framework had set for it.
  • The growth-and-reform word — machinery built. Her November 2026 London account of delivery — Iceland's first industrial policy, a ten-year growth plan to 2035 with a two-year rolling list of 35 measures for 2026–27, and the energy-permitting deadlock broken, with a measure going "from announcement to law in just seven months" — is her own account, recorded as such (Arctic Investments Conference reporting, 5 November 2026); the IMF independently noted the 2026–27 action plan's 35 measures across ten ministries in its June 2026 statement, while cautioning that sustained coordination would decide its effect (IMF, 11 June 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Lower inflation and lower interest rates — the headline economic word, currently reversed. The coalition's stated major aim was to reduce inflation and interest rates, as the government parties had promised in the campaign (Political Data Yearbook 2026). The first year delivered: inflation fell to 4.5% by December 2025 and the key rate to 7.3%. The second year took it back: inflation averaged a projected 5.2% in 2026 on the IMF's June figures and reached 5.9% by October; the Central Bank raised the key rate three times in 2026 to 8%, where it was held on 18 November 2026 (Central Bank via RÚV, November 2026; S&P Global, September 2026). The causes are partly external — the IMF and S&P both tie the 2026 rise to Middle East war energy prices on top of domestic wage and housing pressure — and the no-good-choice mechanics of Test 3 apply to the policy response. But the word was a result, not an effort, and on results the promise stands unkept at grading, with the IMF projecting target (2.5%) only by early 2028 — after the next election campaign has begun.
  • The balanced budget — asserted, not yet shown. Ending deficit spending is a central government pledge; the 2027 budget bill assumes a surplus of ISK 5 billion. The Central Bank's November 2026 Monetary Bulletin forecasts a deficit of about ISK 16.5 billion instead — a gap of roughly ISK 22 billion between the two documents, printed here as two sourced figures, never blended (RÚV, 18 November 2026). The government's position — "the zero still stands," the gap to be closed at second reading by restraint and reallocation, two krónur saved for every króna of new revenue (Foreign Minister Þorgerður Katrín Gunnarsdóttir, Althing, 18 November 2026; Frostadóttir, 20 November 2026) — is a word about the future. The opposition's counter-figure for 2026 itself (a deficit heading to ISK 90 billion against ISK 28 billion forecast, per Progressive Party MP Stefán Vagn Stefánsson) is an opposition figure, recorded as such. At grading the pledge is neither kept nor broken; it is contested by her own central bank, which is the weaker position for a word to be in.
  • The no-tax-rise word — broken on the coalition partner's own account. Viðreisn entered the coalition with "no increases in taxes, including income and capital gains taxes" as its stated priority (coalition priorities, December 2024), and Frostadóttir faced a direct parliamentary accusation in September 2026 of breaking a pre-election pledge not to raise taxes on ordinary working people, with ISK 60 billion of increases alleged over two years (Daily Northern, 14 September 2026 — the ISK 60 billion is the accuser's figure). Her answer — revenue sought from banks, tourism, and resources; some charges inherited — concedes increases while disputing their target. The heaviest evidence is internal: her coalition's own MP Ingvar Þóroddsson cited the tax increases as a betrayal of Viðreisn's promises when he crossed to the opposition on 16 November 2026 (Daily Northern, 17 November 2026). A pledge whose breach is announced by the pledging party's own MP, on his way out, is broken in the public record whatever the incidence argument shows.
  • Housing security and the end of poverty — the platform's largest words, receding on present numbers. The coalition's headline commitments were housing security and measures "to eliminate poverty" (coalition priorities, December 2024). Two years in: 10,500 children remain below the low-income threshold on her own minister's figure (October 2026), and the housing package of November 2025 is a set of instruments — grants, loans, a planned neighbourhood, rental rules — whose completions cannot yet be counted against a documented need above 40,000 homes and an annual build rate under half the required 4,000 (Test 4). Neither word is yet breakable in fairness — both were term-length projects — and neither is on a trajectory the documented numbers currently show.

The four crossings, named (word × outcome): 1. Kept her word and it helped: the EU referendum. The promise to ask by 2027 was kept early; the keeping helped — a divisive decade-old question was settled by 82.5% of the electorate, and the loser's acceptance, her own, closed it for the term. 2. Kept her word and it still hurt: the fishing fee. The fair-share promise was kept exactly as written, and the keeping is what rural fishing communities and the industry say hurt them (Test 1) — the hurt is recorded in full in Tests 1 and 4; the keeping earns its credit here. 3. Went against her word and it helped: the tax rises, on the government's documented account of their use. The revenue raised against Viðreisn's no-increase priority funded the state that delivered the disability reform and the wage-indexed pensions — the help is real and recorded in Test 4; the break is recorded above and is not laundered by it. 4. Broke her word and it hurt: the same tax rises' other face, and the rates word. Working households that voted for no rises and lower rates hold, at November 2026, higher charges on the opposition's and a defector's account and an 8% policy rate with 5.9% inflation — the break and the hurt arriving in the same letter.

Why: Her word divides by kind. The procedural and programmatic words — ask the country about Europe by 2027, charge the fishing companies their fair share, rebuild the disability system — were kept precisely, on or ahead of schedule, including the one whose answer she hated. The outcome words — lower inflation, lower rates, a balanced budget, no tax rises, housing security, poverty eliminated — are unkept, reversed, contested, or receding at the two-year mark, with the honest caveat that the 2026 inflation reversal carries a documented external shock and the term has two years to run. A record that keeps every promise about what the government will do and is behind on every promise about what Icelanders will feel is the definition of this grade. The grade is MIXED. Helped: voters who wanted the EU question asked and answered — all of them, both sides; disability pensioners, whose promised system arrived on its date; the Treasury's beneficiaries, funded by the fee she promised and collected. Hurt: Viðreisn's 2024 voters, whose no-tax-rise party raised taxes in government — one of their MPs said so as he left; mortgage-holders and renters waiting on the lower-rates word; her own credibility account on the budget, against which her central bank's ISK 16.5 billion deficit forecast now sits in public.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: HOLDS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED.

Kristrún Frostadóttir is graded two years into a four-year term that has not changed hands, collapsed, or faced an early election — the premise is confirmed — and the record is that of a high-trust democracy's ordinary, consequential government, which is precisely what makes the failures legible. One law is MIXED because her formulas genuinely fell on insiders — the fishing fee doubled what quota-holders pay the nation that owns the fish, with 65% public support — while the wider tax file's incidence is contested by the opposition (ISK 60 billion alleged over two years) and, more heavily, by her own coalition's defecting MP, who called the rises a betrayal of Viðreisn's promises as he crossed to the Independence Party in November 2026. Without gripping HOLDS, the tenure's strongest grade, because every referee bit and every bite stood: the Central Bank raised rates to 8% against her flagship promise and published a ISK 16.5 billion deficit forecast against her budget's ISK 5 billion surplus, and got argument, not retaliation; the Supreme Court unsettled the mortgage market and got a compliant benchmark; the electoral commission certified her referendum defeat; a unanimous parliamentary committee opened an inquiry into her finance minister — the Landsvirkjun affair, an ALLEGATION of interference and misleading parliament that the minister denies and that inquiry has not yet decided, named here as the file most likely to move this grade, alongside the July 2025 Article 71 cloture that left the Speaker's neutrality contested by the whole opposition. The wobble is MIXED because its two largest instances drew opposite answers: the referendum No was accepted within a day, completely, with a rule for the future and a redirect to the EEA — while the fishing fee's 160 hours of objection were closed by cloture with the bill unchanged, and the autumn 2026 coalition erosion (three MPs out of the governing groups, government support down from 69% to 47–50% across separate Gallup polls) has so far been answered with discipline, not adjustment; the 2026 inflation fight itself is recorded under the no-good-choice clause, its external shock documented by the IMF, and not counted against her. Counts everyone is MIXED because the ledger splits by group: disability pensioners gained a unified, wage-indexed pension on 1 September 2025 and child poverty was named from inside her own cabinet at 10,500 children with ISK 21 billion behind it, while asylum seekers lost the 18-month rule in March 2026 and gained a detention centre in June, and renters and borrowers carried a 5.9%-inflation, 8%-rate year. Her word is MIXED because the crossings, named in full in Test 5, run all four ways: kept-and-helped (the referendum, asked early and obeyed), kept-and-hurt (the fishing fee, delivered exactly as promised onto the communities that opposed it), broke-and-helped (tax revenue raised against the no-rise word, funding the welfare delivery), and broke-and-hurt (the same rises, and a lower-rates promise standing reversed at 8%). The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: the Landsvirkjun inquiry's findings, whether the 2027 budget closes the ISK 22 billion gap between her surplus and her central bank's deficit, whether inflation reaches target before the 2028 election, whether Úlfarsárdalur's 4,000 planned apartments become homes, and whether a coalition now holding 33 of 63 seats holds together to November 2028.
