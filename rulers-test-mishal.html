The Ruler's Test — Mishal Al-Ahmad Al-Jaber Al-Sabah, Emir of Kuwait

Subject: Mishal Al-Ahmad Al-Jaber Al-Sabah — Emir of Kuwait since 16 December 2023, when he succeeded his half-brother Nawaf Al-Ahmad Al-Jaber Al-Sabah on the latter's death. The seat graded is the emirate, and the reason is stated plainly: in Kuwait the Emir is not a ceremonial head of state. He is head of state and the decisive executive authority — he appoints the prime minister and, on the prime minister's recommendation, the cabinet; he is commander-in-chief; he appoints all judges; and since 10 May 2024 he also holds legislative power directly (see below). The Prime Minister's role is stated plainly here, as the brief requires: Sheikh Ahmad Abdullah Al-Ahmad Al-Sabah, prime minister since 15 April 2024, is the Emir's appointee and head of government in the administrative sense — he chairs the Council of Ministers and runs the ministries day to day — but he holds office at the Emir's appointment, was never confirmed by an elected assembly (the Assembly was dissolved before his cabinet was formed on 12 May 2024), and cannot override the Emir on any matter in this dossier. Every act graded below — the dissolution, the decree-laws, the citizenship campaign, the cabinet reshuffles of 1 February and September 2026 — is an Amiri act or an act of a government the Emir appointed and can dismiss. Crown Prince Sheikh Sabah Khaled Al-Hamad Al-Sabah, named by the Emir in June 2024, is heir apparent. The defining act of the reign, verified as still in force at grading: on 10 May 2024, five weeks after a general election, the Emir dissolved the National Assembly and suspended Articles 51, 56 (in part), 71 (in part), 79, 107, 174 and 181 of the 1962 constitution for a period not exceeding four years. As of November 2026 that suspension remains in place — no election has been held or scheduled, no constitutional draft has been published, and the four-year window runs to about May 2028 (Congressional Research Service, updated 14 January 2026; IPU Parline, 2026; Electos country profile, updated 16 November 2026). He is graded on just under three years in office, of which two and a half have been spent ruling without a parliament. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform a ruler was seated on is not itself a failure; marks are lost only for how it is done. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The law reached inside the ruling family itself, to verdict. Sheikh Talal Khaled Al-Ahmad Al-Sabah — a senior Al Sabah, former first deputy prime minister and former minister of both Interior and Defense — was tried before the Ministerial Court after referral in July 2024 and convicted on 14 January 2025 of embezzlement and money laundering from the "secret expenses" budgets of the two ministries he had run; he was sentenced to 14 years in prison with fines and restitution totalling roughly USD 98 million (Ministerial Court verdict, 14 January 2025; contemporaneous reporting). A former minister and MP, Mubarak Al-Harees, was likewise sentenced by the Court of Cassation to two years with hard labour and a KD 566,000 fine for using his office to profit from industrial land allocations, while a co-defendant was acquitted for lack of evidence — the same court convicting and acquitting on the evidence in one case (Court of Cassation ruling, reported 2025).
  • The citizenship review was publicly framed as exempting no one: the Supreme Committee to Investigate Kuwaiti Citizenship, established by Amiri decree on 27 May 2024 and chaired by the first deputy prime minister and interior minister, was mandated to re-examine nationality files across the board, with officials stating explicitly that "no one" was exempt from review (committee mandate and official statements, 2024–2025).
  • Clemency ran by general decree rather than by favour: Amiri Decree No. 33 of 2025 freed or reduced the sentences of 781 prisoners for National Day in February 2025, and Amiri Decree No. 21 of 2026 did the same for 539 prisoners in February 2026 (Ministry of Interior statements, February 2025 and February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The source itself now runs two laws. Citizenship — the most basic status a person holds — is decided by an executive committee chaired by the interior minister, and Decree-Law No. 52 of 2026, published 10 May 2026, widened the grounds for withdrawal, extended the consequences to dependants, and classified nationality decisions as acts of sovereignty not subject to judicial review (Decree-Law No. 52 of 2026; Gulf Centre for Human Rights analysis, 2026). A Kuwaiti by origin keeps a status a court can protect; a naturalised Kuwaiti, or one the committee reclassifies, holds a status the executive can end with no judge in between. That is a second law at the source, written by the seat being graded.
  • The franchise was split by origin in 2026. Decree-Law No. 79 of 2026, in force since 24 August 2026, removes the right to vote or stand altogether from naturalised citizens, who previously waited out a long qualifying period; citizens by origin keep the vote (Electos country profile, updated 16 November 2026). Whatever elections eventually return, they will return under two classes of citizen.
  • Opponents and critics meet a different law than allies do. After the May 2024 dissolution, several parliamentarians faced criminal charges for political speech: a former parliamentary candidate was detained in May 2024 charged with insulting the political leadership during his campaign, and former MP Walid al-Tabtabai was charged over a post on X about Kuwaitis' constitutional rights, sentenced to four years by the Criminal Court, reduced to two years on appeal in September 2024 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025). In 2026 the citizenship instrument itself was used against named public critics: Emiri Decree No. 121 of 2026 (28 July 2026) revoked the citizenship of six people including exiled journalists and activists, and on 29 April 2026 the citizenship of journalist Ahmed Shihab-Eldin and his two sisters was revoked days after his release from detention (Committee to Protect Journalists, 29 April and 11 August 2026; GCHR, 2026). Whether each underlying file had a lawful basis is, in each case, an ALLEGATION-level question the executive process does not let a court answer — which is precisely the one-law problem.
  • The parliamentary accountability route that once applied one law to ministers was closed by the seat itself: since May 2024 cabinet ministers, ruling-family members included, are no longer subject to questioning by any parliament, because there is none (Freedom House, 2025). The Talal conviction shows the criminal law can still reach a royal; the closed grillings show the political law no longer reaches anyone in government at all.

Why this grade: The for-shelf is real — a ruling-family former minister is in prison on a court's verdict, and that almost never happens in the region. But Test 1 grades the source, and at the source the Emir has legislated two classes of citizen, placed the decisive status of one class beyond judicial review, and presided over speech prosecutions and citizenship revocations that fall on critics while no equivalent instrument falls on supporters. One law for the criminal courts; a different, executive-only law for citizenship, speech, and the vote. That is a fail, with the conviction recorded in full on the other shelf.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the Kuwaiti public fisc and ordinary litigants, who saw a "secret expenses" conviction with restitution ordered against a former defence and interior minister; prisoners freed under the general National Day decrees (781 in 2025; 539 in 2026). Hurt — naturalised citizens as a class, who lost the vote in August 2026 and hold citizenship revocable without a court; former MPs and candidates prosecuted for campaign and social-media speech; the journalists and activists named in Decree No. 121 of 2026 and in the April 2026 revocations, and their dependants, who lose status with the principal.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The ordinary courts still rule against the powerful in individual cases, on this record: the Ministerial Court convicted a ruling-family former minister (Test 1), the Court of Cassation acquitted a co-defendant it found the evidence did not reach, and in April 2026 a Kuwaiti court acquitted journalist Ahmed Shihab-Eldin on one of the three charges against him (the false-news charge), though it convicted on the other two with no punishment recorded (UN experts' statement via OHCHR, June 2026; CPJ, April 2026). Kuwaiti courts have a documented longer record of ruling against the government, noted by the drafting committee of the 2026 economic-courts law itself (AGBI commentary by a drafting-committee member, 2026).
  • No documented act in the record assembled here shows the Emir removing a judge, overturning a verdict, or directing the Central Bank of Kuwait's monetary decisions; the Central Bank continued to operate and Kuwait's sovereign ratings were raised by S&P to AA-/A-1+ on 21 November 2025 on reform momentum — a market-facing institution functioning, cited here as environment, not as proof of autonomy (S&P Global Ratings, 21 November 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The legislature — the chief referee of the executive in Kuwait's system — was not merely gripped but removed, by the seat's own hand, and its removal was then extended to every remaining elected body. On 10 May 2024 the Emir dissolved the Assembly elected five weeks earlier and suspended the constitutional articles that would have required elections within two months (Article 107), required the Assembly's approval of laws (Article 79), and governed constitutional amendment (Articles 174, 181) — including Article 181, which forbids suspending the constitution except under martial law (Amiri Diwan address, 10 May 2024; International IDEA, May 2024). Laws are now issued by Emir's decree with the Council of Ministers exercising the Assembly's role, and decrees no longer return to a parliament for approval (Electos, updated 16 November 2026). In May 2026, Law No. 57 of 2026 ended elections to the Municipal Council — the last elected body in the country — making its 12 members all appointed by decree (Law No. 57 of 2026; Electos, 2026). Kuwait therefore has, as of grading, no elected institution at any level.
  • The succession referee was bypassed: the constitution requires the crown prince to be endorsed by the Assembly; the Emir named Sheikh Sabah Khaled Al-Hamad Al-Sabah crown prince in June 2024, a month after dissolving the Assembly that would have voted (Freedom House, 2025).
  • The electoral referee was already switched off before the last election: the independent elections commission created by Law No. 120 of 2023 had its application suspended by decree in February 2024 (Electos, 2026).
  • The prosecution and the courts were restructured by decree in 2026, during the suspension, with no legislature sitting: Decree-Law No. 13 of 2026 restricted reporting on military entities; Decree-Law No. 47 of 2026 created new terrorism offences; a specialised "State Security Crimes, Terrorism, and Financing Prosecution Office" was created in March 2026; and Decree-Law No. 80 of 2026 (26 August 2026) replaced the 1990 Judicial Organization Law, restructured the Supreme Judicial Council, set four-year renewable-once terms for senior judicial posts including the Public Prosecutor, and set a five-year deadline to staff the judiciary and prosecution entirely with Kuwaitis by origin (UN experts via OHCHR, June 2026; HuMENA, 2026; Decree-Law No. 80 of 2026 analysis, Wefaq Law, 2026). Each change may have a rationale; together they mean the referees' rulebooks, tenure, and eligibility are now written by one of the players.
  • The media referee narrowed sharply in 2026. UN experts recorded that journalist Ahmed Shihab-Eldin was arrested in March 2026 while visiting family, held incommunicado for four days — which the experts characterised as enforced disappearance — detained almost two months on charges including "spreading false news" and "harming national security" after reporting on military activity, then stripped of citizenship on release (OHCHR, June 2026). CPJ documented the July 2026 arrest of political commentator Mohammed Saleh al-Sabti after a television interview calling for de-escalation with Iran, and described press freedom in Kuwait as having "worsened considerably since the start of the Iran war in early 2026" (CPJ, 11 August 2026).

Why this grade: A seat holds without gripping when the referees can still say no to it. In Kuwait as of November 2026 the Assembly is dissolved with its return articles suspended, the municipal council is appointed, the elections commission is suspended, the crown prince sits unendorsed, nationality is declared beyond judicial review, and the prosecution and judicial-organisation laws have been rewritten by the executive they are meant to check — while journalists covering the 2026 war were detained and denaturalised. The surviving for-shelf (individual courts still acquitting and convicting on evidence) shows the judiciary's habits outlasting its protections; it does not show the seat holding off. Clear fail.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the executive itself, which has legislated at speed since May 2024 (Test 5); litigants in ordinary commercial and criminal cases, whose courts still function. Hurt — Kuwaiti voters, who have had no elected representative at national or local level since May 2024; the dissolved Assembly's 50 elected members and the Municipal Council's 10 elected members, removed or not replaced; journalists and commentators covering the 2026 war, including the two named above; future defendants in state-security cases, who now face a specialised prosecution created by the executive.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest wobble of the reign — the 2026 Iran war — was answered in the open, with the facts stated against interest. After US and Israeli strikes on Iran on 28 February 2026, Iran struck targets across Kuwait, including Kuwait International Airport and Ali Al Salem Air Base; by 9 March, 12 people had been killed in Kuwait, including members of the armed forces and a child, and 67 Kuwaiti service members had been wounded by 6 March (Kuwaiti Ministry of Defense reporting; Asharq Al-Awsat, March 2026). In a televised address on 9 March 2026 the Emir named the attack plainly — "a brutal attack by a neighbouring Muslim country, which we consider a friend" — and stated on the record the fact most inconvenient to his US alliance: Kuwait "has not permitted the use of our land, airspace, or coasts for any military action against it," and had told Tehran so repeatedly through diplomatic channels. He asserted a proportionate right of self-defence under Article 51 of the UN Charter, raised alert levels and deployments at vital sites, and called for national unity and vigilance against rumours — he did not deny the strikes were happening, did not claim a victory that had not occurred, and did not launch a retaliatory escalation of Kuwait's own (televised address, 9 March 2026, via QNA and Asharq Al-Awsat).
  • Pushback on the citizenship campaign's harshest edge produced a partial, documented adjustment. After the revocations of citizenship from women who had acquired it through marriage (the "Article 8" cases) drew sustained domestic and international criticism, the government announced that affected wives and mothers of Kuwaitis would retain their benefits as Kuwaiti citizens for life, be issued civil cards recording "treatment as Kuwaiti," keep employment and education rights, and be given a one-year grace period from 1 June 2025 to regularise status; a Grievance Committee for Withdrawal, Forfeiture, and Loss of Kuwaiti Citizenship was established under Cabinet Resolution No. 207 of 2025 (Ministry of Interior guidelines, 2025; First Deputy Prime Minister's open meeting with editors-in-chief, reported 2025). The interior minister also stated that restoration decisions rest with the Emir alone — an honest statement of where the door is, if a narrow one.
  • The original May 2024 decision was at least argued in public, with reasons, at the time it was taken: a televised address naming gridlock, interference in the head of state's appointment prerogative, and threats to public wealth, rather than a silent decree (Amiri Diwan, 10 May 2024). Arab Barometer polling in 2024 recorded real public discontent with the dissolved parliament's performance — the frustration he cited existed, even though the same polling showed Kuwaitis were not ready to part with democratic principles (Arab Barometer, 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The central pushback — from voters, from the constitution's own text, and from two years of commentary asking when the promised review would appear — has been met with silence, not adjustment. The expert committee promised in May 2024 (Test 5) has never been publicly named, no draft amendments had been published as of 2026, and no election date has been set; Carnegie Endowment's 2025 assessment records that "the promise of a committee to reform the constitution and pave the way for parliamentary restoration has been ignored to date" and that the failure to honour it "has increased distrust between the public and the government" (Carnegie Endowment, March 2025; Electos, updated November 2026). If another option became available — the order's own stated routes of a referendum or a newly elected Assembly — there is no documented instance of it being considered openly; the routes were simply not taken up.
  • On citizenship, the response to documented harm was to widen the instrument. After the UN Special Rapporteur on violence against women and girls reported in June 2026 that more than 70,000 people had reportedly been affected and warned of severe consequences for women and children across housing, pensions, education, healthcare, banking, and family unity (UN Special Rapporteur report, A/HRC/62/49/Add.1, 10 June 2026), the state's legislative answer in the same period was Decree-Law No. 52 of 2026 (10 May 2026), which broadened withdrawal grounds and removed judicial review — doubling down, in law, on the exact feature the criticism targeted. The benefits-retention measures on the for-shelf soften the landing for one category (Article 8 wives and mothers); they do not restore a single citizenship, and the grievance route ends at the Emir's own discretion.
  • The wartime information wobble was answered by narrowing the public's view rather than by openness: bans and prosecutions around filming and commenting on the Iranian attacks, the Shihab-Eldin and al-Sabti cases (Test 2), and new Decree-Laws No. 13 and 47 of 2026 restricting military reporting and expanding security offences (OHCHR, June 2026; CPJ, 2026; HuMENA, 2026). The Emir's own address was candid; the system beneath it punished candour in others.
  • The no-good-choice clause was considered for the May 2024 dissolution and does not carry it. The Emir's address framed the step as forced — "We were left with no option other than taking this hard decision to rescue the country" (Amiri Diwan, 10 May 2024). The options that actually existed are part of the record: the constitution's own route (dissolution followed by elections within two months, the route taken after the February 2024 dissolution) was open and had just been used; a new cabinet under the sitting Assembly was open. Those options carried real costs — continued gridlock was documented and public frustration was real — but they were lawful doors, not harms of the kind the clause covers, and the seat had not itself been trapped by an emergency it did not choose. The clause is therefore recorded as considered and not applied; what remains gradable — honesty about the choice and willingness to revisit it — is graded on the shelves above.

Why this grade: Two different rulers appear in this record. Facing foreign attack in 2026, the Emir spoke plainly, stated an inconvenient fact about his own alliance, and kept Kuwait's response proportionate and defensive — an open answer to a real wobble. Facing domestic and international pushback on the two things he chose himself — the suspended parliament and the citizenship campaign — he has neither adjusted nor explained the delay: the review committee vanished, the revocation law widened, and wartime criticism was prosecuted. Open abroad, closed at home: MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Kuwait's residents, citizens and non-citizens alike, who heard the facts of the 2026 attacks from their head of state directly and saw a defensive rather than escalatory posture; Article 8 wives and mothers, whose lifetime benefits, civil status, jobs, and children's schooling were formally protected after the first wave of revocations. Hurt — the tens of thousands whose citizenship files were decided while criticism was being answered with a wider law (Test 4 counts); voters waiting since May 2024 for the promised review; journalists and commentators detained for wartime reporting and commentary.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the naturalised, the Bidoon, the women, the expatriate majority, and the critics — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Article 8 mitigation (Test 3) is a counting measure as well as an adjustment: women stripped of citizenship acquired through marriage were formally promised retention of benefits for life, a civil card with "treatment as Kuwaiti" status, continued public-sector employment (short of leadership posts), continued education and scholarships, retention of one private residence, and a grievance route (Ministry of Interior guidelines, 2025). The state counted the harm to this group on paper and budgeted for part of it.
  • Two decree-era reforms counted women in where the old law counted them out: in early 2025 Kuwait raised the minimum marriage age to 18 and repealed Article 153 of the Penal Code, which had mitigated punishment for so-called honour killings (UN and rights reporting on the 2025 reforms; UN Special Rapporteur's Kuwait visit report, June 2026).
  • Clemency and wartime protection were not limited to citizens by origin. The 30 long-serving prisoners released under the Emir's directive included 13 expatriates alongside 17 Kuwaitis (Ministry of Interior reporting), and the 9 March 2026 address framed the state's duty of protection as covering "its people and residents" — the expatriate majority included in the sentence, at least (QNA, 9 March 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The citizenship campaign is the largest counting-out in Kuwait's modern record, and its counts are contested — printed here as separate sourced figures, never blended: reporting in May 2025 put the total revoked at more than 37,000, of whom at least 26,000 were women who had acquired citizenship through marriage (contemporaneous reporting, May 2025); by August 2025 the figure reported was almost 50,000 over the preceding year (The National, via Opinio Juris, October 2025); official statements put the figure at about 50,000 by mid-2025 (via Electos, November 2026); the Gulf Centre for Human Rights estimated about 70,000 people had lost nationality as of September 2026, with a cumulative estimate of 250,000–300,000 affected once dependants are included (GCHR, September 2026); and the UN Special Rapporteur reported in June 2026 that more than 70,000 individuals had reportedly been affected (A/HRC/62/49/Add.1, June 2026). The government's position — that the review targets fraudulently obtained citizenship, dual nationality (illegal under Kuwaiti law), and the repealed marriage provision, and that "no one is exempt" — is recorded; the UN experts' position — that withdrawals on this scale, extended to dependants, without individual judicial process, produce statelessness and breach the prohibition on arbitrary deprivation of nationality — is also recorded. Both disputants are named; the dossier does not blend their numbers or adopt either's characterisation as its own.
  • The burden falls by sex, by law. Kuwaiti women still cannot transmit nationality to their children or spouses on equal terms with men; Decree-Law No. 116 of 2024 ended acquisition of citizenship by foreign wives through marriage going forward, and the revocations then reached back over women who had acquired it under that very provision — roughly 29,000 of those stripped by May 2025 were reported to be Article 8 women (Kuwait Times, May 2025, via The Pundit, April 2026). The UN Special Rapporteur regretted that Kuwait "has not only refrained from changing its nationality laws but has also enacted further discriminatory changes" (A/HRC/62/49/Add.1, June 2026). Documented individual consequences in the UN CERD record include a woman of Egyptian heritage — role only, as the record gives her no public office — deported in March 2025 after criticising her revocation online, separated from her two Kuwaiti daughters, and stripped of job, property, and pension (CERD joint submission, July 2026; Ministry of Interior statement quoted therein).
  • The Bidoon — Kuwait's long-standing stateless population, estimated at roughly 100,000 people (Congressional Research Service, January 2026; GCHR puts the existing stateless Bidoon population at over 90,000) — were counted out before this reign and remain counted out in it: classified as "illegal residents," without a judicial path to citizenship, and now joined, on the UN's warning, by a newly stateless population the campaign is creating (CRS, 2026; UN Special Rapporteur, June 2026). No Bidoon naturalisation initiative appears in the record of this reign assembled here; it is recorded as an absence, not an act.
  • The expatriate majority — about 70% of the population, some 3.3 million people (CRS, January 2026) — holds no vote and no path counted in this record, and its grip tightened: in July 2025 Kuwait introduced a digital exit-permit system requiring employer approval through government apps before a migrant worker may leave the country (rights reporting, 2025). Naturalised citizens, the one non-origin group that did hold political rights, lost them in August 2026 (Test 1).

Why this grade: Counting everyone is where this reign's record is heaviest, and the for-shelf — benefits retained for one category of revoked women, two genuine women's-law reforms, inclusive clemency — is mitigation inside a policy whose main line runs the other way. Tens of thousands of people, disproportionately women, plus their dependants, lost the status on which housing, work, pensions, banking, healthcare, and family unity depend, by executive decision without a court; the already-stateless Bidoon gained company instead of a remedy; and the franchise was formally narrowed by origin. FAILS.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — girls protected by the marriage-age floor of 18 and women covered by the repeal of the honour-killing mitigation; Article 8 wives and mothers who kept benefits, jobs, and schooling under the mitigation rules; the prisoners, Kuwaiti and expatriate, freed under the clemency decrees. Hurt — the revoked, on every sourced count between 37,000 and more than 70,000 principals, and dependants estimated by GCHR at up to 250,000–300,000 affected in total; women as a class, whose nationality rights were narrowed in both directions (cannot transmit; can no longer acquire by marriage; retroactively revoked); the roughly 100,000 Bidoon, still uncounted; migrant workers, whose exit from the country now requires an employer's digital permission.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Public statements and orders vs. the record since 16 December 2023, with dates; the two axes — word kept or broken, people helped or hurt — graded independently.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The four-year ceiling — kept so far, on its own terms. The 10 May 2024 order suspended the named articles "for no more than four years." Two and a half years in, the suspension has not been extended beyond that ceiling and no permanent abolition of the Assembly has been decreed; the window runs to about May 2028 (Amiri order, 10 May 2024; Electos, November 2026). This is a word whose deadline has not yet arrived; it is recorded as kept-so-far, not kept.
  • The anti-corruption word — acted on at the top. The May 2024 address said corruption had reached into institutions and that the judiciary would "purify itself"; the record since includes the Ministerial Court's conviction of a ruling-family former minister of Interior and Defense (January 2025) and the Court of Cassation's conviction of a former minister-MP (Test 1). Whatever the campaign's selectivity elsewhere, the specific promise that the top was not untouchable has one large kept instance.
  • The economic-reform word — delivered in law. The address promised to protect national wealth and unblock a stalled state. The decree government then passed what parliaments had blocked for years: the Financing and Liquidity (public debt) Law in March 2025, setting a KD 30 billion ceiling and maturities up to 50 years, followed by Kuwait's first international bond issuance since 2017 — three bonds totalling USD 11.25 billion in October 2025 — and an S&P sovereign upgrade to AA-/A-1+ on 21 November 2025 citing the law and reform momentum (Finance Ministry statement, March 2025; Reuters, March 2025; S&P Global Ratings, 21 November 2025). Kept word, and on the helped/hurt axis it helped: funding certainty for the state that pays the salaries and subsidies the population depends on.
  • The wartime word — kept as spoken. On 9 March 2026 the Emir said Kuwait had not authorised the use of its territory against Iran and claimed only a proportionate right of self-defence; the subsequent record shows Kuwait condemning the attacks jointly with Gulf partners (26 March 2026), hosting allied air defence, and taking no documented independent retaliatory strike of its own — the conduct matched the address (joint GCC statement, 26 March 2026; 2026 war chronology).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The review promise — the reign's central word, unkept on its own timetable. The Amiri order of 10 May 2024 provided for an expert committee to draw up proposed constitutional amendments within six months, to go to the cabinet and the Emir, and within the four years to be submitted either to a public referendum or to a newly elected parliament (Amiri order, 10 May 2024; Carnegie Endowment, May 2024 and March 2025). As of November 2026 — two years past the six-month mark — the committee's members have not been publicly named, no draft has been published, and neither a referendum nor an election has been scheduled (Carnegie Endowment, March 2025; Electos, updated 16 November 2026). The four-year outer limit has not expired, so the promise is not yet finally broken; its six-month working promise is broken, and the mechanism that was supposed to make the suspension temporary has not visibly begun.
  • "I will not allow democracy to be exploited to destroy the state" — the stated purpose was review, the record is removal. The same address framed the suspension as a pause to revise "the democratic process in its entirety," not an ending. Since then the direction of travel has been away from restoration: the Municipal Council's elections abolished (Law No. 57 of 2026), naturalised citizens disenfranchised (Decree-Law No. 79 of 2026), and the crown prince seated without the Assembly's endorsement. Each step is documented; each makes the promised return harder, not easier.
  • The two axes, named crossings. (1) Kept their word AND it helped people: the debt law and bond return, promised as unblocking the state, delivered on schedule and credited by S&P. (2) Kept their word AND it still hurt people: the citizenship review was carried out as announced — files re-examined, "no one exempt" — and the keeping of that word is exactly what produced the harms ledgered in Test 4; the keeping earns credit here, the hurt stands in full there, and neither launders the other. (3) Went against their word AND it helped people: not documented in the record assembled here — no instance was found of a broken pledge whose breaking benefited people; recorded as not documented rather than invented. (4) Broke their word AND it hurt people: the six-month committee promise — broken, and the hurt is the two years of representation-less government that followed for every Kuwaiti voter, with no draft to judge and no date to wait for.

Why this grade: The Emir's word divides cleanly in two. Where the promise was an act of government — prosecute the top, pass the debt law, hold the wartime line — it was kept, on dates this dossier can cite. Where the promise was a limit on his own power — a committee in six months, amendments to a referendum or an elected Assembly, a suspension that is a review rather than a replacement — it has not been kept, and every subsequent decree has moved the other way. A ruler's word about his own restraint is the word Test 5 weighs most, and it is the one receding; the delivered economic and anti-corruption record keeps the grade from FAILS. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the Kuwaiti treasury and everyone paid from it, by the debt law and the restored market access; the public, by the one senior corruption conviction the anti-corruption word produced. Hurt — Kuwaiti voters, by the unkept review timetable (no draft, no date, no referendum in two and a half years); the revoked and their dependants, by a citizenship promise kept in a form that hurt them (crossing 2, ledgered in Test 4); future assemblies, whose prospective electorate and local-government base were both narrowed while the review was pending.

Overall Summary

Mishal Al-Ahmad Al-Jaber Al-Sabah came to the throne in December 2023 as a caretaker-aged emir (born 27 September 1940) and within five months made the most consequential decision by a Kuwaiti ruler since the 1990–91 invasion: he dissolved the parliament Kuwaitis had elected five weeks earlier, suspended the constitutional articles that protected it — including the article forbidding suspension — and took legislative power into his own hands for up to four years. Everything in this dossier flows from that act and from the two campaigns run under its cover.
