A shape that pits people against each other for what they need trains self-protection: when every hour and dollar is spoken for by your own held needs, other people's needs read as competition or as noise; exhaustion reads as apathy. On that hypothesis, the indifference is manufactured by the same conditions as the four factors above — not a separate character failure.

The plan-side note: the proposed shape's answer is already doctrinal — help when you can, never conscription; nobody is entitled to anybody's energy. A system that has to force help has just rebuilt the handle under a kinder name; the plan asks, it does not compel, and private time is entitled.

Working conditions — the worldwide readings

This plan is directed at the world, not one country. The run being read is the world's system; the United States readings above are one country's instruments — the closest to hand, not the boundary of the subject.

6. Work-related deaths and injuries, worldwide

Reading · What the work itself costs, counted across the world

The reading: nearly 3 million workers die every year from work-related accidents and diseases — 2.93 million in the ILO's estimate: 2.6 million from work-related diseases and 330,000 from work accidents (ILO, A Call for Safer and Healthier Working Environments, 2023; ILO news release, “Nearly 3 million people die of work-related accidents and diseases,” ilo.org). Beside it: an estimated 395 million workers suffer non-fatal work-related injuries each year (ILO estimates).

Its limit, at the same size: these are modelled global estimates built from national reporting of very uneven quality — many countries barely record work deaths at all, so the reading is an estimate of the scale, not a count, and if anything leans low where reporting is weakest.

7. Forced labour, worldwide

Reading · Work held by coercion, not by choice

The reading: an estimated 27.6 million people were in forced labour on any given day in 2021 (ILO, Walk Free and IOM, Global Estimates of Modern Slavery, 2022) — including 3.9 million in state-imposed forced labour; most of the rest imposed by private actors. Counted with forced marriage beside it (next card), the same report's total for modern slavery is 49.6 million people.

Debt bondage, inside this reading: “One-fifth of people in forced labour exploitation are in situations of debt bondage” (same report). The share belongs to the forced-labour-exploitation category, printed with its denominator exactly as the report gives it — and forms of coercion overlap: the same report puts systematic wage withholding at 36%, so coercion shares describe the same people more than once and are never added together.

Its limit, at the same size: a modelled prevalence estimate built from household surveys and case data, not a tally of identified cases; the method is debated among researchers, and the figure measures the shape's floor, not its ceiling.

8. Forced marriage, worldwide

Reading · The other half of modern slavery, counted beside forced labour

The reading: an estimated 22 million people were in forced marriage on any given day in 2021 (ILO, Walk Free and IOM, Global Estimates of Modern Slavery, 2022). With forced labour's 27.6 million, total modern slavery is 49.6 million people — the whole total, printed here in full.

Its limit, at the same size: survey-based estimates, not a count; a stock measure that accumulates, because in most cases a forced marriage is, in the report's words, “a life sentence.”

9. Child labour, worldwide

Reading · Children at work, counted across the world

The reading: 160 million children were in child labour at the beginning of 2020 — almost 1 in 10 of the world's children — and 79 million of them were in hazardous work (ILO and UNICEF, Child Labour: Global Estimates 2020).

The improvement, at the same size: the 2024 estimates put the figure at 138 million and falling again (ILO and UNICEF, Global Estimates 2024) — falling, and still 138 million children.

Its limit: survey-based estimates under a shared definition; national definitions and counting differ.

10. Child recruitment — children used by parties to conflict

Reading · At the far end of the child-labour material

The reading: 7,402 verified cases of recruitment and use of children by parties to conflict in 2024 (UN Secretary-General's Children and Armed Conflict reporting). Context from the same reporting: 41,370 grave violations against children were verified in 2024, the highest since monitoring began.

The improvement, at the same size: more than 16,000 children exited armed forces and groups in 2024 and received protection or reintegration support (UNICEF).

Its limit, at the same size: “verified” is a floor by construction — a case counts only when UN monitors can access and corroborate it; the true figure is higher by an amount nobody can state.

11. China — the 996 schedule and the state's own answer

Reading · A schedule, and the law's answer to it, in the same card

The practice: “996” — 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week, roughly 72 hours — took root in China's tech sector and spread as an expectation, far beyond China's legal overtime cap (36 hours of overtime a month).

The counterweight, at the same size: in August 2021 China's Supreme People's Court and the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security jointly published model overtime cases stating plainly that the 996 schedule violates the law — a worker fired for refusing it was owed compensation.

The honest tail: a ruling is not the same as a stopped practice — official Chinese statistics still showed average weekly working hours around 48.5 in 2025, above the legal standard, and reporting describes off-the-record overtime pressure surviving the ruling. Sources: the joint SPC/MOHRSS model cases of August 2021, as reported; Reuters reporting of official hours data (2025).

12. China — Foxconn, 2010

History · A documented episode from 2010, not a reading of today

The record: in 2010, at least 14 workers died by suicide at Foxconn's giant Shenzhen factory complex — most of them young migrant workers who jumped from dormitory and factory buildings — and the world's largest electronics supplier answered with large wage rises and anti-suicide nets hung on the buildings, which remain the enduring image of the episode.

Both readings of it, attributed, at the same size: the company and its defenders noted that, across a workforce in the hundreds of thousands, the rate was below China's national average; critics answered that a cluster of young workers dying by one method at one employer was itself the signal.

Labeled history: a documented episode from 2010, printed as the record of what the conditions produced then, not as a reading of today.

13. Xinjiang — a contested reading

Contested reading · Both sides carried, attributed; this page does not settle it

Side one, attributed: the UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights, in its assessment of August 31, 2022, concluded that serious human rights violations had been committed in Xinjiang in the context of counter-terrorism and counter-extremism policies, and that the extent of arbitrary and discriminatory detention “may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity”; allegations of forced labour were among those it said required investigation.

Side two, attributed at the same size: the Chinese government rejected the assessment outright — it published a lengthy rebuttal the same night, describes the centres as vocational education and training whose trainees have graduated, maintains its policies were lawful counter-terrorism and de-radicalisation, and called the assessment a product of misinformation manufactured by the United States and some Western forces.

The discipline: the record carries both, attributed; this page does not settle it.

14. Bangladesh — the Accord

Reading · The working-conditions set's documented-improvement instrument

The reading: the Rana Plaza collapse (Apr 24, 2013) killed 1,134 garment workers. The Accord that followed — now the International Accord, with ~300 brand signatories — has carried out more than 74,000 independent factory inspections; the remediation rate stands at 81%; more than 2.9 million workers have been trained in workplace safety; and more than 2,300 complaints have been resolved through its enforceable grievance mechanism (IndustriALL, 2026).

Unfinished, at the same size: hundreds of covered factories still have unremedied hazards, and deadly fires — including exits found locked — have continued to occur (UNI Global Union, 2026); the Bangladesh agreement runs to Dec 31, 2026 with renewal under negotiation.

Its limit: the Accord covers signatory brands' factories (2,000+), not the whole sector, and it is a safety instrument — it does not set wages.

15. The vacancy decomposition — United States

Reading · What “vacant” actually contains, broken open

The reading: 15,812,000 vacant units in total, of which only 4,930,000 were on the market (3,866,000 for rent + 1,064,000 for sale only); 6,509,000 were held off the market — a held-off stock larger than the entire on-market stock; 901,000 were rented or sold but not yet occupied; 3,471,000 were seasonal (US Census Bureau, Housing Vacancy Survey, Q3 2026, Release CB26-161 — the same release the Thriving page's ratio already rests on). This decomposition is the audit trail under the honest ~6.6:1 ratio, against the ~21:1 raw headline.

Its limit, at the same size: “held off market” is a residual category, not an accusation — it bundles renovation, probate and estate settlement, family holding and unfit units alongside any deliberate withholding; no US instrument separates the speculative share, and this page does not pretend to.

16. Institutional landlords — both halves or neither

Reading · The small national share and the concentrated local share, printed together

The reading: investors owning 1,000+ homes held roughly 3% of the national single-family rental stock — the small national share, printed first — and 25.0% of single-family rentals in Atlanta, 20.5% in Jacksonville, 18.3% in Charlotte (20-metro total 10.7%) (US GAO, GAO-24-106643, May 2024; data as of Jun 2022).

GAO's own caution, at the same size: its review of 74 studies found institutional investment may contribute to higher rents and prices where concentration is high, while evidence on crowding out buyers or behaving worse than other landlords is mixed and thin.

Its limit: no consistent definition of “institutional investor” exists across datasets, and subsidiary webs obscure ultimate ownership — measurement itself is contested.

17. Finland — Housing First

Reading · A reading about the run we're inside — a documented programme inside the current system whose outcome moved; not a counted test-run entry

The reading: Finland adopted Housing First as national policy in 2008 — a permanent home first, with the person's name on the lease, support services attached, instead of housing earned through shelter and treatment compliance. Homelessness has fallen ~30% since 2008 and long-term homelessness by more than 35% (government series); longer series put the fall from over 20,000 homeless people in 1985 to 3,806 in 2024. Finland is the only EU country with a sustained long-term decline.

The honest wobble, at the same size: in 2024 the count rose for the first time in 11 years (3,429 → 3,686 on the ARA single-person series) amid cost-of-living pressure and benefit cuts — a policy that must be kept, not a ratchet that locks.

Its limit: a small, wealthy, high-trust country of 5.5 million with a municipal housing stock — scale transfer is argued, not proven; North American trials reproduce the housing-retention result (~80%) with mixed health outcomes, and the housing result is the one claimed.
