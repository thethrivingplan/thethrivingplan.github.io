The Ruler's Test — Terrance Drew, Prime Minister of Saint Kitts and Nevis

Subject: Terrance Michael Drew — Prime Minister of Saint Kitts and Nevis since 6 August 2022. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Saint Kitts and Nevis is a constitutional monarchy whose Governor-General is head of state, while executive power — the government, the federal budget, and, in Drew's own holding of the Finance, National Security, Health and Citizenship by Investment portfolios, much of the state itself — sits with the prime minister. The premise was verified first: the seat has not changed hands. Drew led the Saint Kitts-Nevis Labour Party (SKNLP) to victory in the snap general election of 5 August 2022 — six of the eleven elected seats, 13,438 votes (45.75%), on a turnout recorded as 57.98% — defeating the incumbent Timothy Harris, whose dissolution of parliament on 11 May 2022 had triggered the early poll; Drew was sworn in the next day, 6 August 2022 (OAS Electoral Observation Mission preliminary report, 15 August 2022; SKNIS, 6 August 2022). He was still prime minister in November 2026, giving the Roundtable media sessions of 5 November 2026, the national address on the Citizenship by Investment (CBI) Programme aired 13 November 2026, and talks with a European Commission delegation on 12 November 2026 in that office (SKNIS, 6 and 16 November 2026). He also chaired CARICOM in the first half of 2026 and hosted its 50th Heads of Government meeting (SKNIS, 2026; Nevis News, 2026). One correction to the brief's likely-files list is recorded here at the top, because it changes the evidence: no state of emergency in 2025 — or at any point in his tenure — appears in the documented record assembled here. What the record shows instead is a 90-Day Campaign Against Crime and Violence launched 6 September 2024 under a public-health framing, run through a Citizen Security Secretariat, with no emergency powers documented (SKNIS, 6 September and 5 December 2024). The next general election is expected in 2027 (Inter-Parliamentary Union listing, via State Media Monitor, 2026). He is graded on a full, still-running tenure of just over four years, August 2022 to November 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic printed on the Test card, applied verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure: carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The good-governance suite of 2023 was written as one law for the whole public service, his own side included, and passed with bipartisan support: the Anti-Corruption Act, the Integrity in Public Life (Amendment) Act, the Freedom of Information (Amendment) Act, the Whistleblowers Protection Act and the Unauthorized Disclosure of Official Information Act (Government of Saint Kitts and Nevis, 2023; OAS General Assembly national statement, 2023). Director of Public Prosecutions Adlai Smith rated the Anti-Corruption Act the most comprehensive he had seen in the Caribbean, having compared Antigua, Grenada, Turks and Caicos, Barbados and Jamaica (Government statement via gov.kn, January 2024).
  • The integrity machinery was then switched on against office-holders generally, not aimed at the opposition: the Integrity Commission, chaired by retired judge Pearletta Lanns, is described by Attorney-General Garth Wilkin as "fully staffed, fully funded, and fully active," with annual income, asset and liability declarations filed by 161 public officials for 2023 and 174 for 2024 — senior civil servants, parliamentarians, ministers and board chairs named as the filing classes — and the Saint Kitts and Nevis standards aligned so, in Wilkin's words, "whether you sit in Basseterre or Charlestown, the standard for integrity remains the same" (SKNIS, 9–10 December 2025). The Federation acceded to the UN Convention Against Corruption on 7 August 2024 (SKNIS, December 2025).
  • On CBI — the programme where a second, cheaper law for favoured buyers had been the documented disease of the previous era — his administration criminalised the favour itself. The reforms from December 2022 onward made underselling a criminal offence with enforcement powers for the Director of Public Prosecutions, doubled minimum investment amounts, made the Citizenship by Investment Unit (CIU) a statutory corporation (Citizenship by Investment Unit Act, No. 11 of 2024, in force 1 October 2024) with its own Board of Governors and a dedicated due-diligence and anti-money-laundering compliance officer, imposed mandatory interviews on all applicants and dependants aged 16 and over, and created a Continuing International Due Diligence unit headquartered in Europe to monitor approved citizens on an ongoing basis (IMI Daily, 2024 and 2026; CIU Act reporting, 2024). In March 2024 he signed the regional Memorandum of Agreement with the other four Caribbean CBI states setting a common US$200,000 minimum floor, shared databases of rejected applicants and coordinated suspension of Russian and Belarusian applications — one price rule across five competing programmes, including his own (IMI Daily, February 2026).
  • The external referee confirmed the single standard had bite: on 24 February 2026 the US Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) formally rescinded Advisory FIN-2014-A004, its 2014 warning about abuse of the Saint Kitts and Nevis CBI programme — a rescission the government and the CIU's chairman attributed to three years of due-diligence, compliance and security reform (IMI Daily, February 2026; SKNIS, November 2026).
  • Faced with the MSR Media litigation over the inherited programme (below), Drew stated on the record that he was "prepared to take the necessary statutory steps […] and revoke citizenships obtained by fraud," with or without the lawsuit, and that his administration had instructed King's Counsel in the judicial-review claim seeking revocation of citizenships granted below the legal price under the Galaxy prison project (IMI Daily, May 2024). A Special Prosecutor was appointed to investigate the CBI programme (IMI Daily, 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The Special Sustainability Zones Authorisation Act, 2025 (Act No. 21 of 2025, passed August–September 2025) writes a second legal world into statute and hands its key to the prime minister personally. The Act "authorises and empowers the Prime Minister to enter into development agreements for the establishment of Special Sustainability Zones" (ECLAC Observatory on Principle 10, listing the Act, 2025). The Saint Kitts-Nevis Chamber of Industry and Commerce, in an 11-page letter to Drew dated 12 November 2025, called the Act "vague, general, and uncertain," said it creates "special autonomous zones with separate zone laws and bylaws and with incentives and powers given exclusively to zone developers," found "no legislated protections of important existing systems and laws," said it is "silent on the protection of many important existing statutory rights of citizens" and "does not adequately address the protection of the country's democracy," and asked for repeal or, failing that, suspension of any authorisation pending public consultation (Caribbean Media Corporation via Caribbean Times, November 2025). A law under which one class of developer may live under zone bylaws while everyone else lives under the Federation's laws is, on its face, the thing Test 1 grades — whatever safeguards are later bolted on (Test 3).
  • The first proposed zone shows the scale of the separate law: the Destiny project in Nevis, advanced by its developers under the SSZ framework, was described by the Chamber as contemplating "almost doubling the population of Nevis without any genuine debate on the implications" (CMC, November 2025). Former prime minister Timothy Harris denounced Destiny and the Act as a "state within a state" — an interested opponent's characterisation, recorded here as an ALLEGATION-level claim about effect, not a finding (JAM Radio News, 15 November 2025).
  • The inherited CBI record itself contains the era's clearest two-law allegation, and its handling under Drew is contested. In a US federal RICO suit filed in Florida in May 2024, MSR Media alleged that Caribbean Galaxy received 5,500 "shares" linked to the Estridge prison project, that the statutory price was US$175,000 per share, and that allocations were sold at US$75,000 or less — discounts of 60 percent or more (MSR Media filing, as reported by IMI Daily, May 2024, and SKN Source, December 2025). The defendants named include former prime ministers Timothy Harris and Denzil Douglas — Douglas being a sitting minister in Drew's own cabinet — and former CIU head Les Kahn. Every element of that filing is an ALLEGATION: no court has ruled, and this dossier states no verdict on any person named in it. MSR further alleged that it notified Drew and Attorney-General Wilkin of its material and received no answer, and expressed "disappointment" at a "complete lack of any action" — a claim disputed by Drew's public statements above and by his counter-allegation, also unproven and recorded as an ALLEGATION, that persons representing MSR's principal demanded US$40–100 million to withdraw the suit, which he labelled an "extortion scheme" (IMI Daily, May 2024). The documented residue for Test 1 is narrower than either side's rhetoric: the underselling era predates Drew, the criminalisation of underselling is his, and no completed prosecution or revocation arising from the prison-project file appears in the record assembled here.
  • The October 2024 CBI price cuts cut the other way from the one-price story: approved-development real estate fell from US$400,000 to US$325,000 and the private-home route from US$800,000 to US$600,000, months after the doubling that had made the programme the region's most expensive — a competitive adjustment made by regulation, openly published, but one that re-opened the price race the March 2024 regional floor was meant to close (Sovereo jurisdiction index, reviewed 1 November 2026; IMI Daily, 2024).

Why: In plain words: Drew inherited a programme in which, on the filed allegations, the real price of citizenship had quietly depended on who you bought it from, and he did the two things one law requires — he made the discount itself a crime and he put the unit that sells citizenship under a statutory board, outside checks and a regional floor. That is the strongest one-law record in this dossier. Against it stands a law he himself authored: the SSZ Act, which by design lets a zone live under its own bylaws on the prime minister's signature. One law cannot be the grade while the seat is simultaneously building a lawful door out of it.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — ordinary CBI applicants who paid the full legal price, whose citizenship is worth more when no cheaper back door exists; Kittitian and Nevisian citizens generally, whose passport's standing the FinCEN rescission of February 2026 partially restored; public officials' honest competitors, competing under published integrity rules. Hurt — applicants who, on the ALLEGATION in the MSR filing, were sold discounted citizenships now exposed to revocation proceedings; Nevis residents facing a proposed zone under separate bylaws they did not vote on; citizens whose statutory rights the Chamber found unprotected in the SSZ Act as passed.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The institutions that seated him were not his: the 5 August 2022 election was observed by the OAS, CARICOM and a local NGO coalition, the OAS mission was present in all eleven constituencies and all 128 polling stations, and it recorded an "orderly transfer of authority" from the government that had called the snap poll and lost it (OAS EOM preliminary report, 15 August 2022; SKNIS, 6 August 2022). The next election is listed for 2027 and no move against the electoral machinery appears in the record (State Media Monitor, 2026).
  • The country-level referees still score the Federation as free while he governs it: Freedom House's Freedom in the World 2026 rates Saint Kitts and Nevis 89/100, "Free," unchanged from 2025 — political rights 35/40, civil liberties 54/60 — and its 2025 report scored free and independent media at 4/4 (Freedom House, 2025 and 2026, via State Media Monitor). Reporters Without Borders does not rank the Federation separately; its combined OECS entry stood 57th of 180 in 2026 (RSF, 2026, via State Media Monitor).
  • Courts are used against his government, in the open, by name: MSR Media's judicial-review claim in the Kittitian High Court seeks a mandamus obliging the prime minister himself to revoke citizenships — litigation the government is defending through King's Counsel rather than suppressing (IMI Daily, May 2024). The prosecution service speaks in its own voice: it was the DPP, Adlai Smith, who publicly graded the anti-corruption legislation against regional peers (gov.kn, January 2024).
  • The opposition governs where it won. Nevis is run by the Concerned Citizens Movement under Premier Mark Brantley — who is also federal Leader of the Opposition — and Brantley criticises the prime minister's largest decisions, including the CBI wind-down, in national and international media without any documented interference (The Guardian, 17 November 2026). Private radio, television and digital outlets operate alongside the state broadcaster, and opposition parties publish freely, including the joint Eastern Caribbean opposition statement of 18 November 2026 demanding parliamentary answers on CBI (State Media Monitor, 2026; People's Action Movement publication, 19 November 2026).
  • Where the SSZ Act created a review layer, the bodies were staffed from outside government: the February 2026 Independent Oversight Body for the Destiny application is drawn from faith-based organisations, labour, business, financial-sector bodies, professional associations and heritage interests, is "distinct from, and operates independently of" the prime minister's own ad hoc committee, and must report in writing to Cabinet before any decision (SKNIS, 19 February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The seat itself is unusually concentrated in one pair of hands. Drew holds, simultaneously, the premiership and the portfolios for Finance, Health, National Security, Social Security and the Citizenship by Investment Programme (Nevis News, four-year review, 2026; SKNIS releases throughout). The prime minister who is also finance minister, national-security minister and minister for the citizenship programme is the referee of more of the board than the standard allows, even where no single act of interference is documented.
  • The state broadcaster has no structural independence. ZIZ Broadcasting Corporation — radio, television and digital — is classified State-Controlled by the State Media Monitor: government-owned, governed through a board overseeing major decisions, with "no publicly available ZIZ-specific statute or governance instrument" providing protected terms of office, an external nomination process or legally guaranteed editorial independence; the political opposition has long viewed ZIZ as a government mouthpiece (State Media Monitor, 2026 cycle; Veenendaal, Politics and Democracy in Microstates, 2015). In July 2026 the prime minister's own social-media amplification of ZIZ's crime post drew a documented local critique that the post omitted a rise in larceny — by then a third of all major crimes — and an uptick in violence, while the prime minister's caption presented violence as trending down (Ervin Welsh, ervues.wordpress.com, November 2026). The critique is one commentator's analysis, recorded as such; the structural point it rests on — a state broadcaster with no editorial-independence law — is the Monitor's, not his.
  • The Media Association of Saint Kitts and Nevis, in its 2026 World Press Freedom Day statement, said press freedom was largely upheld while cautioning that particular incidents demonstrated the fragility of those rights — the local profession's own wording, neither a clean bill nor a charge sheet (MASKN statement, 2026, via State Media Monitor).
  • The SSZ Act centralises by design: authorisation power over zones with their own laws sits with the prime minister personally (Act No. 21 of 2025, via ECLAC Observatory). The corrective bodies of 2026 (Test 3) are advisory and oversight bodies created by the same executive; they review, but the signature remains his.

Why: In plain words: nobody in this record has been able to show Drew punishing a referee — elections ran and removed his predecessor, courts entertain suits against him, the opposition runs Nevis and says what it likes, and the freedom scores did not move on his watch. But holding without gripping is also about how much of the board one hand rests on, and his rests on a lot: five portfolios including finance, security and citizenship, a state broadcaster with no independence law, and a zones act keyed to his own signature. The referees are free; the seat is heavy.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — voters, whose 2022 verdict was executed in full and whose 2027 election stands scheduled; the Nevis Island Administration and the federal opposition, who govern and criticise unimpeded; litigants and journalists working in a country still rated Free at 89/100. Hurt — audiences of the state broadcaster, who receive state media without any statutory guarantee it is not the government's voice; future holders of every office Drew currently combines, who inherit the precedent that one person can hold them all; applicants and citizens whose zone, citizenship and security files all terminate at the same desk.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • The SSZ wobble is the clearest adjustment in the file. The Act was passed in August–September 2025 and met immediate, organised pushback: the Chamber of Industry and Commerce's 11-page letter of 12 November 2025 asking for repeal or suspension, and a former prime minister's public campaign against Destiny. Drew did not double down. He established an ad hoc Advisory Committee — Christian Council, Evangelical Association, Chamber, Trades and Labour Union, bankers, credit unions, the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank among those invited, with written opinions also sought from the OECS, the OECS Bar Association and the Caribbean Development Bank — to review the Act itself and recommend amendments (CMC via Caribbean Times, December 2025; SKNIS, 12 January 2026). On 21 January 2026 he said publicly, "I will go back to Parliament as much as I have to strengthen the law," and described the framework as creating no zone by itself (SKNIS, 22 January 2026). In February 2026 the separate Independent Oversight Body began reviewing the Destiny application before any approval, with the government stating expressly that creating the body "does not signal approval" (SKNIS, 19 February 2026). By July 2026 the federal government had written to Premier Brantley identifying four major federal issues with the application, met the Nevis Island Administration on 23 July 2026, and recorded that "no final decision has yet been made" and that the process "had already produced meaningful revisions to what was first put forward" (SKNIS via The Labour Spokesman, 23 July 2026). The wobble was answered with process, delay and stated constitutional limits — "The Constitution is supreme. No agreement, and no development, will be permitted to displace the Constitution" (Drew, March–July 2026 statements).
  • The CBI file is a decade-long wobble answered in stages, each stage openly owned. When he took office in August 2022, Drew states — and the FinCEN advisory then still in force corroborates the exposure — the Federation was "on the brink of losing that precious visa-free access [to the EU and the UK]" (Drew national address, reported by IMI Daily, 2024). He chose reform first: the 2022–2024 integrity rebuild (Test 1), pursued through the February 2023 Six Principles agreed with the United States and the March 2024 five-state Memorandum of Agreement. When reform produced a revenue collapse, he published the collapse himself: CBI earnings of EC$669 million in 2022 and EC$620 million in 2023 falling to EC$218 million through September 2024, a decline of roughly 60 percent he attributed in a national address to his own reforms (Drew, via IMI Daily, 2024). When the legal ground then moved under the reformed programme — Regulation (EU) 2025/2441, in force from 30 December 2025, making the mere operation of an investor-citizenship scheme a ground for suspending Schengen visa-free access, followed by the European Commission's June 2026 letter inviting a wind-down — if another option becomes available, the test is whether he at least considered it openly. He did: the government formally conveyed its decision to the EU, negotiated a transition timeline rather than accepting or defying a date in silence, and Drew explained the decision, its banking rationale and its limits in a national address aired 13 November 2026, stating that the programme would continue under strict vetting during the transition, that no citizen's status changed that night, and that the final application date would be announced once agreed (SKNIS, 16 November 2026; Caribbean360, November 2026).
  • On the no-good-choice mechanics, the CBI wind-down is recorded as such a situation, and is not held against him. The options that actually existed in November 2026 were: (a) keep the programme and risk suspension of Schengen visa-free travel for every Kittitian and Nevisian passport holder under the December 2025 EU rules, plus the correspondent-banking withdrawal Drew identified — "Our banks depend on banks in the United States, Canada, and Europe to move money in and out of this country" — threatening remittances, supplier payments and card services for households and businesses who never touched CBI (SKNIS, 16 November 2026); or (b) wind the programme down and lose, permanently, a revenue stream that had already fallen from 25.8 percent of GDP in 2022 to 6.0 percent in 2025 on IMF figures (IMF, 2026 Article IV). Each door carried real harm to people who did not choose the trap. The trap's origin is partly his predecessors' underselling era and partly the EU's rule change; the part of the origin that is his — the 2023 price doubling that accelerated the revenue fall before the EU moved — is recorded in Test 5. Inside the trap, he was honest about it on national television, chose the door that protected the many passport-holders and bank users over the fewer programme beneficiaries, and negotiated time rather than accepting an abrupt end.
  • The crime wobble was answered with a change of method rather than a state of emergency. After 28 homicides in 2024, the government reframed violence as a public-health problem, launched the 90-Day Campaign on 6 September 2024 with opposition parties, churches, law enforcement and civil society in the same room, and followed it with the "Beyond the 90 Days" programme in February 2025 — explicitly rejecting the predecessor-era cash-for-peace model in Drew's words: "You can't pay for peace. It is a fallacy" (SKNIS, September 2024–February 2025). Police figures then recorded a 19 percent fall in reported crime across the campaign quarters, zero homicides in the first quarter of 2025, and six homicides to mid-December 2025 against 28 in all of 2024 — a 78 percent reduction on the Royal St. Christopher and Nevis Police Force figures the government published (SKNIS, 5 December 2024; SKNIS, 16 December 2025). The homicide counts are printed as sourced: the government's December 2025 budget-address figure is six to date in 2025; a government-style infographic circulated at year-end said seven for the full year (SKNIS, December 2025; St. Kitts Nevis Riddim post, 1 January 2026). They are not blended.

Evidence against:

  • The SSZ adjustment, however genuine, was consultation after enactment. The Chamber's core complaint was sequence: a law "rushed through Parliament" (Harris's phrase, an opponent's) and, on the Chamber's own more measured wording, a first zone contemplated "without any genuine debate on the implications" — the review bodies, the parliamentary return and the oversight machinery all date from after the Act was already law (CMC, November 2025; JAM Radio News, 15 November 2025). Considering the other door once the street is already pushing is more than denial; it is less than considering it first.
  • The CBI reforms' first iteration was itself a wobble handled roughly, on the industry's documented account: NTL Trust's chief executive attributed the revenue collapse to "sudden changes" "rushed through without sufficient forethought or consultation," adding that "while the intention might have been good, the implementation was disastrous" — an interested industry voice, recorded as such (IMI Daily, 2024). The October 2024 price cuts (Test 1) were the government's own partial reversal of its doubling within roughly a year.
  • On the wind-down itself, the parliamentary door was used late. The joint statement of Eastern Caribbean opposition leaders of 18 November 2026 — signed for Saint Kitts and Nevis by People's Action Movement leader Shawn Richards — complained of "the lack of clear public explanations after the November 12 deadline," and demanded immediate parliamentary statements, a comprehensive financial assessment and audit, and a credible transition plan developed with stakeholders (joint statement, 18 November 2026, via PAM publication). Drew's explanation came by national address and Roundtable, not first to the Assembly; Brantley, for Nevis, said he did "not support the winding down of the program" and had seen no corresponding EU assistance offer, calling the 1 June 2028 date the EU proposed to the region "frankly ludicrous" (The Guardian, 17 November 2026). The 1 June 2028 date is the EU's regional proposal as reported for Antigua and Barbuda's letter; for Saint Kitts and Nevis no final date had been agreed or published at grading (Caribbean360, November 2026).

Why: In plain words: three times the ground moved under this government — a zones law its own business community rejected, a citizenship programme first reformed, then priced out, then outlawed in effect by its biggest market, and a homicide count that made the country a regional byword — and each time the response on record is adjustment in the open: review bodies with his critics inside them, his own revenue collapse read out by himself on television, a negotiated rather than defiant or silent exit from CBI, and a crime strategy that changed the method instead of suspending the rules. The deductions are for sequence, not direction: the consultation on SSZ followed the law, and Parliament heard about the CBI end after the country did. Sequence costs trust; it does not, on this record, reverse the grade.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — SSZ critics and the Chamber, whose repeal demand was not met but whose suspension demand effectively was, no zone having been authorised by November 2026; every ordinary passport holder and bank customer, whose Schengen access and correspondent banking the wind-down negotiation is designed to protect; residents of high-violence communities, on the police figures for 2025. Hurt — CBI-sector workers and applicants caught mid-transition, with the CIU staff's redeployment to investment promotion promised but the programme's end certain (Caribbean Today, November 2026); Nevis's government, presented with a federal decision on the Federation's largest revenue source that its premier publicly does not support; investors and agents who priced their plans on the 2024 rules and met two reversals in two years.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Nevis, the poor, low-wage and STEP workers, the young, and those who did not elect him — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The cost-of-living accounting was aimed at, and reached, roughly half the country by name and number. In 2025 the Budget Boost Wallet paid EC$36.6 million in direct assistance to approximately 23,000 beneficiaries for utility bills and basic food, in a federation of roughly 48,000 people; the standard VAT rate was cut from 17 percent to 13 percent for the first six months of 2025; more than EC$80 million had gone into electricity subsidies by June 2026, with cooking-gas subsidies holding a 20-pound cylinder at EC$30; and the April 2026 package halved the gasoline excise tax (EC$1.95 to EC$0.98 per gallon) and the Customs Service Charge on gasoline (6 percent to 3 percent), and zero-rated solar equipment for tax through 2026 (SKNIS, 14 April and 6 November 2026). Civil servants received an 8 percent salary increase and an additional month's salary annually from 2022 through 2025 (SKNIS, November 2026).
  • Low-wage workers were counted in cash, twice: two minimum-wage increases brought the floor to at least EC$500 per week for a 40-hour week, which the government describes as the highest in the independent OECS, and pay for Skills Training and Empowerment Programme (STEP) participants was raised to the same EC$500 per week, with EC$54 million invested in STEP between January and October 2026 sustaining about 2,400 participants (SKNIS, 5–6 November 2026).
  • The young and the sick were counted from the first week: tuition at Clarence Fitzroy Bryant College was made free for citizens and a Children's Medical Fund established for overseas emergency treatment for children from disadvantaged families, both announced at the cabinet swearing-in in August 2022; the ASPIRE children's savings programme followed (SKNIS and four-year reviews, 2022–2026). Housing delivery, on the National Housing Corporation's own count, passed 260 homes built by the end of 2025, with 50 more built in 2026 and 102 under construction at September 2026 — printed separately from the government's broader "housing solutions" figure of 800-plus, which blends NHC builds, first-time-buyer initiatives and bank financing and is not the same count (NHC via SKNIS, 10 September 2026; Drew statements, 2026).
  • Nevis is inside the Federation's two biggest forward projects rather than outside them: the Nevis geothermal development — about 30 megawatts at Hamilton, backed by roughly US$71.6 million in international financing (about US$51.6 million from the Green Climate Fund, Caribbean Development Bank and Inter-American Development Bank, plus US$20 million from the Saudi Fund for Development) — is planned from the start with an inter-island electricity link so power generated in Nevis serves Saint Kitts, with the World Bank considering financing the interconnection (Nevis Island Administration, 10 November 2026; Book Nevis summary of NIA reporting, October 2026). The desalination plant commissioned in February 2026 (two million gallons per day, solar-powered) and the identification of 17 potential new groundwater sources address water insecurity on the larger island (SKNIS, 25 September 2026).
  • The crime reduction, if the police figures hold, counts most for those who were being counted out by violence: homicides fell from 28 in 2024 to six-to-date at mid-December 2025 on police figures, with the Elevate programme moving former gang members and at-risk youth into skills, employment and business as a named part of the method (SKNIS, 16 December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The bill for counting everyone in the present is being sent to everyone in the future, on the IMF's numbers. With CBI fees down to 6.0 percent of GDP in 2025 from 25.8 percent in 2022, the overall fiscal deficit widened to 11.7 percent of GDP in 2025 and public debt reached 58.4 percent of GDP, projected by the Fund to cross the 60 percent regional benchmark in 2026 and reach 68.7 percent by 2027 on current policies; the Fund records "contingent liabilities from public banks and the Social Security Fund" as significant risks and prescribes a front-loaded consolidation of expenditure rationalisation (4.4 points of GDP) and revenue mobilisation (2.7 points) to stabilise debt at the benchmark by 2031 (IMF, 2026 Article IV, concluded 7 May 2026). The VAT relief itself expired on schedule: the standard rate returned to 17 percent on 1 July 2025 (Inland Revenue Department, via La Caribeña News, November 2026).
  • Nevis counts itself partially counted out of the Federation's defining decision. Its premier — the federal opposition leader — does not support the CBI wind-down, says the country "was given very little choice," warns the programme's end "threatens its economy and ability to support people in need," and notes CBI's contribution to water, education and healthcare on his island (The Guardian, 17 November 2026). Whatever the merits (Test 3's no-good-choice finding stands), the smaller island's elected government is on record that the accounting was done to it, not with it.
  • The Destiny file counts Nevisians as a population to be doubled, on the Chamber's reading, before it counts them as a public to be consulted (CMC, November 2025). The federal review machinery of 2026 (Test 3) is the government's answer; the answer postdates the Act.
  • The geothermal promise, the Federation's main post-CBI asset, is not yet electricity for anyone: the Iceland Drilling contract was signed in May 2026 with drilling then expected to begin by September 2026; by 10 November 2026 civil works at Hamilton were still in preparation, the rig was ready but not dispatched, some twelve containers of equipment had arrived, and Premier Brantley stated, "I do not have a date that I can give you" (NIA, 10 November 2026; Book Nevis, October 2026). The delay sits primarily with the Nevis Island Administration as project lead and with multinational funders' processes, which Brantley himself cites — recorded here as a shared federal-Nevis file, not Drew's alone — but the Sustainable Island State accounting Drew presents nationally includes this plant, and at grading it has produced no power.

Why: In plain words: the transfers, the wage floor, the subsidies and the free tuition are real, numbered and unusually broad for a state this small — when 23,000 of 48,000 people receive a payment, "everyone" is close to literal. What pulls the grade down is the other half of counting: the deficit and debt path the IMF prints means tomorrow's Kittitians and Nevisians are being counted as payers without being asked, and Nevis's own elected government says it was counted as an afterthought in the two largest decisions of the term — the zones law and the end of CBI.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — approximately 23,000 Budget Boost recipients; minimum-wage and STEP workers at EC$500 per week; civil servants; CFBC students and their families; children needing overseas treatment; the 260-plus families in NHC homes; households shielded from fuel and electricity prices. Hurt — future taxpayers and Social Security contributors carrying a debt path the IMF projects through the regional ceiling; Nevis residents whose premier opposes the CBI end and whose island faces the Destiny proposal; consumers who lost the 13 percent VAT rate when it expired in July 2025; CBI-dependent workers and businesses whose income the wind-down ends.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 6 August 2022, with dates; the two axes — word-keeping and helped/hurt — graded independently.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The platform itself. The SKNLP campaigned in 2022 on transforming the Federation into a Sustainable Island State built on seven pillars — in the form Drew stated them through 2023: food security, green energy transition, economic diversification, sustainable industries, the creative economy, COVID-19 recovery, and social protection (SKNIS, July 2024, restating the 2022 platform; Labour Spokesman, January 2023). Under the mandate rule, governing on that platform is not a demerit; the word-keeping question is delivery, pillar by pillar, below. (A later, different seven-pillar list circulates in three-year retrospectives — job creation, health care, education, housing, food security, environment, culture (Writeups24, 2025) — and the Sustainable Island State Agenda's own 2026 presentation lists water security first (SKNIS, July 2026). The lists are printed as sourced, not harmonised; the drift in the pillar wording over the term is itself a small word-keeping datum.)
  • Good governance — delivered as legislated. The 2022 promise to operationalise the integrity framework was kept in 2023: the five-act suite passed, the Integrity Commission staffed and funded, declarations rising from 161 filers (2023) to 174 (2024), UNCAC accession on 7 August 2024 (SKNIS, December 2025).
  • CBI integrity — the reform word kept, at cost to himself. He promised to restore the programme's good name; the statutory CIU, criminalised underselling, mandatory interviews, the regional floor, ECCIRA — the Eastern Caribbean Citizenship by Investment Regulatory Authority, agreed by the five OECS CBI states in September 2025, headquartered in Grenada, with the Saint Kitts and Nevis National Assembly passing its implementing bill on 17 October 2025 — and the FinCEN rescission of 24 February 2026 are the documented delivery (IMI Daily, 2026; Sovereo index, 2026). This is crossing 1 in part: word kept, and the country's standing helped — while the same keeping cut the revenue the budget ran on, which is crossing 2's territory and is recorded in full in the against shelf rather than netted off.
  • Social protection — delivered in cash. Free CFBC tuition and the Children's Medical Fund from August 2022; two minimum-wage increases to EC$500 per week; the 8 percent civil-service increase and extra month's salary 2022–2025; Budget Boost's EC$36.6 million to some 23,000 people in 2025 (SKNIS, 2022–2026).
  • Water — substantially delivered. The two-million-gallon-per-day solar-powered desalination plant was commissioned in February 2026, with 24/7 water access across the Federation pledged by the end of 2026 — a date still open at grading (SKNIS, September 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The CBI "saved" word, overtaken. Through 2024 the government's public position was that reform had saved the programme and preserved the Federation's visa-free access. In November 2026 the same government announced the programme's end. The break is recorded plainly, with its cause: the EU's December 2025 rule change and June 2026 letter created a situation that did not exist when the earlier assurance was given, and the wind-down decision is graded under Test 3's no-good-choice mechanics, not as a simple broken promise. On the two axes, independently: the word-axis records a reversal of a stated position; the outcome-axis records that the reversal was made to protect every citizen's travel and banking access (helped) at the price of the sector's income (hurt). Opposition voices go further and allege he misled the country — Times Caribbean's November 2026 analysis asked whether Drew "mislead St. Kitts and Nevis about CBI" and demanded an apology for the 2024 "saved" declaration (Times Caribbean, 7 November 2026). That is an ALLEGATION by a critical outlet, not a finding; the documented facts are the two statements and the intervening EU rule change, and they are printed above.
  • Green energy — the flagship is late. Geothermal has been the centrepiece of the energy pillar since 2022. Four years in: financing of about US$71.6 million assembled, a drilling contract signed in May 2026, a September 2026 drilling start expected at signing — and, at November 2026, no drilling, no revised date, civil works still under way (NIA, 10 November 2026). The 50-megawatt solar farm on Saint Kitts remains at procurement stage (SKNIS, March and September 2026). The pillar is in motion; its promise of cheaper power from the Federation's own ground is not yet kept, and the slippage is shared with the Nevis Island Administration and the funders (Test 4).
  • The Sovereign Wealth Fund — promised early, still planned. In August 2023 Drew said the government intended to create a sovereign wealth fund to safeguard the financial future, alongside a revenue-sharing study between Saint Kitts and Nevis (Labour Spokesman, 5 August 2023). In May 2026 the IMF was still recommending that the authorities "implement the planned Sovereign Wealth Resilience Fund" (IMF, 2026 Article IV). Three years from statement to still-planned is a receding word, recorded as such.
  • Diversification — the direction kept, the destination not reached. Drew's own November 2026 figures concede the shape of it: CBI was "almost 70 percent" of the budget when he took office, is aimed below 20 percent in 2026 and below 10 percent in 2027 — achieved substantially by the revenue's collapse (25.8 to 6.0 percent of GDP, IMF) rather than yet by replacement industries at scale, with tourism the documented bright spot (stayover arrivals up 6 percent to just under 170,000 in 2025; the 2025–26 cruise season passing 918,000 passengers, the highest since 2019) (SKNIS, 5 November 2026; IMF, 2026; Waverley Gateway Guide, October 2026). A critic's version — that diversification talk is "empty rhetoric" without specifics on replacing CBI revenue — is recorded as opposition commentary, not adopted (Labour Party page comment summary, November 2026).
  • The four crossings, named as the method requires: (1) Kept his word AND it helped people — the governance suite, free tuition, the wage floor, Budget Boost. (2) Kept his word AND it still hurt people — the CBI integrity reform: promised, delivered, and by his own published figures it cut programme revenue by roughly 60 percent, widening the deficit to 11.7 percent of GDP in 2025 and squeezing the state that funds the social promises; the keeping earns the credit here and the hurt stands in Tests 4 and 5 in full. (3) Went against his word AND it helped people — the CBI wind-down reverses the "saved" position while protecting visa-free travel and banking access for all citizens; the break is recorded above and the help is recorded with it, neither laundering the other. (4) Broke his word AND it hurt people — not documented as a clean instance in the record assembled here: the geothermal and sovereign-wealth delays are words receding rather than reversed, and their hurt is delay, not yet loss. Where the record does not document a crossing, this dossier records "not documented" rather than inventing one.

Why: In plain words: the small promises were kept in cash and statute — wages, tuition, declarations, a desalination plant — and the biggest promise, a sustainable island state no longer living off selling citizenship, is being kept in the hardest possible way, by actually ending the programme, after a reform that cost his own budget more than any opponent could have. Against that stand a "saved" declaration the EU's rule change turned into a reversal, a geothermal plant four years promised and not yet drilled, and a sovereign wealth fund still at the planning stage three years after it was announced. The word is neither clean nor broken; it is kept where it was cheap, strained where it was expensive, and — on CBI — changed in the open with the reason stated.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — students, wage-earners, public officials working under clear integrity rules, citizens whose passport regained FinCEN's clearance in February 2026, and households reached by the social-protection deliveries. Hurt — the treasury and everyone funded by it, by the revenue cost of the integrity word kept; CBI workers and applicants, by the "saved" word reversed; Nevis and Saint Kitts electricity users, by every year the geothermal word stays undrilled; savers in the future the unbuilt sovereign wealth fund was meant to protect.
