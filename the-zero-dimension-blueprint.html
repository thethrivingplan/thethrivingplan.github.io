Why zero is the only

Travel normally means crossing the gap between here and there. Inside a point there is no gap to cross. Step into the point and — in this concept — you have not moved across space at all.

03 — Size, considered

Has size been considered
as a dimension?

Yes — considered, studied, and measured for centuries. The honest answer has two parts that are both true at once, and this sheet keeps them separate.

The short answer: In standard physics, size is not counted as a dimension of its own. A dimension is an independent direction you can move along — length, width, height, time. Size is how far a thing extends along those directions. But scale — how big you are — changes which physical forces run your life. So an ant, a human, and a giant do not just occupy the same world at different sizes. In every way that matters day to day, they live in different worlds. Fact — measured

Fig. 02 — The same world, at three sizes (not to linear scale — the gaps are too large)

Ant · ~5 mm
Human · ~1.7 m
Elephant · ~3 m
Giant · story only

A human is roughly 340 ants long. A 10× human “giant” is not just a bigger human — see the square–cube law below. That is why the giant stays on the Fiction side of this figure.

Try the three sizes. Same planet, same gravity, same water — different world each time:

What runs the world

Surface forces win — surface tension, stickiness, and air resistance outweigh gravity at this size.

What a fall does

A fall from any height is usually harmless. Terminal velocity is so low that an ant walks away from a drop that would kill a human.

What water is like

Water is thick and dangerous — a droplet can trap or drown. Rain is not weather; it is falling boulders of sticky liquid.

The square–cube law — why the giant breaks

Galileo worked this out in 1638. Make something twice as tall and its weight grows eight times (2³, volume) while the cross-section of its bones and muscles grows only four times (2², area). Double the size, double the stress on every bone. A 10× human would weigh 1,000× as much on bones only 100× as thick — and would overheat, because heat is made by volume but lost only through surface. Real large animals answer with thick legs, big ears, and slow lives: elephants are the redesign, not a scaled-up human.

Why the ant seems super-strong

Muscle strength follows cross-section area (²), weight follows volume (³). Shrink, and strength shrinks slower than weight — so an ant can carry many times its own body weight, not because its muscles are magic, but because it is small. J. B. S. Haldane's 1926 essay On Being the Right Size laid this whole ladder out: for every animal, there is a most convenient size — and a different world that comes with it.

Where size is treated like a dimension

Three real places. (1) Extra dimensions in string theory are hidden by their size — curled near the Planck length, far below an atom; make one large and we would notice it, so a dimension's size decides whether it counts in daily life. (2) Fractals carry fractional dimensions (a coastline measures between 1D and 2D) that change with the size of your ruler. (3) In physics, “scaling dimension” measures how a thing changes as you zoom in or out — scale itself, handled as a working axis.

Why? →

Why does size change the world?

Because the forces do not scale evenly. Area grows with the square, volume with the cube — so changing size changes which force is in charge.

How? →

How does it touch the hub?

The hub is a point with no size at all. If scale changes the world, a hub with zero scale raises a new question: at what size do you come out — and in whose world? That is Open How № 07, labeled unknown.

04 — Species, considered

Has species been considered
as a dimension?

Dog to cat to human to rhinoceros — to tree. Not a change of size this time. A change of life: different senses, different body, different needs, different dangers, different span of years. The question is whether changing the kind of life changes the world as surely as changing the place.

The short answer: In standard physics, species is not counted as a dimension either. A dimension is an independent direction you can move along; a species is not a direction. But biology and philosophy have treated kind-of-life as world-making for over a century: the same field, at the same moment, is a scent-map to a dog, a hunting-ground of edges and movement to a cat, a named and built place to a human, a grazing territory written in smell to a rhinoceros, and a slow contest for light to a tree. Same ground. Five lives. Five worlds that barely overlap. Fact — documented

All five can stand in one field at one moment — sharing every coordinate physics can give — and not one of them is having the same day.

Step into each life. Same planet, same field — a drastically different life each time:

What leads

Smell, far ahead of everything else. A dog carries up to roughly 300 million scent receptors — a human has about 6 million — and hears pitches well above human hearing. The nose is not a sense among senses; it is the headline.

What the world is

A map written in scent, including the past: who was here, how long ago, which way they went, how they felt. A dog reads a lamppost the way you read a noticeboard — and the notices linger after their writers have gone.

A life lived this way

A social, pack-shaped life of about 10–13 years, lived close to the ground and close to other beings. Loyalty, play, and routine are not decorations on this life. They are its structure.

Umwelt — every species in its own world

Biologist Jakob von Uexküll named it in the early 1900s (his Umwelt work, 1909–1934): each animal lives inside its own perceptual world, built from only the signals its body can pick up and act on. His famous tick waits years for one signal — the smell of a mammal's skin — and that signal is nearly its whole world. The meadow is one place physically, and as many worlds as there are kinds of life in it.

The niche is literally drawn in dimensions

In 1957, ecologist G. Evelyn Hutchinson defined a species' niche as a volume in a space of many axes at once — temperature range, food size, humidity, and more — an “n-dimensional hypervolume.” Ecology already plots kind-of-life on dimensional graph paper: change the species and you change the volume, the shape of a life in the space of everything it needs and tolerates.

It is not just the senses — it is the stakes

A mouse in the field is food for the cat; the cat is a rival and a danger to nothing in the field except the mouse; the tree is shade to the rhino and a home to a thousand others. Species decides what counts as food, threat, mate, shelter, and home — the whole table of what things mean — not only what can be sensed. That is why these lives differ drastically, and not just in detail.

Why? →

Why does species change the world?

Because a body is a filter and a set of needs. It admits a thin slice of everything that is happening, and assigns the meanings — food, danger, home — that turn signals into a life.

05 — The capstone · The question underneath size & species

Is a being's experience
its own dimension?

“What I'm getting at is — is a being's experience its own dimension?”
— The owner's question, in his words

This is the question the size question and the species question were both walking toward. An ant and a giant differ in scale. A dog and a tree differ in kind. But underneath both is one difference: what it is like to be them. This section grades that question the way this sheet grades everything — each shelf labeled, nothing dressed up.

Physics says no. In physics, a dimension is a coordinate of spacetime — one of the independent numbers you need to say where and when something is: three of space, one of time, and in the unproven theories, a few more curled too small to see. Experience is not one of those coordinates. Nothing in the equations takes “what it feels like” as an axis, and no experiment has ever measured along one. As a claim about physics, the answer is settled: no. Physics — settled usage

And the question does not die there. Perception science and philosophy give it real standing — not as a spacetime coordinate, but as a space of its own, with its own structure, that science already maps and cannot yet explain. The five shelves below are that standing, each labeled for exactly what it is. Open question — not settled fact

Umwelt — the experienced world is a real object of study

Uexküll's Umwelt (§04) is not a metaphor biologists tolerate; it is a working tool. Each species' experienced world can be described, compared, and predicted from its senses and needs — a tick's world, a dog's world, a bat's world, each closed, each complete for the life inside it. Two beings at the same coordinates are, measurably, in different experienced worlds.

Nagel, 1974 — “What is it like to be a bat?”

Philosopher Thomas Nagel's famous paper argues that even a complete physical account of a bat — every neuron, every echo — leaves out the thing itself: what echolocation is like from inside. Experience has a point of view, and a point of view is not a coordinate. You could know a being's position on every physical axis and know nothing about where it sits on this one.

Every sense already forms its own space

This part is plain measurement. Colour alone is a three-dimensional solid — hue, saturation, brightness — and every colour you can experience is a point inside it; dogs, with two colour channels to our three, live in a smaller colour solid. Pitch, loudness, and timbre do the same for hearing; smell has its own vast space of possible scents. Experience is already charted in dimensions — dimensions of possible experience, not of spacetime. The distinction stays printed: same word, different shelf.

Integrated information theory — experience as a shape in a vast space

One serious current theory goes further. Integrated information theory (Giulio Tononi and colleagues) models each experience as a shape — a structure in a very high-dimensional space of possible states — where the shape is the experience: its geometry is what the experience feels like. On this theory, “where a being is” in experience-space is as definite as where it is in space. Labeled honestly: this is a theory — worked out in mathematics, actively researched, contested, and not settled science.

The hard problem — labeled plainly: unsolved

Underneath all of it sits the hard problem of consciousness (named by David Chalmers, 1995): why does physical processing — light on a retina, chemicals in a nose, neurons firing — feel like anything at all from inside? Science maps which brain states go with which experiences with growing precision. Why there is an inside to begin with, it does not yet explain. This sheet labels it the way the record labels it: unsolved — not a gap being hidden, a gap stated at full size.

The sheet's line: the four dials — WHEN, WHICH WORLD, WHAT SIZE, WHAT KIND OF LIFE — all point at this one axis. Turn any of them and what changes most is not the coordinates. It is the experience. The physical world is shared; the experienced world never is. Two beings can share every coordinate physics can give — same place, same moment, same field — and still not share a world. Whether that makes experience a dimension is, as physics uses the word, answered: no. Whether it is an axis along which lives genuinely differ, as real as any on this sheet: that is the concept's open question, and it stays on the sheet labeled exactly that — open, not settled, and not a no.

Why? →

Why does this question keep arriving?

Because every dial on this sheet, followed honestly, ends at experience. Size matters because it changes what a life feels like from inside. Species matters for the same reason. The dials were always pointing here.

How? →

How would the hub move along it?

Unknown — the deepest unknown on the sheet. A hub that changed only your coordinates would leave the axis untouched; a hub that moved you along it would change the experiencer, not just the trip. That is Open How № 09.

06 — The multiverse, considered

That's the multiverse, right?

“That's the multiverse, right? If you one being could experience so many different things just based off their choices. And you know the variables that we can't control.”
— The owner's framing, in his words

The word “multiverse” carries more than one picture, and this sheet keeps them on separate shelves — because the owner's picture is not quite either of the two physics pictures, and the difference is the interesting part.

Shelf one — Many-worlds: the branching lives Interpretation · Unproven

In 1957, physicist Hugh Everett III proposed an interpretation of quantum mechanics: whenever an event has more than one possible outcome, the universe does not pick one — it branches, and all outcomes happen, each in its own branch, each branch experiencing one path. No collapse, no discarded possibilities: every version continues, unable to see the others. This is a serious, widely held interpretation among physicists — and it is labeled here exactly as it stands: an interpretation, consistent with the mathematics, chosen by many, and unproven. No experiment has ever confirmed a branch.

Shelf two — Worlds as far-away places Theories · Unproven

A different picture wears the same word. In cosmic inflation theories, other universes are places — regions inflated so far away that no signal can ever reach us. In brane pictures (§01's cousin), other worlds are other membranes floating in a higher-dimensional space: near in the extra dimensions, unreachable in ours. Here a “world” is somewhere — distant, separate, located. Kept on its own shelf because it is a different claim from branching: these worlds are not versions of your life. They are other real estate.

Shelf three — the owner's picture, labeled as the concept's own: the multiverse as the full set of one being's possible experienced lives. Not far-away places, and not only quantum events: the branching runs on choices — the turn taken and every turn not taken, each one a life that being could have experienced — and on the variables we can't control: other people's choices, chance, accidents, where and when you were born, what the weather did that day. Every life on this shelf is the same being, at the same starting point, living out one path through everything that could have gone otherwise. It stands closest to many-worlds in shape — branches, all of them lived — but its branching rule is a life, not a particle. The concept's own framing

The hub consequence: the zero point has no distance inside it, and on this sheet it touches every when and every world at zero distance (§01–§02). If every branch of a being touches the zero point the same way, then every version of that being is the same distance from the hub — zero. No version is the far one. No version is the near one. The life you lived and every life you could have lived stand, from the hub, at exactly the same remove: none. Labeled precisely: this is the concept's logic, following from the hub idea as drawn on this sheet — not physics, and not a claim that any branch or hub exists.

Why? →

Why does one life branch so wide?

Because a life is a long chain of forks — choices the being makes, multiplied by variables it never controlled. Change any link early and the experienced life at the end is unrecognisable. The set of possible lives is not a handful of alternates; it is astronomical.

How? →

How would the hub choose a branch?

Unknown. A branch-dial would need an address for a life — which choices, which variables, set how — and a way back to the branch you call home. That is Open How № 10, part of the same unproven concept.

FACT — Documented & measured

REAL, BUT READ THE FINE PRINT

Real time travel in the measured sense is small, one-directional, and proven: clocks genuinely age at different rates. Nothing here is a machine that visits the past.

1905

Special Relativity — Einstein

Time dilation predicted: a moving clock ticks slower than a still one. At the time, pure theory on paper.

Theory → later measured
1915

General Relativity — Einstein

Gravity also slows time. A clock deeper in a gravity well ages less. The mathematics of spacetime allows, on paper, paths that loop back in time — mathematics, not machines.

Theory + Mathematics
1949

Gödel's Rotating Universe

Kurt Gödel found a solution to Einstein's equations in which closed timelike curves exist — time loops permitted by the math. No evidence our universe is that universe.

Mathematics only
1971

Hafele–Keating Experiment

Atomic clocks flown around the world on jets aged differently from clocks left on the ground — by roughly the amounts relativity predicted. The first famous direct flight test.

Measured ✓
1995–

GPS & Satellite Clocks — fully operational 1995

First GPS satellite launched 1978; the system was declared fully operational in 1995. Satellite clocks run fast by about 38 microseconds a day from relativity's combined effects and are corrected for it daily. Uncorrected, positions would drift by roughly 10 km a day. Time dilation, in daily use.

Measured · In use ✓
1638

The Square–Cube Law — Galileo

In Two New Sciences, Galileo shows size is not neutral: volume and weight grow with the cube of length, strength with only the square. Double an animal and its bones face double the stress. The reason a giant human cannot simply be a scaled-up human — and the first hard science of why size changes the world.

Haldane's essay walks the ladder from insect to whale: an ant's world runs on surface forces and harmless falls, a human's on gravity in balance, a giant's on weight its structure cannot carry. “For every type of animal there is a most convenient size” — size treated as a world-making fact, a century ago.

Uexküll's mature statement of the Umwelt: every species lives in its own perceptual world, built from the signals its body can receive and act on. The founding document of the species thread on this sheet (§04) — each kind of life enclosed in a different experienced world at the same coordinates.

09 — The Open Hows Shelf

Unknown is not a no.

An unknown stays on the shelf labeled as an unknown, and the work proceeds on what is known. Every how below belongs to the same unproven concept — the hub itself included.

  1. How do you enter a point with no size?

    A person is 3D; the hub is 0D. Every story solves this with a vehicle or a door. The blueprint has neither yet. Unknown

  2. How do you choose a when?

    A hub equidistant from every time still needs an aim. What sets the exit year — a dial, a thought, a coordinate? Unknown

  3. How do you choose a world as well as a when?

    If different worlds are different dimensions, the hub needs a second aim entirely — and a way to tell worlds apart from inside a point that contains no information about any of them. Part of the same unproven concept. Unknown

  4. How do you get back to your when and world?

    Every traveler story is really a story about the return. A hub with infinite exits needs a home address. Unknown

  5. What stops two exits colliding — or a paradox?

    The Fact shelf's mathematics permits loops on paper and says nothing comforting about meeting yourself. Unknown

  6. Why is the hiding hidden?

    If any claimed account were real, the evidence for it is not on the public record. Document what's hidden — and ask why it's hidden. Unknown

  7. What size do you come out — and in whose world?

    The hub has no size at all, yet scale decides which forces run a world: surface tension rules the ant, gravity in balance rules us, weight would crush the giant. Does a traveler keep their own size at the exit, take the size of the world they enter — or is there a scale-dial nobody has drawn yet? Part of the same unproven concept. Unknown

  8. What kind of life do you come out as?

    Dog, cat, human, rhinoceros, tree — each a drastically different life in the same place (§04). If an exit could change your species, would the one who comes out still be you — and could a tree come back? Part of the same unproven concept. Unknown

  9. Is a being's experience its own dimension — and could a hub move along it?

    The capstone question (§05). Physics says no: experience is not a coordinate of spacetime. Perception science maps spaces of experience; philosophy (Nagel, 1974) says the inside view is left out of every physical account; the hard problem of why experience exists at all is unsolved. The hub, the world-dial, the scale-dial, the life-dial, the branch-dial, and the open hows are one unproven concept, labeled as such. On size, the sheet's verdict stands apart from the concept: size is not a dimension in standard physics, but scale is measured fact — it changes which forces run a world, from ant to human to giant.
