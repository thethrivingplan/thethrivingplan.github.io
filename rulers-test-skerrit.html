The Ruler's Test — Roosevelt Skerrit, Prime Minister of Dominica

Subject: Roosevelt Skerrit — Prime Minister of Dominica since 8 January 2004. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Dominica's presidency is a largely ceremonial head-of-state office, while executive power — command of the government, the budget, and the public service — sits with the prime minister. The president at grading is Sylvanie Burton (in office since October 2023). Skerrit, born 8 June 1972, became prime minister at 31 on the death in office of Prime Minister Pierre Charles on 8 January 2004, was elected leader of the Dominica Labour Party (DLP) in February 2004, and then won the premiership at the polls in 2005, 2009, 2014, 2019 and 2022 (Cananews/CMC, November 2026; contemporaneous reporting, 2004). The premise in the brief was verified and holds, with one material update that changes the framing, not the subject: on 18 November 2026 Skerrit advised President Burton to dissolve Parliament and called a general election for 16 December 2026, with Nomination Day on 30 November 2026 — more than a year before the constitutional deadline — saying he wanted "a fresh mandate" and did not want "to govern for the next 12 months looking over his shoulder at an election" (OPM Pressroom, Address to the Nation, 18 November 2026; Cananews/CMC, 18 November 2026). He is therefore graded on an ongoing tenure of almost twenty-three years, not a completed one: he remains prime minister at grading, and the December 2026 election is a pending event, not a result. No election, succession, or court ruling had removed him as of 24 November 2026. The record graded is dominated by five files: the Citizenship by Investment (CBI) programme and its documented controversies; the contested 2019 election and the boycotted 2022 election; the recovery from Hurricane Maria (18 September 2017) and the climate-resilience programme; the geothermal plant, delivered in 2026 after some fifteen years of development; and a foreign policy built, in his own borrowed words, as "friends of all, satellites of none" — China, Venezuela, Cuba and the United States alike (BBC Caribbean, March 2004; contemporaneous reporting). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic printed on the Test card, above the five tests: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one — the Test grades the holding, never the platform; carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as two independent axes: a kept word that hurt people earns Test 5 credit and a full hurt entry in the ledger; a broken word that helped people is recorded as a break on Test 5 and as help in the ledger. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier says so and grades the choice actually available in the moment, not a hindsight door. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The law has reached his own side through the courts, in the open. In Skerrit and Others v Defoe and Others [2021] CCJ 4 (AJ), the Caribbean Court of Justice — Dominica's final court — dismissed the appeal by Skerrit and other elected DLP members and reinstated magistrate's summonses requiring them to answer the election offence of "treating" (free concerts shortly before the 2014 election, alleged to have been held to corruptly influence voters) (Caribbean Court of Justice judgment summary, 2021; DOM767, 2021). The summonses were reinstated; no conviction is recorded in the record assembled here, and the underlying charge remains an ALLEGATION until a court rules on it. The point for this shelf is narrower: the prime minister litigated to the final court, lost, and the process continued.
  • The CBI programme itself is a creature of law, not of personal gift: it has operated since 1993 under the Citizenship Act and published regulations, through a Citizenship by Investment Unit, with set investment amounts — raised to a US$200,000 minimum under the regional Memorandum of Agreement signed 20 March 2024 by the five Caribbean CBI states, effective 1 July 2024 (IMI Daily, 2024; regional reporting, 2024). Any applicant who pays and passes screening qualifies on the same published terms.
  • The state has used that law against its own customers. On 6 June 2024 the government gazetted a Citizenship Deprivation Order revoking the citizenship of 68 CBI citizens, stating they had obtained it "by means of fraud, false representation or the concealment of a material fact," for citizenships granted between November 2019 and May 2022 (IMI Daily, reporting the gazetted order, June 2024). In October 2025 Parliament passed legislation giving Dominica an expanded power to revoke citizenship and recall passports (OPM Pressroom statement on US engagement, 2026).
  • The ordinary courts have ruled against positions associated with his government and the rulings stood — most visibly the High Court's 2024 ruling striking down sections of the Sexual Offences Act that criminalised consensual same-sex acts, which took effect without executive reversal (Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court ruling, April 2024; regional reporting).

Evidence against:

  • The CBI programme is, in its structure, a second door into citizenship: one law for Dominicans by birth and descent, and a purchasable law for wealthy foreigners who, until the October 2025 reforms, needed no residency in Dominica at all. The scale dwarfs the citizen body it joins. The European Commission reported in October 2023 that Dominica had issued 34,500 CBI passports (the period covered was not specified in the report; the programme has run since 1993) — in a country of roughly 66,000–73,000 residents (European Commission report via OCCRP, October 2023). Investigators from OCCRP, the Government Accountability Project and fourteen partner outlets, working from official gazettes and leaked lists, identified some 7,700 CBI citizens for 2007–2022 alone (OCCRP / Guardian, Dominica: Passports of the Caribbean, October 2023).
  • The screening behind that door failed in documented cases, not merely alleged ones. The 2023 investigation identified among CBI citizens people who had previously been investigated, charged with crimes, or sentenced to prison, including a former Afghan intelligence figure, a Turkish businessman convicted of fraud, and a former Libyan officer; Forbes' reporting for the project identified three Russian businessmen who obtained Dominican citizenship in 2017 and were sanctioned by the UK in 2022 (OCCRP / Guardian, October 2023; Forbes, October 2023). The UK Home Office went further as a matter of official finding: imposing a visa regime on all Dominican nationals on 19 July 2023, it stated that "careful consideration of Dominica's and Vanuatu's operation of a citizenship by investment scheme has shown clear and evident abuse of the scheme, including the granting of citizenship to individuals known to pose a risk to the UK" (UK Home Office, Statement of Changes in Immigration Rules HC 1715 and Written Statement, 19 July 2023). That is a foreign government's finding, contested by Roseau (below), printed here as a finding by its maker, not as a Dominican court's verdict.
  • A separate, graver file sits beside the programme as ALLEGATION. Al Jazeera's Diplomats for Sale (25–27 November 2019) alleged, from undercover filming of former prime minister Oliver Seraphin offering to broker an ambassadorship, and from witnesses, that diplomatic posts and passports had been exchanged for campaign money — including the allegation that Iranian businessman Alireza Monfared, later sentenced in Iran to 20 years for embezzlement, funded Skerrit's 2014 campaign in exchange for an ambassadorship to Malaysia, and an allegation concerning a diplomatic passport for a former Nigerian petroleum minister, Diezani Alison-Madueke. Skerrit strongly denied the claims, denied speaking to Seraphin about any ambassadorship, called the documentary an opposition-linked effort "to destabilize Dominica," and Seraphin himself said he had been name-dropping without authority (Al Jazeera Investigative Unit, November 2019; OCCRP news report, 26 November 2019). No court verdict on these allegations appears in the record assembled here. They are recorded as ALLEGATION, with the denials — but an allegation of selling the state's own honours, unanswered by any documented independent inquiry, is itself part of the one-law record.
  • The fiscal structure made the second door the state's main revenue source, which is where the favouritism becomes systemic rather than episodic. IMF Article IV tables record CBI revenue at 33.0% of GDP in 2020, 26.9% in 2021, and — on the 2024 mission's separate series — a primary balance excluding CBI of −40.9% of GDP in 2023 against −4.3% including it (IMF, 2024 and 2025 Article IV statements). A state financed by selling its citizenship to outsiders has a standing incentive to keep that door wide that does not apply to any other law it administers.
  • His response to the 2023 investigation attacked the messengers rather than answering the lists: the government did not respond to OCCRP's detailed pre-publication questions, and Skerrit held press conferences claiming — in the investigation's documented characterisation, falsely — that reporters were paid by his political opposition, likening them to "terrorists" and "arsonists" (OCCRP, October 2023; Guardian, October 2023). Skerrit's substantive defence is also on the record and is recorded here: that Dominica runs "a robust system" of layered due diligence, and that a citizen who offends after being granted citizenship no more discredits the programme than a crime discredits US residency (Guardian, October 2023).

Why: In plain words: the ordinary law in Dominica reaches the powerful — the treating summonses prove it — but citizenship itself, the most basic membership law a state has, was run for two decades as a priced product with no residency tie, at a scale several times the resident population's annual cohort, with documented grants to people who had been charged, convicted, or were later sanctioned, and with the resulting abuse finding made formally by the UK and acted on by the UK, the EU review process, and the United States. A programme can be lawful and still be a second law; this one was both lawful and, on the documented screening record, loosely kept. That is a failure of one law at the source, and the 2024–2025 tightening (Test 3) repairs the door going forward without changing what the door was.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — CBI applicants who obtained a second citizenship on published terms; the Dominican treasury and, through it, recipients of CBI-funded housing, hotels and infrastructure (Tests 4–5); licensed agents and developers who profited from the programme's volume. Hurt — ordinary Dominican citizens, who since 19 July 2023 need a full UK visa and since 1 January 2026 face US partial entry restrictions because of a programme most of them never used (UK Home Office, July 2023; US Presidential Proclamation, 16 December 2025); the 68 people whose citizenship was revoked in June 2024 on the state's own fraud wording; and Dominican voters, in whose name the ALLEGATION-level diplomatic-passport claims remain publicly unresolved.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The referee courts are structurally outside his gift and have acted like it. Dominica's final court is the Caribbean Court of Justice in Port of Spain; its intermediate courts are the shared Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court. Those courts reinstated criminal summonses against the prime minister himself (CCJ, 2021, Test 1), struck down the sodomy provisions of the Sexual Offences Act in 2024, and in 2019 the High Court refused an opposition-linked application to halt the general election on the ground that the President had already issued the writ — a ruling against the side then challenging him (High Court, November 2019; contemporaneous reporting). No documented removal, packing, or defiance of a court by Skerrit appears in the record assembled here.
  • The money referee is regional, not national: Dominica's central bank is the Eastern Caribbean Central Bank, shared by eight territories, whose governor he does not appoint alone. IMF Article IV consultations — including published criticism of CBI dependence and of under-developed internal CBI reporting — have proceeded and been published throughout his tenure (IMF, 2024; IMF, June 2025).
  • The 2025 electoral legislation strengthened the electoral referee on paper: the Electoral Commission Act (passed March 2025) gives the Electoral Commission its own fund, alongside a Registration of Electors Act requiring in-person confirmation and photo voter ID cards (Parliament, March 2025; Electoral Office records). The confirmation exercise was run by the Electoral Commission and Chief Elections Officer and concluded 16 November 2026 with a published register of 36,923 electors (OPM Pressroom, 18 November 2026).
  • Independent and opposition media operate, publish, and are quoted throughout this dossier — DBS Radio, Dominica News Online, The Dominican, EmoNews — and international monitors rate the country Free: Freedom House's Freedom in the World 2026 scores Dominica 92/100 (37/40 political rights, 55/60 civil liberties) (Freedom House, 2026, via CRS compilation). The World Justice Project's 2026 Rule of Law Index ranks Dominica 54th of 143, score 0.57, down 1.1% in the year, and specifically records that freedom of opinion and expression and freedom of assembly did not decline in Dominica, and that government powers limited by the judiciary did not fall (WJP Rule of Law Index, October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Duration and margin are themselves a grip on the legislature. He has held the seat for almost twenty-three years and, since December 2022, a 19–2 majority in a 21-seat elected House, won in an election the two main opposition parties boycotted (Test 3); six DLP candidates were returned unopposed in 2022 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2023). A parliament that size does not check an executive; it ratifies one. He has also accumulated portfolios personally — at grading he is also Minister for Investment and Governance, and through his tenure has held finance and other senior portfolios (Government Gazette notice, April 2026).
  • The electoral referee's independence was the central grievance of Dominican politics for most of his tenure: observer missions from the OAS, CARICOM and the Commonwealth recommended electoral reform — a cleaned register and voter ID — after successive elections, and by the December 2022 election none of it had been implemented (OAS / Commonwealth / CARICOM observer reporting, 2019–2022; Freedom House, 2023). The reform arrived only in 2025 (Test 5). For roughly two decades the referee played on a register the opposition, and the observers, said was inflated — the pre-reform register carried 74,895 electors in 2019 for a resident population of roughly 70,000, against 36,923 on the cleaned 2026 register (Electoral Office, 2019; OPM Pressroom, November 2026). Those two figures are printed as sourced, not blended; the gap between them is the size of the grievance.
  • The street-level referee — the right to protest — was answered with force on the day reform finally passed. On 19 March 2025, as Parliament debated the three reform bills, police declared the opposition-called protest outside Parliament unlawful for lack of permission, fired tear gas and rubber bullets, and, between 19 March and 9 April 2025, at least 24 people were arrested, including opposition figures, lawyers and human-rights defenders, with charges brought under the Riot Act; a journalist's equipment was grabbed by police (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025; Inter-American Commission on Human Rights statement, 16 April 2025; CMC reporting, 19 March 2025). The Dominica Bar Association expressed "grave concern" on 26 May 2025 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). The IACHR also recorded civil-society concerns about the integrity and independence of electoral entities and the absence of campaign-finance regulation (IACHR, April 2025).
  • His treatment of the press at its most independent moment cuts against this test: in September–October 2023 he publicly likened investigative reporters examining a state programme to "terrorists" and claimed, without documented basis in the record, that they were paid by the opposition (OCCRP; Guardian, October 2023). No law was passed against them and they published freely — the grip here is rhetorical, and it is recorded at that weight, not more.

Why: In plain words: the referees he cannot hold — the CCJ, the Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court, the regional central bank — have stayed free and have ruled against him and his government, and the country remains rated Free with courts and press functioning. The referees inside the national arena tell a different story: a two-decade-old inflated register left unreformed until 2025, a 19–2 parliament that cannot check him, and a protest against his own reform bills dispersed with tear gas and followed by two dozen arrests. He has not seized the referees; he has benefited for a very long time from the ones that were weak, and strengthened them late. That is a mixed hold, not a grip and not a clean one.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — voters on the cleaned 2026 register, who now vote with photo ID on a 36,923-name list; litigants who won against the state in its own courts, including the claimant in the 2024 Sexual Offences Act case. Hurt — the at least 24 people arrested after the 19 March 2025 protest, including opposition figures, lawyers and defenders (IACHR count); opposition voters who contested the 2019 and 2022 elections on a register their parties and observer missions said was not clean; and the journalist whose equipment was grabbed on 19 March 2025 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest wobble of the tenure was an act of God, and the adjustment was open, named, and sustained. Hurricane Maria on 18 September 2017 caused damage assessed at US$1.3 billion — 226% of GDP (World Bank / Government of Dominica post-disaster assessment). Skerrit addressed the UN General Assembly days later ("Eden is broken"), committed Dominica to becoming "the world's first climate-resilient nation," created the Climate Resilience Execution Agency (CREAD) in December 2018, and adopted a Climate Resilience and Recovery Plan 2020–2030 with twenty targets for 2030 (Government of Dominica; World Bank, 2022; World Economic Forum, 2023). The promise was public, specific, and repeatedly re-stated, which is what makes it gradeable on Test 5.
  • Foreign-partner pushback on CBI was, after 2023, answered with adjustment rather than only denial. After the UK visa imposition (July 2023) and sustained US and EU pressure, Dominica: signed the February 2023 six-principles agreement with the United States; introduced mandatory interviews for applicants aged 16+ in July 2023; signed the March 2024 regional Memorandum of Agreement setting the US$200,000 floor and information-sharing; revoked 68 citizenships in June 2024; and on 14 October 2025 became the first OECS state to enact the Eastern Caribbean Citizenship by Investment Regulatory Authority (ECCIRA) agreement — binding regional standards, biometrics, escrow, fines up to US$250,000, residency requirements and name-change controls (OPM Pressroom, October 2025 and 2026; IMI Daily, 2024–2025). ECCIRA was enacted but, as of reporting in September–October 2026, not yet operational (St Kitts and Nevis CIU via industry reporting, September 2026).
  • The December 2025 US wobble was answered diplomatically and on the record. When the US Presidential Proclamation of 16 December 2025 imposed partial entry restrictions on Dominican nationals from 1 January 2026 (suspending immigrant visas and B-1/B-2, F, M and J categories), citing CBI "without residency," Skerrit called it unexpected and a possible "miscommunication" between the White House and the State Department and Treasury — which, he noted, had supported Dominica's CBI legislative review — wrote formally to the US President, and pursued clarification through the embassy in Washington and the US Embassy in Bridgetown, publishing a detailed statement of the engagement and of the reforms already enacted (OPM Pressroom statement, 2026; DBS Radio via TravelPulse, December 2025; OCCRP, December 2025). As of October 2026 no modification of the restrictions for Dominica had been documented (industry tracking, October 2026). The answer was open; it has not, at grading, worked.
  • On the regional wobble of late 2025 he offered adjustment by mediation rather than alignment: in December 2025 he publicly offered Dominica as an intermediary between the United States and Venezuela, stating Dominica is "a good friend of both," and urging dialogue to keep the Caribbean a zone of peace (Trinidad Express, December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The electoral wobble was denied for two full election cycles before it was answered. After the 6 December 2019 election — DLP 18 seats, UWP 3, turnout 54.42% of 74,895 registered electors, with observer missions concluding voters could cast ballots without intimidation and that results reflected the will of those who voted, while also recommending reform (Electoral Office final count, December 2019; CARICOM / Commonwealth / OAS missions, December 2019) — protests and roadblocks preceded the poll and the opposition demanded the reforms the observers themselves had recommended. The government's answer was to govern on and then, on 6 November 2022, to call a snap election for 6 December 2022, two years early and still without the reforms. The UWP, DFP and APP boycotted; the DLP won 19 of 21 seats on a turnout of 31.6%–32%, the lowest in Dominican history, with six seats uncontested (Freedom House, 2023; election records, December 2022). Skerrit dismissed the boycott as an excuse by a party that could not field a slate (CaribMagPlus, November 2022). Calling an early election on the unreformed register, at the moment the opposition was mid-leadership-change, is doubling down in its clearest documented form in this file.
  • Investigative pushback was met with attack, not answer, twice: Al Jazeera in 2019 was called an agent of the opposition working "to destabilize Dominica" (OCCRP news report, November 2019), and the OCCRP consortium in 2023 was met with non-response to its questions followed by press conferences likening its reporters to terrorists (OCCRP; Guardian, October 2023). In neither case does the record assembled here show an independent inquiry, a published rebuttal of the named cases, or a document release answering them.
  • When the street pushed back against the content of his reform — on 19 March 2025 protesters and the IACHR both raised the missing piece, campaign-finance regulation — the adjustment did not come and the protest was dispersed by force (Test 2). The government's stated position was that campaign finance "warrants further analysis" through a regional approach (IACHR, April 2025). If another option becomes available — here, adding campaign-finance rules to bills already before Parliament — the record shows it was noted and deferred, not considered in the open with reasons given to those asking; and the asking itself was tear-gassed. Considering and declining with stated reasons would not have counted against him on this test; a deferral to a regional process, enforced against the askers, does.
  • No-good-choice clause, applied where it genuinely fits: Maria left no good choice. A treasury facing 226% of GDP in damage, dependent on CBI inflows the IMF records at roughly a quarter to a third of GDP, could not refuse the CBI money that funded the rebuild without abandoning the rebuild. The dossier does not grade the use of CBI revenue after Maria against him. What remains gradeable is the origin and the handling: the programme's loose screening predated Maria by years, and the response to exposure in 2019 and 2023 was denial and attack rather than the tightening that only foreign visa losses eventually produced in 2024–2025.

Why: In plain words: faced with nature, he adjusted openly and built a programme around the adjustment; faced with foreign governments, he denied first and adjusted later, but the adjustment — price floor, interviews, revocations, a regional regulator — is real and enacted; faced with his own voters and press, he denied longest of all, winning a boycotted election on an unreformed register in 2022 and dispersing the 2025 reform protest by force. The direction of travel since 2023 is toward adjustment. The twenty years before it are also the record.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — residents rehoused and re-powered under the post-Maria resilience programme (Test 4); CBI applicants and the treasury, whose programme survived because the 2024–2025 reforms answered enough of the foreign case to keep Schengen visa-free access intact at grading. Hurt — opposition voters in 2022, whose parties' boycott left them effectively unrepresented in a 19–2 House; the protesters arrested in March 2025; and Dominican travellers, for whom the late adjustment did not prevent the UK loss in 2023 or the US restrictions in 2026.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition, the diaspora, the Kalinago, the poor, and those who never bought a passport — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The post-Maria rebuild was directed at the whole island, not at supporters' districts on any documented pattern in the record assembled here. The Housing Recovery Project, run with World Bank financing through local contractors, had delivered 1,911 completed homes by mid-2025 toward the 5,000-home target, with some 2,900 families benefited and a further 380+ homes under construction across ten sites for handover by the end of 2025; handovers expressly included families in the Kalinago Territory, and the parallel CBI-funded "Housing Revolution" included the Bellevue Chopin settlement for the village of Petite Savanne, evacuated after Tropical Storm Erika in 2015 (Jamaica Homes News reporting of government figures, June 2025; CBIU, 2024). No sourced account in the record alleges allocation by party.
  • The geothermal dividend is universal by design: the 10 MW Laudat plant's power flows to the single national grid through DOMLEC, and in June 2026 geothermal supplied about 43% of the island's electricity with hydropower about 25% more — displacing imported diesel for every connected household alike (CARILEC / Ormat reporting via ThinkGeoEnergy, 2026).
  • Cost-of-living relief in 2026 was general, not targeted by constituency: in April 2026 Parliament approved extending waivers of VAT and import duties on essential food and goods to July 2026, defended by Skerrit as protection for "innocent bystanders" of the Middle East conflict's price shock (Cananews/CMC, April 2026). His November 2026 address likewise framed the social programmes as delivered across the country, and announced bridge pensions for non-established public officers (OPM Pressroom, November 2026).
  • The Kalinago — Dominica's Indigenous people, holding some 3,700 acres communally in the Kalinago Territory — appear in the record as recipients of housing handovers and, in January 2026, of a dedicated climate-resilient agriculture and ecotourism project steering process (Government reporting; project reporting, January 2026). Dominica under his tenure also kept the Kalinago's elected chief and council structure intact; no documented abolition or land alienation appears in the record.

Evidence against:

  • The heaviest single count against everyone in this file fell on the people least connected to its cause. The UK visa regime of 19 July 2023 and the US partial entry restrictions of 1 January 2026 apply to all Dominican nationals — students, patients, families, traders — because of a citizenship programme used by foreign buyers and administered by his government (UK Home Office, July 2023; US Presidential Proclamation, 16 December 2025). The ordinary Dominican who never sold or bought a passport now queues for a UK visa and is barred from US student and visitor categories. In the ledger's terms: the benefit of CBI was socialised as spending; its documented cost was socialised as suspicion.
  • Opposition voters were counted thinnest at the ballot box. In 2019 they voted on a 74,895-name register and their parties' reform demands went unmet; in 2022 their parties boycotted and the resulting House sat 19–2, with two independents as the only non-government elected members; the two independents, not the UWP, became the parliamentary opposition of record. A government cannot be blamed for a boycott it did not call — the UWP and DFP chose it — but it chose the conditions: a snap election, two years early, on the unreformed register, which the boycotting parties had named in advance as their reason (Freedom House, 2023; UWP statement, November 2022). Turnout fell from 54.42% (2019) to about 31.6% (2022).
  • The diaspora's count is contested in both directions, and both are printed. The IACHR recorded civil-society concern at the removal of the five-year absence rule for voter registration — a change critics said let long-absent overseas voters remain on the roll (IACHR, April 2025) — while the 2025 confirmation system requires electors abroad to return to Dominica in person to confirm, with no voting from abroad (Registration of Electors Act 2025; Electoral Office guidance). UWP leader Thomson Fontaine argued the overseas-voter provisions could enable people who have lived abroad for decades to decide domestic elections (DOM767, March 2025). Which way the diaspora is over- or under-counted is disputed by named parties; what is not disputed is that both the old register and the new confirmation rules were fought over precisely because the diaspora vote can decide Dominican seats.
  • The poor carry the fiscal risk of the model. The IMF's repeated warning is distributional: a state whose primary balance excluding CBI was −40.9% of GDP in 2023 has built its social spending on the most volatile revenue line it has, and the IMF in 2025–2026 pressed for a 3½% of GDP primary surplus and a self-insurance fund of 12% of GDP precisely because the next hurricane, or the next visa decision, lands first on those who depend on the state (IMF, 2024; IMF, June 2025; IMF Article IV reporting via Parliament, March 2026 — the government's own citation of a 0.7% of GDP primary surplus against the IMF's recommended 3.4% target). Public debt remained well above the 60% of GDP target the IMF set for 2035 on the Article IV path (IMF, 2024).

Why: In plain words: the spending counts everyone — homes, power, VAT relief and social programmes reach government and opposition districts, the Kalinago Territory included, on the documented record. The costs do not fall evenly: the travel penalties for the CBI programme fall on every Dominican passport holder, the electoral conditions of 2019–2022 fell on opposition voters, and the fiscal fragility underneath the social programmes falls first on the poor if the revenue stops. Counting everyone in the distribution while counting them into the risk is a mixed count.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the 1,911 households in completed Housing Recovery Project homes by mid-2025 and some 2,900 families benefited in total (government figures, June 2025), including Kalinago Territory families and the evacuated residents of Petite Savanne; every grid-connected household, through geothermal's roughly 43% share of supply in June 2026; consumers, through the 2026 VAT and duty waivers. Hurt — all Dominican nationals travelling to the UK (from July 2023) and the United States (from January 2026), who bear restrictions earned by the CBI file; opposition voters effectively unrepresented in the 2022–2026 House; and future recipients of social spending, whose programmes rest on a revenue line the IMF flags as volatile.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 8 January 2004, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The geothermal plant — kept, very late, and kept in full. Successive DLP programmes promised geothermal power from the Roseau Valley field, explored since the 2000s with EU and World Bank support. After some fifteen years of development: construction began June 2024 under a 25-year agreement with Ormat Technologies signed at COP28 in December 2023; commissioning began December 2025; first power reached the grid 30 March 2026; commercial operation was reached 31 July 2026, with June 2026 generation already at about 43% of national supply (World Bank Implementation Status Report, February 2026; DOMLEC, March 2026; Ormat Q2 2026 results via ThinkGeoEnergy, 2026). Dominica became the first CARICOM member state to generate electricity from its own geothermal resource. The axis crossing is named: the word was kept AND it helped people — cheaper, indigenous, hurricane-independent baseload power for the whole grid.
  • Electoral reform — kept, after being refused for two cycles. The cleaned register and voter ID that observers recommended and the opposition demanded for twenty years were legislated in March 2025, the confirmation exercise ran from October 2025, and on 16 November 2026 a new register of 36,923 electors was published, which Skerrit named in his 18 November 2026 address as "the fulfilment of an important commitment made by this Government" (OPM Pressroom, November 2026). The keeping is documented; so is the delay (below).
  • The CBI reform word to Washington — kept as written. The commitments given to the United States through 2024–2025 — residency requirements, name-change controls, stronger due diligence, revocation powers, an independent regulator — were enacted, with Dominica the first OECS state to pass the ECCIRA bill, on 14 October 2025 (OPM Pressroom statement, 2026). The US restrictions were imposed anyway in December 2025; a promise kept to a partner who then penalised the keeper is recorded as kept, with the outcome in Test 3's ledger, not charged against the keeping.
  • The resilience frame — followed as written. The plan's own targets run to 2030 (including 90% of housing resilient), housing stands at 38% of its target, and the IMF still records a current-account deficit of 32¼% of GDP in 2024 and debt above target paths (IMF, June 2025). A ruler's word includes what he tells his own people about the people checking him.

Why: In plain words: the big physical promises of this tenure — geothermal power, a cleaned electoral register, a regional CBI regulator, resilient housing machinery — were all, in the end, actually built, which is rarer in this series than it should be; and almost every one of them arrived years after the date, or the election, at which it was first owed. A word kept a decade late, after two elections were held without it, is both things at once, and the grade says both: MIXED.
