The Ruler's Test — Jennifer Geerlings-Simons, President of Suriname

Subject: Jennifer Geerlings-Simons (née Simons, born 5 September 1953) — President of Suriname since 16 July 2025. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: Suriname's 1987 constitution vests executive power in the president as head of state and head of government; the National Assembly elects the president but does not govern, and the vice president and council of ministers answer to her. A physician (dermatologist; later in the national HIV/AIDS programme), she chaired the National Assembly from 2010 to 2020 and took over the National Democratic Party (NDP) in July 2024 on the resignation of its founder, Dési Bouterse — who died on 24 December 2024 a fugitive from the 20-year sentence a Surinamese court had imposed on him in December 2023 for the 1982 December murders, a verdict her government has left standing and unchallenged. In the general election of 25 May 2025 — the first held under the nationwide proportional-representation system adopted in 2024 — the NDP won a plurality of 18 of 51 seats (34.18% of the vote) against 17 for the incumbent Progressive Reform Party (VHP) of President Chan Santokhi; turnout was 69.36%. She built a six-party coalition (NDP, ABOP, NPS, Pertjajah Luhur, A20, BEP) holding exactly 34 seats — precisely the two-thirds supermajority the constitution requires to elect a president — and after the VHP declined to field a rival candidate she was elected unopposed on 6 July 2025, with NPS leader Gregory Rusland as vice president, and inaugurated on 16 July 2025 as the country's first woman president. She is graded on roughly sixteen months in office, and the file's thinness is stated where it matters. Her predecessor Santokhi (president 16 July 2020 – 16 July 2025) appears as context where the record she inherited — the completed IMF programme, the restructured debt, the unimplemented land-rights judgments, the oil programme he launched in his final weeks — bears directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The sharpest enforcement of her tenure fell inside her own cabinet. A criminal complaint was filed with the Attorney General against her Minister of Justice and Security, Harish Monorath, over financial arrangements from his earlier chairmanship of the Telecommunications Authority Suriname (TAS) supervisory board — ALLEGATIONs concerning a former director's contract and compensation said to have been arranged for work for the cabinet of a coalition party leader; no guilt has been established, and Monorath maintains he performed his duties properly. Geerlings-Simons publicly called the complaint "serious," declined to decide before hearing him, met him twice in the week of 9 November 2026, stated his position had become untenable, and accepted his resignation on 13 November 2026, effective 16 November 2026, while the Public Prosecution Service's investigation proceeds (CMC/CANA reporting, 9 November 2026; Surinamese press reporting, 13–16 November 2026). A coalition partner, A20's chairman, stated the governing principle aloud: irregularities must be addressed regardless of political background, while cautioning against treating allegations as proven facts (CMC, November 2026).
  • Enforcement reached state companies and the security services, not only politicians of the other camp. At the state dairy company Melkcentrale N.V., an internal audit and a formal report by the Ministry of Agriculture produced a police investigation into more than SRD 17 million in alleged embezzlement dating from 2018 — spanning both previous governments: the director and deputy directors were suspended and taken into custody, more than ten employees detained in total, and the agriculture minister stated publicly that criminal prosecution was inevitable for those involved; all suspects' guilt remains ALLEGATION until a court rules (Surinamese press reporting, October–November 2025). In November 2026, fourteen members of the police's elite Arrest Team were arrested, accused of embezzling seized cocaine — five bags seized, three registered — charges that are likewise ALLEGATIONs, brought by the state's own apparatus against its own officers (Surinamese press reporting, November 2026). A former civil-affairs official was arrested on 14 November 2026 on a ministry's own report, accused of registering four people as ghost employees and diverting their salaries — again an ALLEGATION, again surfaced from inside the state (Korps Politie Suriname announcement, 18 November 2026).
  • The prosecution machinery she inherited was not turned off when it pointed at the previous government, nor when it had earlier pointed at her own party. The Public Prosecution Service's investigation, opened in 2024, into an alleged USD 7.5 million disbursement to Pan-American Real Estate on allegedly falsified documents — a file in which her predecessor Santokhi and two of his ministers were named in reporting as subjects — appears nowhere in the record assembled here as quashed or interfered with; its disposition remains undocumented at grading (Wikipedia summary of Surinamese reporting, 2024). The Central Bank scandal convictions of the Bouterse era — former central bank governor Robert van Trikt (six years), former NDP finance minister Gillmore Hoefdraad, and legal-affairs director Faranaaz Hausil, all convicted under the Santokhi-era prosecution of NDP-era officials — stand unreversed under her government, and no amnesty, pardon, or rehabilitation initiative for her party's convicted or fugitive figures appears in the documented record (Wikipedia, Corruption in Suriname, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • No senior figure of any camp has reached a verdict in a corruption case during her sixteen months. The documented record is arrests, suspensions, complaints, and investigations — including against her own justice minister, who left office by resignation rather than by any completed process, with the TAS investigation still open and its central claims unadjudicated. The outcome measure moved the wrong way: Suriname's standing in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index deteriorated in the 2025 index (ranked ~96th, down eight places on the tracker of indices), and the IMF's January 2026 Article IV statement still lists amending the anti-corruption law and operationalizing the procurement law as unfinished business for her government (Transparency International CPI 2025 via RankTrackr; IMF Press Release 26/025, 28 January 2026).
  • The coalition she governs with contains, at its second pillar, a documented exception to the one-law claim. ABOP chairman Ronnie Brunswijk — her government's partner, holder of ministries in her cabinet, and the man whose former vice-presidential cabinet the TAS complaint alleges was improperly served (an ALLEGATION he disputes, stating documents show the disputed acts were done on the instructions of the then political leadership) — was convicted in absentia by a Dutch court in 1999 of cocaine trafficking, a conviction on the documented record. Brunswijk defended Monorath publicly during the November 2026 meetings and declared the accusations refuted; the president decided otherwise, but the file records that the defense of a minister under criminal complaint was mounted from inside her own coalition's leadership while the complaint was live (Surinamese and CMC reporting, November 2026).
  • The source of the seat itself narrows the one-law claim in one specific way: her coalition holds exactly the two-thirds of the Assembly the constitution requires, assembled from six parties including two (ABOP, BEP) whose electorates are the Maroon interior — a breadth that is a genuine national mandate, and simultaneously a structure in which the price of holding the seat is the continued inclusion of partners the law has previously convicted or is currently investigating. That is stated as a fact about the source, as Test 1 grades the source.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The central bank's governorship was left in the predecessor's hands. Maurice Roemer, governor of the Centrale Bank van Suriname since 2020 under the Bouterse and Santokhi governments, remains governor in November 2026, speaking in his own voice — including publicly advocating, in May 2025, that whichever government emerged should structurally link policy to a new IMF programme, a position her government has not adopted; no documented pressure on him appears in the record (CIA World Leaders listing, September 2025; Starnieuws interview via CMC, July 2025). The reform process amending the Central Bank Act to strengthen the bank's mandate, autonomy, and governance — begun under IMF conditionality in 2024–2025 — has continued under her government rather than being reversed (CBvS reform documentation, 2026).
  • The electoral machinery that seated her was her predecessor's, and it seated his opponent. The 25 May 2025 election was the first under the nationwide proportional system legislated in 2024; the count gave her party 18 seats to the incumbent's 17, the result stood, the incumbent's party declined to contest her election in the Assembly, and power transferred on schedule — the referees of 2025 were inherited, independent in effect, and obeyed by the loser (IPU Parline election record, 2025; International IDEA country tracker, 2025).
  • Parliament checks her in the open, including on money. Her 2026 budget, presented in September 2025 and enlarged by a note of amendment, was adopted only on 8 July 2026 — halfway through the budget year — after Assembly scrutiny; the Assembly also amended the Public Debt Act in 2025 to tighten the debt ceiling and require an annual public debt plan alongside the budget (Dawgen Global Suriname Budget Review, current to October 2026; Surinamese press reporting, 2025). In November 2026 opposition parliamentarians publicly questioned her government over the TAS affair and publicly disputed whether her announced joint police–military patrols had actually deployed — criticism aired freely and answered in the press, not suppressed (Surinamese press reporting, November 2026).
  • The press operates at a level few countries in the hemisphere match. Suriname ranked 34th of 180 in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index (score 73.20, "satisfactory"), one of only four countries in the Americas in that category, with critical outlets covering her government's scandals, strikes, and coalition disputes at length (RSF World Press Freedom Index 2026; LatAm Journalism Review, April 2026). The contrast with the predecessor is on the record: it was Santokhi who sued the publisher, newspapers, and bookstores over a 2024 corruption book; no comparable action by her government appears in the documented record (Wikipedia, Corruption in Suriname, 2026).
  • At the UN Universal Periodic Review on 2 November 2026, her government conceded its own backlogs in the open — acknowledging before the Human Rights Council that the collective rights of Indigenous and tribal peoples remain unrecognized and the Inter-American Court judgments unimplemented, and that the national human rights institute is not yet established (CMC/CANA, 2 November 2026). A government gripping its referees does not usually plead guilty before them.

Evidence against:

  • The presidency and the ruling party's chairmanship are held in one pair of hands. Geerlings-Simons remains president of the NDP while serving as president of the republic; the Speaker of the National Assembly is an NDP member; her coalition commands exactly the two-thirds supermajority. No single act of institutional capture is documented, but the fusion of party and state leadership at the top is structural, and the Assembly her coalition controls amended the draft Collective Rights bill in ways its intended beneficiaries formally rejected as done without consulting them (UPR civil-society factsheet, October 2026).
  • Independent assessors rate the institutional frame weak and the central bank's independence still prospective. S&P Global Ratings, upgrading Suriname to B- on 9 November 2026, stated that it still rates the institutional settings as weak, that the statutory fiscal rules adopted in 2024 are not yet operational, and that the central bank "still needs time to build credibility, operational independence and effectiveness" (S&P Global Ratings research update, 9 November 2026). The IMF's January 2026 statement likewise calls for full operationalization of the public financial management and sovereign wealth fund legislation her government has pledged but not completed (IMF Press Release 26/025, 28 January 2026).
  • Two accountability institutions exist on paper and not in fact, on her watch as on her predecessors'. The Children's Ombudsman law entered into force in January 2025 and the institution is still not functioning; the independent National Human Rights Institute remains unestablished, with her government telling the UPR it intends it operational by 2027 and citing funding as the bottleneck (CMC/CANA, 2 November 2026). The referees she has not gripped include referees that do not yet exist.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Investigative reporting moved her government to reverse a predecessor's decision in the open. After Mongabay's reporting on Mennonite-linked land deals and rainforest clearing — including its 2023 exposure of a plan to transfer hundreds of thousands of hectares to the agriculture ministry — Geerlings-Simons announced in August 2026 a task force to investigate and evict illegal occupiers of rainforest, stated she had instructed officials to reverse the land-transfer decision for forested areas, publicly opposed large-scale rainforest agriculture in October 2026, and enforced the policy through the courts: when a company sought a court order halting the deportations, the court rejected the claim on 29 October 2026, and more than 50 Mennonite settlers with expired permits were removed to Bolivia on 31 October 2026 under the judgment (Mongabay impact reporting, November 2026; Wisevoter, 12 November 2026).
  • The street's largest pushback — the teachers — was answered with machinery, deadlines, and eventually money. When the technical teachers' union BLTO struck for the fifth time in early 2026, demanding the education minister's dismissal over unpaid overtime dating to November 2025, late salaries, and unimplemented court rulings on payroll, Geerlings-Simons refused the dismissals but received the petition personally, committed to the overdue payments, and talks produced a working group with a presidential commission and dated deadlines for clearing arrears by early February 2026; the wider union conflict ended, for now, in a negotiated general salary increase of 15% for civil servants announced jointly on 27 August 2026, with a mixed government–union working group on further terms (Surinamese press reporting, January–February 2026; government–union joint statement, 27 August 2026). The unions' mistrust is documented on the other shelf; the adjustment itself is documented here.
  • The fiscal wobble was conceded at the highest level, in numbers, before it was answered. In her first week she told the country the treasury was in crisis — a 2025 budget covering only fixed obligations, an estimated SRD 1.3 billion deficit on fixed expenditures alone, and some SRD 7 billion in unpaid obligations to companies and individuals inherited — and when the IMF's January 2026 Article IV consultation found that 2025's fiscal and monetary slippages had eroded the stabilization gains, her government did not dispute the finding: it requested the Fund's long-term macro-fiscal expert, presented corrective budgets targeting a narrowing deficit, and the slippage itself was recorded in her own annual address's figures (Surinamese press reporting, July 2025; IMF Press Release 26/025, 28 January 2026).
  • The Indigenous pushback of autumn 2026 was answered with a meeting and a dated commitment rather than silence. After VIDS, the Association of Indigenous Village Leaders, handed formal resolutions to her cabinet on 1 September 2026 urging that the Protection of Residential and Living Areas Act not be promulgated in its current form, and with protest action threatened in late November, Geerlings-Simons received VIDS at the President's Cabinet on 20 November 2026 and announced that the Kaliña and Lokono Community Development Fund — the vehicle for implementing the Inter-American Court's 2015 judgment — will be established in January 2027, with land rights, the contested Act, river pollution, and the Mennonite file all on the table (VIDS resolutions, 1 September 2026; presidential meeting reporting, 20 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the file where the pushback was most formal, the adjustment has not come. The Protection of Residential and Living Areas Act — her government's substitute framework offering a 5-kilometre protective radius around village centres — was advanced without free, prior, and informed consent and was formally rejected by VIDS, which holds that a temporary radius is a poor substitute for the structural recognition the Inter-American Court ordered in 2007 and 2015; the FPIC protocol VIDS presented to her personally in a parliamentary ceremony on 14 October 2025 has still not been integrated into law sixteen months into her term, and Indigenous leaders were preparing protest action in the week of grading because the government had not responded to outstanding demands (UPR civil-society factsheet, October 2026; UNDP Suriname, 14 October 2025; Surinamese press reporting, 21–23 November 2026). Dialogue happened; the asked-for change — halt the substitute law, legislate the collective right — has not.
  • The teachers' file records the limit of her adjustments: five strikes, a union stating on the record that it deeply mistrusts government promises because past assurances never resolved the structural problems, and a president who ruled out guaranteeing salary increases on inflation grounds while asking teachers to return for the students' sake (Surinamese press reporting, January–February 2026). The eventual 15% raise closed the round; it did not close the mistrust, and the raise's critics — including commenters and unions noting pensioners and the private sector were outside it — are documented (government–union statement and public reaction, August 2026).
  • On the November 2026 crime surge — daylight shootings, more than 20 robberies in two weeks on the opposition's count — her answer was announced machinery: army support to police, joint patrols, a reactivated military-police post. A VHP parliamentarian stated publicly on 22 November 2026 that the joint patrols had shown little evidence of deployment — a claim recorded as CONTESTED, the government maintaining the measures are in place (Surinamese press reporting, 9 and 22 November 2026). Announcing is not adjusting; whether the patrols materialized is exactly the kind of fact the next grading should settle.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Maroon and Indigenous peoples, the opposition's voters, the poor, and those who share the streets — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Her social package was designed downward, and its design is documented. The 2026 purchasing-power measures announced by her finance minister give civil servants a monthly allowance rising from SRD 1,000 to SRD 1,500 across the year; pensioners on the state old-age provision (AOV), people with disabilities, and low-income households receive an extra SRD 250 per month; the child benefit (AKB) rises to SRD 250 per child; and senior officials — directors and top managers — are expressly excluded from the allowances, with the SRD 1.8 billion cost part-financed by striking ghost and overseas employees from the payroll (Surinamese press reporting, February–March 2026). The statutory minimum wage rose from SRD 52.47 to SRD 61.25 per hour on 1 July 2026, on the advice of the National Wage Council, applying to all sectors (government announcement, July 2026; WageIndicator, 2026).
  • The coalition itself is the broadest counting Suriname has assembled: six parties spanning the country's ethnic map — the NDP, the Creole-urban NPS, the Maroon parties ABOP and BEP, the Javanese Pertjajah Luhur, and A20 — with the vice presidency held by the NPS. The opposition VHP's voters are counted in the institutional sense that matters most: their party's refusal to contest her election was honored, its parliamentarians question her ministers freely, and power changed hands on the count of 18 seats to 17 (IPU Parline, 2025).
  • On the interior, her government's stated method is registration before punishment. Her November 2026 gold-sector plan — a state Gold Board to buy and export gold, chemical controls, a mercury phase-out over six to seven years from early 2027 — begins, on her own provisional estimate, with registering the at least 10,000 people mining illegally, most of them interior and Maroon miners, rather than with mass expulsion; Ghana has pledged to guide the process (presidential press conference reporting, 9 November 2026). The rainforest evictions of October 2026, by contrast, were directed at foreign settlers with expired permits and were carried out under a court judgment — the counted-in were protected against the unentitled, through process (Mongabay, November 2026; Wisevoter, 12 November 2026).
  • The judgment-implementation commitment of 20 November 2026 — a Kaliña and Lokono Community Development Fund to be established in January 2027 — is the first dated instrument any Surinamese government has announced for executing the 2015 judgment, and the UNDP-supported Land Rights Project 2026–2030, launched in May 2026, is a standing consultation structure with the communities rather than a one-off ceremony (presidential meeting reporting, 20 November 2026; CMC/CANA, 2 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The central counting failure is structural, current, and hers to carry now: Suriname remains the only country in South America that does not legally recognize the collective land rights of its Indigenous and Maroon peoples. Nineteen years after the Inter-American Court's Saramaka judgment (2007) and eleven after Kaliña and Lokono (2015), the collective rights act remains under consideration in the Assembly; the state continues to issue concessions for gold mining, logging, and agribusiness on traditional lands without consent; and Maroon communities in the interior reported increased mercury contamination of their water sources in early 2026 — the counted-out drinking the counted-in economy's waste (UPR civil-society report, April 2026; human-rights country reporting, February 2026). Her government conceded every word of this at Geneva on 2 November 2026 — the concession is honest, and it is also an admission that sixteen months in, the recognition has not moved from promise to law.
  • Her government offered the counted-out a smaller thing than the right, over their formal objection. The Protection of Residential and Living Areas Act's 5-kilometre radius — a temporary ban on new concessions around village centres — was advanced without FPIC and formally rejected by VIDS as a substitute for territorial recognition; Indigenous critics state the obvious arithmetic: their peoples do not live inside 5-kilometre circles but on ancestral territories (UPR civil-society factsheet, October 2026; VIDS-aligned public statements, August 2026). A government counts everyone when the counted get to say what counting them means; on this file, so far, it has decided for them.
  • The fiscal adjustment of 2025 was distributed downward before it was corrected. The return of inflation to double digits — 13.0% at end-2025 — and the weakening currency fell, as inflation does, on wage-earners, pensioners, and the interior poor first; the IMF records the year's overall deficit at 10.0% of GDP and cash buffers sharply reduced (IMF Press Release 26/025, 28 January 2026). Her 2026 allowances and the minimum-wage rise are the documented correction; they are also, on the unions' and pensioners' documented reaction to the August 2026 raise, partial — pensioners asked publicly why they were outside the 15%, and private-sector workers likewise (public reaction to the government–union statement, August 2026).
  • The predecessor's universal promise sits in limbo on her desk. Santokhi's "Royalties for Everyone" programme — a USD 750 certificate for every citizen born before 1 January 2025, 572,186 people eligible — was put on hold by her finance ministry in September 2025 pending evaluation; 61,920 citizens had registered, and the elderly who cashed out early could do so at only one bank because the programme had no statutory basis. Fourteen months later no final decision has been published (Ministry of Finance and Planning via Surinamese press, September 2025; business guidance summary, November 2026). Suspending a legally baseless programme was defensible; leaving its registrants — disproportionately the elderly who acted first — uncounted and unanswered for over a year is a counting failure of the ordinary, administrative kind.

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Coalition programme, campaign and inaugural pledges, and dated public statements vs. the record since 16 July 2025; her predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The debt word — kept, in the open, ahead of schedule. Her government committed to making the deferred 2026–2028 debt repayments manageable and to active liability management rather than default or drift. The record: a liability management operation at the end of 2025 (which is why gross debt reads 106% of GDP — new instruments issued to retire old ones, per the IMF); new bonds in November 2025 and February 2026; and in May–June 2026 the early repayment in full of the restructured Paris Club loans — some €1.2 billion paid, which Finance Minister Adelien Wijnerman stated saves roughly €500 million against the restructured schedule, with the minister herself noting honestly that total debt did not fall structurally because the new bonds replaced the old (IMF Press Release 26/025, 28 January 2026; finance ministry statements via financial press, November 2026). S&P Global Ratings upgraded Suriname from CCC+ to B- with a stable outlook on 9 November 2026, citing the end-2025 restructuring and improved debt management among the grounds (S&P Global Ratings, 9 November 2026).
  • The IMF word — kept as she phrased it. She said before taking office that she was "in discussions with the IMF" but that this did not automatically mean a Santokhi-style aid programme — "We are looking at what is best for Suriname, not what is customary" — and that she supported continued cooperation without repeating the austerity-focused approach (CMC, July 2025). Sixteen months on: the Article IV consultation concluded in January 2026, a long-term macro-fiscal expert was requested and welcomed, and no new Fund programme has been entered. The word was conditional, and the condition is what happened.
  • The purchasing-power word — delivered in instalments, as announced. Inaugural and annual-address commitments to protect purchasing power without raising taxes produced: reduced payroll tax on overtime, vacation pay, and bonuses; a tax cleanup scheme to 31 December 2025; the 2026 allowance ladder for civil servants and the SRD 250 top-up for pensioners, disabled people, and low-income households; the child benefit at SRD 250; the minimum wage at SRD 61.25 per hour from 1 July 2026; and the 15% general civil-service salary increase effective end-September 2026 (annual address summary, September 2025; Surinamese press reporting, 2026; government–union joint statement, 27 August 2026).
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: Santokhi's 2020–2025 tenure delivered the IMF programme to completion (March 2025), the 2023 bond restructuring, and inflation brought down from crisis levels — a substantially kept stabilization word, paid for in austerity that cost him the election; his final programme, Royalties for Everyone, was launched by decree in May 2025 without a statutory basis, weeks before the vote, and is the promise whose suspension now sits on her desk (Test 4). The seat she holds was won against a kept word the voters had stopped wanting to pay for — and the oil word both governments share, first production from GranMorgu in 2028, remains on schedule under hers, with the Petronas Sloanea gas discovery declared commercial in November 2025 (IMF and industry reporting, 2025–2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The discipline word — broken in year one, on a slide she partly inherited. She pledged fiscal discipline — a government that "will not spend money it doesn't have" — while the documented 2025 outcome is an overall deficit of 10.0% of GDP, inflation back to 13.0% by end-2025, cash buffers down from 9.2% to 3.4% of GDP, and the IMF's finding that the slippages "largely reversed" the recent stability gains (inaugural address reporting, July 2025; IMF Press Release 26/025, 28 January 2026). The mitigation is real and stated: the primary balance had already collapsed to −7.8% of GDP by May 2025, before she took office, in an election year under her predecessor — the slide began on his watch and the full-year figure is shared. Her corrective is equally documented: the IMF projects the deficit narrowing to 5.4% of GDP in 2026, and inflation had eased to about 7.3% by September 2026 (IMF projections, 2026; IMF Article IV data via market reporting, November 2026). The word was broken; the repair is in motion; both are on the record.
  • The oil-governance word — pledged with a deadline nature imposed, not yet performed. She has committed, repeatedly and as recently as her 30 September 2026 annual address, that the Savings and Stabilization Fund will be fully operational before the first oil revenue arrives, and outlined its three pots on 4 October 2026 — savings and stabilization, development investment, and a share for housing and healthcare. As of grading, S&P records that none of the 2024 fiscal rules is yet operational, no percentages per pot have been announced, and the local content legislation remains in preparation (S&P Global Ratings, 9 November 2026; presidential statements, September–October 2026). First oil is 2028; the promise is not yet broken, and it is nowhere near kept.
  • The land-rights word — the slowest file against the clearest commitment. At the FPIC ceremony of 14 October 2025 she received the Indigenous protocol and endorsed the stated next steps — demarcation, recognition of collective rights, legal anchoring, with parliament's speaker speaking of a three-year horizon. Thirteen months later the collective rights act is unpassed, the substitute residential-areas act was advanced over the beneficiaries' formal rejection, and her own government told the UPR in November 2026 that implementation of the Moiwana, Saramaka, and Kaliña and Lokono judgments remains a backlog (UNDP Suriname, 14 October 2025; CMC/CANA, 2 November 2026). The 20 November 2026 fund announcement (Test 3) is the first dated counterweight; the word will be graded on whether the fund exists in January 2027 and whether the act follows.
  • The decision word — deferred. She said of Royalties for Everyone at her inauguration that a decision would follow once the government had full information, admitting she did not fully understand the project's structure. The programme was suspended in September 2025 "pending an evaluation"; by November 2026 no evaluation and no decision had been published (inauguration reporting, July 2025; Ministry of Finance and Planning, September 2025). A small promise, precisely the kind a record can check — and it is fourteen months overdue.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED.

Jennifer Geerlings-Simons holds a seat assembled to the exact seat: a six-party coalition with precisely the 34 votes the constitution demands, governing a country of some 640,000 people across the bridge years between an IMF programme's end and an oil boom's beginning. Sixteen months on, the record shows a president whose enforcement machinery points in every direction including inward — her own justice minister resigned in November 2026 under a criminal complaint she publicly refused to bury, state-company and police corruption files moved from internal audits to arrests — and whose grip on the referees is, so far, notable mainly for what she has not done: the predecessor's central bank governor still governs, the press ranks among the freest in the hemisphere, parliament took ten months over her budget and amended her debt law. Against that stands the file that has outlasted five governments: Suriname is still the only South American state that does not recognize the collective land rights of its Indigenous and Maroon peoples, her government answered the court-ordered recognition with a 5-kilometre substitute its beneficiaries formally rejected, and the promise she made receiving their protocol in October 2025 remains unlegislated — though her 20 November 2026 meeting with VIDS and the January 2027 judgment fund are the first dated steps any holder of this seat has announced. Her word splits the same way her country does: the debt was managed exactly as promised and the Paris Club paid off early, while the discipline pledge broke in a 10%-of-GDP deficit year she half inherited, and the oil fund that must exist before 2028 does not yet exist. The grades are all MIXED because the file is sixteen months long and genuinely two-sided; the open files that will move them are named in the dossier: whether the Kaliña and Lokono fund is established in January 2027 and the collective rights act follows, whether the TAS investigation reaches a verdict, whether the fiscal rules are operational before first oil, and whether the suspended Royalties for Everyone programme ever receives the decision she promised its 61,920 registrants.
