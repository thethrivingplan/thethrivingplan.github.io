The Ruler's Test — Ibrahim Traoré, President of Burkina Faso

Subject: Captain Ibrahim Traoré — head of state of Burkina Faso since 30 September 2022. The seat graded is the presidency itself, and the reason is stated plainly: in Burkina Faso's system the president is the executive — head of state, supreme commander of the armed forces, appointer of the prime minister and of the transitional legislature — so there is no higher or separate executive seat to grade. Prime Minister Rimtalba Jean Emmanuel Ouédraogo (in office since 9 December 2024) governs at Traoré's appointment and pleasure. Traoré, an army artillery captain born in 1988, seized power on 30 September 2022 by overthrowing Lieutenant-Colonel Paul-Henri Sandaogo Damiba, who had himself overthrown the elected president, Roch Marc Christian Kaboré, eight months earlier. He was sworn in as transitional president on 21 October 2022 under a Transition Charter that fixed the transition at 21 months, ending 1 July 2024. Two corrections to the brief that commissioned this dossier, recorded here because they change what is being graded. First, the seat is no longer, in law, a "transition" seat at all: the May 2024 amended charter extended the transition by 60 months from 2 July 2024 (to July 2029) and made Traoré eligible to stand in the elections that would end it; then, on 27 March 2026, the appointed legislature adopted a "Charter of the Revolution" that replaced the transition charter, dropped the transition framework and its end-date altogether, and installed his "Progressive and Popular Revolution" as the country's standing order, with no term stated (ARiES Strategic Intelligence Report, April 2026; Maghrebi, October–November 2026). He is therefore graded on just over four years in office, the last eight months of them under a charter that no longer promises an exit. Second, the civil-society file is often reported as "over 1,100 NGOs dissolved"; the documented figures, kept separate as this series keeps all counts separate, are 118 organizations dissolved and 1,056 suspended under the 2025 freedom-of-association law, in decisions running from April to June 2026 (ICNL Civic Freedom Monitor, updated June 2026). The war he inherited and still fights — against Jama'at Nusrat al-Islam wal-Muslimin (JNIM) and Islamic State–Sahel — is the standing condition of every test below, and is graded as context, not as an excuse or a demerit in itself. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test, printed on its card, governs this grading: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them, and casualty figures from different sources are printed as separate counts, never blended. The mandate rule applies: governing on the platform a ruler announced is not graded as a failure in itself — Traoré announced a sovereignty program (French forces out, ECOWAS exit, state control of gold) and a war-first program, and carrying those out is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how they are carried out. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The gold program — the economic centerpiece of his rule — was applied by one published rule to every operator, foreign and Burkinabè alike. The mining code adopted 18 July 2024 raised the state's free-carried stake in new projects from 10% to 15% and gave the state an option to buy up to 30% more, and it was applied across the board: to London-listed Endeavour Mining's former assets, to Australian-listed West African Resources (whose Kiaka stake the state moved to raise toward 50%), and to Lilium Mining, owned by a Burkinabè-American businessman, whose Boungou and Wahgnion mines passed to the state company SOPAMIB with the rest of the five-asset transfer completed in June 2025 (Ecofin Agency, 2025; Engineering News, 12 June 2025; Reuters via Discovery Alert, November 2026). Whatever else the program is, on its face it is not a law with one text for foreigners and another for friends.
  • The penal code adopted on 20 October 2026 raises the price of official theft in the same text that restores the death penalty: embezzlement of public funds above a threshold now carries life imprisonment and a fine of triple the amount taken (Maghrebi, 21 October 2026). The provision is general; no carve-out for office-holders appears in the reporting.
  • Early in the tenure, the ordinary machinery of justice did move against people presented as the government's own supporters — that is precisely what the magistrates' cases in Test 2 were about: a prosecutor in Ouagadougou who ordered police to investigate reported enforced disappearances, a judge in Ziniaré handling the case of a junta supporter implicated in illegal mining that killed about 60 people in a landslide, a prosecutor in Boromo investigating alleged livestock theft by a junta ally and army auxiliaries (HRW, 21 August 2024). The cases were opened. What happened to the magistrates who opened them is the against shelf, here and in Test 2.

Evidence against:

  • The emergency law is two laws in practice. The April 2023 general-mobilization decree — a real war measure on its face — has been used, on a documented pattern, to conscript named critics rather than to staff the war generally: at least a dozen journalists, civil-society activists, and opposition figures were notified of call-up in November 2023 alone, among them the human-rights defender Daouda Diallo (Martin Ennals Award laureate, seized 1 December 2023) and the opposition politician Ablassé Ouédraogo, both of whom later appeared in uniform in army footage before release (HRW, November 2023 and 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024–2026). In March 2025 the leaders of the Journalists' Association — Guezouma Sanogo, Boukari Ouoba, and their colleague Luc Pagbelguem — were detained days after denouncing restrictions on expression and surfaced in military uniform; five journalists and one activist conscripted on this pattern were released only in July 2025 (HRW, 31 July 2025). No comparable documented pattern exists of regime supporters being requisitioned for their speech.
  • The magistrates who applied the law to the government's side were themselves removed by the same instrument. Between 9 and 12 August 2024, at least seven magistrates — four prosecutors, two deputy prosecutors, and an investigative judge — were notified of conscription to security operations in Kaya; six reported to a military base on 14 August 2024 and were not heard from for a period thereafter. A coalition of three magistrates' unions stated the seven were targeted because they "dealt with cases involving people claiming to be staunch supporters of the current government" (HRW, 21 August 2024). A law that conscripts the prosecutor when the accused is a supporter is not one law.
  • Accountability for the state's own violence has no documented output. After the February 2024 Nondin and Soro killings (Test 4), the Ouahigouya prosecutor announced an inquiry on 1 March 2024; as of this grading, no public final report, indictment, or conviction in that file has been located in the record assembled here. No prosecutions of soldiers or VDP members for the documented Solenzo killings of March 2025 appear in the record either — while, in the same period, the state moved with speed against exiled critics (below) and announced coup-plot arrests within days, repeatedly. Speed for one class of case, silence for the other, is the operational definition of a second law.
  • The terrorism law now reaches speech by the government's opponents abroad. On 13 November 2026 the prosecutor general at the Ouagadougou Court of Appeal, Désiré Sawadogo, announced six case files against at least 45 people and entities, with 21 arrest warrants — 14 of them international, with extradition requests sent to Côte d'Ivoire, France, and the United States. Those named include the exiled journalists Ahmed Newton Barry, Issaka Lingani, Inoussa Ouédraogo, Adama Ouédraogo, and Abdoulaye Barry, the activist Naïm Touré, former foreign ministers Djibril Bassolé and Alpha Barry, and the international journalist Wassim Nasr. The charges include complicity in terrorist association and financing terrorism, alongside "glorification of terrorism," defamation, and damage to the country's image (HRW, 18 November 2026; prosecutor's announcement via Africanews, RFI, and BBC reporting, November 2026). Every one of these is an ALLEGATION: none has been tried, the supporting evidence has not been made public, and HRW records that all those targeted had in common that they "exposed serious military abuses during counterinsurgency operations" (HRW, 18 November 2026). A counterterrorism statute whose documented defendants are the war's reporters is being applied as a second law for one side of the argument.
  • Televised confession has repeatedly stood in for process in the plot cases: in January 2026 the security minister presented a detained trader on national television to describe collecting plot funds in Côte d'Ivoire, before any trial (Security Minister Mahamadou Sana's televised address, January 2026, via BBC reporting). The underlying plot allegations are ALLEGATIONs, unverified independently; the presentation of an untried suspect's confession on state TV is not an allegation — it was broadcast.

Why this grade, in plain words: The mining code shows Traoré can write one law and apply it to everyone, which is why this shelf is not empty. But the laws that touch power itself — conscription, counterterrorism, accountability for state violence — run on two tracks: swift and inventive against critics, magistrates who touch supporters, and journalists; inert where the accused wear the state's own uniform. One law at the gold mine, two laws at the courthouse, is a fail on the test as written.

Helped: The Burkinabè treasury and state mining company, by a uniform code that raised the public share of gold rents from every operator. Hurt: Conscripted journalists, activists, and magistrates (named above); exiled reporters and former officials now facing terrorism charges for documented critical work (ALLEGATIONs untried); the families of Nondin, Soro, and Solenzo, whose files have produced no public accountability while plot files produce televised announcements within days.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The referees have not all stopped blowing the whistle, and their rulings are part of this record. On 13 August 2024 the Bobo-Dioulasso High Court ruled the conscription orders against two of the seven magistrates "manifestly illegal" and ordered them not executed. On 6 December 2024 the Ouagadougou administrative court ruled conscription orders against activists unlawful and ordered their suspension. Judges ordered the release of the lawyer Guy Hervé Kam during his five months of detention in 2024. The Ouahigouya prosecutor publicly opened an inquiry into the Nondin and Soro killings on 1 March 2024 (HRW, August 2024; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024; Tom Lantos Human Rights Commission submission, April 2025). A gripped judiciary does not usually rule against the grip in writing, repeatedly. What the executive did with those rulings is the against shelf.
  • The forms of legality are maintained rather than abolished outright: charters are adopted by an assembly, warrants are announced by a prosecutor general at a press conference, the November 2026 warrants went through a specialized anti-terrorism judicial unit (Prosecutor General's announcement, 13 November 2026). The state's coercive acts arrive dressed in legal process, which is more than pure decree — and less than independence, as the rest of this section shows.

Evidence against:

  • The electoral referee was abolished. In 2025 the appointed legislature passed a law abolishing the Independent National Electoral Commission (adopted unanimously, 28 October 2025) and transferring the organization of elections to the ministry of territorial administration — that is, to the executive itself (Afrique Francophone country report, 2026; multiple 2025 reports). There is no longer an independent body between the president and the count.
  • The opposition was abolished as a legal category. Political parties — more than 100 were registered — had been suspended since the 2022 coup; on 29 January 2026 the Council of Ministers decreed all of them dissolved, their assets transferred to the state, and on 9 February 2026 the legislature repealed the laws governing parties, party finance, and the very status of the opposition (Council of Ministers decree, 29 January 2026; assembly vote, 9 February 2026). Civil society followed: under the July 2025 freedom-of-association law, 118 organizations were dissolved and 1,056 suspended in decisions from April to June 2026, and associations are now required to bank exclusively with a state bank (ICNL Civic Freedom Monitor, June 2026). The Journalists' Association was dissolved on 25 March 2025, the day after its president and vice-president were taken (Sahel Horizon, October 2026).
  • Judicial independence was punished in the most literal way available: magistrates who opened cases against government supporters were conscripted to the front (Test 1), and the courts' contrary rulings were simply not executed — the Bobo-Dioulasso court's August 2024 order and the Ouagadougou administrative court's December 2024 suspension order both failed to stop the requisitions they declared illegal (HRW, 21 August 2024; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). A referee whose whistle is answered by his own conscription, and whose written rulings the executive declines to carry out, is not being held without gripping; he is being held up as an example.
  • The media's autonomy was dismantled piece by piece: a dozen outlets or programs suspended on the CIVICUS Monitor's count; foreign broadcasters removed in sequence from 2022 onward; journalists detained, conscripted, or disappeared into claimed conscription — the investigative editor Atiana Serge Oulon, taken in June 2024, was said by the government (October 2024, before the African Commission) to have been requisitioned to the front, while an RSF investigation published in 2026 reports he was in fact secretly detained in Ouagadougou and abused (RSF investigation, 2026 — the government's account and RSF's account are in direct conflict, and both are printed here as their makers state them). In March 2025 Traoré himself declared a "war on communications," warning against information that could "set fire" to the country (statements reported by OCCRP/HRW, March 2025). The UN human rights office was forced to leave the country (HRW, 2026), and in July 2025 Burkina Faso announced, jointly with Mali and Niger, withdrawal from the International Criminal Court — removing the external referee of last resort for the atrocity files in Test 4 (Maghrebi, October 2026).
  • The legislature that adopts his charters, penal codes, and dissolutions is a 71-member appointed body whose recorded votes in this file are unanimous — the party-dissolution repeal, the electoral-commission abolition, and the death-penalty code each passed without a dissenting vote on record (assembly votes, 2025–2026). Unanimity in an appointed chamber is not a check; it is a signature.

Why this grade, in plain words: Every independent referee named in the test has been abolished (the electoral commission), dissolved (the parties, the journalists' association, 118 civic organizations), expelled (the UN human rights office), withdrawn from (the ICC), or punished in person (the magistrates). The surviving courts still rule against him on paper, which is recorded in his favor — and he declines to execute their rulings, which is the grip itself. This is the clearest fail in the dossier.

Helped: The presidency's freedom of action — no institution now stands between Traoré and any decision he wishes to take, including staying. Hurt: The seven conscripted magistrates and the judges whose rulings were defied; voters, who lost both the parties and the independent count; journalists and the 118 dissolved and 1,056 suspended organizations; every future Burkinabè government, which will inherit referees that must be rebuilt from nothing.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Twice, sustained pressure produced a real, if partial, adjustment. In July 2025 the authorities released five journalists and a human-rights activist who had been unlawfully conscripted — an implicit concession that those conscriptions could not be defended as made (HRW, 31 July 2025). And after two years of rupture with ECOWAS, the AES states — with Burkina Faso's foreign minister, Karamoko Jean-Marie Traoré, chairing the AES ministerial side — opened formal negotiations with ECOWAS in Dakar on 17 October 2026 on a cooperation framework, with joint working groups agreed, following earlier consultations in Bamako in May 2025 (Business Day, October 2026). When another option became available — a negotiated framework short of rejoining — he took the meeting. That is the test's standard, met in this instance.
  • Personnel wobbles were answered with personnel changes rather than denial of the problem: in December 2024, after the Barsalogho catastrophe and a deteriorating security year, Traoré replaced his prime minister and reshuffled the government (government announcements, December 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The courts' pushback was answered by defiance, not adjustment (Tests 1–2): two courts declared requisitions illegal in August and December 2024; the requisitions stood, and the practice continued into 2025 and beyond. There is no documented instance in this record of the executive complying with a court order against its security apparatus.
  • The largest documented wobble of the tenure — the evidence of his own forces' massacres — has been answered with blanket denial. When HRW published its 316-page report on 2 April 2026, documenting 57 incidents and at least 1,800 civilians killed between 2023 and 2025 (its separate counts: at least 1,255 killed by the military and VDP in 33 incidents; at least 582 by JNIM), the government's response, issued 5 April 2026 by spokesperson Pingdwendé Gilbert Ouédraogo, dismissed the whole document as "a false report" and "a tissue of conjectures and baseless grave allegations," and questioned HRW's legitimacy to report on the country at all (Government communiqué, 5 April 2026). Communications Minister Rimtalba Jean Emmanuel Ouédraogo had used the same form in 2024, rejecting the Nondin and Soro findings as "baseless" (government statements, April 2024). Denial of a 450-interview record, without a counter-investigation whose results are published, is the definition of doubling down.
  • Barsalogho produced no accounting at all. After JNIM's 24 August 2024 massacre of civilians digging defensive trenches (tolls in Test 4), the government never published a casualty count, announced no public inquiry whose findings have appeared, and the victims' own collective — the Collectif Justice pour Barsalogho — was left to publish its own toll and accuse the authorities of negligence for ordering the digging (Collectif Justice pour Barsalogho statements, 2024; HRW Barsalogho investigation, 2024). The state's public answer was the security minister's vow of retribution against the attackers — an answer to JNIM, not to the families asking why unarmed civilians were digging, on military orders, within reach of a known threat. Witnesses told HRW soldiers required the digging without pay and beat some who refused; Justice Minister Edasso Rodrigue Bayala replied that forced labor is prohibited and the coercion allegations were not proved — the dispute is printed as its makers state it; what is not disputed is that no official accounting followed.
  • The ECOWAS wobble was first answered by refusing the offered door: ECOWAS kept a six-month reversible grace period open from 29 January to 29 July 2025 and continued recognizing Burkinabè documents and free movement unilaterally; the AES rejected the grace proposal outright, and the withdrawal stood (ECOWAS statements, January 2025). The October 2026 talks (for shelf) came twenty-one months later, after the costs of isolation had accumulated — adjustment eventually, refusal first, and the sequence is part of the grade.
  • Criticism from the street and the mosque was answered as disloyalty: the preacher Dr. Mohamed Ishaq Kindo was arrested on 26 May 2026 after criticizing a draft law on religious freedoms (ICNL; Sahel Horizon, 2026), and the April 2025 criticism by the US Africa commander, General Michael Langley — an ALLEGATION that Traoré was using gold reserves for the junta's protection — was answered not with accounts but with a mass rally at which Langley's image was displayed labeled "slave" (AFP/AP reporting, 30 April 2025). Rallying supporters is a political act, not an answer; the gold-reserve accounts Langley asked about have not been published.

Why this grade, in plain words: The pattern across four years is consistent: pushback from courts is defied, pushback from investigators is denied wholesale, pushback from victims is left unanswered, and pushback from abroad is met with rallies. The two genuine adjustments — releasing conscripted journalists in July 2025, and sitting down with ECOWAS in October 2026 — are real and are recorded, but both came late, under accumulated pressure, and neither touched the underlying practice. A ruler who adjusts only at the margins, after refusing every door first, fails this test.

Helped: The five journalists and one activist released in July 2025; Burkinabè traders and travelers, if the ECOWAS framework talks produce a durable arrangement. Hurt: The Barsalogho families, still without an official count or accounting two years on; the courts, whose defied rulings teach every official that orders from Ouagadougou outrank judgments; critics at home, for whom each unanswered report raises the price of speaking.

The no-good-choice clause, applied where it genuinely applies: The security inheritance of September 2022 was a real trap and is recorded as one. The options actually open to any Burkinabè government that month were: continue with French and Western forces whose eight-year presence had coincided with the insurgency's growth (a door the street had already rejected and which had demonstrably not delivered security); attempt a negotiated settlement with JNIM from a position of military collapse; or mass-mobilize — expand the army, arm civilian auxiliaries (the VDP), and seek new suppliers. Each carried real harm, and the mobilization door he chose was, in the moment, a defensible reading of the least-bad options; the general-mobilization framework of April 2023 is therefore recorded as a constraint-driven choice, not a demerit. The clause does not extend to what was done inside that framework: conscripting named critics after courts ruled it illegal, and dissolving every political party rather than merely postponing elections, were choices among visibly less harmful alternatives, and they are graded above on their own merits. The trap explains the mobilization; it does not explain the blacklist.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Fulani herders, the displaced, the besieged towns, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The development side of the program is addressed to the whole population and some of it is delivered fact: the country's first gold refinery (RAFFINOR-BF, ~164 tonnes/year initial capacity) was inaugurated in Ouagadougou on 28 September 2026, keeping processing value at home; a tomato-processing plant opened in December 2024; the government reports a 2025 cereal harvest of about 7.14 million tonnes under its agro-pastoral offensive — a government figure, printed as claimed, not independently verified in this record; and the Patriotic Support Fund reports roughly 497 billion CFA francs raised in citizen contributions to the war effort by February 2026, again a government-reported figure (Sidwaya, 28 September 2026; government and state-media reporting, 2024–2026). S&P Global raised Burkina Faso's sovereign rating from CCC+ to B- on 6 November 2026 on the gold boom (S&P Global Ratings, November 2026) — outside confirmation that the resource program's revenues are real, whatever their distribution.
  • The war effort itself is framed and staffed nationally: VDP recruitment and the mobilization drew from all regions, and the state's public rhetoric counts every Burkinabè as a stakeholder in the reconquest. The counting-out documented below is therefore not the program's stated intent — it is its documented practice in specific files, which is what the test grades.

Evidence against:

  • One community is counted as the enemy. HRW's April 2026 report — based on more than 450 interviews — found that government forces carried out ethnic cleansing against the Fulani, the largely Muslim pastoralist community the authorities have long accused collectively of supporting the insurgents, and documented at least 1,255 civilians killed by the military and VDP in 33 incidents between January 2023 and April 2025, against at least 582 killed by JNIM in the same accounting — the two counts kept separate, as their maker kept them (HRW, None Can Run Away, 2 April 2026). The government rejects the report in full (communiqué, 5 April 2026); the finding stands in this dossier as HRW's documented finding, disputed by the government, and no Burkinabè investigation has published a competing account of the same incidents.
  • The named files: on 25 February 2024, soldiers summarily executed at least 223 civilians, including at least 56 children, in the villages of Nondin (44 killed) and Soro (179 killed) in Yatenga province, in apparent retaliation for an Islamist attack on a nearby camp; HRW compiled victim lists by name. The government rejected the attribution as baseless; the Ouahigouya prosecutor's own preliminary figure, announced 1 March 2024, was about 170 killed across three villages including Komsilga — a separate, official count, printed separately (HRW, April 2024 and April 2026; prosecutor's statement, 1 March 2024). In December 2023, the military and allied militias killed more than 400 civilians in about 16 villages near Djibo during "Operation Tchéfari 2" (HRW, April 2026). On 10–11 March 2025, around Solenzo, the army and VDP massacred at least 130 Fulani civilians — HRW counted 58 bodies in the militias' own videos, a floor, not a total — during Operation Green Whirlwind 2, and found the army "led and participated"; the government on 15 March 2025 denied its forces were involved and called the videos a manipulation (HRW, 12 May 2025; government statement, 15 March 2025). HRW's conclusion that Traoré, as supreme commander, and six senior commanders "may be liable as a matter of command responsibility" is recorded as HRW's legal assessment — an ALLEGATION in this dossier's terms, untried in any court — and it is part of the record that no domestic process has answered it.
  • The besieged and displaced are counted as acceptable losses of the strategy. More than 2 million Burkinabè were internally displaced as of early 2025 (UN data via CRS, May 2025); roughly 1.5 million people remain in areas cut off by insurgent blockade from aid, markets, and services; thousands of schools and hundreds of health centers have closed (CRS, May 2025; humanitarian reporting, 2025). The government's own trench policy put civilians in the line of fire at Barsalogho: on 24 August 2024 JNIM killed the trench-diggers in the deadliest single attack of the war — the counts, kept separate: HRW verified at least 133 dead; the UN estimated at least 200 killed; the Collectif Justice pour Barsalogho states 400; a French government security assessment reported by CNN estimated over 600; JNIM claimed nearly 300, claiming — falsely, on the video evidence HRW reviewed, which showed unarmed civilians — that the dead were militiamen (HRW; UN; CNN/French assessment; SITE Intelligence translation of JNIM's claim, August 2024). The diggers were there on the state's orders; the state has never published its own number.
  • The war's overall arithmetic has moved against the population he promised to protect. ACLED-recorded conflict deaths roughly tripled across his accession: about 6,630 deaths in the three years before the September 2022 coup, against about 17,775 in the three years after (ACLED data, compiled in ARiES Strategic Intelligence Report, April 2026, and ACLED-based conflict trackers, 2026). Territory estimates are contested and are printed as a range with their makers: official estimates put insurgent control at about 30% of the country, described by the US Congressional Research Service as "a potential undercount" (CRS, May 2025); other analyst estimates put the share outside government control at up to 60% (ACLED-based trackers, 2026). On either end of the range, the reconquest has not happened, and the people living in the contested share are counted by neither side's protection.

Why this grade, in plain words: A government can fight a brutal insurgency and still count everyone; this one's documented practice sorts the population into the nation and a suspect community. The Fulani files — named villages, named counts, the army's own videos — are not the fog of war; they are a pattern its own investigators documented and its government answered only with denial. Add two million displaced, besieged towns the state cannot feed, and civilians ordered to dig the trench they died in, and the accounting fails: some Burkinabè are counted as the threat, not as the people.

Helped: Gold-sector workers and the treasury, by the refinery and the state mining build-out; farmers reached by the agro-pastoral program, on the government's own figures. Hurt: Fulani communities above all — the dead of Nondin, Soro, the Djibo villages, and Solenzo, and the survivors displaced en masse in the Boucle du Mouhoun; the more than 2 million displaced; the residents of blockaded towns; the Barsalogho diggers and their families.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 30 September 2022, with dates; the predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The sovereignty program — delivered as announced, on schedule. He said French forces would go: they completed their withdrawal in February 2023. He said Burkina Faso would leave ECOWAS: the withdrawal was announced jointly on 28 January 2024 and took effect 29 January 2025, and the AES Confederation with Mali and Niger was signed on 6 July 2024 (ECOWAS statements, January 2025). He said the state would take back the gold: the 2024 mining code, the SOPAMIB transfers completed June 2025, the Kiaka stake increase in motion, and the first domestic refinery inaugurated 28 September 2026 all happened as described (Ecofin Agency; Engineering News; Sidwaya, 2025–2026). Under the mandate rule, governing on his announced platform is word-keeping, and on this platform his word and his record match.
  • The war-first priority — kept as a priority. He promised the war would come before politics, and it has: mass VDP recruitment, drone and equipment purchases from new suppliers, a citizen-funded Patriotic Support Fund, and his own repeated refusal to schedule politics ahead of security. The priority was kept; the result it was promised to produce is the against shelf.
  • Personal austerity, as reported: Burkinabè state media and his supporters state that Traoré has drawn only his army captain's salary since 2022, declining the presidential salary. This is printed as a widely repeated official claim; no independent audit of the presidency's accounts exists in the record assembled here, and it is graded as a claim, not a verified fact.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The transition word — broken in three stages, each documented. Stage one: the October 2022 charter he swore to uphold fixed the transition at 21 months, ending 1 July 2024, and Traoré said in his first days, "We did not come to continue [in power]" and "We will not be here for long" (radio interview, October 2022). Stage two: on 25 May 2024, weeks before the deadline, a national consultation boycotted by most political parties adopted the amended charter — 60 more months from 2 July 2024, and, for the first time, eligibility for Traoré himself to stand in the elections at the end (national assises, 25 May 2024; International IDEA country report, May 2024). Stage three: on 27 March 2026 the appointed assembly replaced the transition charter with the Charter of the Revolution, which drops the transition and its end-date entirely and frames his rule as a standing revolution with no term (ARiES, April 2026; Maghrebi, 2026). On 3 April 2026 he said on state television that democracy "isn't for us" and that citizens should forget about it (state television, 3 April 2026). The word given in 2022 was a short transition back to constitutional order; the record in 2026 is an indefinite revolutionary mandate. This is the central broken promise of the tenure, and it is not close.
  • The reconquest word — not kept on any published measure. At his swearing-in on 21 October 2022 he vowed to "win back our territory and dignity" from the insurgents, and the coup itself was justified by Damiba's failure on exactly this point. Four years on: ACLED-recorded deaths have roughly tripled against the three pre-coup years (about 6,630 to about 17,775 — ACLED data as compiled, Test 4); the displaced exceed 2 million; insurgent control is estimated at 30% of territory on the official figure CRS calls a potential undercount, and higher elsewhere; JNIM attacked the besieged garrison town of Djibo in May 2025, reportedly killing over 100 (CRS, May 2025). Governments do not control war's outcomes, and the Test does not grade him for the insurgency's existence — it grades the distance between the word ("reconquest," offered as the coup's justification) and the record. The distance is the largest in this dossier after the transition file.
  • The plot announcements — a word that cannot be checked, used on a schedule. The government has announced a long series of foiled plots: a "major plot" based in Côte d'Ivoire on 21 April 2025 (arrests of senior officers followed; Côte d'Ivoire rejects the accusations — ALLEGATIONs, unverified independently); the January 2026 plot attributed to Damiba, announced 6–7 January 2026 as the "23rd attempted destabilization" since 2022, with several arrests and televised confession material (Security Minister's address, January 2026). Damiba himself was handed over by Togo on 17 January 2026 and faces proceedings in Burkina Faso — his guilt on any charge is an ALLEGATION until a court rules. A security claim that recurs at moments of pressure, whose evidence is not published and whose trials do not conclude on any public record assembled here, cannot be entered as kept or broken; it is recorded as unverifiable, and its repeated use as the stated reason for arrests is part of the Test 1 and Test 2 record.
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: Damiba (January–September 2022) took power promising to restore security and signed an ECOWAS-agreed timetable for elections by 2024; he delivered neither and was removed by Traoré on that stated ground. The seat Traoré holds was taken, explicitly, on the argument that the previous holder's word on security and transition had failed. The standard he invoked against Damiba is the standard applied above.

Why this grade, in plain words: On the platform he announced to the region — sovereignty, gold, war first — his word is good, and this dossier says so. But the two promises that founded the seat itself, the short transition and the reconquest, have both been reversed by the record: the transition was extended, then abolished as a concept, by instruments he signed; the territory is less held, and the war dead roughly tripled, across the same years. A ruler is graded on the word he gave to get the seat before the word he gave after he had it.

Helped: Burkinabè who wanted the French departure, the ECOWAS exit, and the gold program — they got what was announced, which is not nothing; it is the strongest shelf in this dossier. Hurt: Every Burkinabè voter promised a transition ending 1 July 2024 — the promise was the legal basis of the regime's first charter; soldiers and VDP recruits and their families, who bore the cost of a reconquest that has not arrived; the country's future candidates and parties, dissolved before any election could test the word against a vote.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Ibrahim Traoré is graded on four years and two months, the last eight of them under a Charter of the Revolution that no longer calls his rule a transition or names an end to it. The grades are what they are because the record, shelf by shelf, is unusually one-directional. On one law: the gold code was applied to foreign and Burkinabè owners alike — and the conscription decree was applied to critics, journalists, and the magistrates who prosecuted his supporters, while the state's own massacre files produced no public accountability in the same years; two tracks, one for power's friends and one for its questioners. On gripping: the electoral commission abolished, the parties dissolved and their assets taken, 118 civic organizations dissolved and 1,056 suspended, the journalists' association dissolved the day after its leaders were taken, the UN human rights office expelled, the ICC abandoned, and judges who ruled against the requisitions ignored and in cases conscripted — the referees were not influenced, they were removed. On the wobble: courts defied, a 316-page documented record dismissed as fiction in a single communiqué, Barsalogho's families left without even an official death toll, and the only adjustments — journalists released in July 2025, ECOWAS talks opened in October 2026 — arriving late and at the margins. On counting everyone: HRW's documented finding of ethnic cleansing against the Fulani, with its separate counts printed above (at least 1,255 civilians killed by state forces and VDP in 33 incidents; at least 582 by JNIM), the named massacres at Nondin and Soro (at least 223, including 56 children, on HRW's named lists; the prosecutor's separate preliminary count was about 170 across three villages), the Djibo villages (more than 400), Solenzo (at least 130, the government denying involvement), and Barsalogho, where JNIM killed civilians the state had ordered into a trench — 133 verified dead at minimum, 200 on the UN estimate, 400 on the victims' collective's count, over 600 on a French assessment, the state itself publishing no number at all. On his word: the sovereignty platform was delivered exactly as announced — French forces out by February 2023, ECOWAS exited 29 January 2025, the gold taken into state hands through 2026 — and the founding promises were not: the 21-month transition became five more years, then no transition at all; the reconquest became a war whose ACLED-recorded dead roughly tripled, from about 6,630 in the three years before his coup to about 17,775 in the three years after.

The two axes, named as the method requires. Word-keeping and helped/hurt cross in this record in three of the four possible ways. Kept his word and it helped people: the gold program — announced, legislated, and applied uniformly; it raised the public share of the country's main export and drew an S&P upgrade to B- in November 2026. Kept his word and it still hurt people: the ECOWAS withdrawal and the war-first mobilization — both delivered as promised; the first put Burkinabè citizens' regional rights and the country's trade lifelines at risk (mitigated, in the event, by ECOWAS's unilateral decision to keep recognizing their documents), and the second was executed in part through the conscription of critics and the arming of auxiliaries whose massacres fill Test 4. Broke his word and it hurt people: the transition — the promise of a short road back to constitutional order, reversed in stages he signed, hurt every voter, party, and candidate the promise was made to, and removed the only scheduled mechanism by which his rule could be peacefully ended. Broke his word and it helped people: not documented in this record — the transition's extension was defended as necessary for security, but on the published measures (deaths, displacement, territory) security worsened across the extension, so the help that would complete this crossing is asserted by the government and not documented. The file that would most change these grades is named plainly: published investigations with verdicts in the Nondin, Soro, and Solenzo cases; execution of the courts' rulings on requisitions; and a dated, binding electoral calendar under an independent commission. Until then, the Sun remains the only complete hold in this series, and this card stands as the third straight fail — the first by a ruler who arrived promising, in writing and on the record, to leave.
