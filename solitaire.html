Klondike — the classic game. Build each suit from Ace up to King on the four home piles.

All four home piles are complete — Ace to King, every suit.

How to move cards: tap a card, then tap where it goes — or drag it there. Mouse works the same. Double-tap a card that can go home and it flies to its home pile. Tap the face-down stock pile to deal.

How to play — tap to open
  • Goal: move all 52 cards to the four home piles (top right), one pile per suit, in order Ace, 2, 3 … King.
  • Columns: build downward in alternating colours — a red 7 goes on a black 8, and so on. Only a King (or a run starting with a King) can fill an empty column.
  • Moving runs: any face-up run that is already in correct downward alternating order moves together — tap or drag its bottom card.
  • Stock: tap the face-down pile (top left) to deal onto the waste pile. Only the top waste card can be played. When the stock runs out, tap it again to turn the waste pile back over — if your redeal rule allows.
  • Turning cards: when you clear the face-up cards off a column, its top face-down card turns over automatically.
  • Undo takes back one move at a time, as far back as the deal. Hint looks at the real position and shows the real choices it can find — when there is more than one way to play, it lists them with what each one gains and what it costs, and it says plainly when there is only one move, or none.
  • Stuck? If no moves remain and the stock is finished, the game is blocked — Undo to try a different line, or deal a new game.
How strong players think — tap to open

Solitaire is luck and choices mixed. You can’t change the cards you’re dealt, but these habits — the ones strong players lean on — make the most of the choices you do get. All in plain words:

  • Turn over face-down cards first. A hidden card can’t help you and might be exactly the card you need. When you have a choice of moves, pick the one that turns a face-down card over. The longest column usually hides the most cards, so working there first often opens the game up fastest.
  • An empty column is only as good as the King that fills it. Only a King can start a new column, so before you clear a column out, check that a King is showing somewhere, ready to move in. Emptying a column with no King ready just leaves a gap you can’t use.
  • Free buried Kings. A King sitting under other cards is stuck — it can’t start a column until the cards on top of it move. Moving those cards off it, when you safely can, gives you a King ready for the next empty column.
  • Don’t cover a card that’s ready to go home. If the top card of a column can already move to its home pile, send it home before you build on top of it. Covering it means digging it out again later.
  • Think twice before breaking up a run you already built. Splitting a run — a set of cards you’ve stacked in the right order — to move part of it somewhere else rarely gains anything unless it turns over a face-down card. If it doesn’t reveal anything new, it usually just shuffles the same cards around.
  • Small cards can go home early; bigger ones, wait a little. Aces and 2s are always safe to send home — nothing ever needs to sit on them. A bigger card, though, might still be needed as a landing spot for the card one below it in the other colour, so strong players check before sending it home.
  • Work the stock steadily. When no moves show, go through the stock pile rather than staring at the same cards. New cards are new chances — and in Draw Three, remember which cards are buried under the top one, because playing the top card brings the next one up.
  • There is rarely only one right move. Most positions offer two or three real ways to play, and they trade different things: playing the waste card now clears the waste, but doing a column move first can keep a landing spot open for a card still to come; emptying a column makes room for a King, but it can also break up a run you had already built. Strong players weigh the trade-off, not just the first move that fits. A single move is only “the” move when it really is the only legal or sensible one.

The Hint button puts these ideas to work on your actual game — at Light and Coach it lays out the real choices when there is more than one, each with its trade-off in plain words, and names a single way only when there is genuinely one option.
