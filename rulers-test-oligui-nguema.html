The Ruler's Test — Brice Oligui Nguema, President of Gabon

Subject: Brice Clotaire Oligui Nguema — President of Gabon. The seat graded is the presidency, which in Gabon is both head of state and head of government: the constitution adopted in November 2024 abolished the post of prime minister and established a pure presidential system, so executive power — government, budget, and command of the armed forces — sits in this one seat. Oligui Nguema, born 3 March 1975, commander of the Republican Guard from 2020 and a cousin of the man he overthrew, took power on 30 August 2023 at the head of the Committee for the Transition and Restoration of Institutions (CTRI), hours after electoral authorities announced in the middle of the night that President Ali Bongo Ondimba had won a third term with about 64% of the vote in the 26 August 2023 election — a count the opposition branded fraudulent, conducted amid an internet shutdown and a curfew. He was sworn in as transitional president on 4 September 2023. The transition he led produced an Inclusive National Dialogue (April 2024), a new constitution approved by referendum on 16 November 2024, and a presidential election on 12 April 2025, which he won and which converted his transitional tenure into an elected seven-year term, renewable once, running to 2032; he was inaugurated on 3 May 2025 as the first president of the Fifth Republic. He is graded on the whole holding since 30 August 2023 — just over three years — weighted to the elected term since May 2025. The Bongo era he ended (Omar Bongo, 1967–2009; Ali Bongo, 2009–2023 — 56 years of one family) appears as context where the record he inherited bears directly on a test. Premise check against the brief: the expected premise is confirmed in substance, with three corrections of detail recorded here. (1) The official final result of the April 2025 election, certified by the Constitutional Court on 25 April 2025, is 94.85% for Oligui Nguema (588,074 votes), not the 90.35% of the provisional results announced on 13 April; both figures are printed in this dossier as separate sourced figures, never blended. Turnout is likewise reported in several non-identical official and observer figures (70.4%, 70.69%, 70.1%), each attributed where used. (2) The November 2024 referendum approved the constitution with 91.64% "yes" on the Constitutional Court's figures; referendum turnout is reported as 53.54% by the interior ministry's contemporaneous reporting and as about 71% in some later summaries — the figures conflict and are not reconciled in the record assembled here. (3) The legislative elections the brief dates to "August 2025" were in fact held on 27 September and 11 October 2025 (with re-runs into 2 November 2025); August 2025 was the transition's original deadline month, not the polling date. No 2026 change of seat-holder is documented: as of November 2026 Oligui Nguema remains president, most recently reshuffling his government in January 2026 and again in October 2026 (Africa Risk Consulting monthly briefings, 2026). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test, printed verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Governing on the platform the voters elected is NOT inherently a failure and is never graded as one; the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Every test closes with a plain-words Why and a named Helped/Hurt ledger. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The flagship prosecution of the new era ran against the old ruling family through a court, to verdicts. On 11–12 November 2025 a special criminal court in Libreville convicted former first lady Sylvia Bongo Ondimba and her son Noureddin Bongo Valentin — tried in absentia in London exile — of embezzlement of public funds, money laundering, concealment, forgery, and criminal association, sentencing each to 20 years, a fine of 100 million CFA francs, and damages orders reported by OCCRP as 1.2 trillion CFA francs against Noureddin Bongo Valentin plus a further 1 trillion CFA francs jointly for "moral harm" to the state; nine of ten associates tried immediately after were also convicted, with sentences up to 15 years (OCCRP, November 2025; International IDEA GSoD tracker, November 2025; Reuters via TradingView, 12 November 2025). Prosecutors put the sum diverted at 4.9 billion CFA francs (~USD 8.7 million) on the IDEA tracker's account; other reporting describes alleged acquisitions far larger — the figures are the prosecution's, printed as such.
  • The anti-corruption instrument also reached inside his own government. On 3 December 2025 his transitional tourism minister, Pascal Ogowé Siffon, was placed under house arrest on suspicion of embezzling nearly USD 18 million (about 10 billion CFA francs) of ministry funds, with a reported conflict-of-interest allegation concerning a contract awarded to his own company — an ALLEGATION, not a verdict; no final court outcome is documented in the record assembled here (2025 in Gabon chronology; Africa Press reporting, December 2025). A June 2026 bribery investigation linked to a Gunvor financing deal with Gabon drew international scrutiny of the new government's own circle (Africa Risk Consulting, June 2026 briefing summary).
  • The August 2025 amnesty cut both ways across the old divide: the order of 12 August 2025 granted a general amnesty not only to the participants in his own 2023 coup but also to the participants in the failed 2019 coup attempt against Ali Bongo, led by Lieutenant Kelly Ondo Obiang, who had been sentenced to 15 years by the Bongo regime's Special Military Court in 2021 and was released on 30 August 2025; the order excludes economic offences and serious human-rights violations (International IDEA GSoD tracker, August 2025).
  • The old ruling party was not banned or barred: the Parti Démocratique Gabonais (PDG) contested the 2025 elections and won 17 of 145 National Assembly seats, and the National Dialogue's April 2024 recommendation that former ruling-party members be barred from elections for three years was omitted from the constitution put to referendum (International IDEA GSoD tracker, April 2024 and October 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The rules of the founding election were written around one man's candidacy. The Transitional Charter barred leading figures of the transitional bodies from the presidential race but made an exception for Oligui Nguema himself — an exception the opposition flagged from the start of the April 2024 National Dialogue (AFP reporting, April 2024; NIIA commentary, May 2025). The January 2025 electoral code then allowed members of the security forces and magistrates to run for office — the provision under which a serving general could stand — and electoral administration sat with the Ministry of the Interior rather than an independent commission (2025 Gabonese presidential election record; Africa Hot analysis, November 2025).
  • The same rulebook excluded his best-known potential rivals by category. The electoral code set a presidential age limit of 70, barring the best-known opposition names, Albert Ondo Ossa and Pierre Claver Maganga Moussavou (Civicus Monitor, 2025). The constitution's eligibility rules — born Gabonese to a Gabonese parent, no dual citizenship within three years of standing, a spouse meeting the same tests, three years' residence — were applied to reject the candidacy of Jean-Rémy Yama, the union leader widely described as the most credible civilian opposition figure, over parental-citizenship and marriage documentation; the Constitutional Court confirmed the rejection, and the Interior Ministry and the Court excluded him again from the legislative elections (Civicus Monitor, 2025; ARIES/Africa Center summary, May 2026). The spouse rule also by itself disqualifies Ali Bongo, whose wife is French — a rule whose general form (no dynastic succession) is defensible and whose incidence falls, in every documented case, on someone other than the incumbent.
  • He exempted his own seizure of power from the law he enforces against others' financial crimes. The 12 August 2025 amnesty — a campaign promise, credited as kept in Test 5 — expunges convictions, halts prosecutions, and bars future prosecutions for the 2023 coup he led: the men who annulled an election, dissolved the institutions, and detained the sitting president face no legal accounting for any of it, by their own order (International IDEA GSoD tracker, August 2025). Whatever its reconciliation rationale, at the source there are two laws: one for the coup-maker, one for everyone else.
  • The one prosecution of a major opponent in the elected term rests on a foundation critics across the record describe as grossly disproportionate. Alain Claude Bilie-By-Nze — Ali Bongo's last prime minister, runner-up in April 2025 with 3.11% — was arrested at his home on 15 April 2026 in an investigation into alleged breach of trust and fraud dating to 2008, over a private claim of 5 million CFA francs (about €7,600) advanced for the 2008 National Festival of Cultures. His lawyers state he spent 100 days in "political and arbitrary" solitary confinement in a five-square-metre cell without light, that they were denied access to the case file, that six months after arrest he had not been questioned once by the investigating judge, and that on 5 October 2026 the judge extended his detention by a further six months; the Libreville Court of Appeal rejected a request to annul the proceedings on 2 June 2026 (Reuters, 24 July 2026; RFI via Genocide Watch republication, 15 October 2026). The underlying 2008 claim is an ALLEGATION, unproven; the political character of the prosecution is the characterization of his lawyers and his party, Ensemble pour le Gabon, recorded here as contested — President Oligui Nguema denied any executive involvement in a France 24 interview and insisted the judiciary is independent (Legalbrief/Africanews reporting, June 2026). The documented procedural facts — an 18-year-old €7,600 private dispute, six months' detention without a single judicial questioning — are not themselves disputed in the record assembled here.

Why this grade: The prosecutions are real and reach both the old family and, in the Siffon case, his own cabinet — that is why this is not a record of pure impunity. But Test 1 grades the source, and at the source the law was personalized three times over: an exception in the charter so he could run, eligibility and age rules that removed the rivals with name recognition, and an amnesty that placed his own coup beyond the law's reach. A ruler who writes himself into the exceptions has not given by one law, however even-handed some later cases appear.

Helped: The Gabonese treasury and public, insofar as the November 2025 verdicts establish a court-ordered recovery claim against the former first family (damages orders totaling over 2 trillion CFA francs on OCCRP's account, collection not documented); Lieutenant Kelly Ondo Obiang and the 2019 coup participants, freed under the same amnesty that covered the 2023 coup; the PDG as a party, which was allowed to compete and survive rather than being banned. Hurt: Jean-Rémy Yama, excluded from both the presidential and legislative races on documentation grounds under rules written during the transition; Albert Ondo Ossa and Pierre Claver Maganga Moussavou, barred by the new age-70 limit; Alain Claude Bilie-By-Nze, detained since 15 April 2026 on an unproven 2008 claim (ALLEGATION) with the procedural record stated above; Sylvia Bongo Ondimba and Noureddin Bongo Valentin, convicted in absentia in a two-day trial they and their lawyers denounce as predetermined — a characterization the court's president, Maixent Essa Assoumou, publicly rejected ("absolutely not an act of revenge, but a restoration of order") — and who allege, in a complaint filed in France in May 2024, that they were tortured in detention, an ALLEGATION President Oligui Nguema has denied.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The transition's referees were, at the decisive moments, allowed to say the election had happened and to certify it, and outside referees were admitted to watch. The Commonwealth Observer Group, led by former Seychelles president Danny Faure, found the 12 April 2025 election "largely reflected the will of the people who voted" and was conducted "in a credible, transparent and inclusive manner" (Commonwealth Ministerial Action Group statement, July 2025); the African Union Peace and Security Council on 30 April 2025 welcomed the transition's completion and lifted Gabon's suspension, and the United States lifted its coup-related sanctions after certifying a democratically elected government had taken office (AU PSC communiqué 1277, 30 April 2025; US State Department via reporting, May 2025). Citizen observers from the Réseau des Observateurs Citoyens reported good, transparent conditions in about 98% of polling stations observed (Deutsche Afrika Stiftung press review, April 2025).
  • The press environment measurably loosened across the transition. Gabon rose from 121st in RSF's World Press Freedom Index in 2020 to 41st in 2025 (score 70.65) and stood 43rd in 2026 (score 70.57); RSF's delegation was received by the president in September 2025, exiled journalists were able to return, and RSF itself called the progress "a success story that should be highlighted" while pressing its recommendations (RSF via IFEX, 10 September 2025; RSF Index 2026 via State Media Monitor).
  • The Constitutional Court did, in the legislative elections, annul results: irregularities including missing ballots and falsified results led to annulments, re-runs in several constituencies (18 October, and a re-run runoff on 2 November 2025), and extensive litigation before the Court declared results for 137 of 145 seats on 1 November 2025 (International IDEA GSoD tracker, October 2025; UN Secretary-General's UNOCA report, 28 November 2025). A court that annuls some of the ruling party's wins is not a pure rubber stamp on election day itself.

Evidence against:

  • The constitutional architecture itself centralizes the seat. The 2024 constitution abolished the prime minister, creating a pure presidential system in which the president appoints the vice-presidents, may dissolve the National Assembly once per term, and holds entrenched emergency powers; the presidency's term was lengthened from five years to seven at the same time as a two-term limit was introduced (2024 constitutional referendum record; Civicus Monitor, 2025). The referee structure was staffed by the player: the members of the Constitutional Court that approved Oligui Nguema's candidacy in March 2025 had all been appointed by him (Civicus Monitor, 2025; ARIES/Africa Center summary, May 2026: "Constitutional Court packed by Oligui").
  • Electoral administration was moved into the executive. Under the January 2025 electoral code, elections are organized by the Ministry of the Interior — the ministry that also announced the results — a transfer from an independent electoral commission that transparency advocates flagged at the time and that remained in force for the September–October 2025 legislative elections, in which the same ministry excluded Yama (Africa Hot analysis, November 2025; Civicus Monitor, 2025).
  • The legislative outcome removed the remaining counterweight. His party, the Democratic Union of Builders (UDB), founded only in July 2025, won the National Assembly by 101 seats of 145 on the Constitutional Court's declared figures (International IDEA, October 2025) — some contemporaneous counts put the UDB at 102; the figures are printed separately and not blended — and then 45 of 70 Senate seats on the Brill yearbook's account, or 47 of 70 in the UN Secretary-General's report's first-round account; again, separate sourced figures. Legislative turnout was about 34%, against 70%-plus for the presidential vote (Coface country file, January 2026). One party personally founded by the president now holds the presidency, both chambers, and most municipalities.
  • The media referee was used, in the elected term, to switch off the public square. The Haute Autorité de la Communication (HAC) — whose nine members are all appointed by the government under a July 2023 law that stripped journalists of their two seats, and which RSF describes as sorely lacking independence — on 17 February 2026 suspended social media platforms nationwide "until further notice," citing defamatory and hateful content and threats to social cohesion, during a nationwide teachers' strike; on 2 March 2026 its president announced action against the VPNs citizens were using to circumvent the suspension (Civicus Monitor update to 24 October 2026; Hivos EU SEE alerts, February–March 2026). The suspension lasted 248 days, until 24 October 2026, affecting by Global Voices' count more than 1.84 million people, and was lifted only under a new framework — presidential Ordinance n° 0011/PR/2026 of 26 February 2026, adopted during parliamentary recess — requiring users to provide verified names, addresses, and ID numbers, effectively ending online anonymity (Global Voices, 11 November 2026; AFP via The Nation, October 2026). On 10 August 2026 the HAC also indefinitely suspended the online news site Info 241 over an article it deemed injurious to the president and his family (Civicus Monitor, 2026 update).
  • Journalists and union voices continued to be summoned and detained through security channels rather than press law. RSF recorded at least four journalists called in for questioning by security and intelligence forces in the first nine months of 2025 (RSF via IFEX, September 2025). Civicus documents the 15 January 2025 night arrest of Gabon Infos editor-in-chief Chartrin Ondimba by the presidential special services directorate after a blog post about a presidential aide's daughter's lifestyle; AFP-sourced reporting in 2026 records three independent online journalists arrested and detained for several days in August 2025, October 2025, and January 2026 after critical content, including Roland Olouba Oyabi of Gabon Mail Info on 15 January 2026 (Civicus Monitor, 2025 and 2026 updates). On 19–20 January 2026 the gendarmerie's investigations directorate arrested teachers' movement figures Marcel Libama and Simon Ndong Edzo, who were taken via the presidency to the military counter-intelligence service (B2) before civil-society pressure secured their provisional release (EU SEE alert, January 2026; Civicus Monitor, 2026 update).

Why this grade: Some referees functioned — observers were admitted, results were certified and in places annulled, and the press index genuinely improved during the transition. But the grip is structural, not episodic: he appointed the court that cleared his candidacy, moved election administration into his own interior ministry, abolished the premiership, built a party that holds both chambers, and presides over a media regulator that suspended the nation's social media for eight months and readmitted it only under compulsory identification. Independence was not merely punished in instances; the institutions were redesigned so that less of it exists. The 2026 record — the Bilie-By-Nze detention (Test 1), the Info 241 suspension, the January 2026 arrests — shows the design in use.

Helped: The UDB and its candidates, who now hold 101 (or 102, on a conflicting count) of 145 Assembly seats and a Senate majority; the executive itself, whose ordinances (including the February 2026 social-media framework) take effect immediately during recess; returning exiled journalists and the wider press corps during the transition window, when conditions measurably improved (RSF Index, 121st in 2020 to 41st in 2025). Hurt: The Constitutional Court's and ACER's institutional independence, compromised at appointment; the HAC's regulated public — more than 1.84 million social-media users cut off for 248 days (Global Voices count) and readmitted only on identified terms; Info 241, suspended indefinitely in August 2026; journalists Chartrin Ondimba and Roland Olouba Oyabi and two further online journalists detained in August and October 2025 (role-described in AFP reporting), each after critical content; Marcel Libama and Simon Ndong Edzo, arrested by military-security channels for supporting a lawful strike.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The founding wobble — international suspension after an unconstitutional seizure of power — was answered by completing a published timetable faster than promised, in the open. The African Union suspended Gabon on 31 August 2023; the transition delivered the National Dialogue (April 2024), the constitutional referendum (16 November 2024), and the presidential election on 12 April 2025, four months ahead of the August 2025 deadline in the transitional charter; the AU lifted the suspension on 30 April 2025, the Commonwealth readmitted Gabon in July 2025, and the United States lifted its sanctions (AU PSC communiqué 1277; Commonwealth CMAG statement; International IDEA GSoD tracker, April 2025).
  • Foreign pressure over the detained former president was accommodated, not defied. After talks between Angolan President João Lourenço, then chairing the African Union, and Oligui Nguema, Ali Bongo — held under house arrest for over 20 months — was released with Sylvia and Noureddin Bongo on the night of 15–16 May 2025 and flown to Luanda, then on to London; Gabonese prosecutor Eddy Minang stated the release of Sylvia and Noureddin was provisional and proceedings would continue, which they did, to the November 2025 verdicts (Angolan presidency statement, 16 May 2025; AFP reporting, May 2025). Bilie-By-Nze's counter-reading — that the president "had to bow to international demands" — is recorded as an interested party's characterization; on either reading, the pushback produced an adjustment.
  • The street's largest pushback of the elected term ended in negotiation, releases, and a protocol. The teachers' strike launched 10 December 2025 by SOS Éducation, SENA, and SYNETECPRO over a decade-long wage freeze, late pay, and precarious conditions spread nationwide and, by February 2026, toward health, higher education, and broadcasting; the government called the demands "legitimate," convened emergency negotiations on 6 January 2026, provisionally released the arrested union figures Libama and Ndong Edzo under civil-society pressure, and reached a protocol agreement with the unions on 28 January 2026, with a fast-track review of some 3,000 teachers' files and an increased education budget following in April 2026 (Civicus Monitor, 2026 update; EU SEE alert, January 2026; News Central TV, 27 January 2026). Teachers voted on 31 January 2026 to extend the strike despite the protocol — the adjustment was partial, and the file was still live months later — but the door taken was negotiation, not only force.
  • The social-media suspension, however imposed, was eventually reversed under sustained civil pressure rather than made permanent: lifted 24 October 2026 after 248 days, following direct discussions with the platform companies (HAC statement via AFP, October 2026). And when his November 2026 road demolitions in Libreville met grief and protest, the response on the record is a top-up: on 9 November 2026 he added 4 billion CFA francs, bringing the compensation allocation for 430 households in Diba-Diba, Okala, and Lac Bleu to 7.5 billion CFA francs (about USD 12.8 million), with titled, serviced plots at Malibé, 1er Campement, and Ntoum promised to each family and an instruction that officials report case by case "until the last family is settled" (Agence Gabonaise de Presse via Africa Business Insight, November 2026).
  • On the no-good-choice clause, applied by its mechanics to the founding moment: on 30 August 2023 the options that actually existed were (a) accept an announced result — about 64% for Ali Bongo, declared at night amid an internet shutdown, a curfew, and no foreign press accreditation — and with it a third term for a president whose capacity since his 2018 stroke was widely questioned; (b) contest it in the street against a regime with a documented history of post-election violence (2016), risking the bloodshed the CTRI said it acted to prevent; or (c) military intervention to annul the count. Each carried real harm. The coup as executed was bloodless — no deaths are documented in the record assembled here — and the trap itself (a captured electoral process under a 56-year dynasty) was not of Oligui Nguema's building, though he had served that regime as Republican Guard commander and is a Bongo cousin. Graded by the clause: the seizure itself is recorded as the least-harm door actually open in the moment, and is not held against him in this test. What the clause does not cover — and what Tests 1, 2, and 5 grade separately — is what he built afterward: the self-exemption, the tailored eligibility rules, and the amnesty. A man may take the least-bad door out of a trap and still be graded on the house he builds on the other side.

Evidence against:

  • The first answer to the teachers' wobble was the security answer. Two union figures were arrested by the gendarmerie and military counter-intelligence in January 2026 (Test 2), and when strike content spread on TikTok and Facebook, the state's response on 17 February 2026 was to suspend social media nationwide indefinitely — answering a wage dispute by closing the channels the dispute was visible on. The Constitutional Court, petitioned by four citizens on 23 February 2026, ruled on 11 March 2026 that it had no authority over the suspension and directed them to the administrative courts; the suspension nonetheless ran eight months (Civicus Monitor, 2026 update). When another option became available — targeted enforcement against unlawful content under the existing Communication Code, the route RSF had recommended for press offences in September 2025 — it was not the route taken; a blanket suspension was. Considering-and-declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence under the Test; here the record shows no documented consideration of the narrower option at all before the blanket measure.
  • Pushback through the ballot's margins was answered with tighter rules, not looser ones. After a presidential election whose fairness the runner-up questioned, the June 2025 party law (Law n°016/2025, promulgated 27 June 2025) imposed a 12,000-member threshold and nationwide presence for party recognition and allowed suspension of parties for "disturbance of public order" — provisions civil-society organizations assess as disproportionately excluding emerging movements, applied in practice to Yama's PNTP, whose gatherings were banned or dispersed (Civicus Monitor, 2025; EU SEE Enabling Environment Snapshot, July 2025). In November 2026, a contested amendment to that party law drew a formal call from a bloc of five opposition parties for the National Assembly to withdraw it (Risk Terminal country reading, 16 November 2026) — the wobble is live at grading.
  • Criticism from the runner-up was followed, within the year, by his arrest on an 18-year-old claim (Test 1). The sequence is documented — public opposition to the social-media suspension and to the nationality-code reform adopted by decree, then arrest on 15 April 2026 — while the causal link is his lawyers' and party's ALLEGATION, denied by the president. Even taking the denial at full weight, the government's documented response to the detention's critics has been insistence, not adjustment: release applications rejected, detention extended in October 2026 without the detainee having been questioned once (RFI, October 2026).
  • The nationality reform itself was pushed through by ordinance during parliamentary recess rather than debated — the specific grievance Bilie-By-Nze had raised — and the February 2026 social-media ordinance was made the same way (Ordinance n° 0011/PR/2026, 26 February 2026; Civicus Monitor, 2026 update). Rule by ordinance in recess is doubling down as a method: the pushback channel (parliament) is bypassed at the moment it is most likely to object.

Why this grade: The wobbles that threatened the regime's legitimacy — the AU suspension, the Bongo detention, the transition timetable — were answered openly and, in the timetable's case, ahead of schedule; the wobbles that threatened only its comfort — striking teachers, critical platforms, an opposition party law — were answered first with arrests, a 248-day blackout, and ordinances in recess, with negotiation and reversal arriving late and, in the social-media case, priced in identification requirements. Adjustment happens in this record, but it tracks the size of the threat to the seat rather than the merits of the pushback. That is a genuine mix, and it is graded as one.

Helped: Gabon's international standing and its citizens' access to the suspended institutions' benefits — AU, Commonwealth, and US normalization restored in 2025; Ali Bongo, released to exile rather than held indefinitely; striking teachers, whose protocol agreement, released leaders, and April 2026 fast-track review were real concessions; the 430 Libreville households of November 2026, whose compensation was topped up by 4 billion CFA francs with titled plots promised. Hurt: Teachers and pupils during a strike the government first answered with arrests — schools stood empty for weeks (RFI reporting, January 2026); the more than 1.84 million users cut off from social media for 248 days (Global Voices count), including businesses and families for whom the platforms are infrastructure; VPN users, threatened with direct action by the HAC president in March 2026; emerging political parties, Yama's PNTP first among them, squeezed by the June 2025 thresholds while already excluded from the ballot.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition, the young, the rural poor, women, and those who did not vote for him — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The dynasty's end itself counted in the majority who had never been counted as winners. The August 2023 coup was met with street celebrations in Libreville (AFP reporting, August–September 2023), and the April 2025 election drew the highest turnout since 1993 — reported at 70.4% by the interior ministry, 70.69% in the certified results table, and 70.1% by the Constitutional Court via IDEA; the figures differ by source and are printed separately — with Afrobarometer's November 2025 youth dispatch recording that Gabonese youth, about half the population, saw the country as moving in the right direction, with assessments of government performance showing "enormous improvement compared to 2021" (Afrobarometer Dispatch No. 1079, 17 November 2025).
  • The transition's early acts ran toward the counted-out: amnesty for prisoners of conscience and facilitation of exiles' return were promised in the 4 September 2023 inaugural address and political prisoners were released; exiled journalists returned (Business Daily Africa/AFP, September 2023; RSF, September 2025). The August 2025 amnesty freed the 2019 coup plotters, men the old regime had imprisoned (Test 1). In October 2023 he announced he would forgo the presidential salary and live on his Republican Guard commander's pay (presidential announcement via biographical record, October 2023) — a gesture toward the poor from the seat, recorded as a gesture.
  • The material ledger moved, modestly, the right way in the transition's first full year. The World Bank's October 2026 Poverty & Equity Brief, using the ENEC 2024 survey, puts poverty at about 29% of the population (roughly 729,000 people) below the national poverty line in 2024, down from 33.4% in 2017, "driven by public investment in infrastructure and urban asset gains"; public works were the transition's signature — 63 billion CFA francs in provincial aid and a programme of 2,591 km of roads by 2030 announced at the coup's first anniversary (World Bank Poverty & Equity Brief, October 2026; Africa Risk Consulting, September 2024).
  • The November 2026 demolitions file shows the counted-in instinct operating even mid-clearance: 430 households in three Libreville neighborhoods are to receive compensation from a 7.5 billion CFA franc allocation plus titled, serviced plots — though L'Union's reporting at the demolitions' start recorded Lac Bleu residents saying they had not yet received compensation and feared the timetable, and no consolidated account of how many households have actually been paid appears in the record assembled here (AGP via Africa Business Insight, November 2026; Afrikactus analysis, 10 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The founding conversation counted out most of the country's political society. About 200 political parties were reportedly excluded from the April 2024 Inclusive National Dialogue, whose 600-plus participants were largely selected by the transitional authorities (ARIES/Africa Center summary, May 2026; International IDEA GSoD tracker, April 2024). A dialogue named "inclusive" that begins by excluding the organized opposition's bulk is an accounting with names missing.
  • The ballot itself counted out identifiable groups. The age-70 rule excluded Ondo Ossa and Maganga Moussavou; the documentation rules excluded Yama, the candidate civil society most feared losing; only one of the eight approved presidential candidates was a woman, Gninga Chaning Zenaba, who won 0.39% (Civicus Monitor, 2025; certified results, April 2025). In the legislative elections, women were 139 of 856 candidates (16.3%) (International IDEA, October 2025). Legislative turnout of about 34% — against 70%-plus for the presidential race five months earlier — is itself a count: a large share of the electorate did not see the second election as theirs (Coface, January 2026).
  • The structural poor remain counted last. The World Bank's 2026 figures: rural poverty at 48.3% against a 29% national rate; unemployment 17.4% nationally, 30.5% among youth and 39.3% among young women; 86.5% of jobs informal; over 32% of 15–35-year-olds not in employment, education, or training; a further 419,000 people just above the poverty line and one shock from falling below it; poverty at the international line projected essentially flat through 2028 (31.6% in 2024, 30.8% projected 2026, 31.1% projected 2027) (World Bank Poverty & Equity Brief, October 2026; World Bank Gabon Economic Update, September 2026). Oil still supplied about half of tax revenue in 2023–2024 while output declines structurally — about 227,000 barrels a day at end-2025, a further ~3% decline expected in 2026 — so the fiscal base for counting everyone in is shrinking under the plan (Fifty4 News analysis, 2026; Coface, January 2026).
  • In 2026 the costs were distributed downward in documented instances. In November 2026 the government raised fuel prices to reduce subsidies "despite risks of higher transport and food costs" (Africa Risk Consulting, November 2026 briefing summary); an October 2026 fuel shortage disrupted residents and businesses; public debt, above the CEMAC 70% ceiling, was projected by the IMF toward 86% of GDP in 2026 on one account, while a September 2026 government debt audit revised the figure down to 68.91% of GDP — conflicting counts, printed separately, not blended (Continuum Economics country reading, 2026; Africa Risk Consulting, September 2026). Teachers — a workforce frozen for a decade under the old regime — found the new one's first answer to their wage claim was arrest and blackout (Tests 2–3), and June 2025's Libreville clearances in the Sainte-Marie valley were denounced by residents and commentators as carried out without sufficient notice or adequate compensation, with an initial rehousing offer reported at 250 houses against thousands of displaced families — figures from partisan local commentary, recorded as contested, against which the government's November 2026 compensation practice (above) is the documented counterweight for the later, smaller operation.

Why this grade: Nobody in this record is counted out by tribe, region, or faith — Gabon's accounting failure is not communal, and the dynasty's fall genuinely counted in a majority that celebrated it and turned out at a 32-year high. The counting-out that is documented is political and generational: 200 parties outside the dialogue, the credible civilian rival outside the ballot, women at one-in-eight on the presidential slate, and a youth cohort — 30.5% unemployed, a third NEET — whose optimism (Afrobarometer) is running ahead of a labor market that has not yet moved for them. Poverty fell on his watch; it fell from a third to just under three-tenths, with the rural half of the country still near one-in-two. That is a mixed ledger, honestly stated.

Helped: The urban majority who gained from transition-era public investment (World Bank attribution for the 2017→2024 poverty fall); political prisoners and exiles of the Bongo era, released and returned; Kelly Ondo Obiang and the 2019 plotters, freed; provincial recipients of the 63 billion CFA franc aid package; the 430 compensated Libreville households of November 2026, subject to the payment-timetable caveat above. Hurt: Jean-Rémy Yama and the supporters of the excluded candidacies; the roughly 200 parties excluded from the National Dialogue; rural Gabonese, at 48.3% poverty; young women, at 39.3% unemployment (World Bank, 2026); teachers on a decade-frozen wage scale; fuel buyers and transport users after the November 2026 price rise; residents displaced in the June 2025 Sainte-Marie valley clearances, on the contested account recorded above.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 30 August 2023, with dates; the two axes — word kept/broken, and people helped/hurt — graded independently, with all four crossings named.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The transition timetable — kept, early. On 4 September 2023 he pledged "free, transparent and credible elections" to restore civilian rule, a new constitution adopted by referendum, new electoral legislation, and a new penal code; the transitional framework set elections for August 2025, two years after the coup. Delivered: National Dialogue April 2024; constitution approved by referendum 16 November 2024 (91.64% yes on the Constitutional Court's figures); electoral code January 2025; presidential election 12 April 2025 — four months ahead of the deadline; legislative and local elections September–October 2025 completing the sequence (Business Daily Africa/AFP, September 2023; AU PSC communiqué 1277, April 2025; International IDEA trackers, 2024–2025). Crossing 1 — kept his word AND it helped people: the timetable's keeping is what restored Gabon's AU seat, Commonwealth membership, and US standing, and ended military rule on schedule in a region where transitions more often extend themselves.
  • The amnesty promise — kept as made. The August 2025 amnesty for the 2023 and 2019 coup participants was, on the record, a promise made during his election campaign and then issued on 12 August 2025 (biographical record; International IDEA, August 2025). Crossing 2 — kept his word AND it still hurt people: the keeping earns the credit here, and the hurt is recorded in full in Test 1 — an amnesty that bars any future prosecution of the coup's authors is a second law at the source, and its beneficiaries include the promiser himself. The same act also helped: it freed the 2019 plotters, the old regime's prisoners (Crossing 1 applies to that half of the same order).
  • Political prisoners and exiles — kept. The 4 September 2023 pledge to amnesty prisoners of conscience and facilitate exiles' return was followed by releases and by the documented return of exiled journalists (AFP, September 2023; RSF, September 2025). Crossing 1.
  • A constitution with term limits — delivered as pledged. The referendum text limits the president to two consecutive seven-year terms, bans dynastic succession, and — on the draft's account — treats a sitting president's attempt to revise the term limits as high treason (Africa Risk Consulting, September 2024; referendum record, November 2024). Whether the limit binds him in 2032 is a future test; the word as given in 2023–2024 was kept in the text adopted. Crossing 1, with the eligibility caveats graded in Test 1.
  • Anti-corruption as a campaign platform — acted on at scale. He warned business chiefs in September 2023 that corruption would no longer be tolerated and campaigned in 2025 on the old regime's waste; the record shows the SEEG utility audit ordered August 2024, the November 2025 Bongo verdicts, and the December 2025 house arrest of his own tourism minister on suspicion of embezzlement (ALLEGATION, no verdict documented) (AFP, September 2023; Africa Risk Consulting, September 2024; December 2025 reporting). The mandate rule applies: governing on the anti-corruption platform the voters elected is evidence for, not against; what is graded against in Test 1 is the selectivity of the rules around it, not the platform itself.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "Handing power back to the civilians" — the central word, bent. The 4 September 2023 pledge framed the promised elections as "the stepping stone to handing power back to the civilians" (AFP, September 2023). Power was handed, in April 2025, to the transitional president himself, in civilian dress, under a charter exception written for him and an electoral code that let soldiers stand. Whether he also made a personal promise not to run is CONTESTED: The Mentor News (March 2025) states flatly that on taking power he declared he would not be a candidate and calls the 3 March 2025 candidacy announcement a shelved promise; other contemporaneous reporting records ambiguity rather than a clear personal renunciation — he gave no date for elections in September 2023, the charter's exception for him existed from the transition's early architecture, and the opposition was already fearing his candidacy at the April 2024 dialogue. This dossier records the dispute rather than resolving it: the "hand back to civilians" framing is documented in his own inaugural words; a verbatim personal no-run pledge is not established on the record assembled here. Crossing 3 — went against the plain sense of his word AND it helped people — applies in part and is recorded without laundering: his candidacy was genuinely popular (94.85% certified, on 70%-plus turnout, in an election observers called credible), continuity spared Gabon a contested vacuum, and the help does not erase the bend; Crossing 4 — broke his word AND it hurt people — applies to the narrower set hurt by the bend itself: the excluded rivals and the voters whose "return to civilian rule" arrived with the general still in the seat (Tests 1 and 4).
  • "Restore dignity" through living standards — the word the street is now grading. The AU's April 2025 communiqué pointedly "encourages the President-Elect to comply with his commitments in restoring dignity to the Gabonese people." By December 2025–January 2026 the teachers' movement was striking over a decade-long wage freeze the new government had inherited and not yet unfrozen; by October–November 2026 the government was managing fuel shortages, raising fuel prices, cutting its 2026 growth target from 6.5% to 4% (the IMF projecting about 2.7%), and seeking a new IMF programme, formally requested in March 2026, with public debt above the regional ceiling (AU PSC 1277; News Central TV, January 2026; Fifty4 News, 2026; Africa Risk Consulting, 2026 briefings). Not yet a broken word — the term runs to 2032 — but the trajectory at grading is away from the promise, and the dossier records it as receding.
  • A fair trial for the Bongos — pledged, and contested in the event. He pledged Sylvia and Noureddin Bongo would receive a "fair trial" and denied their torture allegations (AFP reporting, 2025). The trial that followed was two days long, in absentia, before a court of the state he heads, ending in 20-year sentences the defendants call predetermined — "based on the testimony of witnesses coerced by the military junta," in Noureddin Bongo Valentin's words, an ALLEGATION the court's president publicly rejected (OCCRP, November 2025; court president's statement via The African Mirror, November 2025). The verdict may be substantively just — the prosecution's diversion account was detailed — and the process pledge is nonetheless recorded as contested by its subjects and unproven either way on this record.
  • The 2026 fine print: a June 2026 bribery investigation linked to a Gunvor financing deal with Gabon drew international scrutiny of the new government's own financial dealings (Africa Risk Consulting, June 2026) — an investigation, not a finding, recorded here because the anti-corruption word is his signature word and the file is open. His October 2023 salary gesture (Test 4) is likewise recorded as stated; no independent verification of its continuation into the elected term appears in the record assembled here. Where the record cannot carry a crossing, this dossier says so rather than inventing one: a documented case of a promise kept that hurt no one and helped no one measurably, beyond those named above, is not documented.

Why this grade: The dated, checkable promises of the transition — referendum, electoral code, elections by August 2025, amnesty, prisoner releases — were kept, several of them early, and that is a rarer record among the region's coup-born transitions than the grades of neighboring files suggest. Set against it, the transition's animating sentence — power handed back to the civilians — was fulfilled in form and bent in substance, by rules he controlled; and the elected term's material promise, dignity through better living standards, is at grading moving the wrong way (wage freeze, fuel prices, debt, a growth target halved). Kept words and a bent word, on the same page: MIXED, with the crossings named above so neither axis launders the other.

Helped: Gabonese voters, who got the elections on the date promised and a constitution with term limits in the text; the 2019 coup plotters, freed under a kept campaign promise; prisoners of conscience and returned exiles of 2023–2025; Gabon's external partners and creditors, who got the normalization the timetable unlocked. Hurt: The excluded candidates and their voters, by the bent hand-back (Tests 1, 4); public employees on frozen scales and fuel buyers in 2026, by the receding dignity promise; the defendants of November 2025, whose fair-trial pledge is contested on the record stated; future Gabonese taxpayers, on whom a debt projected by the IMF toward 86% of GDP in 2026 (against the government's audited 68.91% — conflicting counts, unblended) will fall if the diversification word is not kept either.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Brice Oligui Nguema's holding divides cleanly in two, and the grades follow the seam. As a transition manager, the record is strong: he took power in a bloodless coup out of a genuinely captured election — a moment this dossier grades under the no-good-choice clause rather than against him — and then did the dated things he said he would do, early: a national dialogue, a constitution by referendum with term limits and a dynastic-succession ban written into it, elections four months ahead of the charter's deadline that the Commonwealth called credible and the African Union rewarded by lifting Gabon's suspension, and a completed legislative sequence by November 2025. That is why the wobble and the word grade MIXED rather than FAILS, and why the counting test is MIXED: the dynasty's end counted in a celebrating majority, poverty fell from 33.4% (2017) to about 29% (2024) on the World Bank's count, prisoners and exiles came home, and even his November 2026 demolitions came with a topped-up compensation fund and titled plots — while 200 parties stood outside the dialogue, the credible civilian rival stood outside the ballot, rural poverty sits at 48.3%, youth unemployment at 30.5%, and the teachers who struck over a frozen decade were met first with arrests. But the holding fails the two tests that grade the source and the grip, and it fails them on design, not on mood: a charter exception so the transitional president could run, an age limit and documentation rules that removed the rivals with names, an interior ministry running the elections, a Constitutional Court appointed by the candidate it cleared, a premiership abolished into a pure presidency, a party founded in July 2025 holding both chambers by November, an amnesty placing his own coup beyond prosecution, and — in the elected term's second year — an eight-month nationwide suspension of social media lifted only under compulsory identification, an opposition runner-up detained six months without questioning on an unproven 2008 claim of €7,600, and a news site suspended indefinitely for injuring the president's family. The helped ledger is real: voters got their dates, the 2019 plotters got their freedom, the treasury got its verdicts, 430 Libreville households got a compensation fund. The hurt ledger is also real, and it is concentrated exactly where the Test looks hardest: on the people and institutions whose job was to be able to say no to him. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether Alain Claude Bilie-By-Nze is questioned, tried, or released; whether the party-law amendment contested in November 2026 is withdrawn or passed; whether the fuel-price and debt trajectory of late 2026 bends back toward the dignity promise; whether the November 2025 damages are ever collected; and whether the term limit he wrote is still standing, unamended, when 2032 approaches.
