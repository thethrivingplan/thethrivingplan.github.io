Settings & rules

Standard is the classic rule set. Strict and Relaxed are variants: when one is in play, a banner says so above the grid, and games finished under it are labelled with the variant in your record — a variant's results are never dressed up as standard results.

Alias freedom: play as Guest, as Player 1, or under any temporary name you like. A name here is session-only — never claimed, registered, or checked — and it is written on a record entry only if you type one.

How difficulty is graded — honestly

Difficulty here is not just a count of empty squares. Every generated puzzle is run through a logical solver that only uses human techniques, in tiers:

  • Tier 1 — Singles: naked singles and hidden singles.
  • Tier 2 — Pairs: naked pairs and hidden pairs join in.
  • Tier 3 — Pointing: pointing pairs (box–line reductions) join in.
  • Tier 4 — X-wing and beyond: an X-wing, or reasoning past everything above.

The hardest tier a puzzle actually needs is its measured grade, and that grade is printed above the grid next to the timer. The generator keeps making puzzles until one measures at the tier you asked for — most fresh puzzles grade Tier 1 however many holes they have, so it also digs extra givens out of too-easy puzzles and re-grades them; if it settles for a neighbour tier after honest trying, the badge tells you the measured truth rather than the label. Separately, a backtracking solver counts solutions for every puzzle and only a puzzle with exactly one is ever shown.

The strategies the coach teaches

These are the real, documented solving techniques for this puzzle — the same ones puzzle books teach. The Coach looks for them on your actual grid, in this order, and explains the one it finds in plain words with its trade-off. Where more than one road exists, both are named — there is rarely only one honest way forward.

Naked single

What it is: a square whose row, column, and box leave it only one possible digit.

Trade-off: free progress with zero risk — but it only appears after other work has cleared the way, so hunting singles alone stalls on harder puzzles.

Hidden single

What it is: a digit that can only fit in one square of a row, column, or box, even though that square still shows several candidates.

Trade-off: just as certain as a naked single, but slower to spot — you scan digit by digit instead of square by square.

Naked pair

What it is: two squares in one row, column, or box holding the same two candidates — so those two digits belong to those squares, and can be erased everywhere else in that unit.

Trade-off: places nothing by itself; it pays off by shrinking neighbours' candidates. Pencil notes are essential — no notes, no pairs.

Hidden pair

What it is: two digits that can only live in the same two squares of a unit — so every other candidate in those two squares can be erased.

Trade-off: the mirror of the naked pair and harder to see, because the two squares look cluttered. Powerful cleanup, easy to walk past.

Pointing pair

What it is: inside one box, a digit's candidates all sit on a single row or column — so that digit must live in the box on that line, and can be erased from the rest of the line outside the box.

Trade-off: reaches across the grid where pairs cannot, but each use removes only one digit from a few squares — steady pressure, not a breakthrough.

X-wing (an introduction)

What it is: a digit whose candidates in two rows line up in the same two columns, forming a rectangle. The digit must occupy opposite corners, so it can be erased from those two columns everywhere else.

Trade-off: the first truly grid-wide pattern — rare, and it takes a full candidate grid to spot, but when it lands it can crack a puzzle that resisted everything simpler. This page teaches you to recognise it; deeper wings (Swordfish and beyond) build on the same rectangle idea.

Two honest roads, most of the time: you can work square-first (fill notes everywhere, then harvest singles) or digit-first (pick a digit and sweep its rows, columns, and boxes). Square-first is thorough but slow to start; digit-first is fast early and easy to lose track of late. The Coach follows whichever technique your grid offers first — and says which one it used, so your record shows how you actually solved it.

About this puzzle

Where Number Place comes from — tap to open

This puzzle first appeared in print in 1979, in Dell's Pencil Puzzles and Word Games magazine, under the name Number Place, devised by Howard Garns. The same puzzle later travelled to Japan and came back world-famous under another name — it is the puzzle widely called Sudoku. We use the original documented name, Number Place, because the other name is a trademark in some places; the description here is ours, and so are the words, the art, and the code on this page.

The rules have never changed: digits 1–9, once in every row, column, and 3×3 box, from a starting grid that admits exactly one completion. Everything else — the tiers, the coach, the record — is this page's own teaching built on top of those rules.

Playing on paper vs. here — tap to open

On paper, nothing stops a wrong digit until the end. Here, the Standard rule shows clashes as you go; Strict and Relaxed variants change that bargain, and the page labels them as variants whenever they are in play. Notes mode is your pencil: candidates are yours to keep or erase, and the Coach's eliminations only ever touch notes you can see.
