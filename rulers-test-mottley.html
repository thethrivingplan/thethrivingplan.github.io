The Ruler's Test — Mia Mottley, Prime Minister of Barbados

Subject: Mia Amor Mottley — Prime Minister of Barbados since 25 May 2018, and the seat graded is the premiership, for the reason stated plainly: in Barbados the presidency is a ceremonial head-of-state office, while executive power — the government, the budget, the security services — sits with the prime minister. The presidency changed hands during her tenure without touching the seat graded here: Jeffrey Bostic, a former health minister and army officer, became President on 30 November 2025, nominated jointly by Mottley and Opposition Leader Ralph Thorne on 16 September 2025 and declared elected unopposed on 7 October 2025, succeeding Dame Sandra Mason. Mottley is graded on her full tenure, eight and a half years — this is a long file, not a thin one. She won a third consecutive term at a snap election she called on 17 January 2026: Parliament was dissolved on 19 January, the vote held on 11 February 2026, and her Barbados Labour Party took all 30 House seats for the third election running, on 69.83% of the vote (71,109 votes; the Democratic Labour Party took 27.31% and no seats; Thorne lost his own St John race). She was sworn in for the third term on 12 February 2026. Turnout figures conflict between sources — roughly 37% in one published count, 42% in the election's tabulated figures — and are recorded here as contested rather than resolved. She holds the premiership together with the Finance, National Security, Public Service, and Culture portfolios herself; her deputy is Santia Bradshaw. She is, as of November 2026, still in office and active on the world stage (meeting with the UK prime minister, 2 November 2026; billed to deliver the Central Bank's Sir Winston Scott Memorial Lecture, 23 November 2026). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The outcome measure is among the strongest in the Americas: Barbados scores 68 of 100 on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index (2025 index, 24th of 180), essentially stable across her tenure — 65 in 2021 and 2022, 69 in 2023, 68 in 2024 and 2025 (Transparency International, CPI 2021–2025). Perceived public-sector corruption did not grow under a government that has held every seat in the House for eight years.
  • There is no documented pattern of the law being aimed at opponents. Freedom House recorded in its 2020 and 2021 reports that no major officials had faced arrest under the Mottley administration — a fact that sits on this shelf in one specific respect: no opposition figure has been prosecuted at home either. The one senior corruption conviction touching Barbados in the period, former DLP industry minister Donville Inniss, was convicted in a United States federal court (EDNY, January 2020) of laundering some USD 36,000 in insurance-company bribes taken in 2015–16, under the previous government; the conviction was affirmed by the Second Circuit on 5 October 2022. It was an American prosecution of her opponents' man, not a Barbadian prosecution by her government.
  • The legislative framework was built, on paper, in full: her Parliament passed a Prevention of Corruption Act, a Public Procurement Act, a Public Finance Management Act, a Whistleblower Protection Act, and the Integrity in Public Life Act (Government Information Service, August 2023). The statutes exist; what they do is graded on the other shelf.

Evidence against:

  • The one law that would bind the seat's own circle has never been switched on. The Integrity in Public Life Act — asset declarations by senior officials, supervised by an Integrity Commission chaired, on Mottley's own August 2023 outline, by a High Court judge — passed the Senate on 16 August 2023 after a first bill failed there in 2020 for want of the required two-thirds. Three years on, the Act has still not been proclaimed, the Commission does not exist, and its machinery remains dormant (Nation News, August 2025 and 2026; Integrity Group Barbados, "The Integrity Act: A promise deferred," August 2025). A law that applies to everyone in the text and to no one in force is not one law; it is a promise of one.
  • The pattern predates her and she repeated it: the earlier Prevention of Corruption Act 2012 was signed but never proclaimed (Freedom House, 2018). Her administration's own Act has now spent longer unproclaimed than the interval between her two attempts to pass it.
  • Enforcement at home is absent from the record. In 2019–20 her Attorney General, Dale Marshall, told Parliament the government had engaged the London firm Fulcrum Chambers to scope state enterprises for prima facie corruption and assured the House that "some people will face the court" (Caribbean reporting, 2020). In the record assembled here, no major official of either party has faced a Barbadian court on corruption charges in her eight and a half years; the Inniss conviction was obtained by the United States, for conduct under her predecessors.
  • The companion transparency law was never even passed: a Freedom of Information Act, promised by both parties and by her administration since 2018–19, has never been enacted (Freedom House, every year through 2024; Integrity Group Barbados, 2026). Citizens' right to ask the state for its records exists as a campaign commitment, not as a law.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The referees all still work, on the monitors' own numbers: Freedom House scores Barbados 94 of 100, Free, in both the 2025 and 2026 editions (political rights 37 of 40, civil liberties 57 of 60), and V-Dem places it around 34th in the world on liberal democracy (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025 and 2026; V-Dem). Media operate freely and criticize her openly and by name; no journalist, judge, banker, or prosecutor in the record assembled here was punished for ruling, reporting, or deciding against her government.
  • The courts ruled against the inherited order and the ruling stands: on 12 December 2022 the High Court struck down the buggery and "serious indecency" laws as unconstitutional, in a case brought by activists rather than driven by her government; the decriminalization stands in force in 2026.
  • The one genuine opposition office Barbados produced in her tenure was allowed to function, and she shared a state act with its holder: when BLP member Ralph Thorne crossed the floor in February 2024 to become Leader of the Opposition, the office operated; in September 2025 she and Thorne jointly nominated Jeffrey Bostic for the presidency, and he was declared elected unopposed.
  • The central bank speaks in its own voice, even awkwardly: Governor Kevin Greenidge (in post since 1 March 2023) drew public criticism in 2026 — including from former minister Inniss, now a commentator — for refusing to disclose the cost of the Bank's BiMPay app on the ground that the Bank is self-financed; critics answered that the Bank's capital belongs to the people of Barbados. The exchange happened in the open, and the governor was not removed or silenced for it.

Evidence against:

  • Power is concentrated in her own hands to a degree the file must state plainly: alongside the premiership she personally holds the Finance, National Security, Public Service, and Culture portfolios (her own published ministerial listings). The seat that grades the referees also holds the money, the security services, and the public service.
  • The February 2026 anti-defection amendment is a documented grip, applied to the one route by which opposition had re-entered Parliament. Within two weeks of the third sweep, the Constitution (Amendment) Bill 2026 passed the House on 24 February (29–0, one absent) and the Senate on 27 February (15–3): a member now vacates the seat on resigning from, being expelled by, or crossing the floor from the party under whose banner they were elected. It was passed in three days with no public consultation. ConstitutionNet's analysis (February 2026) frames it as elevating party authority over parliamentary independence and consolidating power in party leadership — that is, in her. The documented irony is part of the record: Thorne became Opposition Leader in February 2024 by crossing the floor from her own party; the amendment closes the door he walked through.
  • The central bank governorship is a revolving-door appointment, whatever the governor's subsequent independence of voice: Greenidge was Mottley's own Senior Economic Advisor from 2018 to 2022 and the designer of the BERT program before she appointed him Governor. The referee of the economic program was the program's author.
  • The 2026 election machinery was contested, and the contest is recorded at its true strength. Thorne alleged government interference with the voters' list and the Electoral and Boundaries Commission and called for postponement — ALLEGATIONs, refuted by Mottley, who invited CARICOM and Commonwealth observers. The Commonwealth Expert Team's final report concluded the polls were "credible, transparent and peaceful" and commended the Commission's professionalism, while noting continued inaccuracies in the voters' list; the CARICOM mission (report dated 30 July 2026) found voting well managed and transparent and recorded stakeholder concerns about the register's integrity, the absence of campaign-finance legislation, and the Commission's readiness. The allegation was not sustained; the register's flaws were noted by the referees themselves.
  • Underneath every item on both shelves sits the arithmetic: three consecutive 30–0 sweeps mean no elected opposition member sits in the House at all. The checks the constitution imagines are absent by election result, not by her act — but a seat that has never had to share the chamber is graded on how it behaves with the whole board, and the anti-defection amendment is how it behaved.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clearest wobble of the tenure was answered on television, in the open, within days. The Interception of Communications Bill passed the House on 8 August 2025 including an exception permitting wiretapping without judicial approval; Home Affairs Minister Wilfred Abrahams conceded in debate that wiretapping had already been occurring before any legal framework existed — a concession the Bar Association's Criminal Law Committee convenor called "particularly troubling." After a weekend of protests and consultations, Mottley announced in a televised national address that the exception clause would be removed, citing public concern, and accepted Opposition Leader Thorne's recommendation that the oversight commission be appointed by the President rather than a minister. The government then pressed pause on the bill's later stages (Barbados Today and national coverage, August–October 2025). The street pushed; the clause came out; the concession was made by name, in public, by the prime minister herself.
  • The Cybercrime Bill followed the slower version of the same arc: passed the House in February 2024, its Articles 19–20 criminalizing online expression causing "annoyance," "embarrassment," "humiliation," or "anxiety" (up to seven years' imprisonment or BBD 70,000); after national outcry it went to a Joint Select Committee, the DPP's office and the Bar Association objected to criminal defamation, citizens took the bill to the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights (hearing, 11 November 2024), and it was never enacted — it lapsed with the January 2026 dissolution. The adjustment here is recorded honestly: the Joint Select Committee recommended increasing the penalties (to ten years / BBD 100,000) while removing some terms; what the pushback achieved was the bill's death by delay, not its redrafting to the critics' terms.
  • The pause pattern repeated: the government also held back the Tourist Accommodation Bill after public backlash (Barbados Today, October 2025). On the visible record, public objection in Barbados reliably slows or stops her government's measures — the wobble is answered more often than it is overridden.

Evidence against:

  • The anti-defection amendment (Test 2) was the opposite motion, executed at speed: introduced and passed through both houses in three days in February 2026, with no public consultation, in the immediate aftermath of an election — the one measure of the tenure that most restricted future pushback was itself insulated from pushback.
  • The NIS pension reform shows the consultation question contested at the center of her governing method. The 2023 reform — pensionable age rising from 67 to 67.5 in January 2028 and 68 in January 2034, qualifying contributions from 500 to 750 weeks by 2031, earliest reduced pension from 60 to 63 — was presented by the government as following extensive consultation through the Social Partnership (government, unions, private sector, faith community, NGOs, public). CTUSAB, the union congress, states that the government broke its promise to consult the Social Partnership before tabling, and that it learned of the changes from the 28 June ministerial statement. Both positions are documented; the dispute is recorded as contested, and the reform proceeded as announced. Public reaction in 2026 explainer threads remains negative, with distrust citing the government's use of the fund.
  • Even the Interception adjustment was partial: Thorne demanded full withdrawal and called the episode "maladministration"; the measure proceeded in amended form rather than being withdrawn. She removed the clause the street named; she did not concede the bill. Adjustment, openly made — and adjustment only so far.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's voters, minorities, the poor, and those who share the streets — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The lowest-paid were counted in law for the first time: her government introduced Barbados's first national minimum wage, $8.50 an hour, on 1 April 2021; raised it to $10.50 on 1 June 2025 (security guards $11.43); and raised it again to $10.71 on 21 January 2026 under a legislated automatic 2% annual increase — a count of the poorest workers that now repeats by law rather than waiting on a political decision.
  • The 2022 decriminalization stands for a minority long counted out of the law's protection: the High Court's 12 December 2022 ruling striking down the buggery and serious-indecency provisions remains in force, and her government's Employment (Prevention of Discrimination) Act 2020 prohibits employment discrimination on grounds of sexual orientation. The government did not bring the case; it has not moved against the ruling either.
  • The economic rescue was accounted across the whole society rather than billed to one part of it: the BERT restructuring drew 97% creditor participation with an external haircut of about 26%, and the IMF-supported program ran seven reviews to completion in 2025 — the cost of the 2018 crisis was spread across creditors, the state, and time, and the program's completion is the documented result (IMF; Fitch).

Evidence against:

  • The murder count is the worst number in the file, and it counts the young. Barbados recorded 50 murders in 2024 — 17.7 per 100,000 people, the highest on record (US State Department OSAC country report). Police figures show homicides running at 38 for January–October 2025 against 41 for the same period of 2024 (the two counts use different definitions and are kept separate here), while robberies more than doubled, from 100 to 213, in the same comparison — with perpetrators identified by police as largely aged 13 to 19. The state's documented answer is enforcement: Operation Restore Order (joint police–Defence Force operations) and a Serious Organised Crime unit. A generation of teenage boys appears in her government's accounting principally as a security problem; their victims, and they themselves, are the ones the count is failing.
  • The minority counted by the courts in 2022 is still only partly counted by the state: the September 2020 announcements of "some form" of same-sex civil unions and a referendum on same-sex marriage never materialized — no legal recognition of same-sex relationships exists as of 2026 — and the 2020 discrimination Act covers sexual orientation but not gender identity.
  • More than a quarter of the electorate is counted at the ballot box and nowhere in the chamber: the DLP's 27.31% of February 2026 voters returned no member to the House, for the third consecutive election. That is the electoral system's arithmetic, not her act; but combined with the anti-defection amendment (Test 2), the opposition's voters' only representation runs through Senate seats whose holders now sit under a party-discipline rule her government wrote.

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Dated promises and public statements vs. the record since 25 May 2018.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept, with dates):

  • The economic word — kept almost to the decimal. She inherited a debt that peaked at 178.9% of GDP in 2018 (Fitch) with reserves at five to six weeks of imports. The homegrown BERT program, negotiated through the Social Partnership and supported by an IMF Extended Fund Facility, restructured domestic and external debt (97% creditor participation; external haircut about 26%), ran seven IMF reviews over four years, and the IMF program was completed in 2025. Debt fell to 94.6% of GDP in 2025 — below 100% for the first time in over a decade (Fitch). BERT 2026, the third phase, was laid in Parliament before the 2026 election rather than after it.
  • The republic — delivered on the promised date. Barbados became a republic on 30 November 2021, the date her government had set, ending the monarchy and installing a Barbadian head of state.
  • The minimum wage — introduced as promised and raised on schedule: $8.50 (1 April 2021), $10.50 (1 June 2025), $10.71 under the automatic escalator (21 January 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or still waiting):

  • Integrity in public life — the deferred promise at the center of the file. The 2020 bill failed in the Senate; the 2023 Act passed on 16 August 2023; as of the latest 2026 coverage it has not been proclaimed, and the Integrity Commission she described in detail in August 2023 — High Court judge as chair, declarations filed, supervision operating — does not exist. Integrity Group Barbados titled its August 2025 assessment "The Integrity Act: A promise deferred," and renewed the call through 2026. Three years is not a delay in drafting; it is a word unkept on the record as it stands, correctable the day the proclamation is gazetted.
  • Freedom of information — promised since 2018–19, never enacted in eight and a half years and three mandates (Freedom House, annually; Integrity Group Barbados, 2026).
  • Civil unions and the marriage referendum — announced in September 2020, never delivered; no recognition exists as of 2026 (Test 4).
  • The new constitution — still a report, not a bill. Her Constitutional Reform Commission (chaired by retired Justice Christopher Blackman, appointed June 2022) reported on 30 September 2024 after 27 months, with a draft constitution roughly half again as large as the current one — expanded rights chapter, a Human Rights Commission, a binding ministerial code of conduct, opposition Senate seats raised from two to four, Senate gender parity. It is recorded fairly that the Commission itself declined to recommend prime-ministerial term limits, reasoning that elections or death end terms, and kept first-past-the-post and the prime minister's discretion to call elections. As of February 2026 commentary, the new constitution bill had not been tabled in Parliament.
  • The pattern across the two shelves is itself the finding, and it is stated because the record states it: the promises that bound the economy were kept with unusual precision, on dates, in public, through an IMF program completed in full. The promises that would bind the seat itself — integrity declarations, information access, a new constitution — are the ones still waiting.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED.

Mia Mottley holds a seat almost no democratic ruler holds: three consecutive elections, every seat in the House, eight and a half years — and the referees of Barbados all still work. Courts strike down laws and are obeyed; a central bank governor defies his critics in public and keeps his post; observers call her elections credible while noting the register's flaws; Freedom House scores the country 94 of 100. Against that stands the file's one continuous thread: the machinery that would referee her is the machinery that never got switched on. The Integrity Act passed in 2023 sits unproclaimed; the Freedom of Information Act promised since 2018 was never enacted; the constitution her own commission drafted sits untabled. The wobble, when it comes, comes from the street rather than the chamber — there is no chamber opposition to bring it — and her record there is genuinely two-sided: a wiretapping exception removed on television within days of protest, a cybercrime bill allowed to die, and, in the same tenure, an anti-defection amendment rushed through in three days that closes the very door through which Barbados last got an Opposition Leader. Her economic word is the strongest in this part of the series: a debt of 178.9% of GDP brought below 100%, an IMF program completed, a republic delivered on its promised date. Her accountability word is the weakest part of her own file, and the murder count — 50 in 2024, the highest on record, the perpetrators largely children — is the count her government is failing outright. The open files that will move these grades are named: proclamation of the Integrity Act, enactment of freedom of information, tabling of the constitution bill, and whether the homicide numbers of 2024–25 were a spike or a trend.
