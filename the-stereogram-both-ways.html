The Stereogram, Both Ways

A stereogram is a message written for one standing-place: flat noise from everywhere else, a picture with depth from exactly one alignment of your eyes. This page takes one apart in both directions — first we start with the picture and build the noise around it, then we start with the noise and pull the picture back out. Everything pictured here was generated for this page, by the numbers printed on it.

It is the worked demonstration for The Decoder, where the stereogram stands inside the Perspective Key.

First, meet the noise

How to see it: don't look at the page — look through it, as if at something far behind it, and let your eyes drift apart slightly (the "wall-eyed" way). The dots will seem to double, slide, and then — if you hold it — lock. A shape stands up out of the noise, floating in front of the page: a ring, with a ball at its centre and four small dots on the ring at north, east, south, west. It can take a minute the first time. If it never locks for you, nothing is wrong with you or the picture — the machine read further down proves the figure is in there either way.

Direction One — build it: start with the picture

Step 1The picture. This is the image you're supposed to see — drawn plainly, in the open. A ring, a ball at the centre, four dots on the ring. Nothing hidden yet.

Step 2Turn the picture into distances. A stereogram doesn't carry colours or lines — it carries depth. So the picture becomes a depth map: every point gets a distance. Bright means near, dark means far. The ball is nearest, the ring a step behind it, the background far away.

Step 3One strip of noise. This strip of random dots is the seed — the only "ink" the stereogram has. Everything in the finished picture is this strip, repeated.

Step 4Tile it — and pull the repeats closer where the picture is near. The strip is laid across the page again and again, each copy copied from the one before. Where the depth map says far, the repeat distance stays full width (120 dots). Where it says near, the repeat is pulled in — up to 34 dots closer for the nearest point of the ball.

That is the whole trick, and it is not a trick: the shift at each point is the depth at that point. The picture is not drawn in the noise. It is measured into it.

Why it works on your eyes: when you look through the page, each eye lands on a different repeat of the strip. Your visual system reads the gap between the two repeats as distance — the same signal it uses for real depth. Nearer picture, smaller gap, nearer-looking surface. The figure your brain builds is the depth map, rebuilt by the same machinery that builds depth out of the real world.

The build, animated: picture → depth map → noise. The finished stereogram is the one at the top of this page.

Direction Two — read it: start with the noise

Step 1Take the standing-place. The key is fixed before looking: eyes diverged, as if looking through the page. From every other angle the page stays what it honestly is from there — noise. Noise from the wrong angle is evidence neither for nor against.

Step 2Or let a machine take it. Software has no eyes to diverge, so it does the same job by matching: for every dot, it searches sideways for the repeat — the place where the pattern matches itself — and measures the gap. Small gap, near. Full gap, far. No picture is shown to the machine. It is given the noise and the rule, nothing else.

Step 3The picture comes back out. This is the machine's actual read of the stereogram at the top of this page — the depth map it recovered by matching alone:

The ring, the ball, the four dots — all of it, pulled out of the noise by a reader that was never told what was in it. Measured against the original depth map, the recovered figure matches at 0.998 across the picture. Two honest blemishes, printed rather than hidden: the pale band at the far left is the seed strip itself, where there is nothing yet to match against, so the machine's guess there is worth nothing; and the faint rings ghosting at the right are echoes of the copying chain. Neither touches the figure.

The read, animated: the figure rising out of the noise — which is also, slowed down, what your own eyes do when it locks.

And the null is honest

Take the same strip of noise and tile it with no shifts — no depth written in — and no alignment of anyone's eyes will ever find a figure in it, because there isn't one. That is what makes the stereogram a demonstration and not a parlour trick: it can prove a carrier holds a message invisibly, and it can prove one doesn't. A figure you can only see after someone tells you what it is, is not this. That's priming — your eyes being coached, not a message being read.
