Thrust is just the throw. Your arm gives the plane all its push in the first instant; after that the push is gone and the plane lives on its speed, trading height and speed against drag until it lands. A paper plane has no engine — that fading push is the whole story of its “thrust.”

Fold-along designs — fold the real plane

Each design below folds from one ordinary sheet of paper. Open a design, follow the steps, then throw the real thing and log it under Real Throws in your record.

Classic Dart — tap to open / tap to close

A traditional, documented design. Diagrams are our own drawings.

1. Fold the sheet in half lengthwise, crease hard, then unfold. This centre line guides every fold after it.

2. Fold both top corners in to the centre line. You now have a pointed nose — the dart’s whole secret.

3. Fold the new slanted edges in to the centre line again, sharpening the point further.

4. Fold the plane in half along the centre line, folds on the outside.

5. Fold each side down to make two narrow wings, angled very slightly up. Throw it level and firm.

Glider — tap to open / tap to close

A traditional, documented design. Diagrams are our own drawings.

1. Fold in half lengthwise, crease, unfold — same centre guide line as the dart.

2. Fold the top corners to the centre, then fold the whole nose point down so it lies flat against the body.

3. Fold in half along the centre line, then fold the top corners in once more to meet at the centre.

4. Fold each side out into one wide, flat wing. Wide wings are the point — they carry the plane slowly.

5. Bend the wingtips up a finger’s width for winglets, and give the wings a gentle upward angle. Throw it soft and level — a glider hates a hard throw.

Stunt Plane — tap to open / tap to close

A traditional, documented design. Diagrams are our own drawings.

1. Centre fold first, as always: in half lengthwise, crease, unfold.

2. Corners to the centre for a point — then fold the point down, but only partway, leaving a shorter, blunter nose.

3. Fold in half along the centre. Fold each side out into medium wings.

4. Bend the back edge of each wing up a little. Those up-bent tails are elevators — they keep pushing the nose up, which is what makes the loop.

5. Throw it upward at a steep angle with medium power. If it dives instead of looping, bend the elevators up a touch more.

The Full Why — how paper flight works

Four forces, plainly

Lift is the wings pushing air down and the air pushing back up. Drag is air resisting — every fold and rough edge adds a little. Weight is gravity pulling the plane down the whole flight. Thrust is only your throw: one push at the start, gone the instant the plane leaves your hand.

A gliding plane is always slowly falling through the air around it — good design just makes the fall shallow and slow.

Why a dart goes fast and straight but glides poorly

Small wings mean little drag at speed and a heavy, pointy nose that holds its line like a thrown spear. But small wings make little lift at low speed, so once the throw fades the dart sinks fast. Speed is its whole trick — and speed runs out.

Why big wings glide — and stall

Big wings make plenty of lift while moving slowly, so a glider can float. The cost: it cannot be thrown hard — extra speed just makes drag and can fold the wings’ careful shape. And below its stall speed, air stops flowing smoothly over the wing, lift collapses, and the nose drops. A stall is not the plane “giving up”; it is the wing asking for speed it no longer has.

Nose weight: stability with a price

Weight at the nose moves the balance point forward, so the plane points where it is going and resists tumbling. Too much, and the plane must fly faster just to hold its nose up — it lands sooner and shorter. The right amount is the least that still flies straight.

Dihedral: wings that level themselves

When wings angle upward in a shallow V, a tip that drops meets the air more squarely and gets lifted back up. The plane corrects itself, over and over, with no pilot. Flat wings are a touch faster; downward-angled wings are twitchy and love to spiral in.

Three strategies, three trade-offs

Distance setup: dart or tapered wings, one clip, slight dihedral, firm level throw. Trades hang time for speed and a shallow fast glide.

Hang-time setup: glider, big straight wings, winglets up, no extra clip, gentle throw near best-glide speed. Trades distance for the slowest possible sink.

Trick setup: stunt plane, elevators (here: winglets down reads as up-bent tail in this model’s simplification) and a steep throw. Trades everything for the loop — distance is not the point.
