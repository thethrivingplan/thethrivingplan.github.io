The Ruler's Test — Bart De Wever, Prime Minister of Belgium

Subject: Bart De Wever — Prime Minister of Belgium since 3 February 2025, the first Flemish nationalist to hold the office; longtime leader of the New Flemish Alliance (N-VA) and mayor of Antwerp from 2013 until he became prime minister. He heads the five-party "Arizona" coalition (N-VA, MR, Les Engagés, Vooruit, CD&V) formed on 31 January 2025 after 234 days of negotiations following the 9 June 2024 elections. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His signature reforms are drafted as general rules, not as instruments aimed at enemies or exemptions for friends. The pension reform (law of 30 May 2026) applies its bonus-malus system across employees, civil servants and the self-employed alike, and tightens the privileged civil-service schemes of the state's own personnel alongside everyone else's (Federal Pensions Service reform summary, 29 May 2026; OECD Economic Surveys: Belgium 2026).
  • The consolidation effort, as finally composed in the budget agreement of 24 November 2025, falls on capital as well as on labour: a new 10% capital gains tax from 2026, the securities-account tax doubled from 0.15% to 0.30%, the VVPRbis dividend rate raised from 15% to 18%, a higher bank tax, and an indexation cap that bites only above €4,000 gross in wages and €2,000 in pensions and benefits — measures that fall heavily on the electorate of his own coalition's largest parties (budget agreement analyses, CMS and Vialto Partners, November 2025).
  • No prosecution, investigation or enforcement action directed at a political opponent is documented under his government, and no minister or party figure of his camp is documented to have been shielded from one. The courts that ruled against his government (below) did so in the open and their operative rulings were executed.
  • Where the Constitutional Court suspended parts of his asylum legislation on 26 February 2026, the suspended provisions stayed suspended; where the same Court, in judgment no. 110/2026 of 24 September 2026, required an individualized examination before aid is withdrawn from minor asylum seekers, that requirement stands as the law's reading (Constitutional Court, judgment 110/2026).

Evidence against:

  • On one file, the Belgian state under his government continues to place itself outside the law it enforces on everyone else. Belgian courts and Fedasil had already been condemned more than 10,000 times in the three years to March 2025 for failing to provide the reception the 2007 Reception Act guarantees, with on average some 3,000 asylum seekers a month sleeping rough, in squats or in camps, and single men systematically placed on a waiting list that the dashboard of Caritas International, MSF, Médecins du Monde and partner organisations states "has no legal basis" (Fourth Non-Reception Dashboard, 18 March 2025). The crisis predates De Wever — it began in 2021 — but his government adopted it as policy rather than repairing it: reception capacity and Fedasil's budget were cut further as a deliberate deterrent, and the practice continued through 2025 and 2026.
  • In April 2026 the European Court of Human Rights ruled unanimously that Belgium had violated Article 3 (degrading treatment) and Article 6 of the Convention in the cases of four asylum seekers — the Article 6 violation consisting precisely in the fact that the applicants' victories in the Brussels Labour Court had never been executed by the state (ECtHR judgment reported 10 April 2026; the underlying facts date from 2022–23, the non-execution pattern is the continuing one documented above). A state that time-limits a citizen's benefit to the day, and fines non-compliance, while leaving thousands of judgments against itself unexecuted, is not giving by one law on this file, whoever built the file.
  • The asymmetry is defended openly as deterrence, which grades it as choice rather than incapacity alone: in November 2026 his Asylum Minister Anneleen Van Bossuyt refused extra winter shelter for people without papers, stating that reception exists for asylum seekers, not as homeless shelter, and that those staying illegally must return (statements reported by BRUZZ and De Morgen, November 2026 — her position, stated as policy).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do courts, media, the regions, municipalities, and his own coalition partners keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The regions kept their autonomy even against his own party's interest. The Brussels-Capital Region spent 614 days in caretaker mode after the June 2024 elections; when a seven-party regional coalition was finally agreed on 12–13 February 2026 — excluding De Wever's N-VA entirely — the federal government imposed nothing. De Wever said he would judge the deal on whether it delivers credible restructuring toward balance by 2029 and expressed scepticism about its figures; he did not block, override or defund it (Belga reporting via EU Today, February 2026). Flanders and Wallonia are governed by their own majorities throughout.
  • His coalition partners keep their spin to a degree few prime ministers tolerate: the MR publicly vetoed new taxes, Vooruit publicly vetoed his budget tables, and both did so for two consecutive autumns without expulsion, demotion or retaliation documented. In November 2026 a single partner's refusal left him reporting empty-handed to the King for the fourth time in a month (VRT NWS and BRUZZ reporting, November 2026).
  • The courts checked him and were obeyed (Test 1): a Constitutional Court suspension of his asylum laws, a Council of State that annuls his administration's acts in the ordinary way, and no documented case of a judge, prosecutor, editor or rector punished, transferred or defunded for ruling or publishing against him. Public and private media criticise him daily; the public broadcasters are regional competences and no federal pressure on them is documented.
  • The one constitutional change he is pursuing — abolition of the Senate, written into the Arizona agreement — is being pursued through the constitutional procedure itself: the Senate's own institutional affairs committee took the first of what De Wever himself counted as "eight votes" on 30 March 2026, with the Arizona parties and Open VLD tabling the texts (CRISP analysis, 2026). He accepted the King's refusal of his resignation on 6 November 2026 and returned to negotiation — the head of state visibly sits above him, and he visibly sits under him.

Evidence against:

  • The Senate route is contested at its root. Constitutional scholar Anne-Emmanuelle Bourgaux (UMons) argues in a documented critique that full abolition is legally impossible within the perimeter of constitutional articles declared open to revision in May 2024 — some thirty articles referring to the Senate sit outside it — and that the majority's workaround, provisionally revising Article 195 (the revision procedure itself) to reach articles outside the perimeter, amounts to changing the rules of the game in order to circumvent them; she calls it "caesarism" (Le Soir, 2025). The majority parties maintain their route is constitutional. The criticism is CONTESTED and the process unfinished; what is documented is that the one institutional redesign of his term proceeds by procedural creativity against a named constitutional objection.
  • On asylum, judicial checks were answered with legislative persistence rather than absorption: after the February 2026 suspension, Minister Van Bossuyt stated the government would continue pursuing the policy through other legal avenues and that the suspension was temporary (statements, March 2026), and on 13 November 2026 the Council of Ministers approved a preliminary draft law removing reception rights from Dublin claimants altogether, transposing the revised EU Reception Directive at its strictest reading (reporting of 21 November 2026). Each step uses lawful channels; the pattern is that no court answer has yet changed the policy's direction.
  • Belgium's social concertation — the institutional channel through which unions ordinarily co-decide socio-economic reform — was largely bypassed: the reforms were legislated from the coalition agreement, and union leadership complained on the record that it was not consulted (ACLVB president Gert Truyens, November 2025). Unions are civil-society actors, not state institutions; the item is recorded here because concertation is a load-bearing part of the Belgian institutional design his government chose to route around.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, unions, coalition partners, markets, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Inside the coalition arena, the adjustments are documented and substantial. The budget agreement of 24 November 2025 was not his opening table: it doubled the securities-account tax, raised the VVPRbis rate, delayed €1 billion of the promised income-tax cut to 2030, softened the wage-index measure from a freeze into a cap that only bites above €4,000, and added pension adjustments — sickness periods counted in full as worked, the first career year counted at a lowered 104-day threshold — that answered objections raised by partners and unions (budget agreement of 24 November 2025; pension bill reporting, Belga). The capital measures his liberal partners disliked most survived because his socialist and Christian-democratic partners demanded them; he signed the composite, not his draft.
  • When negotiation failed outright, he used the constitution's own wobble mechanism, twice, in the open. In November 2025 he asked the King for more time and received 50 days, producing the deal of 24 November 2025. On 2 November 2026, after overnight talks on the 2027 budget and a €10 billion multiyear package collapsed, he offered his government's resignation to King Philippe; the King refused it on 6 November after consulting the five party chairs, and De Wever accepted the refusal, reported back on 16 and 23 November, convened the party chairs himself, and on 23 November 2026 set a public deadline in writing: without agreement before provisional twelfths begin on 1 January, "the government will de facto no longer be able to govern, which will lead to the end of its existence" (Royal Palace communiqués; 21 News and VRT NWS reporting, November 2025–November 2026). He submits his government's survival to the process rather than governing around it — Belgium began 2026 under provisional twelfths rather than under a forced budget.
  • He absorbed the Constitutional Court's February 2026 suspension of his asylum laws without attacking the Court, and absorbed a Brussels regional government that excludes his own party (Test 2).

Evidence against:

  • Toward the unions, the answer to the largest social movement in decades was refusal. 2025 saw at least a dozen national strike actions and demonstrations — a general strike on 31 March, some 80,000 demonstrators by police count (over 120,000 by union count) on 14 October, and a three-day general strike on 24–26 November 2025. The unions' joint letter to the prime minister went unanswered and their leaders were not received by him despite repeated requests (EPSU collective bargaining reporting, October 2025; union statements, November 2025). His public line was that the strikes changed nothing: "The unions paralyse, the government works" (statement repeated through autumn 2025). The November 2025 deal was concluded inside the coalition on the first morning of the three-day strike and was not reopened under strike pressure (EU Today, November 2025).
  • Toward the market wobble, the response has so far been deadlines rather than a changed offer: Belgium's ten-year bond yield rose to about 4.38% during the November 2026 impasse (De Tijd via VRT), the US ambassador took the unusual public step of pleading with the coalition parties to reach a deal to protect American investment (US Embassy statement, November 2026), and as of this grading the €10 billion package remains unagreed, with four of five parties accepting De Wever's framework and Vooruit rejecting its balance (BRUZZ and VRT NWS, 23–24 November 2026). Whether the deadline produces adjustment or collapse is, at grading, an open file — the wobble is being answered with an ultimatum to his own partners, a tactic he has now used in two consecutive budget rounds.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include asylum seekers, the unemployed, French-speakers, immigrants, and his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The floor under the counted-out was funded, not abolished. People who exhaust the new two-year unemployment limit fall to the municipal CPAS and its integration income, and the federal government budgeted explicit compensation to the CPAS for the reform: €26 million in 2025 and €300–700 million a year over 2026–2030 by the government's own figures. Workers over 55 with a career of at least 30 years are exempted from the time limit altogether. The minimum wage rose by €50 from 1 April 2026 through an increased work bonus (programme law of 18 July 2025; budget agreement of 24 November 2025; CPAS compensation documentation, 2025).
  • Inside the asylum procedure, adjudication continued to run: 34,439 people applied for international protection in 2025, and of some 30,000 decisions taken that year about 30% were positive (AIDA/ECRE country report, July 2026; OECD International Migration Outlook 2026). The government's stated aim was to shrink the inflow, and on its own metric it did: applications fell 13% in 2025, applications from people already protected in another EU state fell 45.5%, and September 2026 applications were 44% below September 2024 (AIDA, July 2026; CGRS figures via N-VA, 15 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Asylum seekers are counted out by design, in the street. Throughout his tenure the extra-legal waiting list for single men continued, with on average some 3,000 people a month sleeping rough in the reception crisis's fourth and fifth years (Caritas dashboard, March 2025; continuing reporting through 2026), and in November 2026 his minister refused additional winter shelter to people without papers as a matter of stated policy (Test 1). The European Court of Human Rights' April 2026 judgment — degrading treatment, and domestic court orders left unexecuted — is the institutional form of the same fact. A deterrence policy whose mechanism is that a defined group sleeps outside is a counting-out, and it is his government's chosen instrument, not its oversight.
  • The long-term unemployed were moved off the count faster than they were moved into work. The two-year cap (programme law of 18 July 2025) began excluding people in waves from 1 January 2026 — more than 20,000 in the first wave alone (ONEM). By late October 2026 more than 130,000 people had lost unemployment or integration benefits, and the employer federations' own accounting of where they went is the sharpest evidence in this file: about 40% landed on CPAS assistance, 10–15% had found a job, 7–9% had moved to disability rolls — and the situation of roughly 40% was "uncertain or unknown" (FEB and partner employer organisations, 31 October 2026). The CPAS federation projects some 190,000 people affected by July 2027. Brussels registered 102,550 jobseekers in October 2026, the most since 2015, 70% of them now without any benefit (Actiris, 5 November 2026).
  • The burden's geography is documented and uneven: of the projected 190,000 affected by the benefit cap, about 80,000 are in Wallonia and 42,000 in Brussels, against long-term unemployment rates of 15.1% in Wallonia and 10.8% in Brussels versus 5% in Flanders (CPAS federation figures; regional rates as compiled in 2025). The signature reform of a Flemish-led government falls, in its exclusion count, mostly on French-speaking Belgium. The government's answer — activation, plus the CPAS compensation above — is on the for-shelf; the distribution itself is not disputed.
  • Immigrants already inside the door face a raised threshold: the fee for acquiring Belgian nationality was raised by law in July 2025 from €150 to €1,000, and family reunification was tightened the same month — higher income thresholds and a two-year waiting period for beneficiaries of subsidiary protection — before the Constitutional Court suspended those provisions in February 2026 (OECD International Migration Outlook 2026; Constitutional Court, February 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Signature promises and stated positions vs. the record since February 2025, with dates. His central pre-office commitment was twofold: the Arizona coalition agreement of 31 January 2025, and the confederalist programme he has carried for two decades.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The socio-economic core was delivered at unusual fidelity. He called the July 2025 summer agreement "the biggest socio-economic reform this country has seen this century" — and then legislated it: the two-year unemployment cap in the programme law of 18 July 2025, in force in waves from 1 January 2026; the pension bonus-malus in the law of 30 May 2026 (voted 28 May 2026), with the malus applying from 2027 and the new bonus accruing from 2026; the 10% capital gains tax from 2026 (agreement of 24 November 2025). Supporters and opponents agree the programme being executed is the programme that was published.
  • Defence — the word was kept early and then overtaken. The Arizona agreement promised NATO's 2% of GDP by 2029 (already an acceleration from the previous government's 2035) and 2.5% by 2034. In March 2025 the government moved the date to 2025 itself, and the Easter Agreement of 11 April 2025 funded it — an extra €3.9 billion in 2025 within a €21.3 billion package — with NATO figures putting Belgium at 2.0% of GDP in 2025 and 2026. Defence Minister Theo Francken's framing was that this ended a "period of national disgrace" as NATO's "most notorious free rider" (statements, February–April 2025; NATO defence investment figures, July 2026). The honest seam, stated here because it is documented: the 2025 figure was reached substantially with one-off financing — some €1.2 billion from the tax on profits of frozen Russian assets at Euroclear, a €500 million Belfius dividend, reclassifications and roughly €2 billion in loans under the EU's fiscal escape clause (Network for Strategic Analysis review, 2025).
  • "The strictest migration policy yet" — his formation phrase — was delivered as legislation and as numbers (Test 4): the July 2025 laws, the nationality fee, the family-reunification tightening, and applications down by nearly half against the previous government's final year on the September comparison. Where courts trimmed the policy (Tests 1–2), the core survived the Constitutional Court's judgment of 24 September 2026 in large part.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The budget promise is off its own track. The Arizona agreement's headline fiscal commitment was a deficit below the EU's 3% of GDP by the end of the term in 2029, under an excessive deficit procedure opened against Belgium in July 2024 with a 2029 correction deadline. Instead the deficit widened every year of his tenure so far: 4.4% of GDP in 2024, 5.2% in 2025 (Eurostat, October 2026), with the European Commission's autumn 2026 forecast projecting 5.2% in 2026, 5.5% in 2027 and 5.7% in 2028 absent new measures, and debt at 108.1% of GDP at end-2025 and 110.2% by mid-2026. The €10 billion package meant to bend the curve is, at grading, unagreed, with the government's survival publicly tied to a 1 January deadline (Tests 3). The term runs to 2029 and the promise is not yet formally broken; on the documented trajectory it is not on any path to being kept.
  • Confederalism — the life's work — was traded at the formation table and has not moved since. De Wever acknowledged as early as 21 June 2024 that the Arizona formula "would make any prospect of institutional reform more difficult." What the agreement of 31 January 2025 actually promised was preparation only: the prime minister would personally draft legislative texts on competences, financing rules and institutions, with experts of both language groups, toward a new state structure from the next legislature, and would seek the two-thirds support Arizona does not have. As of November 2026 no such texts have been tabled publicly; the term's institutional energy went to the Senate file (Test 2), and its calendar to the budget. The man who built a party on the proposition that Belgium should evolve into a confederation governs Belgium, loyally and by his own account effectively — and the distance between the two facts is the largest single item on this shelf, stated by his own formation remarks rather than by his critics.
  • The tax-relief promise shrank in the keeping: the income-tax reduction in the coalition's programme was delayed in the November 2025 agreement, with €1 billion of the €4 billion pushed to 2030 — beyond this government's own term, for the next government to deliver (budget agreement analyses, November 2025).
  • The Hague signature exceeds the national plan. At the June 2025 Hague summit Belgium, with all allies, committed to invest 5% of GDP in defence and security by 2035 (3.5% core, 1.5% related). Belgium's published national trajectory, defended by Budget Minister Vincent Van Peteghem as "realistic and credible," holds spending at 2% until 2033 and reaches 2.5% from 2034 — a plan that does not reach the commitment he signed, a gap his own defence minister acknowledged the government would have to "reconsider how we align with" (strategic vision reporting, June 2025). The signature and the budget do not yet describe the same future.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Bart De Wever's file is the inverse of the strongman pattern this series usually grades. The grip barely exists in it: his partners veto him in public, a region formed a government without his party and he let it, the courts suspended his laws and he complied, the King refused his resignation and he went back to work, and the reforms he did pass were written as general rules that fall on his own voters and the state's own servants too. What fails is the count, and it fails at the two points where his programme needs someone outside it: the asylum seeker left sleeping rough as a deterrent, under thousands of unexecuted judgments his state has accumulated; and the long-term unemployed person struck from the benefit rolls in waves of tens of thousands, of whom the employers' own count can say where only six in ten have gone. His word splits the same way his politics does — the socio-economic programme he published is being executed almost to the letter, while the fiscal promise that was supposed to pay for it drifts further away each year, and the confederalism he spent twenty years promising waits, by his own formation-day admission, for a Belgium that can hold the majorities it requires. As of this grading he is a prime minister who has set his own government a deadline of 1 January to agree a budget or cease to exist. The record will be corrected, in the open, by what happens next.
