The Ruler's Test — Luc Frieden, Prime Minister of Luxembourg

Subject: Luc Frieden — Prime Minister of Luxembourg since 17 November 2023. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Luxembourg is a constitutional monarchy whose head of state, the Grand Duke, reigns but does not govern; executive power — the government, the budget, and the legislative programme — sits with the prime minister and his Council of Ministers. The premise was verified first, because the head of state did change hands during the tenure: Grand Duke Henri abdicated on 3 October 2025 and his son acceded as Grand Duke Guillaume, with Frieden countersigning the Act of Abdication in his constitutional role (gouvernement.lu, Accession to the Throne, 3 October 2025). The premiership did not change hands. Frieden, of the Christian Social People's Party (CSV), still chaired the Council of Government on 20 November 2026 (gouvernement.lu, government news summaries, November 2026). His mandate: the CSV won the general election of 8 October 2023 with 21 of 60 seats in the Chamber of Deputies, and Frieden, appointed formateur by the Grand Duke, formed a coalition with the Democratic Party (DP, 14 seats) — 35 of 60 seats together — under the 2023–2028 coalition agreement published on 20 November 2023; the government was sworn in on 17 November 2023 (Government of the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg 2023, SIP/gouvernement.lu; Deloitte Tax@Hand summary of the coalition agreement, 21 November 2023). Deputy Prime Minister Xavier Bettel (DP), prime minister for the previous decade, serves as foreign minister. The next general election is due in October 2028. Frieden is therefore graded on a full, ongoing tenure of three years — not a completed tenure — and on the coalition programme he was elected to carry out. Under the mandate rule, governing on that platform is not itself a failure and is never graded as one; the Test grades the holding, never the platform. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The Ethic: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them, and conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Every test closes with a plain-words Why and a named Helped/Hurt ledger. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest structural reform of the tenure removes a status distinction at the source rather than adding one. The single tax class, announced on 6 January 2026 and adopted by the Chamber of Deputies on 28 October 2026, abolishes from 1 January 2028 the three classes that taxed people differently according to marital status (Class 1 singles, Class 1a single parents/widows/over-64s, Class 2 married couples), replacing them with one scale for everyone; the tax-free threshold doubles for former Class 1 taxpayers, from €13,230 to €26,650, and the government states some 85% of taxpayers will pay less (State of the Nation address 2026, gouvernement.lu, 19 May 2026; taxx.lu explainer, updated 5 November 2026). Frieden's stated ground was neutrality itself: "Everyone should be able to choose their family model without the state getting involved. The state must be neutral in this regard" (taxx.lu, November 2026).
  • The pension reform's financing burden was spread across all three sides by the same formula, not loaded onto one: the overall social-security contribution rate rises from 24% to 25.5% from 2026 to 2032, shared between employers, employees, and the state (Bill 8634, adopted 18 December 2025; JD Supra eAlert summary, December 2025).
  • The early tax relief was general in form: the income-tax scale was adjusted by the equivalent of four index tranches in 2024 and a further two and a half tranches from 1 January 2025, and the non-qualified minimum social wage was made fully tax-exempt — measures applying by income, not by connection (State of the Nation address 2024, gouvernement.lu, 11 June 2024; KPMG Luxembourg Tax Alert 2024-06 on Bill 8414).
  • No documented case in the record assembled here shows a prosecution dropped, a contract steered, or an opponent selectively targeted by this government. Luxembourg scored 78 of 100 in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025, ranked 8th — down from 81 in 2024, still among the least corrupt public sectors measured (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • One law at home, a second treatment at the border, on the European Commission's formal finding process: in its October/November 2026 infringements package the Commission sent Luxembourg a letter of formal notice calling on it to end discriminatory tax treatment of interest income on EU and EEA government bonds (European Commission infringements package, reported by Orbitax, 24 November 2026). A formal notice is the opening of an infringement procedure, not a court verdict; it is recorded as the Commission's documented position, disputed by implication by Luxembourg's retention of the rule.
  • The competitiveness package builds a deliberate preference for one class of insider-outsider: the impatriate regime, reshaped from 2025, exempts 50% of the gross annual salary (up to €400,000) of highly skilled employees relocating to Luxembourg — a tax treatment no resident doing the same job beside them receives (Bill 8414, adopted 11 December 2024; BSP and PwC summaries, 2024–2025). It is openly legislated, not hidden; Test 1 grades the favour, not the openness.
  • The people who do nearly half the work are counted under rules they cannot vote on and that have not moved: some 236,000 cross-border workers — about 47% of the workforce — may work from home only 34 days a year before their residence state taxes the income, and talks with France on raising the threshold stalled through December 2025, with France stating it would make no more "gifts" and Luxembourg refusing a compensating transfer (Virgule reporting via EuropeSays, December 2025; treaty summaries, LexisNexis Tolley, updated October 2026). The threshold is set by bilateral treaty, not by Frieden alone; the stall is nonetheless the record of his government at the table.
  • The public-order bills draw the line of the law around the poorest people in public space (see also Test 4): Bill 8418 creates an offence of aggressive begging and Bill 8426 strengthens police removal powers (Platzverweis), a package UN special rapporteurs warned in a communication dated 16 July 2025 risks criminalising street homelessness — a warning the responsible ministers rejected in a parliamentary hearing, arguing the bills target conduct, not status (UN communication AL LUX 1/2025; Delano, parliamentary hearing report, 2025). The Council of State accepted the aggressive-begging approach in principle but warned the threshold between insistence and coercion is hard to define and apply consistently (Council of State opinion, reported by Delano, 2025).

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because both movements are real and documented in law, not rhetoric. At the centre of the tax system Frieden is abolishing a status-based distinction and spreading the pension bill across employers, workers, and the state alike — one-law moves. At the edges he legislates open preferences — for incoming high earners, against non-resident bondholders on the Commission's reading, and around people begging in public — and the cross-border half of his own workforce stays under a stalled, separate regime. No favouritism-by-corruption is documented; favouritism-by-design is.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — single taxpayers and single parents, whose class penalty the 2028 reform removes; minimum-wage earners, whose pay was made tax-free; employers, employees, and the state pension fund alike, who share the contribution rise by one formula. Hurt — cross-border workers, whose 34-day telework ceiling survived three years of talks; resident taxpayers competing beside impatriates taxed on half their salary; people begging or sleeping in public space, who face the new offence and removal powers; holders of other EU/EEA government bonds, on the Commission's discrimination case.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • The European Commission's 2026 Rule of Law Report on Luxembourg (17 July 2026) records perceived judicial independence as very high — 78% of the general public and 75% of companies rating it fairly or very good in 2026 — and its only two recommendations are developmental, not corrective: step up digitalisation of court proceedings, and finalise the reform of access to official documents (European Commission, 2026 Rule of Law Report, Luxembourg country chapter, SWD(2026) 916 final).
  • The government has acted on the second recommendation in law: the law of 24 July 2026 expressly recognised professional journalists' access to administrative documents, broadened the definition of a document, and introduced safeguards for challenging refusals — while retaining limits the Council of State had criticised, a criticism the Commission itself records without treating the reform as captured (Delano report on the 2026 Rule of Law Report, July 2026; Commission country chapter, 2026).
  • The media referee is being strengthened, not weakened, on the documented record: the reform of the media framework aims to replace the audiovisual regulator ALIA with a broader independent media authority and to strengthen the Press Council's self-regulatory role; public broadcaster Radio 100,7 "continues to operate independently"; public aid for journalism continues; and a National Action Plan on the Safety of Journalists (2025–2028) was adopted (European Commission, 2026 Rule of Law Report, Luxembourg chapter). The Media Pluralism Monitor counts Luxembourg among only six of 31 analysed countries offering favourable conditions for free and independent journalism (EFJ summary of MPM, July 2026).
  • The constitutional referee changed hands without the executive's hand on it: Grand Duke Henri's abdication and Grand Duke Guillaume's accession on 3 October 2025 followed the announced, staged transfer (lieutenant-representative from 8 October 2024), with Frieden countersigning and speaking in the constitutional role the office assigns him — continuity performed by the book (gouvernement.lu, Accession to the Throne, 3 October 2025).
  • The government's own advisory referees publish against it, unpunished: the Council of State issued half a dozen formal objections to the Platzverweis bill in February 2025, and the Consultative Commission for Human Rights (CCDH) published in June 2025 that its recommendations are "frequently dismissed or ignored" — both bodies still seated, still funded, still quoted (CIVICUS Monitor update, 2025; CCDH annual report 2024, 4 June 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The institution this government has genuinely weakened is not a court but the country's standing social referee: the tripartite model. After the social round tables failed on 3 September 2025, the government adopted its pension, collective-bargaining, and Sunday-work conclusions unilaterally; the OGBL and LCGB unions describe the replacement as an "I consult — and decide for myself" approach promoted personally by Frieden, and Frieden's own formula — dialogue does not mean co-decision — is on the record (EPSU, May 2025; Delano, 12 September 2025; LuxToday report on the 3 September 2025 failure). Consultation bodies that are heard and routinely overridden keep their voice but lose their weight; the CCDH's June 2025 complaint, above, is the same pattern in the human-rights field.
  • Justice is slow, and slowness is a grip of a kind on everyone waiting: the estimated time to resolve an administrative case at first instance rose from 479 days in 2023 to 720 days in 2024, with a slightly increased backlog — albeit among the EU's lowest — and continuing difficulty filling Court of Appeal vacancies despite recruitment rising to 42 magistrates by end-2025 (European Commission, 2026 Rule of Law Report, citing EU Justice Scoreboard figures; Delano, July 2026).
  • Stakeholders raised concerns, recorded by the Commission, about lawsuits designed to discourage public participation (SLAPPs) — recorded here as stakeholder concern, not as a documented government act (European Commission, 2026 Rule of Law Report, Luxembourg chapter).

Why: The grade is HOLDS because the test's named referees — courts, media regulator, public broadcaster, press council, prosecution, electoral process — show no documented act of punishment, capture, or centralisation by this government in three years, and the two external scorekeepers (the Commission, the Media Pluralism Monitor) place Luxembourg at the top of the class. The strain is real but sits one ring out: the social-partner and human-rights advisory bodies are consulted and overridden, which costs weight under Tests 3 and 4 rather than autonomy here. A dossier that graded that strain as gripping would have to name a referee who lost independence, and the record does not supply one.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — litigants and journalists, who gain a statutory right of access to official documents (24 July 2026) and a judiciary outsiders rate among Europe's most independent; the public broadcaster and press, whose regulator and aid scheme are being strengthened. Hurt — people waiting on administrative justice, whose first-instance wait lengthened by roughly eight months between the 2023 and 2024 measurements; the CCDH and the social partners, whose formal advice carries measurably less weight than the model promised them — hurt in influence, not in freedom to speak.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, the ruler at least considered it openly; considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence, refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The street pushed at full strength and two government positions moved. After the national demonstration of 28 June 2025 — between 12,000 and 14,000 participants on police estimates; closer to 25,000 on the unions' count; both figures printed as sourced, not blended — the government abandoned its plan to redistribute the right to sign collective agreements: the nationally representative unions' monopoly on collective bargaining stands unchanged, and the planned Sunday-work extension in retail (4 to 8 hours) was made conditional on a collective agreement for companies with more than 30 employees (EPSU, May–June 2025; LuxToday and Delano reports on the September 2025 round tables).
  • The pension ask itself shrank under pressure, openly. Frieden's State of the Nation address of 13 May 2025 proposed extending the required career by three months per year over a number of years; the law as adopted on 18 December 2025 (Bill 8634) extends the early-retirement insurance period by a total of eight months, phased from July 2026 to 2030, with the legal retirement age held at 65, early retirement at 57 on 480 months of compulsory contributions untouched, and a re-evaluation of the system set for 2030 (State of the Nation address 2025, gouvernement.lu; Bill 8634 as adopted; JD Supra summary, December 2025; LCGB INFO, January 2026). The LCGB itself records that the mobilisation "prevented" the larger extension originally feared — an opponent's acknowledgement that the adjustment happened.
  • When the labour file became the blockage, the minister changed: Labour Minister Georges Mischo resigned and Marc Spautz — a figure the unions publicly treated as a possible reopening — was sworn in as Minister of Labour on 11 December 2025, a week before the pension and shop-hours votes (gouvernement.lu, government reshuffle, 11 December 2025; Luxembourg Times reporting via EuropeSays, December 2025).
  • On housing, the November 2026 wobble was met face-to-face rather than dismissed: after the Logementskris collective's demonstrations (200–300 people on 27 October 2026; around 850 on 13 November 2026, per Delano's reporting), Frieden went on RTL on 20 November 2026, called housing his government's "absolute priority," itemised the measures taken, and pledged to receive the collective's representatives — a meeting the collective had demanded after its 10 November meeting with the housing minister left it unsatisfied (Delano, November 2026; RTL interview via Bytes Europe, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The September 2025 social round ended in declared failure and the government proceeded anyway — the unions' central complaint is about the manner, not only the matter: negotiations that began in July 2025 were not concluded with the social partners; the government "drew its own conclusions" and legislated (LCGB INFO, January 2026; OGBL-LCGB statements, September 2025). Pension reform and the shop-opening-hours law were both adopted on 18 December 2025 over the united front's opposition.
  • A specific, modest ask was refused without engagement: the unions' pre-Christmas call for a moratorium on longer opening hours and Sunday working while a social-impact assessment was conducted was, in the Luxembourg Times' word, "summarily ignored," and parliament adopted opening hours of 05:00–21:00 on weekdays on 18 December 2025 (Luxembourg Times via EuropeSays, December 2025). If another option becomes available — here, pause-and-assess — the record shows it was not considered openly; it was passed over in silence.
  • On housing, the adjustment is slower than the acknowledgement: Frieden declined the collective's demand for a new national housing committee (a position he later softened — a Housing Monitoring Committee chaired by him held its first meeting on 26 October, per Delano's account), and the coalition's own land-mobilisation and vacant-homes taxes remain undelivered, the land tax now planned for 2032 and the vacant-homes tax without a timetable (RTL interview, November 2026; Delano, November 2026). Explanation was full and public; the demanded instruments were declined or deferred.
  • The human-rights referee's pushback on the begging and removal bills was answered by rejection: ministers told parliament the UN rapporteurs' July 2025 warning misread the bills, and the texts proceeded with amendments the CCDH states did not take its recommendations into account and gave no justification for not doing so (Delano, hearing report, 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2025).

Why: The grade is MIXED because the pattern splits cleanly by the size of the push. Against mass mobilisation he adjusts in substance — the collective-bargaining plan dropped, the pension extension cut from an open-ended three months a year to eight months total, a minister replaced. Against smaller, procedural asks — a moratorium with an impact study, the human-rights commission's amendments, the housing collective's committee — he explains, declines, or defers, and in the moratorium case did not consider the option openly at all. That is neither denial across the board nor adjustment across the board; it is adjustment priced by the size of the crowd, and the shelf records it as such.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — unionised workers, who kept the collective-bargaining monopoly and a collective-agreement gate on Sunday work; workers nearing early retirement, whose added months are eight, not years; housing protesters, who won a prime-ministerial audience and a monitoring committee. Hurt — retail workers, whose longer opening hours were adopted over their unions' moratorium request; the OGBL-LCGB front, whose September 2025 negotiating position was legislated past; people subject to the begging and removal bills, whose referee's objections went unanswered in substance.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the cross-border workers who cannot vote, the tenants, the young, the single parents, the Portuguese and non-EU residents, the poor, and the people in public space — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-poverty architecture was built, and aimed at the counted-out by name: the minimum wage was raised and made tax-free at the non-qualified level, the cost-of-living allowance increased, the energy allowance tripled, rent subsidies extended and simplified, and more than 11,000 people received cost-of-living or energy allowances automatically from early in the year, without applying — a direct attack on non-take-up by the people least likely to claim (State of the Nation address 2025, gouvernement.lu, 13 May 2025). A first national action plan against poverty followed, with a single merged cost-of-living supplement from 2027 replacing five benefits with one application (State of the Nation address 2026, gouvernement.lu, 19 May 2026).
  • Single parenthood — the household type at highest poverty risk — was made an eligibility criterion for affordable housing, and the single-parent tax credit was increased, with a further rise to €4,008 a year in the 2028 package (State of the Nation address 2025; Luxtoday summary of the 6 January 2026 package). The 2028 tax reform's child measures — higher family allowances, a €5,400 childcare deduction for under-threes, up to €3,000 a year for low-income school families — are directed at the same shelf (gouvernement.lu, 6 January 2026 package).
  • Housing money is documented, not only promised: over €600 million invested in affordable housing since the start of the term on Frieden's account, 2,562 homes under development or construction across the Housing Fund and SNHBM on their 2025 reports, and the July 2026 "Booster fir de Wunnengsbau" package adding a €250 million housing-loan scheme and a further €300 million for off-plan purchases on top of €480 million committed (RTL interview, November 2026; Delano housing reports, 2025–2026).
  • Climate counting runs in everyone's favour on the outcome measure so far: Luxembourg met its carbon budget for a sixth straight year in 2025, emissions down 3.4% on 2024 and 36.4% since 2005, and the government approved 51 measures from its 2024 consultation to speed photovoltaic and wind deployment (Paperjam reporting, 29 September 2026; IEA, Luxembourg 2026 review).

Evidence against:

  • The poverty count, honestly kept by the state's own statisticians, shows who is still counted out. STATEC's redesigned SILC survey puts monetary poverty at 14.8% in 2025 (threshold €2,502 a month for a single person); on a comparable method STATEC estimates the fall from 2024 is only 18.1% to 17.5% — the headline drop is a methodology break, and the dossier prints both figures rather than blending them (STATEC, SILC 2025, 29 April 2026; Chamber of Employees Social Panorama, October 2026). The profile is unchanged: 40.5% of single-parent families at risk of poverty, 22.2% of under-18s, 26% of 18–24-year-olds, 36.7% of couples with three or more children, 23.7% of tenants, and 29.2% of residents of Portuguese nationality (STATEC, SILC 2025; CSL Social Panorama, October 2026). The Chamber of Employees' verdict on the anti-poverty plan: housing is "clearly the big omission" (CSL, October 2026).
  • Housing remains the mechanism by which residents are counted out of the country: advertised flat rents rose 4.4% year-on-year in Q1 2026 and 5.8% by Q2 2026, an existing flat sold at an average €7,695/m² in Q1 2026, off-plan sales in Q2 2026 (225 units) ran about 68% below the 2017–2021 average, and building authorisations have fallen four years running (Observatoire de l'habitat, rapport d'analyse n°25, June 2026; STATEC data via housing-market reporting, 2026). Frieden himself, mid-term, acknowledged the market "is not developing as the government would like" (Delano, 2026).
  • Cross-border workers — about 47% of the workforce, none of them voters in Luxembourg — remain outside the count where it costs: the 34-day telework ceiling unmoved (Test 1), and in 2026 Luxembourg's labour and social-security ministries voted against an EU reform extending cross-border jobless benefits from three to six months, securing a transition period to 2033 instead (Paperjam reporting, 29 September 2026). France's documented €137 million loss in 2023 from reimbursing only three months of benefits for its former cross-border workers is the other side of that ledger (Virgule via EuropeSays, December 2025).
  • The pension reform's cost falls on the young end of the count: the added contribution months apply to workers whose early-retirement entitlement arises after June 2026 — current and near retirees are expressly untouched (Frieden's pledge, 13 May 2025) — while youth unemployment, housing costs, and a 26% youth poverty risk are the same cohort's documented present (Bill 8634; EPSU, May 2025; CSL Social Panorama, October 2026). The IEA's 2026 review adds the buildings count: nearly 90% of existing homes still heat with fossil fuels and renovation rates remain low, the sector furthest behind target (IEA, Luxembourg 2026).

Why: The grade is MIXED because the counting is genuine in design and incomplete in outcome. The transfers, the automatic allowances, the single-parent housing criterion, and the 2028 family package all aim at the groups the statistics name — that is counting everyone on paper and in the budget. But three years in, the state's own figures show the same groups at the same risk, the housing shortage that drives most of it unclosed, the near-half of the workforce that cannot vote still capped and under-covered, and the pension bill deliberately handed to the cohort already worst housed. Design counts them; outcomes have not yet.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — low-income households, who received raised, partly automatic allowances and a tax-free minimum wage; single parents, who gained a housing criterion and larger credits; families with children, the 2028 package's main beneficiaries; future pensioners as a class, whose system's financing was shored up. Hurt — tenants and young people, whose rents rose faster than inflation through 2026 while supply shrank; residents of Portuguese nationality and large families, whose poverty risk the record shows essentially unmoved; cross-border workers, counted for contributions but not for telework or full jobless cover; younger workers, who alone carry the added pension months.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Coalition agreement and public pledges vs. the record since 17 November 2023, with dates. The two axes are printed independently: keeping a word and whether it helped are separate questions, and all four crossings are named where they occurred.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Purchasing power — kept, on schedule. The coalition agreement promised adaptation of the tax scale by four index tranches; Frieden's June 2024 address confirmed the four-tranche adjustment for 2024 and a further two and a half tranches from 1 January 2025, and the 2025 State of the Nation address could state, with figures, that a single person on €50,000 takes home nearly €1,000 more than in 2023 and that the tax burden of modest-income single parents and widowed spouses had halved (coalition agreement, November 2023, via PwC summary; State of the Nation addresses 2024 and 2025, gouvernement.lu). Crossing 1 — kept his word AND it helped people: the beneficiaries are documented in the government's own worked examples.
  • Corporate tax — kept to the letter and the date. The agreement promised to bring corporate income tax toward the OECD average; the government proposed 17% to 16% in June 2024, parliament adopted Bill 8414 on 11 December 2024, and the 16% rate (14% for smaller companies) took effect 1 January 2025, giving an aggregate Luxembourg City rate of 23.87% (State of the Nation address 2024; BSP and PwC alerts, 2024–2025). Whether the cut was wise is a platform question the mandate rule excludes; that it was promised and delivered exactly is the Test 5 fact.
  • The single tax class — promised, legislated, adopted. The agreement committed to initiate a single tax class, with transitional relief for Class 1a; the reform was unveiled on 6 January 2026 and adopted on 28 October 2026, effective 1 January 2028, with a 25-year transition preserving Class 2 advantages for couples married before 2028 (coalition agreement via PwC, November 2023; gouvernement.lu, January 2026; taxx.lu, November 2026). The word was kept late in the term rather than early — the 2024 address's summary reporting spoke of creation "the following year" after 2025 relief — and the delivery, not the early phrasing, is what the record carries.
  • Pension promises made to the present generation — kept exactly. Frieden pledged on 13 May 2025: no changes for people already retired or close to retirement, study years and baby years to keep counting, and the legal retirement age to remain 65. Bill 8634 as adopted holds all three (State of the Nation address 2025; JD Supra and LCGB summaries of the adopted law, December 2025–January 2026). Crossing 2 — kept his word AND it still hurt people: the same law's added contribution months and the 24%→25.5% contribution rise fall on younger workers and on all contributors; the keeping earns the credit here, and the hurt is recorded in full in Tests 3 and 4.
  • The financial centre — kept. The agreement promised active support for the financial centre, a framework for alternative funds and digital assets, and analysis of subscription-tax cuts; the record shows the actively-managed ETF subscription-tax exemption enacted in Bill 8414, the framework modernisation Frieden itemised in June 2024, and Luxembourg retaining its AAA rating, confirmed by S&P in January 2025 (coalition agreement via Deloitte, November 2023; State of the Nation address 2024; BGL summary of S&P confirmation, 25 January 2025).

Evidence against (promises not kept, late, or receding):

  • The land and vacancy taxes — in the agreement, not in force, receding. The coalition agreement includes taxation of undeveloped building land and vacant housing; in November 2026 Frieden confirmed both remain the plan and gave the land-mobilisation tax a planned implementation of 2032 — after the next election, and after this government's term — dependent on a property-tax reform still in preparation, and no timetable at all for the vacant-homes tax (RTL interview, November 2026; Delano, November 2026). Crossing 4 in slow motion — a word not kept on any term-relevant date AND the delay hurts people: tenants and first-time buyers carry the cost of the unmobilised land in the rents printed in Test 4. The promise is deferred, not formally renounced; the grade records the deferral.
  • Housing as "absolute priority" — the word outruns the record. The ten housing measures of the first State of the Nation address were implemented, on Frieden's own account — and the supply indicators (Test 4) moved the wrong way through 2026, with the prime minister himself conceding the market is not developing as the government would like (RTL interview, November 2026; Delano, 2026). This is recorded as a pledge of outcome ("absolute priority," crisis to be mastered) not yet matched, not as a broken specific promise: the specific measures were delivered; the promised effect was not.
  • Social dialogue as the country's method — the word the tenure broke. The coalition programme and Luxembourg's governing tradition commit to governing social reform through the social partners; Frieden's own reformulation — dialogue does not mean co-decision — and the unilateral adoption of the pension and shop-hours laws after the failed round of 3 September 2025 are the record against it (EPSU, May 2025; Delano, September 2025). Crossing 3 does not cleanly apply: proceeding without the unions helped the pension fund's finances on the government's figures, but the help is contested by the unions and not independently documented, so the dossier records the break and the contested help separately rather than crediting the crossing. Where the record does not document an outcome, this dossier writes "not documented" rather than inventing one — the same applies to the government's claim that the pension reform as adopted suffices to secure the system long-term, which the 2030 re-evaluation clause itself leaves open.
  • The no-good-choice clause, applied by its mechanics where it fits — the pension file's origin. By 2025 the options that actually existed each carried real harm: leave the system unchanged and let the reserve drain as the contributor-to-pensioner ratio falls (the arithmetic Frieden cited on 13 May 2025: average effective retirement around 60, the lowest in the EU and OECD, against a legal age of 65); raise contributions only, loading current workers; cut benefits, breaking the promise to current retirees; or extend contribution periods, loading future retirees. Hindsight options — a wealth-based revenue source of the kind the unions proposed — were in fact proposed at the time and declined, so they count as options refused, not hindsight. Within the trap, the government took a least-harm-shaped door (eight months total, legal age untouched, current retirees exempt, 2030 review) and was honest about the trap in public; the trap's origin — decades of governments, including Frieden's own party's, promising early retirement on cross-border labour growth — is part of the record and is not his alone. The clause therefore covers the choice of door; it does not cover the manner of the September 2025 override, which is graded in Test 3.

Why: The grade is MIXED because the delivery ledger is unusually checkable and genuinely two-sided. The money promises — tax scale, corporate rate, minimum wage, the single class — were kept with dates and figures that match the pledges. The place promises — land mobilised, vacancy taxed, housing mastered — are deferred past the term or unmet in effect, and the method promise, social dialogue, was redefined downward mid-tenure. A ruler who kept every word would not have a 2032 date for a 2023 promise; a ruler who kept none would not have this tax record.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — taxpayers generally and Class 1/1a taxpayers especially, by the delivered relief; companies, by the delivered corporate rate; current and near retirees, by the kept exemptions; the financial centre, by the kept framework promises. Luc Frieden is graded on three years of an elected, majority-backed tenure — the opposite starting position from a compromise appointee: he ran on the 2023 CSV–DP programme, won, and has largely governed on it, which the mandate rule protects from grading as a fault in itself.
