The Ruler's Test — Joseph Aoun, President of Lebanon

Subject: Joseph Khalil Aoun — President of Lebanon since 9 January 2025, elected by Parliament with 99 of 128 votes in the second round, ending a presidential vacancy of more than two years. He was previously commander of the Lebanese Armed Forces (2017–2025), and is the fifth Lebanese president drawn from the army's command. Lebanon's constitution splits executive power: the presidency is graded here on its own constitutional weight — head of state, commander-in-chief in title, signer or returner of laws, appointer of the prime minister after binding consultations — while the government of Prime Minister Nawaf Salam (in office since 8 February 2025) appears as context wherever the record requires it, since most executive acts of the period are formally the government's. His election was itself contested on constitutional grounds: Article 49 bars a sitting senior official, including the army commander, from election unless he resigned two years prior; the bar has been waived for predecessors, some MPs cast protest votes, and the objection and the precedent are both part of the documented record. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The accountability machinery moved against the financial old order on his watch, and kept moving. Former central bank governor Riad Salameh — arrested in September 2024, before Aoun took office — was released on a record bail of more than $14 million in September 2025, but the cases did not die with his release: in January 2026 the main case was transferred to the Court of Cassation for trial, and the sitting central bank governor, Karim Souaid, announced that the Banque du Liban itself had filed a criminal complaint against his predecessor. In August 2026 a judge indicted Salameh together with former Bank Audi chief executive Samir Hanna — the first commercial banker indicted in the file — over alleged embezzlement of tens of millions, including $154 million (2010) and $102 million (2012) tranches cited by the Accountability Now foundation. Salameh returned to custody in July 2026 after missing a hearing, and by November 2026 faced detention orders in three separate cases (the consulting-account case, the Forry case, and the V-Invest case). All charges are ALLEGATIONs; Salameh denies wrongdoing and no verdict has been issued in any case (Reuters, 10 August 2026; AP, 13 January 2026; L'Orient Today reporting, November 2026).
  • The Beirut port-blast investigation — paralyzed for years by legal challenges from the officials it pursued — was unblocked during his presidency. Investigative Judge Tarek Bitar resumed work in January 2025; obstacles placed by the former prosecutor general were removed; in February 2026 an indictment chamber confirmed Bitar had not usurped his authority; on 30 March 2026 Bitar closed the investigation, having charged some 70 people including former officials, and the prosecution delivered its 270-page review on 15 September 2026. Aoun publicly urged the judiciary to issue the indictment, saying on 3 August 2026 that it "has become a necessity that can no longer be delayed" (AFP via Arab News, August 2026; TIMEP, April 2026). The accused are named here as charged, not convicted: no verdict exists.
  • The first financial reform of the era applied to the banking sector as a class: in April 2025 Parliament passed the law lifting banking secrecy, a precondition for auditing the losses of the 2019 collapse, and Aoun promulgated it (IMF and Washington Institute accounts, 2025–2026).
  • Judicial appointments to long-vacant posts — the Higher Judicial Council, the Judicial Inspection Authority, the Financial Prosecutor's Office — were completed in 2025, and on 30 July 2025 the Higher Judicial Council approved a reshuffle of 524 judges, the first full judicial formation in years. Justice Minister Adel Nassar states the appointments followed the Council's own procedures rather than quota-sharing; that is the minister's characterization, printed as such (Ministry of Justice interview, The Beiruter, 2026; ALEF analysis, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The central-bank governorship — the appointment that decides whose interests the financial reckoning serves — was filled by his candidate over his prime minister's documented objection on precisely one-law grounds. On 27 March 2025 the cabinet appointed asset manager Karim Souaid by 17 of 24 votes. Reuters reported, from government officials and a Western diplomat, that Aoun backed Souaid, that Salam opposed him, and that Souaid was the candidate favoured by the commercial banking sector after extensive lobbying in Lebanon and the United States; Salam said publicly after the vote that he had objected for reasons including "keenness on protecting depositors' rights and preserving the state's assets." The Depositors' Union told The National that none of the rumoured candidates was acceptable to it and called Souaid's selection a significant setback (Reuters, 27 March 2025; The National, 27 March 2025).
  • In October 2026 Aoun used his constitutional appeal power against the amended Bank Resolution Law in a direction that aligned the presidency with the central bank against the IMF-backed parliamentary text. Parliament had passed an amended law in August 2026 removing a clause subordinating restructuring to Article 70 of the Code of Money and Credit; on 1 October 2026 Aoun challenged Article 3 at the Constitutional Council, and on 20 October the Council annulled Article 3 and phrases in Articles 19 and 27 on procedural grounds (failure to secure the required majority), restoring the original wording that retains the central bank's preeminent role. The IMF had welcomed the amended version; Banque du Liban had been pressing for carve-outs from the depositor-first hierarchy of claims. Aoun's stated ground was the procedural defect and the central bank's statutory role; critics, including IMF-aligned officials, read the move as protecting banking-sector interests and delaying the reform programme. Both readings are documented; the effect — the law sent back with no timeline — is not disputed (Mercy Corps Lebanon Crisis Update, October 2026; Enterprise, 16 November 2026; Washington Institute, November 2026).
  • Two years into the accountability era, it has produced process, not verdicts: no trial date had been set in Salameh's Court of Cassation case as of January 2026 reporting, no port-blast indictment had been issued as of November 2026 despite the investigation's closure in March, and the financial-gap law that would assign the 2019 losses has never been put to a parliamentary vote (see Test 5). A law that reaches everyone in principle but concludes against no one in fact is graded on both halves of that sentence.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the media, the electoral authority, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Constitutional Council ruled against laws the presidency and parliament had produced, and its rulings took effect without retaliation. On 25 February 2026 the Council annulled the Judicial Independence Law in its entirety — not on substance but because Parliament had failed to consult the Higher Judicial Council on the final text — and the process was restarted with the Council formally consulted, delivering its observations in the summer of 2026. The same Council rejected appeals against Parliament's term extension in April 2026, a ruling that favoured the presidency's acquiescence (see Test 3); it cannot be described as captured in either direction on this record (Levant Time legal review, 2026; Constitutional Council decisions as reported).
  • Aoun used the presidency's return power against a provision that would have weakened prosecutorial independence — including against his own government's interest. Parliament's Judicial Independence Law of 31 July 2025 cut the executive's share of Higher Judicial Council appointments from eight to four of ten, but preserved a power for the executive-appointed Prosecutor General to order the suspension of ongoing prosecutions — a provision Human Rights Watch and Lebanese legal organizations flagged as a standing instrument of political interference. On 5 September 2025 Aoun referred the law back to Parliament objecting, among other points, to that suspension power; Parliament re-adopted the law on 18 December 2025 with the provision removed (IDEA Global State of Democracy report, July 2025; USJ law review, 2026).
  • The electoral machinery ran on time where the presidency controlled the calendar: municipal and mukhtar elections — the first in nine years, after three extensions under his predecessors — were held in four rounds across all governorates on 4–24 May 2025, including in the south under active security conditions, with alternative polling arrangements for displaced voters, and were assessed by UNDP's programme as organized and violence-free (UNDP Lebanon, 2025 key results and lessons-learned reports).
  • The central bank under his appointee acted against the presidency's own predecessor network: Governor Souaid's Banque du Liban filed the criminal complaint that produced the August 2026 Salameh–Hanna indictment, an institutional act against the most protected financial figure of the old order (Reuters, January and August 2026).
  • No documented case exists of Aoun's presidency closing, raiding, or defunding a media outlet, dismissing a judge, or removing an official for an adverse ruling. Critical coverage of the presidency — including of the Souaid appointment and the bank-law appeal — ran openly in Lebanese outlets throughout the period.

Evidence against:

  • The summons machinery against journalists and activists continued to operate throughout his tenure, unreformed. Amnesty International's 2025 report records more than 15 individuals summoned on criminal-defamation and vague charges, mostly after complaints by political and financial figures; in February 2025 the Cybercrimes Bureau summoned staff of Daraj Media and Megaphone News after reporting critical of candidates for the central-bank governorship and of government financial decisions — reporting, that is, on the appointment at the centre of Test 1 — and in November 2025 the Cassation prosecutor summoned Megaphone's editor-in-chief and director. A new media law was enacted in 2026, but its Article 104, punishing "false news" with imprisonment, drew a formal demand for suspension from the heads of the Press and Editors' syndicates on 29 October 2026 after a journalist was summoned to police questioning over company reporting; in that case the judiciary refused the police appearance and the information minister backed the journalist — the correction came from inside the system, but so did the summons (Amnesty International, Lebanon 2025 report; RSF recommendations, 2025; EuSEE alert, October–November 2026).
  • Parliament's two-year self-extension of March 2026 was executed in an exceptional session that Aoun, with Salam, convened by decree for 2–16 March 2026 — the institutional vehicle for the extension was a presidential act, whatever his stated preference for on-time elections (see Test 3) (The Beiruter, March 2026; Reuters, 9 March 2026).
  • The June 2026 Trilateral Framework with Israel and the United States — committing Lebanon to verified disarmament of non-state groups in exchange for progressive Israeli redeployment, with a classified security annex reported to preserve Israel's freedom of action inside a "security zone" — was negotiated and signed by the executive without a parliamentary vote, over Hezbollah's rejection and with Lebanese negotiators recorded as failing to secure either a permanent-ceasefire reference or a withdrawal timetable. Committing the state's sovereign territory arrangements by executive framework is a documented centralization of the war-and-peace decision, which Aoun's own inaugural address had promised to lodge in the state as a whole (UN Secretary-General's report S/2026/790, 7 October 2026; Le Monde, 27 June 2026; US State Department text, 26 June 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Parliament, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He adjusted his method on Hezbollah's weapons when the first method stalled. In April 2025 Aoun proposed transferring the group's arms to the state through direct dialogue with the presidency, insisting 2025 would be "the year of state monopoly on all weapons." When dialogue produced no movement, the government he heads took the decision itself: on 5 August 2025 the cabinet instructed the army to prepare a plan to confine all weapons to the state by year-end, and on 7 August endorsed the objectives of the US envoy's paper; on 5 September 2025 the cabinet accepted the army's five-phase plan. The Shiite ministers' walkouts from both sessions were absorbed without the government falling or the decision being withdrawn (cabinet records and Reuters/AP reporting, August–September 2025).
  • He accepted adverse institutional rulings and re-ran the process rather than overriding it: after the Constitutional Council annulled the Judicial Independence Law in February 2026 for failure to consult the Higher Judicial Council, the consultation was carried out and the redrafting proceeded through 2026 (Levant Time, 2026).
  • On the port-blast file, when the sixth anniversary approached with the indictment still unissued, he said so publicly and pressed his own judiciary in the open rather than quietly burying the file (AFP, 3 August 2026).
  • Faced with the March 2026 war — Hezbollah's 2 March rocket attacks on Israel, launched in the slipstream of the US-Israeli war on Iran, and Israel's ground invasion of the south — his government banned Hezbollah's military activities and entered the first direct Lebanon–Israel negotiations in decades, signing the Trilateral Framework on 26 June 2026 after a 16 April cessation of hostilities. Whatever the framework's defects (Test 2), treating with the adversary under fire was an adjustment of position, openly taken (UN S/2026/790; State Department, 26 June 2026; Le Monde, 27 June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The clearest reversal of his tenure is the parliamentary election. Aoun committed publicly and repeatedly to holding the vote on schedule: at Christmas 2025, from Bkerke, he announced — in stated agreement with the prime minister and the speaker — that the elections would take place on time; his interior minister reaffirmed that there was no intention to propose any postponement; and on 2 February 2026 Aoun signed Decree No. 2438 formally setting the elections for 1–10 May 2026. Five weeks later, on 9 March 2026, Parliament voted 76–41–4 to extend its own mandate by two years, to May 2028. The documented circumstances are real: the vote came a week into a resumed war that had already killed some 400 people and displaced nearly half a million, and Reuters reported leaders had agreed postponement as unavoidable. Political sources reported by Lebanese outlets describe an understanding among the three presidencies, with Aoun — who had initially insisted on the timetable — reassessing as the security situation deteriorated. He did not return the extension law, campaign against it, or put his objection on the public record at the time; the Constitutional Council rejected appeals against it in April 2026. The adjustment was made, but it was made quietly, and it adjusted away his own signed decree and public word (Reuters, 3 and 9 March 2026; The Beiruter, March 2026; Wikipedia's election record citing Decree 2438 and the 9 March vote).
  • The arms deadline has been re-set in public, repeatedly, without the objective changing hands. "2025 will be the year of state monopoly on all weapons" (April 2025) became a year-end 2025 deadline in the August cabinet decision, which passed unmet; the army declared phase one (south of the Litani) at operational control on 8 January 2026 while stating "work in the sector is still ongoing" and stopping short of declaring Hezbollah disarmed there; phase two (Litani to Awali) was granted four months, renewable, in February 2026; the March war then suspended the sequence, and the June framework re-founded it on pilot zones. By October 2026 the UN Secretary-General reported implementation stalled at the pilot-zone stage, with Israel holding a self-declared security zone up to 10 km deep and Hezbollah still armed north of the Litani. Aoun's 21 November 2026 Independence Day address restated the demand at full strength — "the time of weapons outside the state is over" — twenty-two months after the first deadline, with no new instrument announced (UN S/2026/790; Command Eleven assessment, October 2026; Independence Day address reporting, 21–22 November 2026).
  • On reconstruction, the pushback came from the World Bank in public and produced explanation rather than acceleration: on 17 November 2026 the Bank's managing director of operations said in Beirut that Lebanon had been "slow" to use approved aid — of $250 million approved in June 2025 for rubble clearance and infrastructure repair, $4.2 million had been disbursed by September 2026 against a $100 million projection. The prime minister's office attributed the delay to parliamentary ratification and the security situation. Both statements are documented; the disbursement figure is the Bank's (AP, 17 November 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include all confessional communities, Palestinian and Syrian refugees, the poor, the depositors — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The municipal elections of May 2025 were held in every governorate including the front-line south, with the state providing alternative polling stations for displaced voters rather than disenfranchising the war's displaced; turnout in the south and Nabatieh ran at 34–40% under those conditions (UNDP, 2025).
  • The Palestinian file was handled by negotiation rather than by force against a stateless community. Under the 21 May 2025 Aoun–Abbas summit agreement — which affirmed both Lebanese sovereignty and Palestinian refugee rights — Fatah and PLO factions handed over some twenty truckloads of weapons, including heavy weapons, from camps in Beirut, Tyre, the north and the south between 21 August and mid-September 2025, the first such handovers since the 1969 Cairo Agreement era. The process was partial — Hamas and other factions did not surrender arms, and analysts recorded that light weapons used for internal camp security were not required — but what was collected was collected by agreement (Reuters, 21 August 2025; MERIP, February 2026; Arab Center DC, 2025).
  • The depositors were counted into the government's financial design at the small end first: the cabinet's draft financial-gap law of 26 December 2025 provides for depositors under $100,000 — stated by the prime minister to be about 85% of all depositors, and by the finance minister in November 2026 as roughly 90% — to recover their deposits in full in cash over four years, with larger deposits receiving the first $100,000 in cash and the remainder in central-bank-backed securities (AP, 26 December 2025; Reuters, 19 December 2025; Enterprise, November 2026).
  • His public language toward the community most exposed to the disarmament file has been inclusionary on the record: in the 21 November 2026 address he coupled the demand to surrender weapons with a pledge of "no persecution, no revenge, no retribution," and described the army as protecting "all of Lebanon and every Lebanese" (Independence Day address, 21 November 2026, as reported by Arab News, All Arab News and The Insight International).

Evidence against:

  • Roughly 300,000 Lebanese — overwhelmingly the Shia population of the south and the Bekaa — remained displaced in November 2026, unable to return, because the territory they come from is under Israeli occupation or demolition and because reconstruction has barely begun (Test 3). The framework his government signed ties civilian return in the pilot zones to verified disarmament, and Israel bars civilians from its security zone altogether; the displaced are, in the documented structure of the settlement, the last in the queue — counted after the armed questions are settled (UN S/2026/790; State Department framework text, 26 June 2026; AP, 17 November 2026).
  • The depositors as a whole remain uncounted in law. Seven years after the collapse and twenty-two months into his presidency, no financial-gap law has passed; depositors' access remains governed by central-bank circulars (up to $1,000 a month under Circular 158 from December 2025, capped at $50,000 in total). Depositor organizations protested the cabinet draft outside the government headquarters on the day it was approved, judging it insufficient; economists analyzing the draft estimated the largest depositors would recover only about a quarter to a third of their money in present-value terms. After the IMF mission of 11–13 November 2026 ended without agreement, the law was sent back for redrafting by a joint Finance Ministry–central bank–IMF committee, with passage targeted for March 2027 — the depositors' wait extended by announcement (AP, 26 December 2025; Enterprise and The SHIFT reporting, November 2026).
  • The two-year parliamentary extension removed every Lebanese voter's say until 2028 — the whole electorate counted out at once, with his acquiescence (Test 3). The majority of Christian parties and the change MPs voted against; Hezbollah's bloc voted in favour (Reuters and Naharnet reporting, 9 March 2026).
  • Palestinian refugees' civil position — exclusion from dozens of professions and from property ownership, inherited from long-standing law — was affirmed as a rights commitment in the May 2025 joint statement but had produced no legislative change by November 2026; and Syrian refugees, still present in large numbers, faced a policy environment centred on return, with the state's counting of them framed by departure rather than by status. Both are documented as continuities of policy under his presidency rather than new acts of it (MERIP, February 2026; joint statement reporting, May 2025).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

The January 2025 inaugural address and his subsequent dated pledges vs. the record.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • A prime minister "who will be a partner and not an adversary." Aoun designated Nawaf Salam — the sitting president of the International Court of Justice, named by 84 MPs against Hezbollah's preference — within days of his election, and the partnership has carried the government's programme for twenty-two months. The one serious public split, over the central-bank governorship in March 2025 (Test 1), was absorbed without resignation threats being carried out (Reuters, January–March 2025).
  • Judicial appointments and an unblocked judiciary. The inaugural pledges of judicial reform, appointments on integrity and competence, and an end to interference produced: completed Higher Judicial Council and inspection appointments (2025), the 524-judge formation, the resumption and completion of the port-blast investigation, and a judicial-independence law passed twice by Parliament — even though that law is not yet in force (Test 2). The direction of the promise was honoured in machinery, if not yet in final form.
  • Banking secrecy and the financial reckoning's first steps. The banking-secrecy reform he pledged was enacted in April 2025; the Salameh prosecutions widened rather than closed (Test 1).
  • Elections where his signature controlled them. Municipal elections were held on their constitutional date for the first time in nine years, under war conditions (Test 2).
  • The army in the south. The inaugural commitment to implement the ceasefire and deploy the state south was substantially executed for phase one: by December 2025 the army had deployed nearly 10,000 troops south of the Litani and reported dismantling hundreds of tunnels, launchers and missiles, and on 8 January 2026 declared operational control of the sector excepting the Israeli-held points (Lebanese Armed Forces figures as reported, December 2025–January 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "2025 will be the year of state monopoly on all weapons." Not kept. The year ended with the army holding the south bank of the Litani, Hezbollah armed everywhere north of it, and the deadline re-issued in new forms — four-month phase two, then the June 2026 framework's pilot zones. As of November 2026 Hezbollah retains an arsenal Israeli estimates put at up to 25,000 rockets and missiles and is assessed by multiple monitors to be rebuilding with locally produced weapons; its secretary-general stated on 3 January 2026 that demanding exclusive arms control while Israel attacks "means you are not working in Lebanon's interest," and the group has called the June framework "null and void." Aoun's own November 2026 address concedes the fact it protests: "the decision of war and peace is not yet in the hands of the state alone" (Al Jazeera, January 2026; UN S/2026/790; Independence Day address, 21 November 2026).
  • "The Lebanese state — I repeat, the Lebanese state — will get rid of the Israeli occupation." Not kept, and the position worsened on his watch through no act of his: Israel retained five positions past the ceasefire's 60-day withdrawal deadline, maintained a strike campaign UNIFIL counts at more than 10,000 air and ground violations between 27 November 2024 and February 2026 with some 330–340 Lebanese killed in that period per health-ministry-based tallies, and after the March 2026 war occupied a self-declared security zone up to 10 km deep, which the UN Secretary-General's October 2026 report states constitutes a violation of Lebanese sovereignty. The 2026 war killed more than 4,200 people in Lebanon by late June 2026 per Le Monde's count, the majority civilians. The ceasefire's other side is documented too: Hezbollah's 2 March 2026 rocket fire into Israel, in declared response to the war on Iran, was the act that collapsed the cessation — the occupation's continuation and Hezbollah's breach are both in the record, and Aoun's government banned the group's military activity in response (UNIFIL figures via UN reporting; AAI brief, April 2026; Le Monde, 27 June 2026; UN S/2026/790).
  • Depositors' rights. Inaugural-era pledges to restore depositors' money produced a cabinet draft in December 2025 and no law by November 2026, with the target for passage moved to March 2027 (Test 4). His October 2026 bank-law appeal, whatever its procedural merits, was read by the IMF-facing camp as a further delay, and the Fund stated on the record that both financial laws remain "essential" and unpassed (IMF statements, October–November 2026).
  • Parliamentary elections on time. Pledged repeatedly through February 2026, reversed in March 2026 (Test 3).
  • Reconstruction of what the war destroyed. The inaugural pledge to rebuild has a World Bank framework ($250 million, June 2025) and, by November 2026, $4.2 million disbursed and a second war's damage — provisionally estimated by the finance minister at $3 billion or more on top of the $11 billion assessed for 2024 — largely unsurveyed. The promise stands open, with the state's own slowness now part of the documented record alongside the occupation that prevents return (World Bank RDNA, 2025; AP, 17 November 2026).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Joseph Aoun holds the weakest executive seat this series has graded in the region — a presidency that must work through a prime minister, a parliament that can extend itself, an army that cannot fight the militia it is ordered to disarm, and two foreign powers holding pieces of the board — and the record shows a seat used, within those limits, in a recognizably consistent direction: the port-blast file unblocked and driven to the edge of indictment, the old central banker's prosecutions widening to reach a commercial banker, banking secrecy lifted, judges appointed and reshuffled in the hundreds, municipal ballots held on time under fire, and a disarmament decision taken in cabinet over the walkout of the ministers whose parties it targets. Against that stand the presidency's two accommodations with the order it was elected to end: a central-bank governor chosen as the banking sector's candidate over his own prime minister's depositors'-rights objection, and a constitutional appeal that sided with that bank against the IMF-backed text — followed, in the spring of 2026, by the quiet acquiescence in Parliament's two-year self-extension, five weeks after he had signed the decree calling the election he had promised, in public, would be held on time. His two headline inaugural pledges — the state's monopoly on arms and the end of the occupation — remain unmet at twenty-two months, blocked by Hezbollah's refusal and Israel's zone respectively, and restated by him at full strength on 21 November 2026. The tests measure the seat, not the constraints; on the evidence assembled here the seat has moved the state's machinery further than any Lebanese presidency in a generation, and has not yet moved the two facts it defined itself against.
