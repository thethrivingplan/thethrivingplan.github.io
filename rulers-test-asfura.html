The Ruler's Test — Nasry Asfura, President of Honduras

Subject: Nasry Juan Asfura Zablah — President of Honduras since 27 January 2026, for a single four-year term ending in January 2030. A construction businessman of Palestinian immigrant descent, he was mayor of Tegucigalpa (the Central District) from 2014 to 2022, lost the 2021 presidential election to Xiomara Castro, became president of the National Party in May 2025, and won the 30 November 2025 general election with 40.26% of the vote. He is graded here on roughly ten months in office, plus his prior public record where it bears on the tests. That is a thin file, and the thinness is stated plainly: several grades rest on the pattern of his first year rather than on a completed tenure, and the record is correctable as the term runs. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

The seat, and how he came to it. The 30 November 2025 election was the closest and most disputed in Honduras's democratic history. The preliminary-results system (TREP) failed repeatedly; with the two front-runners separated by a fraction of a point, roughly 15% of tally sheets — some 2,800 sheets covering nearly half a million votes — had to be examined in a special hand count. The National Electoral Council (CNE) declared Asfura the winner on 24 December 2025, by two of its three councilors: 1,481,414 votes (40.26%) against 1,455,076 (39.54%) for Salvador Nasralla of the Liberal Party — a margin of about 26,000 votes — with Rixi Moncada of the governing Libre party third at 19.19%. The third councilor, Marlon Ochoa of Libre, refused to join the declaration. The OAS observation mission reported that it "did not identify any decisive fraudulent elements" and that the certified results reflected the will expressed at the ballot boxes counted, while calling the counting delays unjustifiable and condemning violence at the counting centers that forced its observers to withdraw three times; the EU mission reported it had "not observed any serious irregularities that could affect the current preliminary results." Nasralla rejected the declaration, alleged that ballots were excluded and that the online tally had been manipulated overnight on 4 December (his claim, not an observer finding), demanded a full recount, urged his supporters to stay calm — and has never conceded. Moncada called for the election to be annulled. President Castro alleged tampering and foreign interference. The interference charge had a documented core: on 26–28 November 2025, days before the vote, U.S. President Donald Trump endorsed Asfura on Truth Social as the only candidate Washington would work with, and on 28 November announced he would pardon Juan Orlando Hernández — the National Party's former president, then serving a 45-year U.S. sentence for cocaine trafficking. The pardon was granted on 1 December 2025 and Hernández was released the same day. Latinobarómetro's 2026 report describes the endorsement as an act of interference that coerced Honduran voters; Asfura's camp describes it as legitimate international support. The transition then nearly broke: on 8 January 2026 an explosive device wounded National Party deputy Gladys Aurora López at a party press briefing inside the Legislative Palace (the perpetrator is unidentified; the National Party accused Libre figures of incitement, an accusation Libre rejected). On 9–10 January the outgoing Congress ordered a full review of the election and President Castro decreed a recount of all polling stations; on 12 January the CNE declared the decree unconstitutional and an attempted usurpation of electoral authority, the armed forces chief said the military would safeguard the transition, eight regional governments and the EU rejected the decree, and on 15 January Castro ordered the transition to begin while standing by her fraud allegations. Asfura was sworn in on 27 January 2026, on the constitutional date, in an austere ceremony at Congress. His party holds a plurality, not a majority, in Congress — 49 of 128 seats, against 41 Liberal, 35 Libre, and 3 others — and governs with Liberal support.

The predecessor, briefly. Xiomara Castro (2022–2026), Honduras's first woman president, left office on schedule. Her tenure's documented record includes the homicide rate falling to 26.2 per 100,000 in 2025, the lowest in two decades (National Violence Observatory, UNAH), under a partial state of exception in force from December 2022; household poverty falling to 60.1% in 2025 (INE); and her signature promise of a UN-backed anti-corruption commission (CICIH) never fulfilled. Her party's candidate finished third, and her final-month attempt to decree a recount is recorded above. She is context here, not the subject.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The largest corruption-adjacent decision of his first year was a court's, not his. The definitive dismissal of the Pandora II fraud and money-laundering case against Juan Orlando Hernández (1 September 2026) was issued by an organized-crime judge who found, in the judiciary spokesman's summary, that Hernández "did not authorize, administer, had no patrimonial increase" and that no evidence showed diverted funds reaching his 2013 campaign. The prosecution was notified and retained the right to appeal. Fairness requires the context: two co-defendants in the same case — former president Porfirio Lobo and former finance secretary Wilfredo Cerrato, both of the National Party — had already obtained dismissals under the Castro administration, on the same evidentiary theory, before Asfura took office (Reuters, 26 July 2026; El País, 2 September 2026; CNN Español, 1 September 2026).
  • The law also moved against the outgoing camp's figures on a grave charge: on 12 May 2026 the former Libre mayor of Tocoa, Adán Fúnez, was arrested on suspicion of masterminding the September 2024 murder of environmental defender Juan López — an ALLEGATION, unadjudicated, but a prosecution of a former ruling-party officeholder for the killing of a government critic, proceeding in the open (2026-in-Honduras chronologies; Honduran press, May 2026).
  • Asfura himself has never been convicted of anything. The 2020–2021 judicial decisions that first blocked his prosecution (below) were issued years before his presidency, by courts seated under other governments, and his position — that the case is political persecution by prosecutors of the opposing camp — is on the record and is the same defense every Honduran political figure raises; it is printed here as his position.
  • His government continued the IMF program it inherited, with its governance and transparency conditions, and the Fund's June 2026 reviews found program performance "favorable" (IMF Country Report 26/160, June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The president's own corruption case does not move. In October 2020 the anti-corruption prosecution unit UFERCO sought to prosecute then-mayor Asfura, alleging the diversion of 29.4 million lempiras (about US$1.12 million) of municipal funds between 2017 and 2018 — including 23 checks totaling 17.4 million lempiras deposited into accounts labeled as campaign contributions, personal assets, and accrued salary (UFERCO filing; CESPAD report; InSight Crime, November 2020). The alleged offenses — embezzlement, money laundering, fraud, abuse of authority, document falsification — are ALLEGATIONs, never tried. The Supreme Court's Criminal Chamber blocked the first request in 2021 pending an audit; the Constitutional Chamber's December 2022 amparo halted prosecution; seized properties were ordered returned in 2023–2024 after asset forfeiture was denied. UFERCO filed a fresh fiscal requirement in October 2024; Asfura posted 15 million lempiras bail and defended himself at liberty; an appeals court then nullified the proceedings on competence grounds and remitted the file to the Supreme Court. There it sat: in March 2026 the former head of UFERCO, Luis Javier Santos, stated publicly that no natural judge had been designated and no initial hearing scheduled, and that no law prevents a sitting president from being called to hearing. Honduran outlets reported on inauguration day that Asfura "assumes the presidency with an open judicial process for corruption" (Criterio, 27 January 2026). Ten months into his term, the case against the president remains unheard — not dismissed, not tried, frozen at the court.
  • The prosecutor who held the warrants was removed, and then the patriarch's case unwound. Attorney General Johel Zelaya — whose office had issued the December 2025 arrest warrant for Hernández and whose UFERCO held the Asfura file — was suspended and then definitively removed by Congress in late March 2026 (Test 2). What followed for Hernández is a sequence of documented steps, each formally judicial: in June 2026 a judge suspended the arrest warrant and lifted the Interpol notice after accepting his voluntary-presentation filing; he returned to Honduras on 26 July 2026; in early August the court refused the prosecution's request for preventive detention and let him face the case at liberty; on 1 September 2026 the fraud and money-laundering charges were definitively dismissed. The dismissal's evidentiary reasoning is stated on the for-shelf. But the sequence — warrant under one prosecutor, suspension and dismissal under his replacement, in the first year of the pardoned man's party returning to power — is the pattern this test measures. (The U.S. conviction itself was vacated by the Second Circuit on 8 April 2026 as a legal consequence of Trump's pardon; the pardon was Washington's act, not Asfura's, and is recorded here only because its timing — announced two days before the election, beside the endorsement — is part of how the seat was won.)
  • The electoral officials who refused to certify his victory are being prosecuted. Marlon Ochoa, the CNE councilor who declined to join the 24 December declaration and alleged fraud, was removed by Congress on 16 April 2026 (Test 2), left the country, and on 18 November 2026 a Tegucigalpa court issued an arrest warrant against him for alleged electoral crimes and violation of officials' duties — ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated. Ochoa, from exile, calls the warrant retaliation designed to silence him; Libre calls it political persecution; the judicial order states its grounds as obstruction of the electoral calendar. Both characterizations are printed; the asymmetry of outcomes is the fact: the officials who certified Asfura govern the electoral bodies' succession, and the one who refused faces capture.
  • No senior figure of his own government or party has been charged with anything in his first ten months, in a country where the prosecution service changed hands in March. The former attorney general himself now faces prejudicial proceedings opened by the office he used to lead (Infobae, 2026) — the law's motion, in year one, runs overwhelmingly in one direction.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the prosecution, the electoral bodies, Congress, and the media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • He let the emergency lapse. The partial state of exception under which Honduras had been governed since December 2022 — suspending guarantees on arrest and search across 226 of 298 municipalities — expired on 26 January 2026 and Asfura did not renew it, governing his first eight months without emergency powers before turning to ordinary legislation for his security program (country-conditions reporting, November 2026). A president intent on grip had a ready-made instrument and set it down.
  • Every removal described below was executed by Congress through the constitution's impeachment mechanism (juicio político), with the qualified majorities the constitution requires, and with the votes of the Liberal Party — not his own — supplying most of the margin. His party alone holds 49 of 128 seats and cannot remove anyone, pass a budget, or amend a law by itself. His penal reforms passed with up to 119 votes. Whatever else this is, it is not one-man rule, and the forms were observed.
  • The attorney general who was removed was himself a contested figure. Johel Zelaya had been appointed on 1 November 2023 by a congressional permanent commission while the full Congress was deadlocked — an appointment the then-opposition contested as illegitimate — and his tenure drew formal accusations of misconduct: witnesses in the impeachment proceedings alleged abuses, threats, and "selective justice," and after his removal Zelaya himself acknowledged that his office had faced political pressures, including pressure to favor a company manager (Infobae, 2026). The impeachment's stated grounds exist and are printed here; Zelaya's counter-claim — that due process was violated and that he was removed for pursuing cases that touched the new majority — is likewise printed. The dispute is CONTESTED between the principals.
  • The media operate and bite. Criterio, ConfidencialHN, El Pulso, and other independent outlets publish investigations and harsh criticism of the president — including of his open corruption case — without documented closure, raid, or prosecution in his first ten months. The dossier you are reading is sourced substantially from Honduran journalism published under his government.

Evidence against:

  • Within his first ninety days, every referee institution that had touched his election or his party's legal exposure changed hands or lost its head. In late March 2026 Congress suspended and then definitively removed Attorney General Zelaya by 93 votes (49 National, 41 Liberal, 3 others) and swore in Pablo Emilio Reyes — described by critics as aligned with the National Party — in the same session. In the same days, Supreme Court president Rebeca Raquel Obando resigned as moves toward an impeachment against her began. On 16 April 2026, by Decree 61-2026, Congress removed CNE councilor Marlon Ochoa, Electoral Justice Tribunal magistrate Mario Morazán, and two alternate electoral officials, on a special commission's report adopted the previous day; of the four cited, only Morazán appeared to defend himself. Congress is now filling the electoral vacancies, with its own governance reforms for the CNE and TJE prioritized (Criterio, 19 November 2026). Each act has a stated justification; the pattern across prosecution, supreme court, and both electoral bodies, in one quarter, is the grip this test measures.
  • The removed prosecutor's removal was followed by movement in the sensitive cases. Honduran reporting noted that after Zelaya's exit, judicial measures in sensitive cases involving business executives began to change (Infobae, 2026); the Hernández warrant suspension followed in June and the dismissal in September (Test 1); and the office Zelaya led opened proceedings against Zelaya himself. Whether each step is independently justified is adjudicated case by case; the direction of the whole is documented.
  • The security program rebuilds coercive capacity under the executive's security council. On 18–19 September 2026 the National Defense and Security Council — chaired by Asfura — declared MS-13, Barrio 18, and the Cártel del Diablo terrorist organizations; Plan Escudo followed on 21 September with a special prison regime and isolation of gang inmates; and Decree 258-2026 (published 30 October 2026) raised gang membership to 30–40 years and life for leaders and financiers, made pretrial detention mandatory for terrorist association, and doubled the time police may hold such suspects before seeing a judge, from 24 to 48 hours. A CECOT-inspired maximum-security prison for some 1,300 inmates is planned. These were laws passed by Congress — the for-shelf point stands — but their design concentrates discretion in the security apparatus the president chairs, modeled explicitly on El Salvador's exception regime, adopted months after he let Honduras's own exception lapse.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Congress, unions, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His first wobble was existential and he answered it with restraint. When the outgoing president decreed a full recount and the outgoing Congress ordered a review of his victory in January 2026, Asfura did not call his supporters into the streets or threaten the institutions; he traveled to Washington (12 January) and Israel (18 January), let the CNE and the armed forces answer the decree, and took office on the constitutional date. The man who had most to lose from the process defended the process.
  • His government concedes failure in public, in writing, with numbers. On 23 November 2026 the Labor Secretariat reported that the Part-Time Employment Law — a centerpiece jobs measure approved by Congress in March 2026 over union protests — had produced only 788 registered contracts since March, and the labor subsecretary acknowledged on the record that the law "did not achieve the employment-generation expectations," proposing tripartite dialogue through the Economic and Social Council and leaving reform to Congress (Secretaría de Trabajo, 23 November 2026). Union protests against the law at Congress in March 2026 were policed without documented repression, and the unions' substantive criticism — that part-time contracts precarize work — is now echoed by his own ministry's data.
  • After the worst massacre of his term he owned the stage rather than the blame-shift. On 20 May 2026 gunmen in police uniforms ambushed palm-plantation workers at Rigores, Trujillo, killing about twenty; five anti-gang police officers were killed in Corinto, Omoa, in the same days. Asfura addressed the nation within two days — "this is one more wound Honduras does not deserve… this will not remain unpunished" — militarized Colón, and deployed a joint police, military, prosecutor, and intelligence intervention; the anti-gang directorate DIPAMPCO was itself placed under intervention amid irregularities involving its agents, and his security secretary publicly admitted open questions about the Corinto operation ("we want to clarify what happened"). The UN human rights office condemned the massacre and urged an independent investigation, tying Aguán violence to the region's historic land conflict (OHCHR, May 2026; El País, 23 May 2026; Televisión Nacional de Honduras, 29 May 2026).
  • The Taiwan reversal (Test 5) was at least done openly: his foreign minister stated in July 2026 that the campaign pledge had been "overtaken by reality," rather than pretending it had never been made.

Evidence against:

  • On the central promise — security — flat results were answered with escalation and claims of instant success, not revision. Police figures show 2,120 homicides from 1 January to 27 October 2026 against 2,136 in the same period of 2025 (SEPOL, preliminary): statistically unchanged. Extortion, the tax gangs levy on transport workers and small business, did not fall at all. The government's response to the pushback of reality was the September terrorist designations and the October penal decree, accompanied by official claims — the Congress president announced some 75 gang leaders captured "within days" — that the outcome data do not yet support (SEPOL via country-conditions reporting, November 2026; CES Intelligence assessment, November 2026).
  • Institutional pushback was answered by removal, not argument: the prosecutor who pursued the cases, the electoral councilor who refused certification, and the court president who faced impeachment until she resigned (Test 2). A ruler who adjusts argues with referees; a ruler who replaces them has answered the wobble by ending it.
  • His approval rating stood at 31% in June 2026 (poll cited in his biographical record) — a majority of the country pushing back within five months — and no course correction on the economic program followed; the adjustment came only on security, and it was toward more force.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — the poor, campesinos, Indigenous and Garífuna communities, migrants and the returned, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The wage was settled by consensus, not decree. The minimum-wage increase for 2026–2027 — 6 to 7 percent — was agreed tripartitely among government, employers, and workers, a negotiation that counts organized labor as a party rather than an obstacle (labor-policy reporting, 2026).
  • The broad indicators he inherited continued in the right direction through the latest published data: household poverty fell from 62.9% in 2024 to 60.1% in 2025 (INE, via World Bank Poverty & Equity Brief, October 2026), and the IMF program his government continued carries social-spending commitments the Fund's June 2026 review found compatible with a "contained fiscal deficit" and favorable performance. His austerity — five ministries abolished — was presented, including by foreign admirers, as protecting health, education, and security allocations; that presentation is his government's claim and is recorded as such.
  • His inauguration language was inclusionary in form — "I extend my hand so we can walk together… I will not fail you" — and he has governed with the second-place party's congressional bloc rather than attempting to rule against the 59.7% who voted for someone else.

Evidence against:

  • The baseline he governs is a country where three of five households are poor and the historically excluded are poorer still: 60.1% of households in poverty and 38.3% in extreme poverty in 2025, and 76.9% poverty among Indigenous and Afro-Honduran people (INE; UNFPA country programme document, November 2026). Ten months in, no signature program addressed to those populations is documented; the agrarian policy of his government is described by campesino and environmental organizations in Olancho as a rollback in which "the great absentee will be the campesino and the Indigenous peoples" — an interested source's characterization, printed as such, but unanswered by any documented counter-program (Rebelión, March 2026).
  • The Rigores dead were counted as a security statistic, not as parties to a settlement. The roughly twenty workers killed on 20 May 2026 were ambushed at an African-palm plantation in the Bajo Aguán, a valley where the UN human rights office counts some 200 deaths across decades of unresolved land conflict. The state's answer was militarization and investigation — two of thirteen implicated men were reported captured — but no land-conflict settlement mechanism accompanied it, in a region where the conflict's parties are campesino organizations, plantation companies, and criminal structures (OHCHR, May 2026; El País, 23 May 2026).
  • Honduras under Asfura serves as Washington's return desk, and the returned are processed more than they are counted. By September 2026 at least 568 third-country nationals had been removed by the United States to Honduran territory, every flight processed at San Pedro Sula, across some 25 U.S. military deportation flights by November (CES Intelligence, November 2026) — an arrangement analysts describe as the rent paid for tariff forbearance and security protection. Simultaneously, tens of thousands of Hondurans were deported home through 2026 (advocacy compilations put returns at roughly 39,000 from January to October 2026, after U.S. Temporary Protected Status for Hondurans ended in February 2026) into an economy with 74.8% labor informality and remittances — 30.1% of GDP in 2025 — projected to decline. No reintegration program at the scale of the return flow is documented.
  • The 39.54% who voted for Nasralla remain outside the count in the only way that finally matters here: their candidate has never conceded, their fraud belief has never been answered by the full recount they demanded (the OAS found no decisive fraud, and also found the process's delays and the CNE's partisan structure indefensible), and the electoral councilor whose refusal to certify embodied their objection is now a fugitive from an arrest warrant (Test 1). A president cannot manufacture his opponents' consent; he can only be graded on whether his conduct gave it room. Removal and prosecution gave it less.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since January 2026, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The U.S.-first alignment — kept, immediately. He campaigned on a preferential alliance with the United States and closeness to Israel. He met U.S. officials in Washington on 12 January 2026, visited Israel on 18 January, and met President Trump at Mar-a-Lago on 7 February 2026, days after inauguration; Secretary of State Rubio publicly commended his "advocacy of U.S. strategic objectives." Whatever one thinks of the alignment, it is the one he promised.
  • Austerity and legal certainty — kept in form. He promised to shrink the state and unblock the economy: five ministries and other bodies were abolished; the maquila tax regime (RIT) was extended; and on 6 March 2026 Honduras re-signed the ICSID convention, reversing the previous government's withdrawal — the "legal security for investors" plank made literal, at the documented price of reviving the Próspera ZEDE arbitration, whose merits filing seeks restitution or US$1.63 billion (Atlantic Council, March 2026; CES Intelligence, November 2026).
  • Macroeconomic continuity — kept. He promised stability and delivered the unglamorous version: the inherited IMF program continued without rupture, reviews completed 29 June 2026 with a disbursement of about US$242 million, reserves accumulated, deficit contained (IMF, June 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Taiwan — reversed, on the record. He campaigned on restoring diplomatic relations with Taiwan, saying Honduras was "100 times better off with Taiwan"; his vice president confirmed the intention after inauguration, and in May 2026 he said the agreements signed with China must first be reviewed and asked Taipei for patience. On 4 July 2026 his foreign minister announced the pledge had been "overtaken by reality," and Honduras reaffirmed its ties with Beijing (TeleSUR, 4 July 2026; CES Intelligence, November 2026). The pledge was a headline of the campaign; its abandonment took five months.
  • Jobs — the centerpiece conceded as failed by his own government. The campaign plan ("Juntos Vamos a Estar Bien," the "Visión 5 Estrellas") promised work, "work, and more work," formalization in an economy over 70% informal. Its first legislative fruit, the Part-Time Employment Law, registered 788 contracts in eight months; on 23 November 2026 his own Labor Secretariat stated it had not met expectations (Test 3). Unemployment measures aside, the promise's chosen instrument failed on the government's own count.
  • Security — the method changed more than the numbers. He campaigned on police strengthening, community prevention, and recovered public spaces — "work and live without fear." In office: homicides flat through October 2026 (2,120 against 2,136, SEPOL), extortion unreduced, and the community-prevention emphasis displaced by a penal program — terrorist designations, membership penalties of 30–40 years, a planned CECOT-style prison — that belongs to a different doctrine than the one he described to voters. The direction of the promise (safety) is unmet on the data; the means were substituted without a campaign mandate for them.
  • The long arc, factually: Asfura built his name as a mayor who poured concrete — eight years of roadworks in the capital under the slogan "Papi a la orden" — and carried two permanent shadows into the presidency: the UFERCO file (Test 1), which has followed him since 2020 and remains unheard, and his party's. He won the National Party's March 2025 primary with 75.8% against Ana García, the wife of Juan Orlando Hernández, and then won the presidency as the candidate of Hernández's party, endorsed by the president who pardoned Hernández, while Hernández's own legal fate was resolved favorably at every step of Asfura's first year. Asfura has said his predecessor-but-one's case was political persecution; the documented sequence is printed in Test 1 and the reader can weigh it. His word, measured across the arc, is strongest on alignment and administration — the builder's promises — and weakest where the promise required confronting the interests that carried him: Taiwan against Beijing's market, jobs against informality, and one law against his own file.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Nasry Asfura took office on the constitutional date, by a margin of about 26,000 votes, out of the most disputed count in his country's history — and the manner of his taking it is the best thing in his file: he defended the process that seated him while the outgoing president tried to decree it away. What followed is a study in two directions. As an administrator he has been recognizably the man he campaigned as: austere, aligned with Washington, continuous with the IMF program, capable of conceding a failed jobs law in writing and of addressing a massacre without blaming its victims. As a holder of the referees, his first year runs the other way: the prosecutor removed, the court president resigned under threat, the electoral officials purged and one of them now facing an arrest warrant in exile — while the corruption case against the president himself sits unheard at the Supreme Court, and the case against his party's pardoned former president dissolved step by step under the new prosecution. The file is ten months thin, and several of its worst items are formally other people's acts — Congress's votes, judges' rulings — which is precisely how grip usually works when it works through institutions. The grades are correctable: a scheduled hearing in his own case, a prosecution that survives contact with his own camp, and an electoral council refilled by consensus rather than by purge would each move them, in the open.
