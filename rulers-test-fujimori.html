The Ruler's Test — Keiko Fujimori, President of Peru

Subject: Keiko Sofía Fujimori Higuchi — President of Peru since 28 July 2026, the first woman elected to the office, leader of Popular Force (Fuerza Popular) since 2010, former congresswoman (2006–2011) and First Lady (1994–2000) under her father, President Alberto Fujimori. She holds the seat at the end of a decade of churn: Pedro Pablo Kuczynski (resigned 2018), Martín Vizcarra (removed 2020), Manuel Merino (six days, 2020), Francisco Sagasti (caretaker, 2020–2021), Pedro Castillo (removed after his attempted self-coup, 7 December 2022), Dina Boluarte (removed by Congress on 10 October 2025, by 118 of 122 votes cast, on grounds of "permanent moral incapacity" amid a crime emergency), José Jerí (succeeded as president of Congress; removed on 17 February 2026 by a simple-majority no-confidence vote after undisclosed meetings with a Chinese businessman, Zhihua Yang, came to light — he accepted the outcome), and José María Balcázar (elected by Congress on 18 February 2026, 60–54, and served as interim president until the handover). Fujimori won the presidency at her fourth attempt, defeating Roberto Sánchez of Together for Peru (Juntos por el Perú) in the 7 June 2026 runoff by 49,641 votes — 50.135% to 49.865% of valid votes — was proclaimed by the National Jury of Elections (JNE) on 3 July 2026, and was inaugurated on 28 July 2026 for the 2026–2031 term. Her tenure at grading is just under four months; where the in-office record is thin, her prior public record is graded where it bears on the tests, and the thinness is stated rather than filled in. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The case that defined her legal life was ended by courts, not by her hand, and before she held executive power. Fujimori was charged in the "Cocktails Case" with criminal organization, money laundering, obstruction of justice and false statement, over allegedly undeclared Odebrecht-linked contributions to her 2011 and 2016 campaigns; prosecutors sought 30 years and 10 months, and filed a further accusation in July 2025 seeking 35 years. She spent more than thirteen months in pretrial detention (2018–2020) and was never convicted. On 20 October 2025 the Constitutional Court upheld her habeas corpus (Sentencia 185/2025) and annulled the proceedings from the start of the preliminary investigation, finding a violation of the principle of legality; on 13 January 2026 a national preparatory-investigation judge ordered the definitive dismissal of the money-laundering and criminal-organization charges (returning lesser false-declaration counts to prosecutors for a new decision), and in June 2026 the national appeals chamber confirmed the dismissal, holding that prosecutorial autonomy "operates within the Constitution, not against it," while noting prosecutors could still seek cassation (Constitutional Court ruling, October 2025; Poder Judicial records, January and June 2026, as compiled by the Lava Jato Outcomes project from Caretas and La República reporting). The underlying conduct — the receipt of the contributions — remains an ALLEGATION that no court ever adjudicated on the merits, and the legality argument the courts accepted (that the conduct was not typified as money laundering when committed) is a genuine legal position, contested by the prosecution team and by legal commentators who dispute it.
  • On her watch, the accountability machinery moved in the hardest inherited case: in November 2026, nearly four years after the events, the Public Ministry formally charged those allegedly responsible for the deaths of 18 people and injuries to 107 others in Juliaca, Puno, on 9 January 2023 (Fiscalía announcement, reported November 2026). The charging decision belongs to the formally autonomous prosecution service, not to her; it is recorded here because it advanced, and was not obstructed, under her government.
  • No pardon, amnesty or case intervention benefiting an ally is documented in her first four months. Her cabinet is drawn substantially from outside her party — the economy minister is an independent former central-bank board member, and the foreign, defense and justice portfolios went to figures from other parties or none (cabinet record, 28 July 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The rules under which she was judged were reshaped, before she took office, by the political movement she leads. The documented scholarly assessment (Baraybar and Gonzalez-Ocantos, cited in the case record) is that the Congress in which Popular Force was the largest bloc and repeatedly held the presidency used legislation — including a new, lower-penalty offense of illegal campaign financing — in ways that shielded her from prosecution; and the Constitutional Court that annulled her trial in October 2025 was composed of magistrates elected in May 2022 by that same Congress, in a selection process the Inter-American Commission and UN experts criticized for opacity. She announced her fourth candidacy on 30 October 2025, ten days after the annulment. The dismissal may be legally sound — that is contested — but the terrain on which it was won was built by her own side, and no equivalent reshaping has ever been available to an ordinary defendant.
  • In office, she promptly seated her co-investigated at her right hand. By resolution published in mid-September 2026 she created a Presidential Advisory Commission (Comisión Consultiva del Despacho Presidencial) whose five unpaid members include Jaime Yoshiyama, José Chlimper and Arsenio Oré — all three investigated alongside her in the Cocktails Case. Opposition lawmakers objected on exactly that ground; government allies defended the appointments as technically qualified (Panamericana Televisión and Peruvian press coverage, 16 September 2026). La República subsequently reported (31 October 2026, drawing on the weekly Hildebrandt en sus trece) that Chlimper — a documented contributor of more than S/459,000 to Popular Force between 2017 and 2026 — functions as a central power broker of the government, reportedly sounded out even on a future replacement for the prime minister. That reporting is the newspapers' sourcing, not an adjudicated fact; the appointments themselves are official and undisputed.
  • Her solicitude for defendants runs in one direction. At the United Nations General Assembly on 22 September 2026 she used the presidency's largest stage to protest that the service members who defeated terrorism "are still being prosecuted, despite it having been shown that due process was violated" (Infobae, 22 September 2026). No comparable presidential advocacy is documented for the other class of pending defendants and victims in the system — the security personnel and officials implicated in the 49 protest deaths of 2022–2023, whose case spent nearly four years reaching formal charges (Test 4).
  • Her bloc defends the jurisdictional shield. Law 32735 (promulgated 20 June 2026, under her predecessor — inherited, not hers) expanded the "function crime" concept so that cases against police and military personnel pass preferentially to military-police jurisdiction, a change Amnesty International warned could transfer or halt ongoing investigations, including cases with first-instance convictions (Amnesty International, 22 July 2026). In October 2026, when the Chamber of Deputies' Justice Committee approved (12–7) a partial repeal restoring ordinary-court competence, a Popular Force deputy moved to widen and slow the debate, and her party used a procedural motion to postpone the committee's vote on repealing the "Ley Soto" limitations law (Congress communications; Infobae, 20–21 October 2026). In November 2026 Congress gave first approval to a further bill assigning function-related crimes by police and military exclusively to military jurisdiction (Demócrata, 21–22 November 2026). The pattern is legislative, and it is hers by party leadership: the rules for judging the state's own agents keep moving away from ordinary courts, and her movement holds the line.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the prosecution, the electoral authority, Congress and the media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • She governs without a majority, and Congress has already cut her down to size in public. Popular Force holds 22 of 60 Senate seats and 41 of 130 Chamber seats; with its ally Popular Renewal it commands exactly half the Senate (30) and a minority of deputies (56). She requested delegated legislative powers over 66 matters; after a long fight — including a Constitution Committee recommendation to archive the request — Congress granted powers in 27 areas, on security and El Niño (presidential 100-day balance, 5–6 November 2026; congressional record). The Chamber of Deputies is presided over by Óscar Reto of the Good Government Party, outside her coalition (26 July 2026), and the Chamber's Justice Committee, chaired by a Together for Peru deputy, approved repeal of two laws her bloc defends (October 2026).
  • Congressional control over her ministers is functioning, and she has publicly accepted its forms. A censure motion against Interior Minister César Astudillo was debated in early October 2026; he remained in post, and in November Together for Peru announced a further censure motion after the killing of a regional-election candidate. On 20 November 2026 opposition blocs filed an interpellation of Foreign Minister Carlos Espá — 21 signatures, a 48-question docket over S/11.05 million in non-competitive contracts for the papal Mass at Las Palmas air base — with the opposition holding 56 of the 66 votes a censure would need. On 23 November Fujimori backed Espá and her cabinet in terms, and affirmed that he would appear before Congress, in what she called that democratic spirit (press reporting, 20–23 November 2026).
  • The judiciary's head rebuked her to her face and nothing happened to her. After the 22 September 2026 UN speech, the president of the Judiciary, Janet Tello, "completely" rejected the president's remarks, recalling that judges too were murdered in the counter-terrorism war and that it was judges who convicted Abimael Guzmán (Infobae, September 2026). No retaliation against Tello or the judiciary is documented. Independent media publish her collapsing approval ratings and investigations of her advisers without documented interference.

Evidence against:

  • The executive's own ticket occupies the two posts that should check it. Her first vice president, Luis Galarreta, serves simultaneously as prime minister; her second vice president, Miguel Torres, presides over the Senate — elected on 26 July 2026 with exactly the 30 votes of her coalition. The arrangement is constitutional and was voted, but it means the cabinet and the upper house are chaired by the president's own running mates (congressional record, July 2026).
  • The next Constitutional Court will be chosen under rules her Senate bloc wrote. Six of the seven magistrates' terms end in May 2027; on 16 November 2026 the Court's president formally asked the Senate to begin the election, and on 28 October 2026 the Senate — its spokespersons' board chaired by a Fujimorist — approved new selection regulations (a 70/30 curriculum/interview scoring scheme) as an urgent procedure (Senate record, October–November 2026). Critics, including the removed former attorney general speaking as Lima Bar dean, describe the Fujimorist-led Senate as a "bunker" built to control exactly such appointments (public statements, 1 November 2026). The election has not happened; the risk is documented, the capture is not yet a fact.
  • Days before her inauguration, the Constitutional Court reversed its October 2022 ruling that had given Congress control of the national budget, moving spending power back to the executive — a material strengthening of her hand, delivered by the court her party's congressional era had staffed, at the moment she took office (documented in the presidential record, July 2026). She did not issue the ruling; its timing and source are the facts.
  • The prosecution service she inherited is headed by a man installed through the removal of his predecessor, and it is being folded into her security apparatus. Attorney General Delia Espinoza — who had charged ex-president Boluarte over the 2022–2023 protest deaths — was banned from office for ten years by Congress on 3 December 2025 and dismissed by the National Board of Justice on 23 January 2026, a process the UN Special Rapporteur on judicial independence warned against; her successor, Tomás Gálvez, himself faces corruption ALLEGATIONs and, in November 2026, a conflict-of-interest complaint over co-ownership of land linked to a mining project in Cajamarca (he states the land is a declared maternal inheritance — contested, unadjudicated). All of that predates Fujimori's presidency. What is hers: her government works with Gálvez without distance, he announced on 19 November 2026 that prosecutors are being integrated into her Unified Security Command, and in the same weeks he publicly accused the Lava Jato prosecutors who had prosecuted her — José Domingo Pérez and Rafael Vela — of wasting some S/40 billion to benefit Odebrecht (his accusation, as reported from his 19 November 2026 interview). The National Board of Justice, meanwhile, took up a third removal request against Espinoza on 5 November 2026. No step by her government to restore the removed prosecutor or to rebuild the office's independence is documented.
  • In late November 2026 her cabinet coupled a fresh request for delegated powers with a warning that, if Congress refused, it would fund intelligence, weapons and police equipment by emergency decree instead (reported 23 November 2026) — pressure on the legislature's core prerogative, applied a month after that legislature had already trimmed her first request.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Congress or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • As the winner of the disputed 2026 count, she defended the referees and the calendar. Through the weeks-long runoff count she pressed publicly for speed — "every day of delay in knowing the results is a day lost for the transition" — but through the electoral bodies, not against them; she said she hoped to speak with Roberto Sánchez, and her party answered his annulment filings in the electoral courts on the merits, arguing that "if one files a request for annulment, one must present evidence" (press coverage, June–July 2026). She accepted the adjudication process that also heard her opponent's challenges, and took office on the constitutional date.
  • She has absorbed congressional defeats without breaking the frame: the delegated-powers cut from 66 matters to 27 was accepted and then used (Test 2); the interpellation of her foreign minister was answered with a public commitment that he would appear (23 November 2026).
  • Her first cabinet loss was handled as routine government: Transport Minister Rafael Rey resigned on 18 September 2026 citing documented health reasons — he had forewarned the prime minister weeks earlier — and was replaced the same day by José Tangherlini, appearing in the official photograph of his successor's swearing-in (PCM statements and Peruvian press, September 2026).
  • Her longer record includes two prompt concessions: beaten narrowly in the 2011 and 2016 runoffs, she recognized the winners, Ollanta Humala and Pedro Pablo Kuczynski, within days.

Evidence against:

  • The defining wobble of her career is 2021, and it is the mirror image of 2026. Beaten by Pedro Castillo by 44,263 votes (50.13% to 49.87%), she alleged electoral fraud from 8 June 2021, promoted claims that international observers (the OAS and the EU) and subsequent forensic study found unsubstantiated, and sought annulments that would have voided some 200,000 ballots. Only on 19 July 2021, when the JNE proclaimed Castillo, did she say she would "recognise the results because that is what the law and the Constitution mandate" — while continuing to call the outcome fraudulent (JNE proclamation and her statement, 19 July 2021). In 2026 the identical playbook was run against her: Sánchez alleged the process was "seriously compromised," sought annulment of the overseas vote at 119 consulates — the ballot that had in fact decided the election — had a mass-nullity filing declared inadmissible by the Special Electoral Jury for a formal defect and his overseas-annulment request declared unfounded by the JNE plenary, vowed not to recognize her government, called a national protest for 27 June, and announced an appeal to the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights (Reuters and JNE record, June–July 2026). One compiled chronology records a concession by Sánchez on 6 July; his own statements and his party's appeal contradict it in substance, and Reuters reported on 28 July that he still alleged fraud without evidence while preparing to lead the opposition bloc in Congress — which is where he now sits, inside the institutions. The point for this test is not Sánchez's conduct but hers: the institutional patience she demanded, and received, in 2026 is the patience she refused the same institutions in 2021. (For the first round of 2026, the same pattern ran on her flank: her ally-turned-rival Rafael López Aliaga alleged fraud over the ONPE's genuine logistics failure — a contractor's non-delivery left 13% of metropolitan Lima's polling stations uninstalled on 12 April, 63,000-plus voters affected, voting extended a day — demanded annulment and called supporters into the streets; EU observers found no fraud, the JNE refused annulment, police detained an election official and raided the contractor, and López Aliaga was later criminally charged with alleged incitement of civil disorder, an ALLEGATION he contests. Fujimori, then the first-round leader, is not documented as joining the fraud claims.)
  • Challenged by the judiciary in September 2026, she doubled down. After Janet Tello's rebuke of her UN remarks, Fujimori answered that she would not "stay silent" about "the mistakes of the past" (Infobae, September 2026) — words, not retaliation, but a refusal to adjust the claim itself.
  • On her central promise, the response to failure signals has been escalation of the same recipe rather than revision. With homicides running at roughly six a day (Test 5) and disapproval above 50%, her government toured El Salvador's CECOT mega-prison (29–30 October 2026), announced two CECOT-inspired prisons, militarized prison perimeters by Legislative Decree 1742 (8 November 2026), and coupled a new powers request with the emergency-decree warning described in Test 2. Consistency is a defense her government offers; the record shows the dial turned one way.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Indigenous and rural communities, the victims of the last crisis, the poor, her opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

The inherited file, stated as context and not graded to her: Between December 2022 and March 2023, security forces killed 49 protesters and bystanders (Ombudsperson's Office count; the UN documented 50 civilian deaths; 67 deaths in total by April 2023), concentrated in the Indigenous and rural south — Ayacucho, and Juliaca in Puno, where 18 people were killed on 9 January 2023. Amnesty International's documentation suggested at least 20 of the 49 were extrajudicial executions. As of October 2026 no one had been convicted for any of those deaths (country-conditions reporting, October 2026). The prosecutor who charged then-president Boluarte over the deaths was removed (Test 2). This is the population whose counting is the live question of her presidency.

Evidence for:

  • Her program's stated beneficiaries are national and weighted toward the exposed: S/1.5 billion in El Niño preparation (179 km of river dredging, 85% of plan, by her 100-day balance), a 60-day emergency declared on 3 November 2026 across 1,380 districts in 24 departments ahead of the rains, an eight-month fuel-supply emergency on 19 November 2026, 13 million school materials distributed, 2,000 new scholarships for graduates of the regional High-Performance Schools, and a public pledge to halve childhood anemia within five years (presidential balance, 5–6 November 2026; official gazette, November 2026). She reports more than 20 trips to the regions in her first hundred days, keeping a campaign promise of a government "close to the people."
  • Her security agenda is aimed at the crime that preys on the poor: the extortion and contract-killing wave whose documented victims are transport workers, market traders and small businesses. Her 100-day balance claimed 16,325 wanted persons captured, numerous criminal organizations dismantled, and control retaken over five penitentiaries.
  • Her government opened a door to the Puno families. Her Housing Minister, Mauricio Arnillas, used a Juliaca centenary ceremony in October 2026 to invite the Association of Victims of 9 January 2023 to an open dialogue; the association agreed to meet, in public in Puno, on conditions (below). And the Juliaca case itself reached formal charges in November 2026 (Test 1).

Evidence against:

  • The families' conditions name what has not been counted. The association requires, before substantive talks: a formal apology from Minister Arnillas for statements he made in March 2026 as a Senate candidate attributing the Juliaca clashes to foreign agitators — the local practice known as terruqueo, branding protesters terrorists — and a written pledge from Fujimorist senators to repeal the "pro-crime laws," including Law 32735's military-jurisdiction shield (association statements, October 2026). Neither the apology nor the pledge is documented as given. The minister who speaks for her government in Puno is on record with the smear the families want retracted.
  • Her own loudest accountability statement counted the other side. At the UN she championed the prosecuted counter-terrorism personnel (Test 1); no equivalent statement for the 2022–2023 dead is documented. On 14 November 2026 the victims' families gathered in Cusco ahead of Pope Leo XIV's visit, asking the Pope for the hearing they say the state has not given them, and criticizing the slow investigations (Associated Press, 14–15 November 2026) — under her government, four months in.
  • Half the country voted against her, and she lost the domestic ballot. Sánchez won the vote inside Peru; her 49,641-vote margin was assembled from the overseas count, where expatriates broke heavily for her. She pledged on proclamation to govern "with humility and responsibility" (3 July 2026) — and opposition legislators walked out of her inaugural address on 28 July holding photographs of the southern victims, demanding justice and reparations (Peruvian coverage, 28 July 2026). Governing for the half that voted no is the stated intention; it is not yet a documented result.
  • The allied bloc's social legislation narrows who counts in the classroom: Law 32535, sponsored by a Popular Renewal congresswoman and passed by the congressional majority her government works with, replaced the education system's "gender approach" with "equal opportunities," repealing comprehensive sex education — criticized by education and women's organizations as a counter-reform, and implemented by her education ministry (congressional record and critical assessments, 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since July 2026, with dates; her long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The security-first government she campaigned on exists. The Unified Command of 1,350 armed-forces and police personnel was created; prison reform on the Salvadoran model was studied in person (CECOT, 29–30 October 2026) and announced — two new prisons, one for high-risk offenders, with the "Cero Ocio" prison-labor program adopted as a reference — and Legislative Decree 1742 (8 November 2026) put the armed forces on prison perimeters. She joined the US-backed Shield of the Americas, suspended relations with Iran on 1 September 2026, and aligned openly with Washington, all as campaigned (government record, September–November 2026).
  • El Niño preparation was done before the rains, as promised: S/1.5 billion spent, 179 km of rivers dredged, emergency decrees issued ahead of the season (November 2026).
  • Economic continuity, as promised to markets: an independent former central-bank director at the Economy Ministry, growth projected at about 4% on central-bank figures, exports projected at US$107 billion (presidential balance, November 2026).
  • The long arc, where her word held: her January 2021 announcement that she would pardon her father if elected was never hers to execute — the Constitutional Court ordered Alberto Fujimori's release on 5 December 2023 (defying an Inter-American Court order to the contrary), he walked free the next day, and he died on 11 September 2024. She had announced in July 2024 that he would himself run in 2026; after his death she announced her own candidacy in October 2025. The pledge is recorded as overtaken by events, not broken by her.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The central promise — security results — has not arrived on her own numbers. Peru recorded a record 2,253 homicides in 2025; between January and October 2026 the national death registry (SINADEF) counted 1,763, roughly six a day. Her 100-day claim that daily homicides had fallen from 7.5 to 6.5 was disputed by analysts who alleged manipulation of the death registries — an ALLEGATION by critics, unadjudicated — and polling at 100 days found 68% of Peruvians seeing no progress on security (Datum and press compilations, November 2026). The two announced mega-prisons have no published construction start date ("she did not specify when work would begin," 29 October 2026).
  • The delivery ledger is thin against her own plan. A citizen promise-tracking platform (Government Plan Fulfillment Observatory) counted verifiable progress on only 6 of her government's 68 plan proposals in the first 100 days. Her approval fell accordingly: AtlasIntel measured 28.3% approval against 53.6% disapproval in mid-October 2026, and Datum found 51% saying she has not demonstrated the capacity to govern, with her approval down some 17 points between August and October.
  • Her word on elections has two entries, and they contradict. In 2011 and 2016 she conceded promptly (Test 3). In 2021, having signed on to respect the process, she spent six weeks alleging a fraud that observers could not find, and recognized the result only when the law left no alternative — in her own words, because "the law and the Constitution mandate" it. In 2026 she demanded, and received, the respect for electoral institutions that she had withheld. Both entries stand; the file does not reconcile them.
  • The stability she sold has not materialized. She campaigned as the end of a decade of churn; at four months her government has lost a minister, faces interpellation and censure processes against two others, governs by emergency decree across 1,380 districts, and is requesting fresh legislative powers under threat of ruling by decree (Tests 2–3). Some of that is the seat, not the woman — Peru has had nine presidents in ten years and no completed term since 2016 — but the promise was made against exactly that record.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED.

Keiko Fujimori reached the presidency at the fourth attempt, by 49,641 votes, four months ago — and the file is exactly as young as that sounds. What can already be graded is the terrain and the first moves. The terrain was prepared by her own movement: a Constitutional Court seated by the Congress her party led annulled her corruption trial on contested legality grounds months before she announced her candidacy; the attorney general who charged a president over protest deaths was removed and replaced before she took office; and days before her inauguration the Constitutional Court handed the executive back control of the budget. In office she has governed inside that order rather than disturbing it — seating three of her co-investigated as presidential advisers, defending prosecuted soldiers from the UN podium while the Juliaca families wait for an apology her own minister owes them, and letting her bloc hold the line for military jurisdiction over the state's agents. Against that stands a record of real, functioning checks that she has so far respected: a Congress that cut her requested powers by more than half and censures her ministers in the open, a judiciary president who rebuked her without consequence, courts and prosecutors who moved the Juliaca case to formal charges on her watch, and an opposition that — having run her own 2021 playbook against her — now fights her from inside the institutions. She does not grip what she cannot hold; the open question the next years will answer is whether she uses the considerable power the prepared terrain gives her to make the law one law — or merely to keep it hers.
