The Ruler's Test — Bernardo Arévalo, President of Guatemala

Subject: César Bernardo Arévalo de León — President of Guatemala since the early hours of 15 January 2024 (the inauguration, scheduled for 14 January, was delayed some ten hours by disputes in the outgoing Congress). A sociologist, diplomat, and co-founder of the Movimiento Semilla, he won the 20 August 2023 runoff with 60.91% of the vote against Sandra Torres, after the Public Ministry (Ministerio Público, MP) under Attorney General María Consuelo Porras had sought to suspend his party mid-campaign. His single four-year term ends on 14 January 2028; the constitution bars reelection. He remains in office at grading. His party no longer exists: the Supreme Electoral Tribunal's Registry published the definitive cancellation of Movimiento Semilla on 27 March 2026, and Arévalo now governs without a party — the deputies elected under Semilla split in 2025, one faction founding a successor party, Raíces (registered 7 September 2026), which moved into opposition to his government in November 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The law's machinery reached his own circle throughout his term, and he did not obstruct it. The MP under Porras — an attorney general he could not remove and publicly opposed — investigated his party, sought repeatedly to lift his own presidential immunity, raided his Health Ministry, and in October 2025 presented a case it named "UNOPS: corrupción presidencial," seeking the lifting of Health Minister Joaquín Barnoya's immunity over the government's medicine-procurement agreement with the UN agency UNOPS and freezing some US$912 million of the contract balance (Public Ministry presentation, 21 October 2025; Congress Health Commission record, 2026). Arévalo's government answered in court and in public — it called the measures illegal and pointed to a Contraloría audit of fiscal 2024 that reported no findings on the agreement — but it did not block the raids, dismiss the investigators, or shield the minister from the process. The underlying conduct is an ALLEGATION, unadjudicated; investigative reporting that reviewed the judicial file (No Ficción, 2026) found it documented possible administrative faults but did not substantiate the prosecutors' claim of a criminal structure headed by the executive. The government's position — that the agreement was lawful and cut medicine prices by some 31% — is recorded; so is the critics' position (e.g., Universidad Francisco Marroquín analysts, December 2025) that routing state purchasing around the national procurement law through an international intermediary was itself a departure from one common rulebook.
  • His own anti-corruption instrument filed against power, in volume and in public. The National Anti-Corruption Commission (CNC), created in February 2024 under commissioner Santiago Palomo, published its case files on an open portal and, by its own dashboard, had referred 358 complaints to the MP by late 2025 — 63 dismissed and 18 archived by the prosecutor's office, 242 left under investigation — a figure Arévalo updated to 450 complaints in a press conference on 15 November 2026 (CNC quarterly reports, 2025; presidential press conference, 15 November 2026). The complaints overwhelmingly concern the previous administration, which is also the documented pattern of where the alleged looting occurred.
  • When failure occurred inside his own security chain, consequences followed within days. After 20 Barrio 18 leaders escaped from the Fraijanes II prison — an escape that occurred on 8–10 October 2025 and was revealed publicly days later — Arévalo accepted the resignations of Interior Minister Francisco Jiménez and his two senior vice ministers on 15–17 October 2025, removed the prison-system leadership, and appointed a respected high-risk-court judge, Marco Antonio Villeda, as interior minister (presidential address and EFE reporting, 15–17 October 2025). The MP's follow-on investigation reached former officials of his own ministry; the government stated the outgoing minister retained his legal immunity until his successor was sworn in and did not contest the raids (Agencia Ocote, 18 October 2025).

Evidence against:

  • One law was promised as results, and the results are thin. Nearly three years in, no senior figure of the networks Arévalo calls the "pact of the corrupt" has been convicted on the strength of his government's complaints; a fifth of the CNC's referrals were dismissed or archived by Porras's MP and most of the rest sat unacted-on, and the CNC itself is a preventive and administrative body — ethics codes, probity offices, training — without prosecutorial power. Critics across the spectrum, including former allies, describe an anti-corruption policy of filings rather than outcomes (CNC dashboard, 2025; Fitch Solutions country analysis, December 2025; Aula/ICEFI analysis, July 2026).
  • His signature procurement decision cut both ways on this test. The April 2024 UNOPS agreement — up to US$900 million over 47 months — was justified as an anti-corruption measure, and the government cites audited savings; but it moved the state's largest purchasing stream outside the ordinary state-contracting system and its public portal, so the rule applied to everyone else's purchases did not apply to his government's largest one. That is the documented core of the dispute, separate from the MP's unproven criminal framing (UNOPS agreement records, 2024; UFM analysis, 4 December 2025).
  • The successor he chose at the MP carries a proximity question, answered but not erased by process. Attorney General Gabriel García Luna, appointed 5 May 2026, was selected from the nominating commission's shortlist of six, as the constitution requires; he had, however, been serving immediately before as a penal adviser within the executive's own legal office (the Procuraduría General de la Nación), a closeness noted by InSight Crime (May 2026). No evidence documents an instruction from Arévalo to the new prosecutor; García Luna's first acts — liquidating the FECI unit that had run the cases against Semilla and the exiles, and opening a case-by-case review of the exiles' files — are recorded under Test 2.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, the prosecution, Congress, and the media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • He governed for 28 months beside an attorney general who had tried to annul his election, and he left her in place because the law gave him no power to remove her. The documented sequence of the transition: on 12 July 2023 the MP announced Semilla's suspension mid-campaign (a Constitutional Court amparo reversed it the next day); on 30 September 2023 MP agents raided the Supreme Electoral Tribunal (TSE) and seized the tally-sheet boxes — some 125,000 pages of electoral records; on 8 December 2023 the MP declared the election records irregular and said the elections should be annulled; the Constitutional Court ordered the transition protected on 14 December 2023 (electoral records; GHRC, Fall 2023; Constitutional Court ruling, December 2023). In office, Arévalo's responses to Porras ran through legal channels only: criminal complaints against her for abuse of authority, a May 2024 legislative initiative to reform the MP's organic law — which the opposition Congress let die — and public argument (U.S. Congressional Research Service, April 2025). She served out her term to 17 May 2026.
  • The succession, when it came, followed the constitutional machinery rather than his preference. A nominating commission of the Supreme Court president, law-school deans, and the bar produced the shortlist of six from 59 applicants; the commission left Porras — who had applied for a third term — off the list, and kept her off even after the Constitutional Court froze the process and ordered a review of the finalists' qualifications. Arévalo chose García Luna from that list on 5 May 2026 (Reuters, 6 May 2026; Inter-American Dialogue, Latin America Advisor, 1 May 2026).
  • Courts ruled against him and his movement, and the rulings stood. On 16 January 2024 the Constitutional Court invalidated the election of his ally Samuel Pérez as president of Congress; Congress re-voted on 19 January and Arévalo's side complied (IPU Parline record, 2024). On 29 October 2025 the Constitutional Court overturned Judge Fredy Orellana's order declaring Semilla's registration null — and when the TSE Registry nonetheless published the party's definitive cancellation on 27 March 2026, executing the November 2024 judicial cancellation order, Arévalo contested it politically and legally but did not defy the registry, purge the tribunal, or move against the judges; he governs on as an independent (International IDEA democracy tracker, October 2025; TSE cancellation edicts published 27 March 2026; the legal interplay between the Court's October 2025 ruling and the Registry's March 2026 act is contested between the institutions). His one direct move against a judge — a disciplinary complaint against Judge Orellana — was filed with the Judicial Discipline Board, the body the law provides (eP Investiga, 2025).
  • Congress is opposition-controlled and has behaved like it: it elected the Supreme Court against his preferences, let his MP-reform bill die, presides over his budget fights, and in November 2026 his own former party's successor bloc conditioned its votes on the 2027 budget. He has neither dissolved it, packed it, nor stripped its powers; its autonomy is not a concession he grants but a fact he lives with (Reuters, 11 March 2026; La Portada, 13 November 2026).
  • The 2026 renewal of the referees happened on schedule and not at his direction. Congress elected a new TSE for 2026–2032 in March 2026 (Reuters, 11 March 2026); the Constitutional Court was reconstituted on 14 April 2026 by its five separate appointing bodies. For his own two appointments Arévalo first issued a public call for applications — a first — and on 12 March 2026 designated Gladys Annabella Morfín as titular magistrate and María Magdalena Jocholá, the court's first Indigenous woman member, as alternate (Acuerdo Gubernativo 29-2026; Diario de Centro América, March 2026). Observers criticized other bodies' choices — Congress re-elected Roberto Molina Barreto and the Supreme Court re-elected Dina Ochoa, both faulted by international observers for past rulings against Arévalo's movement — and those choices also stood, unvetoed and unchallenged by force (Reuters, 11 March 2026; No Ficción, March 2026).
  • The media's autonomy is documented in the negative space of his record: the persecution of the press under his presidency was run by the institution he did not control. José Rubén Zamora, founder of elPeriódico, arrested in July 2022 under the previous government, spent much of Arévalo's first two years in prison or fighting return-to-prison orders at the MP's insistence; it was judges — not the executive — who released him to house arrest in October 2024, and again on 12 February 2026 after 1,295 cumulative days in prison, and the Supreme Court's definitive amparo of 20 March 2026 and an appeals court's second annulment of his money-laundering conviction on 28 July 2026 came from the courts (case timeline compiled by the Free Zamora campaign and Clooney Foundation TrialWatch, 2026; Reporters Without Borders, February 2026). The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found Zamora's detention arbitrary. His cases remain open, and prosecutors sought a further arrest warrant in May 2026 — under the outgoing Porras administration — which is recorded here as the MP's act, not the president's; no documented act of Arévalo's executive has moved against an outlet or a journalist.

Evidence against (the seams):

  • Emergency powers became a recurring instrument in his third year. After Barrio 18 seized three prisons on 17 January 2026, took 46 hostages, and murdered 10 police officers in retaliation attacks, Arévalo declared a 30-day nationwide state of siege, which Congress approved 149–1; it restricted movement and allowed detention without prior court order. He let it lapse on schedule on 16 February 2026 and stepped down to rolling 15-day "states of prevention," and in December 2025 he had declared a state of emergency in municipalities of Sololá after armed attacks on soldiers. But on 14 November 2026 he declared a further 30-day state of siege in the departments of Zacapa and Izabal, which Congress ratified on 17 November, additionally authorizing the security forces to take control of the prisons (AP, 18–19 January 2026; GHRC update, February 2026; country-conditions reporting, November 2026). Each measure was congressionally approved and time-limited, and none was used against the political opposition — but the cadence of exception is the documented seam in an otherwise non-gripping record.
  • The anti-gang statute he supported widens the state's frame: Congress's Decree 11-2025 of 21 October 2025, passed in the wake of the Fraijanes escape with his government's backing, declares the maras terrorist organizations and raises extortion penalties to 18 years. Rights organizations' concern — that the terrorism label built for gangs is the same label Porras's prosecutors had applied to protesters (Test 4) — is recorded as their position.
  • His rhetoric pressed on the institutions even where his hands did not. For two years he publicly named the attorney general and judges as members of "political-criminal mafias" and called the MP a kidnapped institution; supporters call this naming a documented reality (Porras was sanctioned by more than 40 countries, a fact), and critics — including the UN Special Rapporteur on judicial independence, who in January 2026 urged an end to the instrumentalization of justice by all sides — treat sustained presidential denunciation of sitting judges as pressure in itself (OHCHR, January 2026). The grade weighs acts over words: the acts show a president who complied with adverse rulings, including the ruling that killed his own party.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Congress, the prosecutor's office, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His fastest reversal was total and public. On 16–17 March 2025 his government published long-delayed regulations making third-party vehicle insurance mandatory within 45 days; on 18–19 March, transport and popular blockades shut down routes in the capital. After an initial day of insisting there would be no retreat, Arévalo addressed the nation on the night of 19 March, repealed all four regulations, and replaced them with a dialogue table charged with producing a consensus rule within a year (Government Agreements 56–59-2025, published 20 March 2025; Agencia Guatemalteca de Noticias, 20 March 2025).
  • He absorbed judicial defeats without reprisal: the Constitutional Court's annulment of his congressional board (January 2024), the courts' protection of process in the Zamora case, and Congress's October 2024 election of a Supreme Court he had criticized — he had invited the OAS to observe that election, the first such observation mission, and accepted its outcome while disputing its quality (OAS and Panel of Independent Experts reporting, October 2024; GHRC court-elections report, October 2024).
  • The Fraijanes escape produced acknowledgment before defense: his interior minister admitted the failure on the record, the president called the escape "unacceptable" in a national address, accepted the security cabinet's resignations, and announced a prison census, a new maximum-security prison, and a corrective task force (EFE, 15–17 October 2025).
  • The January 2026 siege was de-escalated on his own schedule: declared for 30 days, it was allowed to expire on 16 February 2026 and replaced with the less restrictive state of prevention, with the government publishing its claimed results (83 arrests of gang members it described as highly dangerous) rather than seeking indefinite renewal — the contrast with the region's permanent emergencies is documented in the analysis of the measure itself (Just Security, January 2026; GHRC, February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The insurance episode cuts both ways: the reversal came only after two days of blockades and an initial threat to clear roads by force, and the promised sequel did not arrive — victims' organizations state that at the repeal he undertook to re-publish the regulations by March 2026, and on 27 October 2026 a national alliance of victims' and transport organizations mobilized to protest that the commitment had not been met and the file had been left to them alone (Quinto Poder, 31 October 2026; the alliance's account, contested by implication — the government has pointed to the ongoing dialogue table).
  • On Porras, two years of demanding a resignation he had no constitutional power to compel — while his lawful alternative, the organic-law reform, sat unpassed in a Congress he could not move — read to many observers, including sympathetic ones, as a wobble answered with rhetoric rather than adjustment: the stand-off consumed the political capital his delivery agenda needed (InSight Crime analysis of the stand-off, 2025; Fitch Solutions, December 2025).
  • The delivery failures were conceded late. Budget execution — the core complaint of his third year — stood at roughly 72% government-wide by late October 2026, with the infrastructure ministry at about 53% and independent analysts putting road-infrastructure execution near 16% as of July 2026 (congressional oversight hearing on MIDES/CIV execution, November 2026; ICEFI analysis, July 2026). His public answers through 2026 emphasized obstruction by the "pact" over his own administration's execution capacity, and the reckoning inside his own movement came from outside the palace: in November 2026 the Raíces leadership publicly catalogued the government's errors, and Arévalo's reply — "recognizing mistakes is easy" — asked for action over acknowledgment without himself itemizing the mistakes (Soy502, 14 November 2026; Centra News, November 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Indigenous peoples, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The people who physically defended his inauguration were counted in as governing counterparts, at least at the level of recognition: his administration adopted the Public Policy for the Protection of Human Rights Defenders 2025–2035, maintained dialogue structures with ancestral authorities, and appointed Indigenous officials — most visibly Luis Pacheco of the 48 Cantones as Vice Minister of Sustainable Development in the Ministry of Energy and Mines, and, in March 2026, the first Indigenous woman to sit on the Constitutional Court, as his own alternate designation (ICNL Civic Freedom Monitor, October 2026; Diario de Centro América, March 2026). A U.S. House resolution of April 2024 (H.Res.1186) records the fact this test starts from: the Indigenous-led national strike launched 2 October 2023 — sustained for some 106 days by the 48 Cantones of Totonicapán and allied ancestral authorities — was the indispensable defense of the transfer of power.
  • The social floor was built without a partisan test and reached documented scale: the Bono Social cash transfer reached more than 350,000 households across 322 municipalities by late 2026, and the Mano a Mano strategy — concrete floors, stoves, water filters, latrines delivered in the poorest municipalities — had benefited more than 75,000 families in its first two years, with a Universidad del Valle study cited by the government attributing a 5% reduction in the poverty index of the prioritized municipalities (MIDES reporting to Congress, November 2026). No documented program of his excludes opposition regions or voters; his budgets passed an opposition Congress that represents them.
  • The macro-accounts include everyone by construction and improved on his watch: growth of about 4.3% in 2025 with inflation under 2% and record international reserves, and a January 2026 trade agreement under which the United States grants tariff-free access to more than 70% of Guatemalan exports (IMF projections and reported 2025 outturns; government statements, January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The sharpest item in this dossier sits here. On 23 April 2025, police arrested Luis Pacheco — then serving as Arévalo's own vice minister — and Héctor Chaclán, the former president and treasurer of the 48 Cantones, on charges of terrorism, unlawful association, and obstruction brought by Porras's MP for their leadership of the October 2023 protests that had saved the transition. A judge ordered pretrial detention; the case was sealed; hearings stalled for months amid judicial recusals. Amnesty International designated both men prisoners of conscience in May 2026, finding the proceedings unfounded and part of a documented pattern of criminalization; Human Rights Watch documented the due-process violations in letters to the UN and EU (Amnesty International, October 2025 and 6 May 2026; Human Rights Watch, May and December 2025). They were released with alternative measures only on 5 August 2026, after 468 days in pretrial detention — and the charges have not been dropped (Cultural Survival, 5 August 2026). Attribution matters and is stated exactly: the prosecution was the MP's, not the executive's; Arévalo's government publicly condemned the arrests as criminalization. But the men were counted out of Guatemalan public life for over a year of his presidency, his government could not or did not find a lawful instrument to end it sooner, and the file closes his term's third year with the defenders of the count themselves still facing trial for having defended it.
  • The security ledger fell hardest on the poor while he governed. Homicides fell in 2024 to 2,869 (16.1 per 100,000) but rose in 2025 to 3,139 (17.4 per 100,000) — Guatemala was the only Central American country where killings increased that year — and extortion complaints climbed from 18,235 in 2023 to 24,978 in 2024 and 25,961 in 2025, the gangs' tax on bus drivers, market vendors, and small shops (InSight Crime homicide round-up, 2026; CIEN data via U.S. State Department OSAC report, 2025; country-conditions reporting, November 2026). The trend bent in 2026 — homicides through September ran 62 below the prior year, and extortion complaints fell sharply during and after the siege (CIEN, 2026) — but the two worst years of the extortion epidemic are his.
  • Delivery to the counted-in lagged the rhetoric of inclusion: with overall budget execution near 72% and infrastructure near the bottom of the table in late 2026 (Test 3), the schools, roads, and clinics promised to the rural and Indigenous majority arrived slower than the promises; chronic child malnutrition, long measured near 46% of children under five — among the highest rates in the world — has no documented step-change on his watch (World Bank country data; ICEFI, July 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises and stated positions vs. the record since January 2024, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The constitutional word — kept. He promised to govern one term within the constitution and to defend the transfer of power; he has made no move toward reelection (which the constitution bars), and the 2026 renewal of the TSE, the Constitutional Court, and the attorney general's office — the machinery that will run the 2027 election he cannot contest — proceeded on its constitutional timetable under his presidency, including the appointment of a prosecutor he chose only from the lawful shortlist (Reuters, March–May 2026).
  • The anti-corruption architecture — built as designed, if not yet fruitful. He promised a national anti-corruption commission and open case files: the CNC was created in February 2024, publishes its referrals, and reached 450 complaints by November 2026 (Test 1). He promised to seek Porras's removal by law: he filed the complaints and the reform bill — the promise's method was kept even where its object was not achieved (CRS, April 2025).
  • The open door to Washington — kept, on stated terms. He told the United States he would cooperate on migration while insisting development was the permanent answer; on 5 February 2025, beside Secretary of State Marco Rubio, he announced a 40% increase in deportation flights and acceptance of third-country deportees for onward repatriation at U.S. expense, and the arrangement has operated since — alongside, not instead of, his stated development position (AP/NPR reporting, 5 February 2025). Guatemala's recognition of Taiwan, which he pledged to maintain, stands.
  • Economic stewardship — as stated. He promised stability without austerity shocks: growth near 4%, inflation under 2% at the end of 2025, upgraded sovereign ratings (which he cited on 15 November 2026), and the January 2026 U.S. trade agreement (IMF, 2026; presidential press conference, November 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The "Guatemalan spring" — not delivered at the promised scale. The 2023 campaign's central promise was a state recovered from corruption: by November 2026 the measure the public applies is outcomes — no landmark convictions, a prosecutor's office only six months into new hands, and his own party cancelled by the institutions he pledged to reform. His approval fell from 54% in May 2024 to 39% in January 2025 and to the bottom of the regional table in 2026 — 33.1% in June and 28.2% in September in CB Global Data's regional surveys (Prensa Libre polling via CRS, 2025; CB Global Data, June and September 2026). A promise can fail by obstruction and still be unkept; the record shows both the obstruction and the shortfall.
  • Security — the promise ran backwards before it bent. He pledged a safer Guatemala through intelligence-led, rights-respecting policing; the documented mid-term record is the 2025 homicide rise, the extortion epidemic's peak years, a prison system — 23,000 inmates in space for some 6,800 — that twice exploded into national crisis on his watch, and a government driven by January 2026 to the region's classic instrument, the state of siege, however lawfully and temporarily applied (Tests 2 and 4; CIEN, 2026).
  • The insurance undertaking — a dated promise missed. At the 19 March 2025 repeal he committed to a one-year dialogue and, on the victims' alliance's documented account, to re-publishing the road-insurance regulations by March 2026; in October 2026 the alliance was in the streets noting the date had passed (Quinto Poder, 31 October 2026).
  • Infrastructure and the visible state — behind his own schedule. The campaign promised roads, hospitals, and schools as the proof of honest government; by his third year the infrastructure ministry's execution stood near half its budget and independent estimates of road execution near 16%, and his communications minister for infrastructure, Félix Alvarado, had resigned in November 2024 amid criticism of non-delivery (congressional execution hearings, November 2026; ICEFI, July 2026). His late-2026 exchanges with his own movement's successor party — it conditioning budget support, he answering that recognizing mistakes is easy — are the sound of this promise being argued over, not discharged (Soy502, 14 November 2026).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: HOLDS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Bernardo Arévalo is the rarest subject this test has graded: a president whose central documented fact is the power he did not take. Besieged from before his inauguration by a prosecutor's office that raided the electoral tribunal, declared his election void, cancelled his party, prosecuted the Indigenous leaders who had defended his transfer of power, and named a corruption case after his presidency, he answered every move through the courts, the Congress, and the calendar — and lost most of those contests without once breaking the frame that contained them. The attorney general who tried to annul his election left office on the day her term expired, replaced through the constitutional shortlist; the courts that dissolved his party still sit; the Congress that blocks him still blocks him. Against that stand the failures of a government that could not convert survival into delivery: an anti-corruption policy of complaints without convictions, an extortion epidemic that peaked on his watch, prisons that exploded twice, infrastructure unbuilt at the promised pace, and an approval rating that fell from 54% to the bottom of the region. The seat, on this evidence, holds without gripping — and the flock is counted in the design of nearly everything and in the results of too little. His remaining year, and the 2027 election run by institutions renewed under his restraint, will decide whether the holding was the achievement or only the precondition.
