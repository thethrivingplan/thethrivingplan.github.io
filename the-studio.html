The tuning underneath — the Ladder & the Weave +
Tuning

Two ways of tuning the same notes. Ladder: one big number halved down into hearing. Weave: three numbers around the same note, with a found center between them. Switching never loses your work — each tuning keeps its own grid. You never need this door to make music; it's here for when you get curious.

The ladder — pick a starting number

One starting number, halved again and again until it lands in hearing — every rung the same note, C♯, just higher or lower. Change the number and the whole studio re-tunes around it. The full math is at the bottom of the page.

The three numbers — the whole tuning of the weave

Three numbers, each making its own ladder of the same note. The grid holds all of them at once — clusters of three around every C♯ — and at the heart a fourth row joins: the exact middle of the outer two, found by math, not chosen. The full story is at the bottom of the page.

More controls — how it listens +

In beat mode each hit is sorted by how bright it sounds: a low thump (a "boots" kick, a chest tap) lands on the heart rung; a high snap (a "cats" hiss, a finger snap) lands three rungs higher. The slider sets where low becomes high. It is a rough sort, honestly labeled — a guess from brightness, not a drum machine's brain. In melody mode a note is written when your pitch holds steady on a rung for a moment; timing wraps into the 32 steps of the loop, counting from when you pressed listen — press Play in the Studio room first if you want to hum along with the loop. Honest label: microphone capture can't be tested from inside this page's sandbox — nothing here can prove your mic works until you try it, so your device is the referee. Pitch tracking is a good ear, not a perfect one: one clear note at a time, close to the mic, in a quiet-ish room, and it follows you well.

The ladder — the math, and where 144,000 is really from +

The octave math is exact. Halving a frequency drops it exactly one octave; the note name survives every halving, only the height changes. Start at 144,000 Hz and halve: 72,000 → 36,000 are still ultrasound. The first rung inside the textbook audible range is 18,000 Hz (three octaves down — most adults won't hear it; a child might). Then 9,000 · 4,500 · 2,250 · 1,125 · 562.5 — the heart, nearest the middle of hearing · 281.25 · 140.625 · 70.31 · 35.16 Hz, near the floor where tone turns into pulse. Against the piano's grid (A = 440), every rung lands on C♯, sharp by exactly 25 cents — octaves preserve the offset, so the whole family sits a quarter-semitone above the C♯ line, all the way down. That is verified arithmetic, and this studio is tuned to it: crown the ladder's own grid (A = 446.4) with the toggle in Ladder mode and those same rungs read as exact C♯.

Where 144,000 is really from. The number has two real homes. In Revelation, the 144,000 are the sealed — 12,000 from each of twelve tribes: 12 × 12 × 1,000, completeness squared and multiplied; in the text they are a people, and nothing calls them a frequency. In the Maya calendar, one baktun is exactly 144,000 days — about 394 years — a genuine unit from a genuine chain of use. Both are real, old, and checkable.

What is not supported: 144,000 as a frequency is 144 kHz — ultrasound, seven times above the top of human hearing. No ear hears it and no ordinary speaker plays it. Claims that this frequency heals, awakens, or aligns anything are not supported by evidence. What is true is smaller and better: a real number, a real ratio, and — once you octave it down — a real note you can play, record, and build with. That is what this page does with it, and it does not claim more.

Two laws under the studio. 1 — Size sets the pitch, shape sets the pattern. Grow or shrink a vibrating thing and its frequency moves in exact inverse ratio to the size — twice as big, half the frequency, one octave down — and the ratio holds at every size of change, with no smallest stair. It doesn't step; it slides, like water, like a glissando. And through the whole slide the pattern never changes. That is why the ladder works at all: change the base number and every rung slides together, the note — the pattern — untouched. The Glide voice is this law with a fader.

2 — A tuning is a rule written while calm. A grid is a choice, crowned once and then measured against. Concert pitch has wandered for centuries — Baroque orchestras sat near A = 415, a full semitone below today; several modern orchestras play at 443–446 because sharp reads as brilliant. This ladder's grid, A = 446.4, sits inside the range humans already use. Neither grid is nature. Both are rules. The honest move is to say which ruler you're holding — so this studio prints both, and lets you switch.

The weave — matched, not fitted +

Three numbers, three different kinds of truth. 554.3653 Hz is the piano's C♯5 — true by definition of the standard grid, A = 440; it is not measured, it is declared, and the declaration is the grid. 562.5 Hz is the ladder's C♯5 — 144,000 Hz halved by octaves eight times; halving is exact, so this one is pure arithmetic, 25.2 cents sharp of the piano. 569 Hz is the church video's claimed C♯5 for the Funchal Cathedral rose window — the video never showed its conversion rule, so this one is graded as what it is: a claim, 45 cents sharp. Each of the three makes its own octave ladder, and the weave roll holds all of them, interleaved.

Matched perfectly. Nothing was moved. The center was found, not fitted.

That is the weave's default setting, and it is a rule about hands off. The piano's 554.3653 stands untouched, exactly as given. The claim's 569 stands untouched, exactly as given — nobody nudged it a hair to force the picture symmetric; that would be fitting, and fitting is forbidden here. Between them, the middle voice is not chosen and not averaged into place: it is computed — the exact pitch-midpoint of the outer two, the geometric mean √(554.3653 × 569) = 561.635 Hz, sitting exactly 22.55 cents from each. That found center is the default middle voice: it is what the beacon sounds, what the center pad plays, and where the Fluctuate slider rests.

And the ladder's own rung? 562.5 keeps its ladder, its cluster, and its row — labeled as what it is. It sits 0.87 Hz above the exact center: 2.66 cents off — 25.2 cents above the piano, 19.9 cents below the claim. Near the center, not on it. It is not the default center, and this page does not call it one. Almost is a real address; it just isn't this one.

The screen rounds — the sound doesn't. Labels on this page round to a decimal or two so they can be read. The synthesis underneath computes every frequency at full precision, straight from the formulas — 440 × 24/12, √(554.3653… × 569), 144,000 ÷ 256 — not a cent of a Hertz off, anywhere.

What three tones together actually make: no new frequency. Tones adding in air stay exactly the tones they are. What they create is a pattern on top — the beating, pulsing at their difference rates. The matched set beats at 7.3, 7.4, and 14.6 times a second. A beacon is not defined by its pitch; it is defined by its pulse — which is why the three together sound like something calling through fog. If the three stood in exact whole-number ratios, they would fuse into a single voice, the way an instrument's harmonics blend into one note. These refuse to fuse, because they are not three notes. They are three tunings of the same note.

The words for what you hear. The man who asked for this studio named it himself:

“Cognitive dissonance between the three of them — but still how they're beautiful and go together perfectly at the same time.”

Beside his phrase, at the same size, the acoustic facts: in sound, this clash has a technical name — roughness. Tones this close together beat inside the ear's critical band, and the ear registers that beating as roughness, a felt friction. Yet the three are also, genuinely, the same note — so the ear holds clash and sameness at once, and neither cancels the other. His words and the acoustics are describing the same hearing from two sides. Both are printed. Neither is corrected into the other.

The weave is the Diamond teaching: the center is a weave, not a middle value. The matched set sharpens the teaching rather than breaking it. There is an exact middle value — 561.635, found by math, not fitted by hand — and it sings here as a voice. But the weave itself, the thing you actually hear when the beacon sounds, is still not that value or any value: it is the three sounding together, the pulse riding on top of them. The center was found. The weave is what the found center sounds like with its neighbors. A middle value can be computed; a weave can only be heard.

How the nine instruments work +

Every voice is synthesized live with the Web Audio API — oscillators, harmonic stacks, filters, and gain envelopes. No samples, nothing fetched. Each voice also carries starting presets (in the rack, above): a tone, a level, and a note-length feel, so a first tap already sounds like something. One line each:

VoiceHow it's made
PureA single sine wave — the fundamental alone, with a soft attack and release. The ladder with nothing added.
OrganSine harmonics stacked like drawbars (1×, 2×, 3×, 4×), held at full sustain until the step ends.
PianoA bright harmonic stack struck hard, then decaying exponentially — loud overtones first, fundamental last.
StringsTwo detuned sawtooth waves through a lowpass filter, bowing in slowly — chorus from the detune itself.
FluteA sine with gentle vibrato and a breathy slow attack; a whisper of the octave above, nothing more.
BellInharmonic partials (1×, 2.76×, 5.4×) that decay at different speeds — metal, not a tuned string.
PluckA triangle wave through a filter that slams shut — the Karplus snap of a string let go.
PadThree detuned saws, heavily lowpassed, swelling in and out slowly — weather, not melody.
GlideOne continuous sine voice that slides — portamento — from the last note to the next, never stepping. The glissando voice.

The mic track is the tenth voice and the only real one: your own sound, recorded on this device, looped at step 1.
