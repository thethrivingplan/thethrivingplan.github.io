In Theatre — a surgery knowledge simulator

Work a real operation as a chain of decisions, learn why every rule in the operating theatre exists, and practise a steady hand on the suture bench. This page carries the knowledge behind surgery — the theory, the reasons, the strategies — in full.

Honest limits — read this first

This page teaches knowledge only. It explains how surgery works and why it is done the way it is. It does not train you, qualify you, or license you to operate.

Real surgery takes years of supervised training — medical school, then years of operating alongside surgeons who watch every move — because living tissue, bleeding, and a person's life answer back in ways no page can reproduce.

Nothing here is ever to be attempted on a person. The suture bench is a steadiness exercise on a screen, not surgical skill. If you ever need an operation, the person holding the scalpel will have spent a decade earning the right to.

Procedure Rounds — the appendectomy

The appendix is a small blind-ended tube hanging off the caecum, the first part of the large bowel. When it blocks and inflames — appendicitis — it can burst and spill bowel contents into the abdomen, so the standard treatment is to remove it: an appendectomy, the classic teaching operation. You will work it as the surgeon, decision by decision, from the checklist before anyone is cut to the observations after they wake.

Wrong choices teach immediately, with the why. One kind of error stands above the rest: breaking the sterile field. In a real theatre it can seed an infection that kills, so this page grades it as the gravest error there is — and says why in full, every time.

The stake is the patient. There is deliberately no money in this theatre — no cash scores, no currency, nothing to buy. What you earn is a person recovering well, the trust of the team working beside you, and knowledge that makes you more capable the next time you scrub in. That is the whole economy of this page, and it is the real one.

Your patient

Choose a case from the theatre list below and that person becomes your patient. Their outcome — a clean recovery, a longer one, or a complication — is decided by your decisions, and it is written into your record under their name.

Team standing: New to the team — nobody knows your hands yet. The scrub nurse, the anaesthetist, and the consultant all notice discipline. Clean work earns trust; sterile breaks and sloppy counts cost it. Standing is kept in your record, in plain words.

The theatre list — Shift 1

Capacity is real. This shift has 3 case slots, and more patients are waiting than there are slots — exactly how a real theatre list works. Choosing who goes first is part of the surgery: weigh how urgent the condition is, how long it can safely wait, how long the operation takes, and what your own energy allows. Cases are labelled with their difficulty before you commit a slot — the list starts with straightforward cases and gets harder across shifts, and an easy case and a hard case are not the same spend.

Your energy: Fresh — 100 of 100.

Long cases drain more; back-to-back hard cases drain most. Tired hands are a clinical fact, not a character flaw: as your energy falls, tempting shortcuts appear in the rounds and your needle genuinely drifts on the suture bench — fatigue degrades fine motor control and vigilance, and this page models that honestly instead of just saying it. Rest is a real action with real trade-offs, below the list.

Between cases — rest is a decision

A proper rest means a colleague takes the longest-waiting patient now — they are cared for, but not by you, and the record says so. A short break costs nobody a case, but buys less of you back. Pushing on keeps every slot yours and spends the surgeon.

The Suture Bench — a steadiness exercise

Closing a wound well means stitches that are evenly spaced, with an equal bite of tissue on each side, sitting level across the wound — too tight strangles tissue, too loose leaves gaps, uneven bites pucker and weaken the line. Surgeons build that evenness over thousands of supervised repetitions. This bench trains only the small honest part a screen can train: a steady, even hand. It is a steadiness exercise, not surgical skill.

How to play: tap a point on the left of the wound (the needle goes in), then tap a point on the right (the needle comes out). That places one stitch. Place five stitches, one per marked row — the faint dots show the ideal entry and exit. You can be graded from three stitches up.

Your energy carries onto this bench. Fresh hands place the needle where you tap; tired hands drift — watch the status line, because the drift is honestly in the stitches and in the grade.

Anatomy — the layers you pass through

To reach the appendix, the surgeon passes through the wall of the abdomen, layer by layer. Each layer has a job, and each is handled differently — that is why the operation is taught as a journey through named layers, not as "cutting in".
