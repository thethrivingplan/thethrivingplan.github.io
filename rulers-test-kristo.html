The Ruler's Test — Borjana Krišto, Chair of the Council of Ministers of Bosnia and Herzegovina

Subject: Borjana Krišto (HDZ BiH, born 13 August 1961) — Chair of the Council of Ministers of Bosnia and Herzegovina since 25 January 2023, the first woman to hold the seat. The seat graded is the Chair of the Council of Ministers, and the reason is stated plainly: Bosnia and Herzegovina's head of state is a collective, tripartite Presidency — one Bosniak, one Serb and one Croat member, with the chair rotating every eight months — which holds foreign-policy and ceremonial functions, while the government is run by the Council of Ministers under its Chair, who functions as prime minister. The Presidency's role is stated where it bears on a test: it nominates the Chair, it approved the Frontex agreement in May 2025, and its own composition is the subject of Test 4. PREMISE VERIFIED AND CORRECTED WHERE NEEDED: the general elections scheduled for October 2026 did happen, on 4 October 2026 (Central Election Commission confirmed results 3 November 2026; turnout 43.39% presidential and 43.36% parliamentary, the lowest ever recorded for a Bosnian general election — the two figures are printed separately, as sourced, and never blended). A new Presidency took office on 16 November 2026: Denis Bećirović (Bosniak, SDP BiH) and Željka Cvijanović (Serb, SNSD) began second terms, and Darijana Filipović (HDZ BiH) succeeded Željko Komšić in the Croat seat, giving the body two women for the first time since Dayton. The election did NOT change the government by grading day: no new Council of Ministers had been formed, and Krišto remains Chair — incumbent, in a caretaker-like position pending formation — on the record as of late November 2026 (EU Delegation handover of the 2026 country report to Krišto as Chair, 4 November 2026; Council of Ministers statements, 28 October 2026). She is therefore graded on a substantial, effectively complete first tenure: 25 January 2023 to November 2026, nearly three years and ten months, formed after the 2 October 2022 general election by a coalition of the SNSD, HDZ BiH and the Troika alliance (SDP BiH, NiP, Naša stranka), with the Presidency nominating her on 22 December 2022 and the House of Representatives confirming her on 28 December 2022 and her cabinet on 25 January 2023. The structural fact that governs every test below is also stated plainly: the Chair's power is real but narrow — entity and cantonal governments hold most competences, state decisions can be blocked by entity and ethnic veto mechanisms, and from January 2025 her own coalition had collapsed in practice (Test 3) while remaining in office in form. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test is printed here verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as two independent axes, and all four crossings are named in Test 5 where the record shows them; where a crossing is not documented, the dossier records "not documented" rather than inventing it. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier says so, names the options that actually existed and why each carried real harm, and grades the choice as the best available in the moment — while still grading whether she built the trap and whether she was honest inside it. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The law reached the most powerful man in her own coalition while she chaired the government. On 26 February 2025 the Court of Bosnia and Herzegovina sentenced Milorad Dodik — president of Republika Srpska and leader of the SNSD, a governing coalition partner — to one year in prison and a six-year ban from political office for failing to enforce decisions of the High Representative; the verdict was upheld on appeal on 1 August 2025, the Central Election Commission stripped Dodik of his presidential mandate on 6 August 2025, and he avoided prison by paying 36,500 KM, the legally provided conversion (Court of BiH verdict, February 2025; UN High Representative's 67th report to the Secretary-General, May 2025; International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, 2025). No documented act by Krišto obstructed the prosecution, the appeal, or the mandate-stripping.
  • The law also reached inside her own cabinet. Her Minister of Security, Nenad Nešić (DNS, in coalition), was arrested on 26 December 2024 and later indicted as a suspect for conspiracy, money laundering, abuse of office and accepting bribes — the indictment and his guilt are an ALLEGATION until a court rules — and he resigned on 23 January 2025 (contemporaneous reporting; Krišto cabinet record, 2025). He was not shielded in office.
  • The Constitutional Court's rulings were applied against an entity government of her coalition's partner: in May 2025 the Constitutional Court struck down Republika Srpska's February 2025 "foreign agents" law, and RS legislation banning state judicial and law-enforcement bodies from operating on RS territory, adopted after the Dodik verdict, was likewise treated as unconstitutional in the state order (Constitutional Court of BiH, May 2025; High Representative's 67th report, May 2025).
  • Where one law was newly written, it was written generally: the Council of Ministers adopted, and parliament passed, the law on prevention of conflict of interest and the law on anti-money laundering and countering terrorist financing in early 2024, and integrity amendments to the High Judicial and Prosecutorial Council (HJPC) law were adopted in September 2023 — rules that on their face bind office-holders of every party (European Commission progress report, March 2024).

Evidence against:

  • Enforcement is selective on the documented monitors' record. Transparency International BiH states that prosecution "remains selective," that in the past year there were no indictments or verdicts in high-level corruption cases at the level it tracks, and that some prosecutor's offices recorded not a single high-corruption indictment (TI BiH, via Sarajevo Times, February 2026). The European Commission's assessment, as reported from its 2025/2026 reporting, is sharper: no law-enforcement agency has submitted a report on high-level corruption to the state Prosecutor's Office since 2022, and the number of individuals finally convicted of high-level corruption fell from ten in 2024 to seven in 2025 (European Commission country reporting, November 2026).
  • The one law she personally controls — appointments under the Law on the Council of Ministers — was applied one way for her coalition's convenience and another way for its opponents. After Nešić resigned in January 2025, the Security Ministry was left to an acting minister, Ivica Bošnjak of her own HDZ BiH, for the rest of the tenure. A proposal signed by 19 representatives and supported in the House of Representatives to appoint Nebojša Vukanović — the Troika-backed RS opposition politician — was never put by Krišto into appointment procedure; Troika figures and delegate Zlatko Miletić state on the record that the non-appointment protected the SNSD, and Krišto's answer in May 2025 was to ask critics to tell her where she had broken the law (Bosnia Today and Sarajevo Times reporting, 2025; N1 reporting, 27 May 2025). The accusation of intent is recorded here as her critics' claim, contested by her; the asymmetry itself — a ministry held by her party in acting capacity while a parliament-backed nominee from outside the coalition waited unprocessed — is the documented fact.
  • The general rules adopted in 2023–2024 were then applied unevenly: the European Commission finds the conflict-of-interest law "applied inconsistently" (five cases found in 2025, one sanction, the rest escaping sanction because the persons had left state office), and compliance with asset-declaration duties fell — 261 of 600 state officials filed for verification, 24 fewer than in 2024 (European Commission country reporting, November 2026).
  • The outcome measures moved the wrong way on her watch: Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index gives Bosnia and Herzegovina a score of 33, down nine points over a decade and its worst ever, ranked 114th of 180 (TI CPI, as reported February 2026; a separate February 2026 broadcast summary placed the country 109th — the figures are printed as separately sourced and not blended), and TI BiH's State Capture Index (9 December 2025) rates the country 2 of 5, finding party dominance over institutions and public resources, with the Commission's own language quoted in it: political authorities "have failed to address widespread corruption and have actively hindered progress, leading to prolonged stagnation and increasingly evident signs of state capture" (TI BiH State Capture Index, December 2025).

Why this grade: The courts and prosecutors under her government did apply the law to a coalition kingpin and to her own minister — that is not nothing, and in this region it is not automatic. But the appointment power that is hers alone ran on two tracks — instant continuity for her own party in an acting seat, indefinite non-processing for the other side's nominee — and every independent measure of even enforcement (selective prosecution, inconsistent conflict-of-interest sanctions, falling asset compliance, a record-low corruption score) points the same way. One law at the courthouse door, two laws at her own desk: MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — citizens in both entities who saw a state court verdict against an entity president actually executed (mandate stripped, ban imposed), which is the precondition for any law meaning anything; the reform record of 2023–2024, which gave prosecutors and the HJPC better written tools. Hurt — the RS opposition and the parliamentary majority that backed Vukanović, whose lawful appointment route was never opened; honest officials inside a system TI grades as captured, where loyalty is rewarded and enforcement is selective; and, by the Commission's count, every citizen whose high-level corruption cases were never even reported upward after 2022.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The referees ruled against her coalition, repeatedly, and stood. The Court of BiH convicted Dodik and its appellate panel upheld the conviction; the Constitutional Court annulled RS separatist and "foreign agents" legislation; the Central Election Commission stripped a sitting entity president of his mandate, annulled results in 136 polling stations in the November 2025 RS presidential rerun process citing irregularities, and ran the 4 October 2026 general elections — in which her own HDZ BiH did not lead the state parliament — without documented interference from her office (Court of BiH, 2025; CEC decisions, August and December 2025; International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, December 2025).
  • The 2026 election itself was administered independently and, on the observers' account, genuinely: the international observation mission (ODIHR, OSCE PA, PACE, European Parliament, NATO PA) found "genuine competition," contestants "largely able to reach voters," and a first countrywide use of biometric identification and ballot scanners that the High Representative's 70th report credits with a biometric success rate above 97% and only minor manual-count discrepancies (ODIHR preliminary statement, 5 October 2026; OHR 70th report, October 2026).
  • She did not use the state's remaining levers against the referees who ruled against her partners: there is no documented attempt by her Council to defund, restaff or legislate around the Court of BiH, the Constitutional Court or the CEC during the 2025 crisis — the attacks on those bodies came from the RS entity government, not from her chair (High Representative's 67th report, May 2025; UK Parliament research briefing, 12 December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • One ministry was gripped by the acting-appointment route for the whole remainder of the tenure. Keeping the Security Ministry in the hands of her own party's deputy as "acting" minister from January 2025 onward, while declining to process a parliament-backed replacement, kept a sovereign ministry under her party's control without a confirmation vote — control centralized by vacancy (cabinet record, 2025; Bosnia Today reporting, December 2025).
  • The chief negotiator file shows the same pattern at the level of the state's EU machinery: by late 2025 the authorities were pursuing competing structures — the House of Representatives voted in October 2025 to establish a negotiator's office appointed through parliament, while the Chair's office supported a temporary government body — and the dispute went to the Constitutional Court, leaving the post empty for the entire period since negotiations were opened in March 2024 (civil-society Alternative Report on BiH progress 2025–2026, 9 November 2026; China-CEE Institute monthly briefing, October 2025). A post that must be filled for the state to negotiate sat empty amid a fight over who controls the appointment.
  • The media referee weakened on her watch without a documented defence from her chair. Bosnia and Herzegovina fell to 90th of 180 in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index (score 54.29), from 86th (56.33) in 2025 — described by the BH Journalists Association in May 2026 as its worst ranking in 25 years; the European Commission found "deep stagnation in media freedom, with worrying signs of deterioration," the Communications Regulatory Agency still lacking political and financial independence, and the state broadcaster BHRT in its worst financial crisis since its establishment, with no progress on its sustainability in 2025 (RSF Index 2026; European Commission 2025 report; SafeJournalists / BH Journalists, 26 May 2026). The worst single instruments — RS's 2023 recriminalisation of defamation (273 criminal complaints received by RS prosecutors by end-2025, 47 concerning media) and its 2025 "foreign agents" law — are entity acts, not hers; the Constitutional Court struck the latter down in May 2025. What is hers is the absence: no state-level counterweight, funding settlement or defence of the state broadcaster appears in the record assembled here.
  • The judiciary's independence remained formally intact but functionally unreformed under her government: the new HJPC law and the law on the Courts of BiH — the two laws the EU required to move to an intergovernmental conference — had still not been adopted by mid-2026, more than 1.6 million first-instance cases remained unresolved, and over 500 civil servants had failed to disclose asset declarations, on figures cited at the 2026 report's presentation (European Commission reporting and presentation coverage, November 2026; Alternative Report, November 2026).

Why this grade: She did not grip the referees who mattered most in the crisis — the courts and the election commission ruled against her partners and she let the rulings stand, which is the core of this test and earns the FOR shelf its weight. But holding without gripping also means not keeping what you can hold by leaving it empty: a ministry held in acting capacity by her own party for nearly two years, a chief negotiator never appointed amid a control dispute her office was party to, and a state broadcaster and regulator left to sink unreformed. She neither seized the referees nor kept them strong: MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — voters in the 2025 RS rerun and the 2026 general election, whose commission acted independently, including against the incumbent entity machine; litigants and citizens whose constitutional challenges to entity overreach were heard and upheld. Hurt — journalists and media workers (35 attacks and threats documented in 2025, at least 16 by politicians and public officials, per SafeJournalists figures compiled October 2026), BHRT's staff and audience, and every party to the 1.6 million unresolved first-instance cases; the state's own negotiating capacity, hurt by the empty chief-negotiator chair.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The gravest wobble of the tenure — the 2025 constitutional crisis after the Dodik verdict, when the RS National Assembly adopted laws banning state judiciary and law enforcement from RS territory and moved toward a new entity constitution with self-determination provisions — was met by her government with restraint rather than escalation: the state answered through its courts and the election commission, the separatist laws were annulled or struck down through legal process, and by 18 October 2025 the RS Assembly itself had appointed an acting president and annulled a series of separatist laws adopted since 2023, easing tensions (High Representative's 67th report, May 2025; International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, October 2025). No-good-choice clause, applied by its mechanics: the options actually open to her chair were (a) confrontation through state enforcement on RS territory, risking the armed standoff every actor feared; (b) conceding the RS laws' validity, dissolving the state order; or (c) holding the legal line and letting courts, the CEC and time work. Each of the first two carried real and immediate harm; she took (c), the least-harm door actually open, and the crisis de-escalated without violence. She did not build this trap — the verdict and the RS reaction were not of her making — and her public line inside it was consistent: on 28 October 2026, commenting on the OHR report to the UN Security Council, she stated that decisions in BiH "should be made by domestic institutions and political representatives coming from all three constituent peoples" (SRNA, 28 October 2026).
  • When the money wobbled, she adjusted — at the last possible hour, but openly and successfully. After the Reform Agenda missed its July 2025 deadline and the Commission cut the allocation by 10% (€108 million lost), and after two September 2025 Council sessions failed on SNSD obstruction, Krišto's Council adopted the Agenda unanimously at an emergency telephone session on 30 September 2025, the final day before a further 10% would be lost; the Commission approved it on 4 December 2025, unlocking up to €976.6 million conditional on delivery (New Union Post, 1 October 2025; China-CEE Institute briefing, October 2025; European Commission approval, 4 December 2025).
  • She answered parliamentary pushback in person and on the record: at the House of Representatives session dedicated to the European path and the Growth Plan, she committed to continue "with the same passion and intensity," stated that on the European path "there is no position or opposition," and laid out the Council's steps and the state of the funds in detail (Sarajevo Times reporting of the session, 2025).

Evidence against:

  • When another option became available on the Security Ministry, she refused to look at it. The Vukanović appointment — backed by 19 representatives' signatures and House support, which its proponents argued would remove the SNSD's blocking element inside the Council — was an option that became available in early 2025; the documented record shows no consideration of it by her chair, only deflection: in May 2025 she defended the non-appointment by challenging critics to name the law she had broken, and in later exchanges dismissed the question's significance for the Council's work (N1, 27 May 2025; Bosnia Today reporting, 2025). Considering it and declining with stated reasons would not have counted against her; refusing to engage it at all is what this test grades.
  • The same refusal pattern governed the chief negotiator: rather than take up the parliament-adopted route of October 2025 or settle the competing structure, her office maintained its own temporary-body proposal and the file went to the Constitutional Court — two and a half years after the March 2024 decision to open negotiations, the country still had no chief negotiator when the Commission reported in November 2026 (Alternative Report, November 2026; Commission 2026 report coverage, November 2026).
  • The 2026 wobble from Brussels was answered with words the record does not support. Handed a report whose core message was that the country "has increasingly disengaged from its EU path" and that 2025–2026 was "essentially… a lost year in terms of tangible progress," with no progress in 21 of 35 chapters on the presentation coverage, the Council's response was a statement of readiness to draw up a timetable; and on the lost Growth Plan funds she had earlier told parliament that even the 10% said to be lost could be made up if reforms were implemented — a claim the Commission's own accounting contradicts, the cut being recorded as applied and the maximum now €976.6 million (EEAS statement by Luigi Soreca, 4 November 2026; Sarajevo Times session reporting, 2025; Commission 2026 report coverage, November 2026).
  • The voters' wobble — turnout collapsing to 43.39%/43.36% in October 2026, the lowest ever — was produced on the whole political class's watch, hers at its head at state level; no documented adjustment of course by her chair followed the result before grading, the new Presidency taking office on 16 November 2026 while government formation remained unbegun (CEC confirmed results, 3 November 2026; Balkan Insight reporting, 16 November 2026).

Why this grade: In the crisis that could have broken the state, she answered the wobble the right way — legally, patiently, without doubling down into confrontation — and on the Reform Agenda she moved, late but decisively, when the cost of not moving became concrete. On the two files where adjustment required giving up a piece of her own side's control — the Security Ministry and the chief negotiator — she did not adjust, did not openly consider the option that had become available, and answered criticism with deflection; and her public account of the lost year and the lost funds softened what the documents say. Restraint under fire, rigidity at her own desk: MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — every citizen of both entities, by a 2025 crisis that ended in courtrooms and an assembly vote rather than in confrontation; taxpayers and would-be beneficiaries of the Growth Plan, by the €976.6 million kept alive on 30 September 2025. Hurt — citizens again, by the €108 million already lost before the adjustment came, and by the first-tranche reforms whose grace period expires at the end of 2026 with the facility and loan agreements still unsigned (Commission 2026 report); the RS opposition and Troika partners, whose offered route out of the Council's blockade was never examined.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the constituent peoples and those outside them — the "Others," minorities, returnees, and citizens who declare no ethnicity — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The government she led was, by design and in fact, a government of all three constituent peoples: her cabinet seated Bosniak (Troika), Serb (SNSD and partners) and Croat (HDZ BiH) ministers, and the state she chaired maintained full alignment with the EU's Common Foreign and Security Policy throughout — a policy stance taken in the name of the whole country (European Commission 2026 report, via EEAS statement, 4 November 2026).
  • One inclusion file did move under her signature: on 11 June 2025 she signed the Frontex status agreement with the EU in Brussels, making Bosnia a "credible partner in securing the external borders of the EU" in her office's words, with joint border operations applying along all the country's borders — a state-level function exercised for the whole territory, both entities included (European Commission press release, 11 June 2025; Reuters, 11 June 2025).
  • Procedural motion on the counted-out exists on paper: a revised action plan for executing the European Court of Human Rights judgments was adopted in July 2025 and a working group to prepare constitutional and election-law amendments was established in October 2025 (ECRI conclusions on BiH, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The central counting fact of the Bosnian constitution stood uncorrected through her whole tenure and through another election held under it. Since Sejdić and Finci (22 December 2009), the European Court of Human Rights has held — in five rulings in the group — that barring citizens who are not Bosniak, Croat or Serb (the applicants include Roma and Jewish citizens, an Albanian, and a citizen of undeclared origin) from standing for the Presidency and the House of Peoples is discrimination; a Bosniak resident in Republika Srpska is likewise barred from the Serb seat. None of the judgments was implemented by November 2026: ECRI's 2026 conclusions find the July/October 2025 steps "largely procedural and preparatory," with no concrete legislative or constitutional reform, and conclude its priority recommendation "not implemented"; the October 2026 elections were again held under the same restrictions, which ODIHR's preliminary statement records as suffrage rights "subject to discriminatory restrictions based on ethnicity and residence" (Council of Europe Committee of Ministers supervision documents, March 2025; ECRI conclusions, 2026; ODIHR preliminary statement, 5 October 2026). An interfaith and civil-society coalition campaigning in October 2026 put the number of citizens blocked from high office at an estimated 100,000 — an estimate by the campaigners, printed as such (Religion News Service, 9 October 2026).
  • This was not an omission against her stated intent; it was against her stated promise. In May 2023 she said the governing partners planned to implement, within six months, a limited constitutional reform and election-law changes "to remove discrimination against minorities and the outvoting of Croats" (HINA reporting, 11 May 2023). Three and a half years later, neither had happened; the electoral-reform energy her party did spend went to the Croat-member question — the seat her own party retook in 2026 — while the minority-discrimination half of the promise produced an action plan and a working group in the tenure's final year.
  • Inside the Federation, the practice of "two schools under one roof" — ethnically separated schooling in shared buildings — continued in several cantons despite binding domestic court decisions finding it discriminatory, and ECRI concluded its inclusive-education priority recommendation also "not implemented," with Roma children's support measures "limited in scope, fragmented, largely project-based and unevenly implemented" (ECRI conclusions, 2026). Education sits at entity/cantonal level, not her chair; the finding is recorded as the state of the country her government leads, and no state-level initiative from her Council on either file appears in the record assembled here.
  • The Presidency's bearing on this test is stated plainly, as the brief requires: the body that nominates her is itself the office from which the counted-out are constitutionally barred, and the new Presidency seated on 16 November 2026 was elected under those same rules. That is Dayton's design, not her act — but her 2023 promise made its repair her file, and the file is empty of result.

Why this grade: Test 4 does not ask whether the three constituent peoples were counted — in Bosnia they are counted by constitutional machinery, and her coalition embodied it. It asks whether everyone is counted, and on the one measure that decides it — whether a Roma, Jewish, Albanian, undeclared or wrongly-resident citizen may stand for the state's highest offices — the answer at the end of her tenure is the same as at its start and the same as in 2009: no, under judgments five times affirmed and never executed, despite her own six-month promise to fix exactly that. Procedural steps in the final year do not carry the grade. FAILS.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the constituent peoples' parties and voters, whose guaranteed shares of power her government faithfully operated; border communities in both entities covered by a single state-level Frontex framework. Hurt — the "Others": Roma, Jewish, Albanian and other minority citizens and citizens of no declared ethnicity, an estimated 100,000 people on the campaigners' figure, who completed another full election cycle barred from the Presidency and the House of Peoples; Roma children in a fragmented, project-based support system; children in the "two schools under one roof" cantons, whose court-won right to non-discriminatory schooling remained unenforced.

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 25 January 2023, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The founding pledge — delivered in its first act. On taking office on 25 January 2023 she pledged to lead a government that would "work hard to restart delayed integration of Bosnia and Herzegovina into the European Union," and in May 2023 named the main priority as beginning EU accession talks and achieving the 14 key priorities (inaugural reporting, January 2023; HINA, 11 May 2023). On 21 March 2024, fourteen months in, all 27 EU leaders unanimously agreed to open accession negotiations with Bosnia and Herzegovina, after her Council adopted the conflict-of-interest and anti-money-laundering laws among other steps (European Council decision, 21 March 2024). Presidency chair Denis Bećirović's assessment at the time — that BiH had made more progress in the last year than in the entire previous decade — is recorded as a supporting office-holder's characterization, not a neutral finding. The opening of negotiations is a fact, and it was the pledge's headline.
  • Full foreign-policy alignment — kept continuously. Her letter to Commissioner Várhelyi claimed "fully harmonizing its foreign policy positions with those of the European Union" among the government's achievements; the Commission's 2026 report — in a document otherwise damning — confirms Bosnia maintained full alignment with the EU's Common Foreign and Security Policy through September 2026 (Krišto letter to Várhelyi, 2023; EEAS statement, 4 November 2026).
  • The Reform Agenda — submitted, approved, money unlocked. She committed the Council to the Growth Plan agenda through 2025, adopted it on 30 September 2025, and the Commission approved it on 4 December 2025, unlocking up to €976.6 million against delivered reforms (Commission approval, 4 December 2025).
  • The two axes, crossings 1 and 2, named: Crossing 1 — kept her word AND it helped people — occurred: the EU-restart pledge and the March 2024 opening of negotiations, and the Frontex agreement of June 2025 signed as promised partnership, both kept and both benefiting citizens of all groups. Crossing 2 — kept her word AND it still hurt people — also occurred in a narrower file: her commitment to the coalition agreement's allocation of posts was kept toward the SNSD through 2025, and keeping it (leaving the Security Ministry unrefilled by the opposition nominee and SNSD ministers in place after Troika withdrew) hurt the RS opposition and, on Troika's stated account, the passage of European laws. The keeping earns the credit recorded here; the hurt is recorded in full in Tests 1 and 3.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The six-month constitutional promise — not kept. The May 2023 plan for limited constitutional reform and election-law change within six months, to remove discrimination against minorities and Croat outvoting, had produced no constitutional or electoral reform on either point by November 2026 — thirty months past its own deadline (HINA, 11 May 2023; ECRI conclusions, 2026; Test 4).
  • The negotiations she opened were never staffed. "Begin talks" was the priority she named; two and a half years after the European Council's decision, Bosnia had no chief negotiator, no adopted national programme for the adoption of the acquis in force, and the two required judiciary laws (HJPC, Courts of BiH) unadopted — so no first intergovernmental conference had been held (Commission 2026 report; Alternative Report, November 2026). The pledge's second half — talks actually beginning — receded throughout 2025–2026, a period the Commission calls a lost year with most prior recommendations unimplemented (EEAS statement, 4 November 2026).
  • The Growth Plan timetable — kept only at the last whistle, at a price. The agenda due in mid-2024 was submitted fifteen months late; €108 million was permanently lost and the allocation cut 10% to €976.6 million; and by November 2026 the facility and loan agreements remained unsigned and no Reform Agenda coordinator appointed, so Bosnia — uniquely in the Western Balkans — had received not a single euro, including the €68 million pre-financing her own parliamentary statement anticipated drawing by the end of 2025 (Commission 2026 report; Alternative Report, November 2026; New Union Post, October 2025). Her statement that the lost 10% could be made up through implementation is contradicted by the Commission's accounting and is recorded as a word the record does not carry.
  • The two axes, crossings 3 and 4, named: Crossing 3 — went against her word AND it helped people — is not documented in the record assembled here: no instance was found of a pledge of hers being broken in a way that benefited people. Crossing 4 — broke her word AND it hurt people — occurred: the six-month constitutional/electoral promise (hurt: the "Others" of Test 4, who faced the 2026 election under the same bar) and the Growth Plan timetable (hurt: citizens in both entities, by €108 million permanently lost and a further tranche at risk at the end of 2026). Both breaks are recorded on this test; the hurts are recorded in full in Tests 3 and 4 and are not laundered by anything on the FOR shelf.

Why this grade: Her word divides cleanly by period and by kind. The big directional pledge — restart the European path — was kept in the only way that could be checked early: negotiations were opened, alignment was held for four years, the agenda was eventually delivered and approved. The dated, specific pledges were not kept: six months for constitutional repair became three and a half years of nothing enacted; "begin talks" produced no negotiator to talk with; the money timetable slipped fifteen months and cost €108 million. A record that keeps its direction and breaks its dates is MIXED — and the dossier notes the trajectory is worsening, since every broken item sits in the tenure's second half.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — all citizens, by the opening of accession negotiations and four years of full EU foreign-policy alignment, the two durable assets of the tenure; future beneficiaries of up to €976.6 million in reform-conditional funds that remain unlockable. Hurt — the minority and undeclared citizens of Test 4, by the unkept six-month promise; taxpayers and service users in both entities, by the €108 million lost to lateness and by pre-financing still undrawn at grading; reform-minded partners in her own coalition, whose platform she was elected on and whose second-half delivery she did not match.

Overall Summary

Borjana Krišto's tenure is the record of a narrow seat held through a wide crisis. She took office in January 2023 as the consensus chair of an SNSD–HDZ–Troika coalition, with one governing idea — restart the European path — and in fourteen months she and her partners delivered its headline: the unanimous European Council decision of 21 March 2024 to open accession negotiations, on the back of real laws on conflicts of interest, money laundering and judicial integrity. That is Test 5's FOR shelf and the tenure's permanent asset, and it was kept in a way that helped everyone in the country regardless of group. Test 1 lands MIXED because the law, in her years, genuinely reached upward — her own security minister indicted, her coalition's strongest man convicted, banned and stripped of his mandate by independent courts and an independent election commission — while enforcement underneath stayed selective on every monitor's measure, the corruption index hit its worst-ever score, and the one appointment power that was hers alone ran on two tracks. Test 2 lands MIXED because she never gripped the referees who ruled against her side — she let verdicts, annulments and a mandate-stripping stand — yet held a sovereign ministry in her own party's acting hands for nearly two years, left the chief negotiator's chair empty amid a control dispute her office was party to, and offered no defence to a state broadcaster and media regulator sinking on her watch. Test 3 lands MIXED because her finest hour and her most rigid one are both documented: in the 2025 Dodik crisis she took the least-harm door actually open — law, patience, no confrontation — and de-escalated a situation with no good choice in it, and on the Reform Agenda she moved at the last hour to save €976.6 million; but when another option became available on the Security Ministry and the negotiator, she refused to look at it, and her public account of the lost year and the lost €108 million softened what Brussels' documents plainly say. Test 4 FAILS outright, the dossier's one clean verdict: she promised in May 2023 to remove, within six months, the constitutional discrimination that bars Roma, Jewish, Albanian, undeclared and wrongly-resident citizens from the Presidency and the House of Peoples — five times condemned at Strasbourg — and at the end of her tenure another election had been held under the same bar, with an action plan and a working group standing where the reform was promised. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether a successor government — or Krišto again, if formation returns her — appoints a chief negotiator and signs the Growth Plan agreements before the first-tranche grace expires at the end of 2026; whether the Nešić indictment reaches a verdict; whether the HJPC and Courts laws pass; and whether any government finally executes Sejdić and Finci. As graded, on nearly four years of evidence, the holding was careful, lawful in the crisis, and empty where it cost her side something.
