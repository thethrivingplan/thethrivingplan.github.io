The Ruler's Test — Duma Boko, President of Botswana

Subject: Duma Gideon Boko — President of the Republic of Botswana since 1 November 2024. The seat graded is the presidency, which in Botswana is both head of state and head of government: the president commands the executive, appoints and dismisses ministers, judges (on the advice of the Judicial Service Commission), the heads of the security and anti-corruption agencies, and the central bank's leadership, and no execution can proceed without his signature. Boko, a Harvard-trained human rights lawyer who built the Umbrella for Democratic Change (UDC) coalition from 2012 and leads its largest component, the Botswana National Front, came to the seat by the route no predecessor had ever taken: he won an election against the ruling party. In the general election of 30 October 2024 the UDC took 36 of 61 seats on 37.21% of the vote; the Botswana Democratic Party (BDP), which had governed without interruption since independence in 1966, fell to four seats on 30.47% (2024 Botswana general election, Wikipedia tabulation of official results). President Mokgweetsi Masisi conceded on the morning of 1 November 2024, before certification was complete; Boko was sworn in the same day by Chief Justice Terence Rannowane and formally inaugurated on 8 November 2024, with Masisi in attendance (Al Jazeera, November 2024; Wikipedia). He is graded on two years in office — the first transfer of power to an opposition party in Botswana's history — against an inheritance that must be stated because several tests turn on it: a diamond market in structural decline (Debswana cut output from 24.7 million carats in 2023 to 15.1 million in 2025), GDP contractions of 2.8% in 2024 and between 0.4% and 0.7% in 2025 depending on the statistical vintage (IMF and S&P figures via Torch Media Africa; African Development Bank, Botswana Economic Outlook 2026), a fiscal deficit of 9.5% of GDP in 2025 (AfDB), and a Government Investment Account drawn down from P5.4 billion in mid-2024 to P846 million by the end of 2025 (IMF/S&P reporting). His predecessor Masisi (president 2018–2024) and the long BDP tenure appear as context where the record Boko inherited — or the promises the seat carried into the election — bear directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic of the Test, printed above the five tests: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one — the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Marks are lost only for how it is done: a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). Test 3 also grades whether, when another option became available, he at least considered it openly; considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence — refusing to look at it is. Under every test, a plain-words why and a helped/hurt ledger close the section: who his actions on that test helped, and who they hurt, named as the record allows. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes, and the Overall Summary names which of the four crossings occurred. Where the record shows a situation in which every available option carried real harm, the dossier says so, names the options and why each was bad, and grades the choice as the best possible available in the moment — while still grading whether he built the trap himself.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The feud file was closed, not reversed. Within weeks of the transfer of power, the state withdrew all 14 criminal charges against former president Ian Khama — the firearms and related charges brought under Masisi in 2021 that had driven Khama into nearly three years of exile (Africa Press, reporting the Gaborone Regional Magistrate's discharge order). The related 2019 "Butterfly" affair, in which Khama and others were accused of stealing some $10 billion from the treasury, had already collapsed on its facts: in August 2021 the High Court in Gaborone ruled that the charge sheet had been "deliberately fabricated," and the government of the day lost its appeal (CAJ News Africa, October 2024). A new ruler handed the state's most politicised prosecution could have kept it as a weapon or turned the same machinery on its authors. On the record assembled here, Boko's government did neither: the charges were dropped, Khama came home, and no retaliatory prosecution of Masisi or his family has been documented.
  • The accountability instrument he built was aimed at a decade, not at a man. The forensic audit commissioned by his government and conducted by Alvarez & Marsal covered expenditure from 2014 to 2024 across 30 public institutions — spanning the end of the Khama era and the whole Masisi tenure, and reaching ministries and state-owned enterprises as institutions rather than naming a single political camp (State of the Nation Address, 9 November 2026; Mmegi reporting via Botswana Briefing, November 2026). It identified suspected corruption, fraud, embezzlement and mismanagement with potential losses estimated at P33 billion out of some P160 billion reviewed, and flagged 80 matters for criminal or disciplinary investigation (Daily News column, May 2026; News&All Botswana editorial, November 2026).
  • Part of the follow-through has run through the ordinary prosecutor rather than a presidential channel. The Director of Public Prosecutions, Kgosietsile Ngakaagae — appointed in August 2025 — told Gabz FM in November 2026 that the DPP had registered nearly 10 major corruption and fraud cases over the past year, valued at more than P1 billion and involving ministers and other senior officials (Gabz FM, November 2026). No case in the record assembled here shows the law being bent to shield a figure of the governing coalition, and no prosecution of an opposition figure on a charge a court has called fabricated appears anywhere in his two years.

Evidence against:

  • The president has publicly reserved the very channel his own audit condemns. In November 2026 Boko told lawmakers he would continue awarding government contracts without tendering, defending direct appointments as an instrument of his office — this after the forensic audit attributed a large share of the decade's P33 billion in losses to tender mismanagement and found that direct appointments had become a normalised practice (The Botswana Gazette, via Botswana Briefing, 4 November 2026). Whatever the stated purpose — speed of delivery — a president who keeps a non-competitive door open for his own hand, while prosecuting the consequences of that door under his predecessors, is running two tracks at the source.
  • The audit's own procurement travelled by the same door. Alvarez & Marsal was engaged at a cost reported at nearly P60 million without an open tender; when the Leader of the Opposition, Dumelang Saleshando, pressed the point in Parliament — whether the firm had been directly appointed and whether Botswana firms were considered — the responsible minister defended the selection as a specialist appointment chosen, after negotiations, precisely because a foreign firm had no connections to the persons under audit (Daily News report of the parliamentary exchange, via The Free Library). The impartiality reason is stated and is not frivolous; the method is nonetheless the method the audit exists to criticise.
  • The flagship file did not travel by the same road as other files. The detailed audit reports were withheld from the Directorate on Corruption and Economic Crime (DCEC) for more than six months after the report reached the president on 30 April 2026, and in his November 2026 address Boko assigned the findings not to the DCEC and the DPP but to a new Special Investigations and Asset Recovery Task Force, on his stated ground that some of the institutions expected to pursue suspects are themselves compromised (Mmegi, 6 November 2026; Mmegi reporting via ExeReco, November 2026). A process designed by the president, for the largest case in the country's history, outside the standing institutions every other case must use, is a second procedural law — whatever its merits. (The institutional-grip side of the same facts is graded in Test 2.)
  • One judicial appointment carried a process smell. When Attorney General Mmako Abram was appointed to the High Court bench in November 2025, controversy erupted over the appointment's propriety, and Abram was sworn in by President Boko before the threatened legal challenge could be tested in court — a lawful appointment whose timing closed the courtroom door on its own review (Botswana Gazette judiciary review of 2025, via Africa Press).

Why this grade: The one-law failures of the previous era — a fabricated charge sheet against a former president, prosecutions as factional instruments — have not recurred, and the era's outstanding political prosecution was ended rather than inherited. That is the strongest FOR shelf this test has carried in the recent series. But a grade of HOLDS would require the source itself to be clean, and at the source the president personally maintains two exceptions: direct contract awards by his own hand, defended in Parliament, and a bespoke channel for the biggest accountability file in the country's history. The law is one for the governed; at the top, it has a door marked with his name. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Ian Khama and his co-accused, freed of a prosecution the courts had already wounded; every former officeholder, who can see that losing power in Botswana does not currently mean prosecution by the winner; Batswana owed a single accounting of a decade's money, which the audit at least began. Hurt — the officials and firms inside the 30 audited institutions, who sit under a published P33 billion cloud while the individual findings that would clear or charge them remain unpublished (a perception recorded as perception, not as a verdict on any person); taxpayers, who funded a nearly P60 million audit procured without competition; and every future bidder for government work who must compete against a door the president keeps open for direct award.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The seat itself changed hands without a hand closing on it. Masisi conceded on the morning of 1 November 2024, Boko was sworn in the same day, and the outgoing president attended the inauguration and was publicly thanked by his successor (Wikipedia; Al Jazeera, November 2024). The electoral machinery that produced the result — against the party that had appointed much of it — was left standing and unblamed.
  • The referees he inherited served out their terms under him, including the one at the centre of the old regime's wars. Peter Magosi, the Masisi-appointed Director General of the Directorate of Intelligence and Security (DIS) whose tenure ran through the Khama persecution files, was retained by Boko until his tenure ended on 13 October 2026; the Permanent Secretary to the President was likewise kept into 2025. The early criticism of Boko from his own supporters was precisely that he would not wield the axe (Africa Press, February 2025). When the successions came, they were announced as completed tenures with named successors in the open: Professor Lekoko Kenosi at the DIS from 9 November 2026, and a new DCEC Director General, Batlhalefi Josti Moeletsi, in post from 1 November 2026 after his predecessor's contract ended (State of the Nation Address, 9 November 2026).
  • The judiciary argues with him in public and keeps its seat. In January 2026 Chief Justice Gaolapelwe Ketlogetswe — appointed by Boko himself in January 2025 after Chief Justice Rannowane stepped down on medical grounds — publicly insisted that the courts would continue imposing the death penalty as the Constitution provides, drawing a line against the president's well-known reluctance to sign death warrants: "Our duty is to interpret and apply the law, not to impose our personal views" (Botswana Gazette, via Africa Press, January 2026). No sanction, transfer or public rebuke of the Chief Justice followed.
  • The press that grades him hardest operates untouched. Mmegi, the Sunday Standard and The Botswana Gazette publish daily editorials accusing the president of stalling accountability, defending untendered contracts and sitting on a referendum; the News&All Botswana editorial of November 2026 ("Six Months Later, Still No Accountability") is as hostile a text as any opposition paper could print. No closure, raid, licence action or prosecution of a media house appears in the record assembled here. The DIS's internal wars, it should be noted, continued into his tenure in the courts rather than in cells: serving and former intelligence officers sued the state over arrests and searches dating to the previous administration (Botswana Gazette judiciary review, 2025), and a senior DIS officer's October 2025 legal letter alleged interference reaching back into the Masisi-era Air Botswana investigation — an ALLEGATION, unproven, printed here because it shows the inherited security apparatus litigating its grievances in the open rather than settling them in the dark (News&All Botswana, October 2025).
  • His own flagship reform cannot be enacted by his majority alone. The Constitution (Amendment) Bill establishing a Constitutional Court passed Parliament in March 2026, but section 89 of the Constitution requires a national referendum for the change to take effect — the final referee is the electorate, and Boko told Parliament in November 2026 that the bill "will be subjected to a national referendum" (State of the Nation Address, 9 November 2026). He also went in person to Ntlo Ya Dikgosi in January 2026, acknowledged it had been an oversight to gazette the bill before consulting the chiefs' chamber, and apologised for it (Daily News, 19 January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The referendum waits on his writ. More than seven months after Parliament passed the Constitutional Court amendment, the Independent Electoral Commission said it was still waiting for presidential authorisation to begin preparing the national vote, and the Botswana Council of NGOs warned that the silence was creating uncertainty for citizens and stakeholders (The Botswana Gazette, 5 November 2026). The referee process is constitutionally mandatory and publicly promised; its start date sits in the president's drawer.
  • The task-force bypass (the same facts as Test 1, graded here for their institutional weight): the largest accountability file in the country's history was taken away from the two standing institutions built for it — the DCEC, which had not received the evidence files six months after the report was delivered, and the DPP — and given to a Special Investigations and Asset Recovery Task Force announced by the president, justified by his public statement that some of the institutions expected to pursue suspects are compromised (Mmegi, November 2026). Mmegi's editorial answer is the against-shelf in the country's own words: a task force may help, but the DCEC and DPP "must not be left to struggle while the new body is built," and must be restored to competence and credibility rather than bypassed. Senior DCEC investigators have separately complained of intimidation and workplace bullying inside the directorate (The Patriot on Sunday, via ExeReco, November 2026) — reported complaints, not findings, recorded because a referee institution under internal strain is the environment in which a bypass becomes permanent.
  • A small item with a long shadow: in September 2026 the government launched a public primary school in Gaborone bearing the sitting president's own name — the Duma Boko School, Block 7 (State of the Nation Address, 9 November 2026). Lawful, popular with supporters, and recorded here because incumbents who begin naming the state after themselves are supplying trajectory evidence about the grip, however light it currently sits.

Why this grade: No referee in Botswana has been removed, silenced, raided or punished in two years; the judiciary publicly defies the president's own position, the press flays him weekly, his predecessor's security chief served out his full tenure, and the biggest constitutional change he wants must survive a vote he cannot control. That for-shelf is the strongest this test has seen in the recent series, and it is why this grade is not lower. It is not HOLDS because of two documented hands on two referee processes: a constitutionally required referendum left unstarted for seven months for want of his signature, and the flagship accountability file withheld from the statutory investigator for six months and then routed to a body of his own creation. A hold that waits on the holder's convenience is a grip at half pressure. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the judiciary, the electoral commission's formal independence, the independent press, and former officials of the old regime, all of whom kept their positions, voices and safety; future oppositions, who inherit referees that have now survived one transfer of power intact. Hurt — the DCEC as an institution, publicly pronounced compromised by the president and sidelined from the largest file in its history; voters, whose constitutionally guaranteed referendum on the Constitutional Court has no date; and the accused-but-uncharged across 30 institutions, whose cases wait on a task force that did not exist when the audit landed.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The medicine collapse was answered with admission first. When the public health system's supply chain failed — availability of critical life-saving medicines fell to 17% at the height of the crisis — Boko declared a State of Public Health Emergency on 25 August 2025 and named the failure in a televised address: "The medical supply chain as run by Central Medical Stores has failed" (Reuters, via The African Chronicle). The government released P250 million in emergency procurement funding, put the military on distribution, restructured the procurement body, and backed local manufacture; by 2026 Botswana had produced its first locally made antiretroviral medicines, the Health Minister reported availability recovering to 64% with a 12-month ARV supply secured, and the Botswana Development Corporation was directed to invest up to P85 million reviving the country's only beta-lactam antibiotic manufacturer (Health Minister's statement to Parliament, via News&All Botswana; State of the Nation Address, 9 November 2026). Pushback was answered in the open, with money, logistics and structural change.
  • The De Beers file is the other-option test in its purest form in this series. Boko's opening position, from the 2024 campaign through 2025, was majority control of De Beers as Anglo American exited. When another option became available — Botswana doubling its stake to 30% as the single largest shareholder inside the Global Diamond Consortium's takeover, with Angola and Namibia participating — he considered it, took it, and answered the criticism in the State of the Nation Address itself, stating his reasons: greater strategic influence with the attendant rights and protections, without sole funding of a distressed asset in a falling market (mining.com and Rapaport News, November 2026; State of the Nation Address, 9 November 2026). He did not pretend 30% was the majority he had demanded; he argued for what it buys. Considering a new option, changing course, and stating the reasons in public is precisely what this test grades as passing behaviour.
  • The fuel shock of 2026 was answered the same way: when the Middle East conflict drove energy costs up, a presidential directive of 6 April 2026 suspended the fuel, road and security-of-supply levies for six months — capping the increase at about P0.58 per litre — with three months of supplementary payments to some 279,000 old-age pensioners, disability-grant recipients, destitute beneficiaries and home-based care patients; the African Development Bank approved a $503.75 million loan (with $170 million from the OPEC Fund) in October 2026 backing the programme (AfDB press release, October 2026). And when the six months expired, the levies were restored on 8 October 2026 as scheduled rather than extended for applause, with the regulator adjusting pump prices separately on import costs (BERA, via Newsbase, November 2026).
  • Smaller wobbles, answered straight: the apology to Ntlo Ya Dikgosi for the consultation oversight (January 2026); a foot-and-mouth disease outbreak met with P97 million in emergency funding and a programme to restore Botswana's FMD-free status on which its beef exports depend (State of the Nation Address, 9 November 2026); and a foreign-exchange reserve position a government MP could report to Parliament as recovered to P63.2 billion — 8.3 months of import cover — by July 2026 (SONA debate, Daily News, 19 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the loudest question of 2026 — what has the forensic audit done? — the answer has been repetition. Six months after the report reached him, the president's November address offered, in the words of the News&All Botswana editorial, "essentially the same assurances we heard in May": recommendations adopted, reforms under way, a task force being established — with no accounting of how many investigations have commenced, how many cases have reached the DPP, whether anyone has been charged, or whether any public money has been recovered. The editorial's distinction is the grade's distinction: "there is a difference between allowing investigations to run their course and failing to demonstrate that anything meaningful is happening."
  • The referendum silence (Test 2) is also an unanswered wobble: the IEC, the NGO council, civic organisations and the Dikgosi have all, in their different voices, asked when the Constitutional Court vote will happen; the record assembled here contains no date and no reason for the delay.
  • The opposition's counter-account of the health file is contested and is printed as such: Leader of the Opposition Dumelang Saleshando told the November 2026 SONA debate that the medicine shortages the president had promised to end "remained unresolved," and that promised online access to public services had not materialised — against the Health Minister's recovery figures (Daily News, 10 November 2026). Both accounts cannot be wholly right; the ministry's own formulation — availability improved, "full stabilisation not yet achieved" (Ministry of Health, via Daily News) — is the shelf they share.
  • A coherence wobble stands unanswered: the presidency promises jobs and expanded support while the Finance Ministry, under Vice President Ndaba Gaolathe, runs austerity and rebuilds reserves — "two parallel governments," in the News&All Botswana editorial's phrase, with businesses squeezed by delayed state payments while the president promises them relief. The government says it cleared more than 216,000 outstanding supplier invoices in ten months (State of the Nation Address, via Daily News, 12 November 2026); the editorial page says the squeeze continues. The tension between the two voices of his own government has not been publicly reconciled by the man at the top of both.

The no-good-choice clause, applied: The fiscal trap itself is graded under the clause. Boko inherited an economy in which one commodity supplied roughly 80% of exports and a third of state revenue, at the moment that commodity's market turned structural — lab-grown substitution and weak demand are not a cycle he can wait out, and the dependence was built over 58 years of BDP rule, not by him. The options that actually existed in 2025–26 each carried real harm: borrow domestically at Treasury-bill yields that rose past 10.5% and push debt toward 45% of GDP; cut the social floor he had just raised, in the country's poorest households; raise taxes on a shrinking non-mining economy; or spend the last of the reserves. There was no good door, and the dossier does not grade the macro-fiscal outcome against him; it grades the origin (not his), his honesty inside the trap (high — the "not yet" on jobs, the published deficit numbers, the finance ministry's open budget consultations), and the least-harm doors he did take (levy suspension targeted at transport and food costs, supplementary payments aimed at the vulnerable). The clause is expressly not applied to the audit follow-through or the referendum writ: publishing a count of investigations, or signing a writ, costs nothing but pride, and a trap that consists of embarrassment is not a trap within the clause's meaning.

Why this grade: On events — a health-system collapse, an energy shock, a disease outbreak, a collapsing core market — this president adjusts openly, admits failure in his own voice, and changes course with reasons stated, including abandoning his own opening demand on De Beers when another option became available. On accountability — the file where the pushback comes from auditors, editors and the opposition rather than from events — the answer for six months has been the same paragraph repeated. A ruler who answers the weather but not the questions has answered the easier half. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — patients, above all people living with HIV, whose medicines were prioritised and secured; motorists, transport users and food buyers shielded from the full fuel shock; pensioners and disability and destitute beneficiaries who received supplementary payments; cattle farmers defended by the FMD response. Hurt — citizens waiting since May 2026 for a single number from the audit follow-through; voters waiting on a referendum date; and businesses caught between presidential promises of payment and treasury practice, on the editorial record.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the minorities, the opposition's voters, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The most counted-out people in the country were counted first, by name, with land. The San (Basarwa) of the Central Kalahari Game Reserve were removed from their ancestral land in waves from the 1990s; a 2006 High Court ruling that the evictions were unlawful was never implemented by the governments that followed. Boko's government established an Inter-Ministerial Committee on the Rights of the San Community in February 2025; secured the burial at his ancestral home in Metsiamanong of Pitseng Gaoberekwe, a San man who died in December 2021 and whose body had lain in a mortuary for nearly three years while the state resisted (Hivos/EU SEE case report, November 2026); approved a CKGR Integrated Roadmap in September 2026; received the committee's report on 5 November 2026; and on 9 November 2026 announced the restitution of the 52,000 km² reserve to its indigenous communities — described by the government as the largest structured return of land rights to an indigenous population within a protected area on the African continent (State of the Nation Address, 9 November 2026; Daily News, 9 November 2026). An indigenous-languages initiative in cultural policy runs in the same direction (Daily News, November 2026).
  • The social floor he raised is universal, not factional. The old-age pension rose from P830 (see Test 5 for the dispute over the current figure); technical and vocational students' allowances rose from P300 to P1,900; water tariffs were cut 30% with a 50% amnesty on outstanding household bills; free sanitary pads reached 80,498 schoolgirls on independent tracking, with supply interruptions recorded (manifesto tracker, October 2026); and the 2026 fuel-shock top-ups went to pensioners, disability-grant recipients, destitute beneficiaries and home-based care patients as categories of need, not of loyalty (State of the Nation Address, 10 November 2025; AfDB, October 2026).
  • In the health emergency, the largest patient community in the country was counted first: antiretroviral supply was explicitly prioritised through the shortage and secured for twelve months, in a country where roughly one in five adults lives with HIV (Health Minister's statement to Parliament, via News&All Botswana).
  • No policy in the record assembled here counts out the 30.47% who voted BDP, any region, or any ethnic group; the forensic audit's institution list spans the state itself, and the new government's flagship land act benefits a community that has never been an electoral constituency of consequence — the San number in the low tens of thousands and have historically been governed at, not courted.

Evidence against:

  • The young carried him into office and remain the least counted in outcomes. Unemployment stands at 27.3% overall and 39.8% among youth (Ministry of Finance, Budget Strategy Paper consultations, November 2026). In the second quarter of 2026 headline GDP grew 8.7% on a mining rebound while non-mining output slipped 0.2% and manufacturing contracted 9.2% (AfDB reporting, via Devdiscourse, November 2026) — the recovery, so far, is happening in the sector that employs almost nobody, and the generation that voted for transformation is still queuing.
  • The medicine recovery is incomplete and falls where shortages always fall. Availability recovered from 17% to about 64% by early 2026, with the ministry itself stating that "full stabilisation had not yet been achieved" and rural facilities last in the distribution line (Ministry of Health, via Daily News and News&All Botswana). The AfDB records that the state's own payment arrears to medicine suppliers contributed to the emergency (AfDB, October 2026) — a counting failure in which the patients at public clinics, the poorest users of the system, bore a treasury decision.
  • A small group sits in a corridor he built by restraint. Fifteen men were reported on death row as of May 2026 (Amnesty International); the Prisons Department's figure in separate reporting is nineteen — the two counts are printed separately, as they stand. None has been executed under Boko, none has been commuted by him, and four whose appeals were exhausted petitioned him for mercy with no outcome traced in the record assembled here (Africa Press, 2026). DITSHWANELO, the national human-rights centre, challenged the government in October 2026 to formalise a moratorium and open a national dialogue on abolition; Botswana remains the only country in southern Africa still carrying out the legal machinery of executions, retained by a president who spent his legal career challenging that machinery's legitimacy (News&All Botswana, October 2026; Amnesty International, July 2025). The no-good-choice clause is noted here as it applies to the man: the Constitution he swore to uphold retains the penalty, 82% of Batswana support it for serious crimes (Afrobarometer, 2024), and abolition requires a constitutional process he has started for other purposes but not this one. His restraint — no warrant signed in two years — is recorded; so is the limbo it leaves.
  • The CKGR restitution is announced, not yet effected. Implementation law, compensation for the relocated, services, and the precedent it sets are all untested; in the SONA debate a government MP himself hoped compensation would accompany the return, and MPs from other districts asked whether communities relocated elsewhere would receive similar consideration (Daily News, 17 November 2026); the Sunday Standard warned the announcement may have "opened Pandora's Box" of ancestral claims the state is unprepared to settle (Sunday Standard, November 2026). The door is open on paper, and this shelf records the opening, not the homecoming.

Why this grade: No group in Botswana is counted out by design under this president — the signature act of his second year counts in the people every predecessor counted out, and the welfare floor rose for citizens as citizens, in a fiscal collapse, without a loyalty test. That is well beyond the midpoint of this series. The grade is MIXED rather than HOLDS because counting is measured in outcomes as well as intentions: the young who elected him remain uncounted in work at a 39.8% unemployment rate, the clinic shelves are not yet reliably full, nineteen-or-fifteen men wait in a death-row corridor that is his to end in either direction, and the CKGR's people hold an announcement where their grandparents held a court judgment that was also never implemented. He will be graded again on the delivery.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the San communities of the CKGR, named and landed after three decades; pensioners, TVET students, schoolgirls, water-bill debtors, and people living with HIV, all reached by universal measures in a revenue collapse. Hurt — unemployed youth, whose wait lengthened through a mining-led recovery; rural patients still facing incomplete medicine shelves; the men on death row and their families, held between a law unapplied and a mercy ungranted; and, prospectively, other displaced communities whose claims the CKGR precedent raises without yet answering.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Manifesto pledges and public statements vs. the record since 1 November 2024, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The De Beers deal — concluded. The UDC promised to settle the diamond partnership that had been unsigned since 2023. On 25 February 2025, four months into the term, the government and De Beers signed the formal agreements: a ten-year sales agreement under which the state-owned Okavango Diamond Company sells 30% of Debswana's production for five years, 40% for the next five, and 50% in a five-year extension period; the Debswana mining licences extended from 2029 to July 2054; and a Diamonds for Development Fund with P1 billion upfront from De Beers and up to P10 billion over the decade (De Beers Group statement, 25 February 2025).
  • The social-floor words — substantially kept, at cost, in a collapse. Old-age pension raised from P830 (the current figure is disputed — see below); TVET allowances raised from P300 to P1,900; water tariffs cut 30% with a 50% arrears amnesty; free sanitary pads distributed; a Sovereign Wealth Fund established; 100,000 housing units under the Bonno programme commenced (State of the Nation Addresses, 10 November 2025 and 9 November 2026; SONA debate, Daily News, November 2025).
  • Constitutional reform — delivered to the voters' door. He promised a Constitutional Court as the precursor to a comprehensive review. The Constitution (Amendment) Bill was gazetted, debated against strong opposition and civic resistance, and passed by Parliament in March 2026; what remains is the referendum the Constitution itself requires (2nd Republic News, 31 March 2026; State of the Nation Address, 9 November 2026). The writ's delay is graded in Tests 2 and 3; the legislative word was kept.
  • The forensic audit — commissioned and published, as promised. A campaign promise of accountability for the decade's money became an Alvarez & Marsal audit of 30 institutions (2014–2024), received by the president on 30 April 2026, with a high-level summary made public in May 2026 (News&All Botswana, November 2026). The consequences word is on the against shelf; the audit word itself was kept.
  • The accountability of manner — kept. He promised a different kind of presidency: no reprisals, institutions respected, a predecessor treated with courtesy. Two years on, that word is the one the whole record supports (Tests 1 and 2).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Jobs — the headline word, unmet and admitted. The manifesto promised 450,000 to 500,000 jobs in five years. Two years in, unemployment is 27.3% and youth unemployment 39.8%; asked directly by an SABC anchor whether the jobs target had been achieved, Boko answered: "Not yet" (AfDB Botswana Economic Outlook 2026 reporting, via TJTimes). The admission is honest and is credited in Test 3; the word remains unkept.
  • The P4,000 living wage — kept for the state's own, not for the country. The manifesto's P4,000 figure was delivered as a pay agreement between government and seven public-sector unions, effective July 2025 — the government as employer paying its own workers (State of the Nation Address, 10 November 2025). The statutory national minimum wage was not raised to P4,000, and in November 2026 the Leader of the Opposition was still putting "unanswered questions" about the P4,000 minimum wage to the government in the SONA debate (Daily News, 10 November 2026). Private-sector low-wage workers — the promise's natural audience — have not received it.
  • The pension number — the word and the ledger differ. The manifesto promised P1,800 a month and eligibility from age 60. In his November 2025 address Boko claimed the pension had been increased "from the then P830 to P1,800." In the parliamentary debate on the same address, a UDC MP put the increase at 68% — P830 to P1,400 — and an independent manifesto tracker in October 2026 likewise lists the pension at P1,400 (Daily News, 17 November 2025; manifesto tracker, October 2026). The two figures are printed as contested: the president's claimed P1,800 against the parliamentary and tracker record of P1,400. Neither the age-60 eligibility nor the P2,500 National Community Service stipend is documented as delivered.
  • The De Beers majority — the word was control; the outcome is influence. In October 2024 Boko campaigned on establishing a global consortium to buy De Beers (CAJ News Africa, October 2024), and through 2025 he demanded a controlling stake in the Anglo American exit. The November 2026 outcome is 30% and largest-shareholder status inside the consortium — which he announced while acknowledging the criticism that it falls short of the controlling stake he had promised (mining.com, November 2026). The adjustment is graded as good wobble-answering in Test 3; as a kept word, it is not one.
  • Audit consequences — the second half of the promise. On receiving the report on 30 April 2026, the message was unmistakable that those responsible for looting public resources would face the consequences (News&All Botswana, November 2026). Six months later there is no public accounting of investigations opened, charges laid, or money recovered, and the follow-through was re-announced in November in substantially the same terms as in May. Former president Ian Khama, a BPF figure whose party sits outside the government, publicly set 2027 as the year delivery must become visible — patience from an ally, with a date on it (The Botswana Gazette, 16 November 2026).

Why this grade: The pattern is consistent and two-sided. The words that cost money he controls — pensions, allowances, tariffs, pads — were kept early and at real fiscal sacrifice. The words that required machinery — jobs at half-a-million scale, a statutory living wage, a referendum writ, prosecutions with numbers attached — are the words still waiting, and one word (the pension) is claimed at a figure the parliamentary record does not corroborate. Two years of a five-year term is early to close this file; it is not too early to record which kind of promise this president keeps first. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — pensioners, students, indebted households and schoolgirls, who received the kept words in cash and kind; the diamond industry's workers and the fiscus, given a signed ten-year framework. Hurt — the low-paid private-sector workers still below the promised wage floor; the unemployed young, the promise's largest intended beneficiaries; and pensioners themselves, if the trackers' P1,400 is the true figure, by the P400 a month between the word and the payment.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Duma Boko is the first ruler in this series who took his seat from the incumbent party at the ballot box and then had to be graded, two years on, on what he did with a state whose money had run out. The whys, in brief: Test 1 lands MIXED because the political prosecution he inherited was ended rather than inherited as a weapon, and the audit he commissioned aimed at a decade rather than a man — while the president personally keeps two exceptions at the source, direct contract awards defended in Parliament and a bespoke channel for the audit's own findings; the ledger there helps every former officeholder who can now lose power safely, and hurts the uncharged-but-clouded across 30 institutions and every bidder facing his open door. Test 2 lands MIXED because no referee has been removed, silenced or punished — his own Chief Justice defies him in public, his predecessor's intelligence chief served out his full term, the hostile press is untouched — while a mandatory referendum waits seven months on his writ and the flagship file was withheld from the statutory investigator for six months and rerouted to a task force of his creation; it is the strongest for-shelf this test has carried in the recent series, decided against HOLDS by those two process files, both of which remain within his power to close. Test 3 lands MIXED because he answers events — health collapse, fuel shock, disease outbreak, a dying core market — with admission, money and stated reasons, including abandoning his own majority-stake demand on De Beers when another option became available, while answering the accountability questions of 2026 with the same paragraph for six months; the fiscal trap he inherited is graded under the no-good-choice clause and not held against him, and the audit and referendum silences are expressly outside that clause's protection. Test 4 lands MIXED because the San of the CKGR were counted in with 52,000 km² of ancestral land after three decades of being counted out, and the welfare floor rose universally in a collapse — while the young who elected him stand at 39.8% unemployment, the clinic shelves are not yet reliably full, and fifteen-to-nineteen men wait on a death row he will neither use nor close. Test 5 lands MIXED because the affordable words were kept at real sacrifice and the machinery words — jobs, the statutory wage, consequences with numbers — are still waiting, with the pension claimed at P1,800 against a parliamentary record of P1,400.

The four crossings, named as they occurred: (1) Kept his word and it helped — the social floor: pension, allowances, tariff cuts and pads, delivered during a revenue collapse to the elderly, students and indebted households. (2) Kept his word and it still hurt — the fiscal-discipline word: the "steady path" of restraint and reserve-rebuilding under his finance ministry was kept as promised, and its keeping squeezed the suppliers, businesses and clinic budgets at the end of the chain — the state arrears to medicine suppliers that fed the 2025 health emergency are part of this crossing's ledger (AfDB, October 2026). (3) Went against his word and it helped — De Beers: the controlling stake he promised became 30% and the largest single voice at the table, a climbdown that unblocked the Anglo exit and bought strategic influence without majority funding of a distressed asset; the break is recorded here and in Test 5, the help is recorded in full. (4) Broke his word and it hurt — jobs and the statutory living wage: the two promises aimed at the young and the low-paid, unmet at two years, in an economy where youth unemployment stands at 39.8%.

No test is failed, and none is held. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier and are all, unusually, within the graded man's own gift: whether the referendum writ is signed and the vote held; whether the audit produces investigations, charges and recoveries that can be counted; whether the De Beers transaction closes on the announced terms; whether the CKGR restitution is implemented in law rather than announced in speeches; and whether the pension ledger is reconciled with the presidential figure. The record is correctable, and this one is built to be corrected by events.
