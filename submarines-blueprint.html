Submarines

The documented machine, designed forward. Submarines are solved engineering within hard limits — pressure hull, ballast, buoyancy, propulsion — and this blueprint keeps every one of those proven answers, then asks what it would take to remove the failure that has defined them: being lost with all hands.

No more lost submarines — a submarine that cannot be crushed and cannot sink beyond rescue.— the aim of this design, at full size. Not a claim. A specification to engineer toward.

WHY?

Submarines fail rarely — and when they fail at depth, they fail completely. The record below is short and it is total: boats that went down and did not come back, crews that could not be reached in time, or at all. That pattern, not any single accident, is what this design exists to end.

HOW?

Keep the proven boat whole — hull, ballast, buoyancy, propulsion — and add three design asks on top of it: a field that shares the pressure load and takes collision energy, an emergency ascent that does not depend on the systems that just failed, and rescue designed in from the first line. Every ask is labeled design, not fact.

ACT.

The blueprint is released. Read it, test its pieces in a pressure chamber before open water, build on it, improve it. What is not yet engineered is labeled OPEN HOW at full size — those are the doors, and they are open on purpose.

Standing Laws of This Design

Two house laws govern every sheet in this family. This section states what they honestly mean for a submarine — graded from this sheet's own material, and refused wherever a label doesn't fit.

The Three Modes — the standing law, set down November 21, 2026, under copyleft (CC BY-SA 4.0) — rule every spec that distributes, spaces, proportions, or flows. Golden = spread, never align: each new element into the biggest gap, at the golden angle 137.5077641°. Platinum = align exactly: equal, on the line, 1:1 — the right mode where alignment is the function. Diamond = the weave: align locally, spread globally. Spec law: every spec on this sheet involving distribution, proportion, spacing, or flow states which mode it runs and why. The full treatment lives on the Super Suit sheet as System 9 and on Sheet № 0 (The Zero Dimension) §14. Here is this boat's own grading:

Platinum — ballast & neutral buoyancy

This sheet's vertical drive is an exact balance, stated in its own words in §01: flood and the boat is heavier than the water it displaces; blow and it is lighter; trimmed exactly even, it hovers. Neutral buoyancy is not approximate — it is weight matched to displaced water, 1:1, held by the trim system. Where this boat balances, alignment is the function itself, so it runs Platinum.

Golden — the absorption field, spread over the whole hull

Both jobs §03 asks of the field are distribution jobs. Sharing the pressure load means the sea's squeeze is borne across the whole wrapped hull, never concentrated where it strikes; taking collision and grounding energy means a blow arriving at one point is spread out and spent, not kept at the point of impact. And the rescue fit spreads the same way over the search problem: a releasable beacon that rises and reports, acoustic signalling that carries with main power dead — coverage placed so a disabled boat leaves no silent gap to be lost in. Where this design spreads load, energy, or signal, it runs Golden.

Diamond — the boat as a layered whole

The proven systems each hold their exact local alignment — a round hull with no corners for stress to gather in, ballast trimmed to the kilogram, a rescue seat kept clear and standard — while the field wraps over all of it at once, spreading load and impact across the whole boat, and rescue is designed in across every system rather than bolted onto one. Exact locally, spread globally: the layered design runs Diamond. That is the weave this sheet actually draws in Fig. 1 — nothing more is claimed for it.

And the refusals, printed at the same size. The pressure arithmetic — one atmosphere every ten metres — is the ocean's physics, not an arrangement, and takes no mode. The stakes shelf is a record of losses, graded as evidence, not a spacing. The build order is a sequence, not a distribution. Propulsion's cavitation trade is a limit, not a flow pattern. And the independent lift of §03 carries no mode at all: its mechanism is an open how, unwritten, and a mode cannot honestly be graded on a mechanism that does not exist yet — when it is engineered, it will be graded then. The modes' own law governs the modes too: use it when it works — don't force it.

Adaptation — Adapt and Overcome. A top law, in the words it was given in: learn your lessons and grow. This blueprint is built to take in new knowledge and get better. The stakes shelf already carries the proof that the principle works: after Thresher, the US Navy answered with SUBSAFE, and no SUBSAFE-certified US boat has been lost since — a loss, honestly faced, became a stricter design. This sheet is written the same way. Today's version is the floor, not the ceiling: when a chamber test measures the field, when an open how closes, when a loss or a rescue adds a lesson, the design is revised to fit the verified improvement in — fitted when verified better, never forced, and never a from-scratch rebuild of the proven boat that already works. The aim does not move: no more lost submarines. The design that serves it is expected to grow.

01

The Proven Base — what submarines already are

This page starts from respect for the real machine. A submarine is not a mystery and not a speculation: it is one of the most carefully engineered vehicles humans build, and its core answers are DOCUMENTED ENGINEERING — printed here as proven, because they are.

The pressure hull. A thick cylinder — spheres at the ends, round in cross-section — of high-strength steel, titanium, or composite. Round is not a style choice: a curved shell spreads the sea's squeeze evenly, with no corners for stress to gather in. The hull is the boat. Everything else is arranged around keeping it intact and keeping the people inside it alive.

Ballast and buoyancy — the vertical drive. A submarine does not swim up and down. It balances. Main ballast tanks flood with seawater and the boat becomes heavier than the water it displaces, so it sinks; compressed air blows the tanks empty and the boat becomes lighter, so it rises; trimmed exactly even, it hovers. Depth-keeping planes then fly it gently up or down while it has forward speed. Lose the ability to become lighter than water, and the boat has lost its way home — remember that sentence when you reach the buoyancy insurance below.

Propulsion. Diesel-electric boats run diesels on the surface and batteries below; nuclear boats run a reactor that needs no air at all. Either way, a shaft turns a propeller or pump-jet, and the propeller sets a hard trade: spin it faster and the water boils into bubbles on the blades — cavitation — which is loud, wasteful, and wearing. Submarines are quiet on purpose, and speed is spent carefully.

The ocean's terms. None of that engineering changes the terms it works under, and the terms are printed here at full size because the whole design is an answer to them:

  • Pressure grows about one atmosphere every ten metres. At the surface you live under one atmosphere. At 100 metres the sea adds about ten more. At 1,000 metres, about a hundred. There is no depth at which it stops growing.
  • Hull failure at depth is total. A hull that fails under that pressure does not leak politely and wait. The failure is structural and immediate. That is why submarine safety is built on prevention and margins, not on surviving a breach at depth.
  • Time is air. A disabled boat that stays intact on the bottom is a clock: the crew's air, heat, and power run out on a schedule set by chemistry, not by rescue wishes. Every rescue system ever built is a race against that clock.

02

The Stakes — the lost boats, named plainly

This shelf is the reason for the page. These are documented losses — real boats, real crews, recorded in official inquiries and histories. They are named here without spectacle, because a design that claims to answer a pattern should have to look at the pattern.

USS Thresher 1963 · 129 LOST

Lost during deep-diving trials off the US East Coast. The investigation pointed to a flooding casualty that cascaded — a reactor shutdown, a failed emergency blow — until the boat passed its crush depth. The US Navy answered with SUBSAFE, a safety programme so strict that no SUBSAFE-certified US boat has been lost since. Proof that a loss pattern, honestly faced, can be engineered down.

USS Scorpion 1968 · 99 LOST

Lost in the Atlantic near the Azores on the way home from deployment. The wreck was found on the bottom months later; the exact initiating cause was never conclusively settled. She stands on this shelf for a harder lesson: sometimes the sea does not even return an explanation.

Kursk 2000 · 118 LOST

A Russian nuclear submarine lost in the Barents Sea after explosions in the torpedo compartment during an exercise. The boat settled on the bottom in about 108 metres of water — shallow, by submarine standards — and still the rescue effort could not mate with the escape hatch in time. The clock, and the mating surface, are design problems. This loss is why both appear in this blueprint as design inputs.

ARA San Juan 2017 · 44 LOST

An Argentine submarine lost in the South Atlantic after reporting a water ingress and electrical fire. The wreck was found a year later, deep beyond rescue. The pattern again: a casualty that began as one system's failure and became the whole boat's.

Titan 2023 · 5 LOST

A commercial deep-diving submersible lost on a dive to the Titanic wreck. Investigators concluded the experimental carbon-fibre hull failed under pressure. It stands here as the modern form of the oldest lesson on this shelf: at depth, the hull is not the place to improvise — margins and proven practice are the safety system.

The pattern, not just the boats

Read across the whole record and the losses rhyme: flooding that cascades from one failure into propulsion, power, and buoyancy together; fire or explosion that disables the boat faster than the crew can isolate it; collision and grounding — boats that struck another vessel or the seabed itself; and hull failure at depth, which allows no second act. Four doors, and every lost boat went through one of them. The design below is organised door by door.

One more documented fact belongs on this shelf, at the same size as the losses: rescue has worked when the boat stayed intact, shallow, and findable. In 2005 the Russian submersible AS-28, trapped on the bottom, was freed by an uncrewed rescue vehicle in time; in 1973 the submersible Pisces III was lifted from 480 metres with both crew alive. The difference between those stories and the ones above was not luck. It was depth, time, a beacon's worth of information, and something for rescuers to grab. All four can be designed.

03

The Design — field, lift, and rescue

Three asks, laid on top of the proven boat — never instead of it. Each one answers a door from the stakes shelf. Each one is labeled with exactly how much of it is known.

1 — The absorption field as a second hull. DESIGN The same absorption field carried by the Suit, the Ship, and the Cars on this shelf of blueprints is asked here to wrap the pressure hull and do two jobs. First, share the pressure load — bear part of the sea's steady squeeze, so the hull works further from its limits at any given depth. Second, take collision and grounding energy — running into the seabed or another vessel is a documented loss pattern, and a sudden blow is precisely the kind of spike the field is designed, elsewhere on this shelf, to absorb. If the field does either job, the boat gains margin. If it does both, two of the four loss doors narrow at once.

Steady pressure is a different ask than impact — the field answering one does not prove it answers the other. Printed at the same size as the design itself, on purpose. A crash is a spike: enormous energy, gone in a moment. Depth is a siege: smaller force, applied from every side, forever. This blueprint asks the field to do both jobs and claims neither until each is tested on its own terms — the siege in a pressure chamber, held, not struck.

2 — Buoyancy insurance: a way up that cannot fail the same way twice. DESIGN OPEN HOW Look at the cascade pattern on the stakes shelf: the emergency and the way out of the emergency too often share the same systems — the same air banks, the same power, the same valves — so one failure takes both. This design therefore specifies the emergency ascent by its independence, not by a mechanism it doesn't honestly have: an ascent answer that does not depend on the systems whose failure caused the emergency. The candidate named here is formed-energy lift — the field itself, asked to make the boat lighter than water, or to lift it directly. Whether a field can do that, and at what power cost, is an open how at full size. What is not open is the requirement: whatever answers it must still work with the reactor shut down, the batteries flat, and the air banks empty — because that is the day it will be needed.

3 — Rescue by design. DESIGN — FROM PROVEN PARTS A boat designed so its crew can always be reached treats rescue as a design input, not an afterthought. Three inputs, each drawn from the record:

  • Beacon. A boat that cannot be found cannot be rescued. The design carries a releasable emergency beacon that rises and reports position, plus fixed acoustic signalling that works with the main power dead — because a disabled boat is exactly the boat that needs to be loud.
  • Mating surfaces. Rescue submersibles save crews by sealing onto a flat hatch surround and pumping out the space between. That only works if the surface is there, standard, unobstructed, and reachable at the angle the boat comes to rest. This design keeps a rescue seat clear on the hull as a hard requirement — nothing mounted over it, nothing stowed against it, ever.
  • Time (air). Air is the currency rescue is bought with. Endurance — oxygen generation, carbon-dioxide removal, stored emergency air, and the discipline of designing for days of it, not hours — is sized at the drawing board, alongside hull thickness and battery capacity, because on the bottom it matters exactly as much as both.

04

Systems

The boat, system by system. Each one is labeled with what it honestly is today: proven submarine engineering, part of this design, or an open how still owed.

Pressure hull PROVEN

The primary answer to the squeeze, and the design's non-negotiable floor: a round, thick hull of high-strength material, rated with margin for the boat's full depth. The fallback rule of this whole blueprint lives here — with the field dead, the hull must still be a pressure hull. The field adds margin; it never replaces it.

Absorption field — second hull OPEN HOW — STEADY LOAD

Asked to share the steady pressure load and to take collision and grounding energy. The impact job matches what the field is asked to do across this shelf of blueprints. The steady-load job is a different ask — a siege, not a spike — and is unproven until a chamber test holds it under pressure, over time, at a measured power cost.

Ballast & buoyancy PROVEN

Flood to sink, blow to rise, trim to hover. The proven vertical drive, kept whole: main ballast tanks, trim system, and a high-pressure air blow for emergencies. This is the way up on every normal day of the boat's life — and the system whose failure the insurance below must not repeat.

Buoyancy insurance — independent lift OPEN HOW

An emergency ascent specified by independence: it must work with propulsion, power, and air banks all failed, because cascading failure is the documented pattern it exists to answer. Formed-energy / field lift is the candidate. The requirement is fixed; the mechanism is owed.

Propulsion PROVEN — WITHIN LIMITS

Diesel-electric or nuclear drive turning a propeller or pump-jet, with cavitation as the standing speed-and-noise limit. Kept as proven: this design spends its unknowns on survival, not on re-inventing how a boat moves.

Rescue fit DESIGN — PROVEN PARTS

Releasable position beacon, acoustic signalling independent of main power, a standard rescue mating surface kept clear for the life of the boat, and escape and rescue provisions sized at the drawing board. No new physics — only the decision to treat being rescued as a system, with a budget, like any other.

Life support & endurance PROVEN

Oxygen, carbon-dioxide removal, water, and food for the crew — solved submarine arts, sized here with rescue time in mind: the boat must be able to wait on the bottom, alive, for as long as a rescue can honestly take to arrive and mate.

Power OPEN HOW — FIELD BUDGET

A submarine's power budget is already spoken for: propulsion, life support, sensors. This design adds a tenant — a field holding back the sea, continuously, for the whole dive. How large that draw is, and what source carries it alongside everything else, is an open how, and it gates how much of the field's promise can be used at depth.

05

Build Order — uncrewed and shallow first

The order is the safety system. Depth is earned in stages, and the chamber speaks before the ocean does: pressure-chamber proof before open water, at every stage where the chamber can honestly answer the question.

  1. Prove the proven boat, uncrewed and shallow.

    An uncrewed hull in a tank and in shallow water: ballast down, ballast up, hover, hold depth, blow and surface. The base is proven engineering, but this article proves it again at small scale before anything new is added to it.

  2. Put the hull in the chamber, past its rating.

    Pressure-test the hull alone — field off, field absent — beyond its rated depth, uncrewed, until the margin is measured rather than assumed. This step certifies the fallback: the hull is a pressure hull with the field dead.

  3. Put the field under steady pressure in the chamber.

    The candidate question, asked on the siege's own terms: field on, pressure applied and held — hours, not milliseconds. Measure how much load it truly shares, how the share changes with depth, and what it costs in power. A strike test proves nothing here.

  4. Strike it, separately.

    Only now, the impact job: collision and grounding energy against the field-wrapped hull, instrumented, at test scale. Two asks, two tests — the results of one are never used to claim the other.

  5. Prove the independent ascent with everything else dead.

    An uncrewed article on a test bottom, propulsion off, power off, air banks isolated — then the insurance fires. If the way up needs any of the systems it insures against, it goes back to the drawing board, not to sea.

  6. Rehearse the rescue, for real.

    Beacon release, locating, and a rescue vehicle mating to the seat — practised on the actual hull, at rest at realistic angles, against the air clock. Rescue by design is only real if it has been done, not just drawn.

  7. Go deeper in stages. Crew it last.

    Each new depth is earned by the stage before it, uncrewed first. People go aboard at a given depth only when hull, field, lift, and rescue have each been proven at that depth without them. The ocean does not grade on effort — so the order is not negotiable.

06

Open Hows — printed at full size

These are the questions this blueprint does not answer. They are not fine print and they are not physics walls — an unknown is not a no. They are engineering specifications waiting for their engineers. Anyone who closes one improves the blueprint for everybody, under the same licence.

The field under steady crushing pressure OPEN HOW

The field's job everywhere else on this shelf is impact — a spike of energy, over in a moment. Depth asks the opposite: a modest force per square centimetre, multiplied by the whole hull, applied from every side, without pause, for the entire dive. Does a field hold under that kind of load at all? Does it creep, leak, or tire? The chamber test in the Build Order is where this gets its first honest answer.

Capacity at full ocean depth OPEN HOW

Even if the field shares load at working depths, the ocean goes down to roughly 1,100 atmospheres. How does the field's share scale as pressure climbs — evenly, or does it fade exactly where it is needed most? Until that curve is measured, the design claims the field only where it has been tested, and the hull alone carries every depth rating.

The power budget for a submerged field OPEN HOW

A field that holds back the sea is a field that works continuously, and continuous work is a continuous power draw, for weeks, inside a boat whose power is already fully budgeted. How large is the draw at depth? Can a submarine's sources carry it alongside propulsion and life support — and if the field must choose between depth and endurance, which does the design protect?

Field lift as the independent way up OPEN HOW

The buoyancy-insurance candidate: formed energy making a disabled boat rise. Can a field produce net lift on a flooded, powerless hull — enough, fast enough, and gently enough for the crew inside? And can it do so drawing on a source that survives the cascade it insures against? The requirement is printed in full in the Design section; the mechanism is owed.

The fallback, stated as a rule OPEN HOW — BY DESIGN RULE

Not a question of physics but of discipline, printed here so it can never quietly become fine print: the pressure hull must still be a pressure hull with the field dead. Every depth rating on any boat built from this blueprint is a hull rating. The field may add margin above it; it may never be counted inside it. Any version of this design that lets the hull lean on the field has left the blueprint — and the stakes shelf explains why.
