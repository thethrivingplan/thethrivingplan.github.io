The Ruler's Test — Abdullah II, King of Jordan

Subject: Abdullah II bin Al Hussein — King of Jordan since 7 February 1999, when he succeeded on the death of his father, King Hussein, who had reigned 47 years (Congressional Research Service, Jordan: Background and U.S. Relations, updated July 2024). The seat graded is the throne, and the reason is stated plainly: Jordan is formally a parliamentary constitutional monarchy, but the constitution, most recently amended in 2022, vests decisive executive power in the king. He appoints the prime minister and may dismiss him, appoints cabinet ministers, all members of the Senate, senior military leaders and justices of the Constitutional Court; he commands the armed forces, declares war, ratifies treaties, must approve laws before they take effect, may dissolve both houses of parliament and postpone lower-house elections for up to two years, and can issue royal decrees not subject to parliamentary scrutiny (CRS, July 2024). The prime minister governs day-to-day under him: Jafar Hassan, the king's former chief of staff, has been prime minister since his designation on 15 September 2024, his cabinet sworn in on 18 September 2024, reshuffled by royal decree as recently as 27 January 2026 (Royal Hashemite Court, September 2024; State Media Monitor, Jordan 2026 cycle). Hassan's acts are therefore graded here as acts of the seat the king appointed and can dismiss — Jordanian governments have lasted on average no more than about 15 months before dissolution by royal decree (CRS, 2019–2024). The king's mandate is hereditary succession, not election: there is no platform the voters chose, so under the mandate rule nothing in this dossier grades a platform; it grades only the holding — how power has been used across a reign of more than 27 years, the longest tenure in this batch, with events through November 2026 in scope. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test, printed verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Every test closes with the grade's reason in plain words and a named ledger of who was helped and who was hurt. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machinery exists, is used, and has reached a former insider of the palace itself. Jordan's Anti-Corruption Commission (now the Integrity and Anti-Corruption Commission) examines cases and refers them to the judiciary, and in the 2021 "sedition" case the State Security Court convicted Bassem Awadallah — a former head of the Royal Court (2007–2008) and former finance minister — sentencing him to 15 years, a verdict upheld by the Court of Cassation in September 2021 (Petra via Voice of Nigeria, July and September 2021). Whatever else that trial was (see below), the defendant was no outsider: he had run the king's own court.
  • The outcome measure sits mid-table and has edged the right way: Jordan scored 50 of 100 in Transparency International's 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index (56th of 182, fifth among Arab states, tied with Bahrain), up one point from 49 in 2024, with the national chapter Rasheed noting improved V-Dem judicial anti-corruption scores and a one-point rise on the World Justice Project Rule of Law Index (Rasheed / Transparency International Jordan, CPI 2025, released 10 February 2026).
  • The 2025 ban of the Muslim Brotherhood was applied through a stated legal sequence — a 2020 Court of Cassation ruling had already ordered the Brotherhood dissolved, its headquarters had been closed in 2016 — and its political party, the Islamic Action Front (IAF), was left operating legally with its 31 parliamentary seats intact, a distinction the Atlantic Council describes as a "compromise" the movement itself could partly accept (CRS, May 2025 and updated 2025; Atlantic Council, 2025). The law, on this file, did not simply erase the largest opposition bloc the voters had just chosen.

Evidence against:

  • The source itself is a second law. The seat is hereditary and its holder stands outside the accountability applied to everyone else: the king appoints the judges who would judge, the senators who would legislate, and the prime minister who would answer — and can dismiss all three. A system in which one man is the appointer of every referee cannot, at the source, be one law for the appointer and the appointed. This is a fact about the constitution he has amended repeatedly in his own favour (Test 2), not an allegation.
  • Anti-corruption enforcement is selective by design, on the strongest study available. A peer-reviewed multi-method study of two decades of Jordanian anti-corruption work (Yom & Monroe, Mediterranean Politics, published online 5 November 2025) found that Jordan's efforts "prioritize punishing petty corruption involving low-level civil servants, rather than grand corruption by powerful regime elites," that the selectivity "reflects the personalistic nature of Jordan's regime, given the importance of sustaining a solidary loyal elite that supports the king," and that investigations cluster after major protests and before elections — timing that serves the palace's standing, not the law's calendar.
  • The favour system the law claims to criminalise runs untouched underneath. Wasta — favouritism, cronyism and nepotism through personal connections for jobs and access — is documented as the normal channel through which Jordanians reach the state; Jordan criminalised wasta as trading in influence after joining the UN Convention Against Corruption in 2005, and in the fourteen years that followed not a single wasta case resulted in a conviction (Doughan, LSE, EASA 2020 paper; University of Ottawa thesis research on Jordan's anti-corruption initiative, 2016).
  • The 2021 sedition file shows the two laws side by side in a single case. Awadallah and Sharif Hassan bin Zaid were tried in a military-led State Security Court in seven sessions over three weeks, the court refusing to hear any defence witnesses including Prince Hamzah; both were sentenced to 15 years (Human Rights Watch / DAWN, August 2021). Prince Hamzah himself — the king's half-brother, the man prosecutors alleged the plot aimed to install — was never charged at all: the king announced on 7 April 2021 that Hamzah was "with his family, at his palace, in my care," and the matter was handled inside the family (HRW, August 2021). Awadallah's US lawyer alleged his confession was extracted under torture — an ALLEGATION, denied by the public prosecution, recorded here as contested, not as a finding. The documented asymmetry needs no allegation: the commoner and the minor royal went to prison for 15 years; the senior royal at the centre of the alleged plot faced no court.

Why this grade: One law fails at the source and in the pattern. At the source, the graded man is constitutionally unaccountable and appoints everyone who could hold him to account. In the pattern, the best systematic evidence (Yom & Monroe, 2025) shows enforcement aimed downward at petty offenders and timed for political effect, the favour system that actually allocates jobs and access has produced zero convictions in fourteen years, and the one case that touched the palace ended with the royal family member at its centre exempted from the process his alleged partners received. The CPI score of 50 and the Awadallah conviction keep this from being a record of pure impunity for insiders — but a former court chief convicted in a case about loyalty to the king is not the same thing as the law reaching the loyal.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — low-level integrity in the public sector, where petty-corruption prosecutions are real; the palace's public standing after protests and before elections, which the study shows the crackdown timing serves. Hurt — low-level civil servants, who carry the enforcement burden; Jordanians without connections, for whom wasta remains the working price of access; Bassem Awadallah and Sharif Hassan bin Zaid, who received a three-week military-court trial with no defence witnesses while the alleged plot's central figure received family care.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Two referees were genuinely created on his watch and still stand. The 2011 constitutional amendments — amending roughly a third of the constitution — established a Constitutional Court and an Independent Election Commission (IEC), taking election administration out of the Interior Ministry, required elections within four months of a dissolution rather than allowing a two-year postponement, and provided for ministers to be tried in civilian courts (King Abdullah II interview, AFP, September 2012; Oxford Business Group overview of the 2011 amendments). The IEC ran the 10 September 2024 election, which the EU Election Observation Mission called "well-run" and "inclusive," with voter and candidate registration "efficiently administered" and a genuine choice among 1,623 candidates (EU EOM Preliminary Statement, 12 September 2024).
  • A court pushed back, on the record, in 2025: the Court of Cassation ruled on 10 July 2025 that activist Ayman Sandouka — sentenced by the State Security Court to five years for a 2023 Facebook post addressed to the king — had been convicted under the wrong, graver article, and that the correct charge was the lesser offence of insulting the king, carrying one to three years (Human Rights Watch, June 2026). The ruling did not free him, but the kingdom's highest court corrected a security court's excess in a case about speech to the king himself.
  • The 2024 election's result was allowed to stand against the palace's preference: the opposition IAF won 31 of 138 seats, the largest bloc, in an election the king had called by dissolving parliament on 25 July 2024 — and he then appointed a government and let the chamber sit with that bloc in it (2024 election results; EU EOM, 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The grip is constitutional, and he has tightened it by amendment three times. Amendments in 2014 and 2016 gave the king sole appointment of senior military and security chiefs and Constitutional Court justices; the January 2022 amendments let him appoint and dismiss by royal decree, without consulting the Council of Ministers, the Chief Justice, the head of the Sharia Judicial Council, the Grand Mufti, the Chief of the Royal Court, the Minister of the Court and his own advisers, and created a National Security Council headed by the king with wide-ranging political and security powers — described by critics as "a fourth branch of government" able to bypass cabinet and parliament (Carnegie Endowment, Constitutional Amendments in Jordan, March 2022; European Forum for Democracy and Solidarity, March 2022). He already appointed all 65–75 senators (the upper house, equal in legislative power to the elected chamber), all judges by decree, the prime minister and cabinet (CRS, July 2024).
  • The prosecution of speech runs through a military court and a law he signed over public objection. The State Security Court — a military institution whose leaders and prosecutors are military officials, trying civilians — handles the speech cases (HRW, August 2021; HRW, June 2026). The 2023 Cybercrime Law, enacted by royal decree assenting to parliament's text despite sustained domestic and international criticism, criminalises vague offences — "fake news," "provoking strife," "undermining national unity" — at a minimum three months' jail and fines of 5,000–20,000 dinars; Jordan's own National Centre for Human Rights counted 3,170 prosecutions under it in 2024 alone, the vast majority for online defamation (HRW World Report 2026, Jordan chapter). On 14 May 2025 authorities blocked 12 news websites under the law for reporting on Jordanian aid to Gaza (HRW World Report 2026). Jordan ranked 142nd of 180 in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index (score 39.33, up from 147th in 2025) — an improvement that still leaves it in the index's restrictive band (State Media Monitor, 2026 cycle; RSF 2026).
  • Independent organisation that challenged the government has been dissolved, twice over. The Jordanian Teachers' Syndicate — 140,000 members, the country's largest independent organisation — was raided and closed in July 2020 after it accused the government of reneging on its 2019 pay deal; its 13 board members were arrested, a gag order barred public discussion of the case, more than 250 protesters were detained, and a court ordered its permanent dissolution in December 2020 (UN human rights experts, August 2020; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2021). Freedom House demoted Jordan from Partly Free to Not Free in 2021 citing that crackdown among its reasons; the country has since recovered only to Partly Free, scoring 34 of 100 in Freedom in the World 2026 (political rights 12 of 40, civil liberties 22 of 60), with free media and due process each scored 1 of 4 (Freedom House, 2021 and 2026 editions).
  • The pattern continued through the grading window: Sandouka's five-year sentence in January 2025 for a peaceful Facebook post; the April 2025 Brotherhood ban executed by interior-ministry announcement, with raids on IAF offices; the detention in July 2025 of sitting IAF MP Yanal Freihat over a social-media post criticising the ban — the first detention of a sitting MP since the ban (HRW World Report 2026; International IDEA Democracy Tracker, July 2025). In 2026, the Gulf Centre for Human Rights documented continued cybercrime prosecutions of journalists and activists, including journalist Hiba Abu Taha, sentenced in March 2026 to two years for a Facebook post — recorded here from a rights-monitor report, with the case details as the monitor states them (GCHR, 2026).

Why this grade: Test 2 is the test this seat is built to fail, and the record matches the blueprint. Every referee in the kingdom — Senate, Constitutional Court, chief justice, security chiefs, election-day government — is either appointed by the graded man or dismissible by him, and his own amendments in 2014, 2016 and 2022 moved the grip from practice into explicit sole-decree law. The institutions created in 2011 are real, the 2024 election was honestly administered, and the Cassation's Sandouka correction shows a court can still say no at the margin — but a referee who can be replaced by the player is not independent, and the speech prosecutions, website blockings and union dissolution show the grip being used, not merely held.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — voters in 2024, who got a competently run election and a result the palace disliked but accepted; the state's stability constituency, which values a single decisive centre. Hurt — journalists and online commentators (3,170 cybercrime prosecutions in 2024; 12 outlets blocked in May 2025); Ayman Sandouka, imprisoned for a post to the king; the 140,000 members of the dissolved Teachers' Syndicate; MP Yanal Freihat, detained for criticising a government ban; parliament as an institution, whose upper house and timetable the king controls outright.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • 2011, the Arab Spring: the pushback was answered with structural concession, openly made. As protests spread in January–February 2011, the king dismissed Prime Minister Samir Rifai's government on 1 February 2011, created a National Dialogue Committee and a Royal Committee on Constitutional Review, and in September 2011 approved amendments to 42 articles — Constitutional Court, independent election commission, elections within four months of dissolution, torture prohibited (Wilson Center chronology, 2011; Oxford Business Group). When the replacement government stalled, he dismissed it too, in October 2011. The concessions were partial (Test 2 records what was later taken back), but they were made in public, in the direction the street was pushing.
  • 2018, the tax protests: the clearest open adjustment of the reign. After more than 30 trade unions struck on 30 May 2018 against an IMF-backed income-tax bill and fuel and electricity price rises, the king intervened on 1 June to freeze the price hikes, accepted Prime Minister Hani Mulki's resignation on 4 June, appointed Omar Razzaz with instructions to review the whole tax system, and the new government withdrew the tax bill; a revised law followed in September that exempted lower-paid individuals from filing (Amnesty International, Review of 2018; contemporaneous reporting, June 2018). The protests ended because the demand was substantially met, in the open.
  • On the Cybercrime Law, the king has at least put review on the record: at a meeting with the National Centre for Human Rights and the Jordan Press Association, he stated that combating cybercrime "should not be at the expense of Jordanians' right to express their opinion and criticise public policies," directed the government to review the access-to-information draft law, and said the Cybercrime Law's implementation would be "the decisive factor" in evaluating it and revising provisions (Royal Hashemite Court, kingabdullah.jo). That is the other-option test in his own words: if another option becomes available — here, revision after an implementation review — he has publicly committed to considering it. The commitment is recorded; its fulfilment is graded in Test 5.
  • The interception file, 2024–2026 — graded under the no-good-choice clause. In April and October 2024, in June 2025, and again during the February–April 2026 regional escalation, Jordanian air defences intercepted Iranian missiles and drones crossing Jordanian airspace toward Israel; in the 2026 round the armed forces reported intercepting 261 of 281 missiles and drones targeting or transiting the kingdom over five weeks, with some 28–30 people wounded by debris (Qatar News Agency, 4 April 2026; military statements). The options that actually existed were two, and both carried real harm: intercept, and be accused at home — as he was, in street protest — of defending Israel while Gaza burned; or stand aside, and let live munitions fall on Jordanian towns beneath the flight paths. No third door was open in the moment. The choice made — intercept everything entering Jordanian airspace, from any side, while publicly condemning escalation, insisting "Jordan will not become a battleground," and framing the act as defence of Jordanian sovereignty and civilians — is recorded under the clause as the best possible choice available given the situation in the moment, and it is not held against him here. What remains gradable is the trap's origin and the honesty inside it: the exposure exists because Jordan hosts US forces and holds a 1994 peace treaty with Israel — both his own long-standing choices, made openly and defended openly — and the government's public framing (sovereignty, not alliance) was candid about the act even where it could not satisfy the street's reading of it.

Evidence against:

  • 2020, the teachers: pushback answered by dissolution, not adjustment. When the Teachers' Syndicate protested that the government had cancelled the pay rise agreed in October 2019 to end a month-long strike, the response was not renegotiation but the syndicate's closure, its board's arrest, a media gag order and permanent dissolution (UN experts, August 2020; Freedom House, 2021). The wobble — a union saying "you broke the deal" — was answered by abolishing the body that said it.
  • 2023, the Cybercrime Law: the other option was available and was not taken at the time. The draft drew public protest, professional-association opposition and UN Human Rights Council recommendations; it was enacted anyway, in broader form, and Jordan "has yet to review the amendments despite public criticism" (HRW World Report 2026). The king's later statement that implementation would decide revision (above) came after 3,170 prosecutions in the law's first full year — the consideration, if it comes, follows the harm rather than preceding it.
  • 2024–2025, Gaza-era dissent: the street's pushback on Jordan's Israel policy was met with sustained restriction rather than adjustment — detention and harassment of pro-Palestine protesters and online activists continuing through 2025, a protest moratorium in force from spring 2025 on monitors' accounts, and the April 2025 Brotherhood ban with raids on the offices of the kingdom's largest elected party (HRW World Report 2026; EU SEE / Hivos Jordan snapshot, March 2026). Policy did move at the diplomatic margin — Jordan recalled its ambassador from Israel in November 2023 and led aid airdrops over Gaza — but the domestic demand to revisit the treaty itself was refused a hearing and policed.
  • The adjustment pattern has a consistent direction: economic pain moves him (2011 prices, 2018 taxes); challenges to the structure of power do not. Each economic concession was also followed by partial re-imposition — the 2018 tax law returned in amended form within three months; the 2011 constitutional opening was followed by the power-concentrating amendments of 2014–2022.

Why this grade: The record is genuinely two-sided and the two sides sort by subject. On bread-and-butter wobbles — 2011, above all 2018 — he adjusted openly, quickly and at cost to his own government's programme, and in 2018 the adjustment is what ended the crisis. On power wobbles — a union enforcing a deal, a speech law's critics, Gaza-era dissent — he denied, dissolved, prosecuted or banned. The interception file is excluded from the debit side under the no-good-choice clause, for the reasons stated on its shelf. A ruler who hears the street on prices and stops his ears on power earns MIXED, and the split itself is the finding.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — protesters and lower-paid taxpayers in 2018, whose central demand was met and whose exemption was written into the revised law; Jordanian civilians under the 2024–2026 flight paths, protected by interceptions; households in June 2018, when the price freeze was ordered. Hurt — the Teachers' Syndicate's members, whose organisation was abolished for pressing a signed deal; pro-Palestine protesters and online activists detained through 2024–2025; the treasury and, later, taxpayers, who absorbed the freeze's cost (the government put it at USD 20 million for the 2018 freeze alone) and the re-imposed taxes that followed each concession.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Palestinian-origin majority, the East Bank tribes, women, Christians and the smaller minorities, refugees, protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The quota architecture counts the small in by law, and the 2022 law widened it. The 138-seat House elected in 2024 carries reserved seats — nine for Christians, three for Circassians and Chechens, eighteen for women under the new law (up from fifteen) — plus a new national party list of 41 seats with list rules requiring women and under-35 candidates in the top positions, and the candidacy age lowered from 30 to 25 (2024 election law as summarised in EU EOM and election reporting, 2024). The 2022 constitutional amendments also wrote women into the constitution's rights chapter heading — "Rights and duties of Jordanian men and Jordanian women" (European Forum, March 2022).
  • Refugees are counted in fact, at a scale few states have matched. Jordan has hosted successive refugee populations for decades; as of December 2025, 444,000 refugees and asylum-seekers were registered with UNHCR (95% Syrian), some 80.8% living in host communities rather than camps, with access to public education and public health services, general protection from detention and refoulement, and — after the fall of the Assad government — supported voluntary return: over 173,000 registered refugees returned to Syria in 2025, with a further 74,000-plus supported to return in the first half of 2026 (UNHCR Annual Results Report 2025, Jordan; UNHCR Jordan Factsheet, January 2026 and June 2026). The returns are documented as voluntary and UNHCR-supported; where pressure-to-return concerns exist in the analysis literature they are recorded as analysis, not as a documented refoulement finding (SWP Comment, October 2026).
  • The poor are at least partially shielded inside the fiscal programme: the IMF programme the kingdom is implementing (Extended Fund Facility, January 2024) pairs consolidation with protected social spending floors, and the 2018 revised tax law exempted lower-paid individuals from filing (IMF programme documents via EU Commission MFA proposal, August 2025; Amnesty, 2018 review).

Evidence against:

  • The largest community in the country is counted at a discount, by district design. Jordanians of Palestinian origin are widely understood to be at least half the citizen population — the government does not publish the figure — and the electoral system has for decades favoured rural, East Bank tribal districts over the urban, largely Palestinian-origin centres: Amman's second district, containing the Wihdat refugee camp, held over 73,000 registered voters for three seats while Ma'an governorate's roughly 23,000 voters held five (Freedom House, Countries at the Crossroads 2006, documenting the districting; US State Department reporting, 2005, recording seven of 21 ministers and 17 of 110 deputies of Palestinian origin and none of 12 governorships). The 2022 party-list reform dilutes but does not remove the weighting: 97 of 138 seats are still elected in the local districts where the imbalance lives (2024 election law). East Bankers dominate the security services, the senior bureaucracy and the palace's own base — the state is, in the documented summary, "dominated by East Bankers," with Palestinian-origin Jordanians holding "little political representation" (Elections in Jordan, scholarly summary of the system).
  • Women are counted as voters and quota members but not as equal citizens at the root of the family. Under the Nationality Law No. 6 of 1954, a Jordanian man passes citizenship to his children and foreign wife automatically; a Jordanian woman cannot pass it to her children or husband at all. Human Rights Watch documented the consequence in a 64-page report: the non-citizen children of Jordanian mothers — a population advocates and HRW describe in the hundreds of thousands — live as foreigners in their own country, needing permits to reside and work, paying foreigner rates for university, restricted in property and healthcare; a 2014 government "privileges" decision meant to ease this had issued special ID cards to fewer than 20% of those eligible by February 2018 and was never published in the official gazette (HRW, I Just Want Him to Live Like Other Jordanians, April 2018). Jordan maintains its CEDAW reservation on nationality. In the labour market the count is stark: unemployment among Jordanian women rose to 34.8% in the fourth quarter of 2025 even as the male rate fell to 17.2%, and female labour-force participation, around 14%, is among the world's lowest (Department of Statistics figures via MEED, June 2026).
  • The opposition's voters were counted in September 2024 and discounted by April 2025. The IAF's 31 seats made it the largest bloc on Gaza-era anger and economic distress; seven months later its parent movement was banned by ministry announcement, its offices raided, and by July 2025 its sitting MP Yanal Freihat was detained for criticising the ban (CRS, 2025; IDEA, July 2025). The party itself survives (Test 1) — but the voters who chose it learned their choice operates at the palace's tolerance.
  • The economic count falls hardest on those with least. Overall unemployment stood at 21.3–21.4% in 2024–2025 (IMF), youth unemployment above 40%, and refugees in Jordan report poverty at very high rates — one 2025 assessment put 67% of refugees below the poverty line (WFP/UN reporting, 2025–2026). The consolidation that protects the currency is funded substantially by consumption and income taxes on this population, the grievance at the centre of both the 2018 and the 2022 fuel-price (Hirak) protests.

Why this grade: Everyone is counted somewhere in this system — that is what keeps the grade at MIXED. Christians, Circassians, Chechens and women hold guaranteed seats; refugees hold schooling, healthcare and protection from refoulement across decades; the 2024 election counted the opposition's votes honestly. But the counting is weighted at the source: the Palestinian-origin half of the citizenry votes in districts designed to halve its weight, a Jordanian mother's children are foreigners by statute, and the largest bloc's mandate was answered with a ban on its movement. A count that includes everyone but weights the palace's own people double is not a full count.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Christian, Circassian and Chechen communities, whose representation is guaranteed beyond their numbers; women candidates, via quota and list rules; Syrian and other refugees, who received education, healthcare and non-refoulement over fourteen years; Syrian returnees in 2025–2026, whose voluntary return was materially supported. Hurt — Palestinian-origin Jordanians, structurally under-represented in the chamber that taxes them; the non-citizen children of Jordanian women, counted out of citizenship itself; Jordanian women in the labour market (34.8% unemployment, Q4 2025); IAF voters, whose 2024 choice was curtailed in 2025; the unemployed young, above 40%, who are counted in every speech and reached by few programmes.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 7 February 1999, with dates. A hereditary ruler makes no election promises; his word is graded on the pledges he chose to give — in accession-era commitments, discussion papers, royal-committee charters and named national visions.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The political-modernisation sequence — delivered on its stated timetable, so far. In June 2021 the king appointed the Royal Committee to Modernise the Political System and pledged a phased move toward party-based parliamentary government over three electoral cycles. The committee's recommendations became the 2022 Election and Political Parties Laws; the first election under them was held on schedule on 10 September 2024, despite the Gaza war on the border — 41 party-list seats, candidacy at 25, parties permitted on university campuses and protected from membership discrimination for the first time (EU EOM, 2024; KAS country report, 2024). The design's own logic raises the party share in the next two cycles. The pledge was gradual by its terms, and the first step happened when and as described.
  • Elections held, repeatedly, through every crisis. Parliamentary elections were held in 2003, 2007, 2010, 2013, 2016, 2020 and 2024 — including in November 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic and in September 2024 during a regional war. The 2011 promise that elections would follow within four months of any dissolution has been kept in practice: the July 2024 dissolution was followed by a September 2024 vote.
  • Stability and the refugee pledge — kept at cost. The king's standing commitment that Jordan would remain a stable refuge and would not close its border to Syrians fleeing war was honoured from 2011 onward at a documented scale of over 900,000 Syrians protected across the period (SWP, October 2026), and the 1994 peace treaty with Israel — his father's signature, his own inheritance — has been maintained through the Gaza war despite the largest domestic pressure against it since 1994, with Jordan simultaneously running aid airdrops into Gaza and recalling its ambassador (2023–2025 record).
  • The Economic Modernisation Vision — in motion, behind its own curve. Launched under royal patronage in June 2022, the Vision promises one million jobs and USD 41 billion in investment by 2033; its second executive programme (2026–2029, some 180 initiatives across nearly 400 projects) was launched on schedule in 2025, and the IMF programme beneath it is being implemented — deficit narrowing from 7.3% of GDP in 2024 toward a projected 5.4% in 2026, growth 2.6–2.8% (IMF data via MEED, June 2026; EU Commission, 2025). The machinery of the promise is real and running.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The founding promise — democratisation — has receded for 27 years. He began his reign in 1999 promising democratisation and political liberalisation (The New Humanitarian, Year in Review 2005, recording both the promise and the early failure to deliver). In June 2011 he pledged a parliamentary-majority government; fifteen years later the prime minister is still his own former chief of staff, appointed by decree, and governments still fall by royal decision rather than parliamentary vote. He has said he wants to be a constitutional monarch with a parliamentary democracy within ten years (statement recorded in European Forum analysis, March 2022, of the king's expressed aim) — while the 2014, 2016 and 2022 amendments moved formal power the other way, into his sole hands (Carnegie, March 2022). The direction of the constitution and the direction of the promise are opposite, and both are documented.
  • The teachers' pay deal — the seat's word, broken, then the counterparty abolished. The October 2019 agreement ending the teachers' strike promised a 35–50% pay rise; in April 2020 the government cancelled the increase, citing COVID-19; when the syndicate protested the cancellation, it was dissolved (Front Line Defenders UPR submission, 2023; Freedom House, 2021). The cancellation had a stated emergency reason; the dissolution is what converts a broken word into a Test 5 failure rather than a misfortune — the party owed the money was removed from the table.
  • The Vision's numbers are not on their promised path. The EMV requires average growth of about 5.6% to double GDP and create one million jobs by 2033; actual and projected growth is 2.6–2.8% — roughly half the required rate, four years in — and the Vision's pledge to double female labour-force participation from 14% to 28% by 2033 is currently moving backwards on the unemployment measure (MEED, June 2026; IMF). Not yet broken — the horizon is 2033 — but recorded here as off-track on its own arithmetic, so the record can grade it later.
  • The Cybercrime review pledge — outstanding. The king's public statement that the law's implementation would decide its revision, and his direction to review the access-to-information law (Royal Hashemite Court), has produced no documented review or amendment through November 2026; HRW records that Jordan "has yet to review the amendments despite public criticism and recommendations from the UN Human Rights Council" (HRW World Report 2026).

The two axes, named separately (word × outcome): (1) Kept his word AND it helped: the 2024 election held on schedule under the new law — the modernisation timetable kept, and voters, parties and the IAF itself helped by a real count. (2) Kept his word AND it still hurt: the IMF-anchored fiscal consolidation, pledged to donors and delivered as promised across successive programmes — the keeping is credited here; the hurt is recorded in full in Tests 3 and 4 — the 2018 tax-and-prices package that triggered the kingdom's largest protests, and a tax burden carried by a population at 21% unemployment. The promise's content, not its keeping, is what Tests 1 and 4 grade. Why this grade: His word is reliable exactly where it is specific, dated and within his gift — election timetables, committee sequences, treaty maintenance, the refugee door — and unreliable exactly where it is largest: the 1999 and 2011 promise that power would migrate from the palace to a parliament. A reign can be graded on trajectory, and the trajectory of the central promise runs against the trajectory of his constitutional amendments. The specific pledges kept earn real credit; the founding pledge's 27-year recession, the teachers' deal, and the Vision's halved growth rate hold the grade at MIXED.

King Abdullah II has held Jordan's executive seat for more than 27 years — longer than any other ruler in this batch — and the dossier grades a reign, not a season. On one law, the grade is FAILS: the seat is constitutionally unaccountable, the best systematic study (Yom & Monroe, 2025) shows anti-corruption enforcement aimed at petty offenders and timed for political effect, wasta has produced no conviction in fourteen years, and the 2021 sedition case imprisoned a prince's alleged partners for 15 years while the prince himself stayed in family care — helped: petty-level integrity and the palace's standing; hurt: unconnected Jordanians, low-level officials, and Awadallah and bin Zaid.
