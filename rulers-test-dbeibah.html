The Ruler's Test — Libya (a divided seat): Abdul Hamid Dbeibah, Prime Minister of the Government of National Unity

Subject: Libya has no single seat of power, and this dossier grades the division as it actually stands in November 2026 rather than pretending otherwise. Two authorities claim the state. In the west, the Government of National Unity (GNU) in Tripoli, led by Prime Minister Abdul Hamid Dbeibah since March 2021, is the government the United Nations and most states recognise; it holds the capital, the headquarters of the Central Bank and the National Oil Corporation, the oil-revenue accounts, and the western coastal strip where a majority of Libyans — commonly estimated at around two-thirds of a population of roughly seven million — live. In the east and much of the south, the House of Representatives' Government of National Stability (GNS), headed since May 2023 by Osama Hammad in an acting capacity, is the civilian façade of a military authority: real power sits with Field Marshal Khalifa Haftar, commander of the Libyan Arab Armed Forces (LAAF/LNA), whose forces hold the larger share of the land, most oil fields and export terminals, and whose sons now occupy the structure beneath him — Saddam Haftar as Deputy General Commander (appointed August 2025 and widely read as heir apparent), Khaled Haftar as Chief of Staff, and Belqasim Haftar as head of the Libya Development and Reconstruction Fund. The seat graded here is Dbeibah's: the premiership of the recognised government, governing the largest share of the country's people and its treasury. That is a statement about where governing power over the most Libyans sits, not about legitimacy, and the rival claim is printed at full strength throughout: Haftar's eastern authority is a functioning counter-seat with its own record on every test, and where a test's evidence runs through the east, the eastern record is graded in the open inside that test rather than waved past. Three corrections to the brief's framing are recorded. First, the eastern government is not "around Haftar" as a court is around a king — it is formally headed by Hammad and the House of Representatives, and Haftar's supremacy over both is the documented fact that the grading has to name. Second, the House itself changed hands during grading: on 6 October 2026, after amending its internal rules to limit the speakership to a single one-year term, the House elected Al-Salihin Abdel Nabi al-Obeidi speaker, ending Aguila Saleh's twelve years in the post; Saleh disputes the session's legality, and the dispute is recorded as contested. Third, the division is now formally negotiating its own end: on 30 August 2026, representatives of the GNU and of Haftar's forces signed the "4+4" (Smaller Convening) agreement — elections within 24 months, a reconstituted elections commission board, and a single unified executive — under UNSMIL auspices, an agreement the Security Council has since welcomed (resolution 2835, 30 October 2026) and which the High Council of State voted to reject on 5 October 2026. Implementation had not begun at grading. Dbeibah is graded on five years and eight months in office; Haftar's eastern record is graded across the same period, with his longer file admitted where it bears directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that the seat passes, then the strongest evidence that it fails. Every test closes with a Why in plain words and a helped/hurt ledger naming who bore the acts on each shelf; a grade with no receipts means nothing, and no grade in this dossier is stated without them. The mandate rule applies: governing on the platform a ruler was sent to deliver is never itself a failure — carrying out a mandate is evidence on the for-shelf of Test 5 — and marks are lost only for how it is done: a second law for their own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not choose them (Test 4). Test 3 also grades whether, when another option became available, the ruler at least considered it openly; considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence, and refusing to look at it is. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are separate axes and are printed independently — a kept word that hurt people earns its Test 5 credit and carries its hurt in the ledger in full, and a broken word that helped people is recorded the same way; neither launders the other. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, or none that appeared to be at the time, the dossier says so and grades it as the best possible choice available in the moment — naming the options that actually existed, why each carried harm, whether the ruler built the trap earlier, whether they were honest inside it, and whether they took the least-harm door actually open; hindsight options do not count as options refused. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution service reached into the GNU's own cabinet, repeatedly, and the GNU did not stop it. Attorney General Siddiq al-Sour's office prosecuted Education Minister Musa al-Magharief over the school-textbook contracting scandal to a court conviction and prison sentence; on 1 November 2025 it arrested his successor-but-one, Acting Education Minister Ali al-Abed Abu Azoum — a Dbeibah appointee of May 2025 — together with the director general of the Educational Curricula and Research Center, in the same textbook file, after the Audit Bureau subjected the contracts to concurrent audit (Office of the Attorney General statement, 1 November 2025; Audit Bureau statement, 31 October 2025). In October 2026 the same office detained ten officials of the water and wastewater sector, including the chairman of the General Water and Sanitation Company, over pipeline contracts worth more than 16 million dinars flagged by the Audit Bureau (Attorney General's office via Libya Review, October 2026). These are detentions and charges — ALLEGATIONs until verdicts — but the direction of travel is documented: the law's arm in the west lands on the government's own ministers, not only on its enemies.
  • A jailer of the government's own security family was convicted in the government's own courts. Ossama Anjiem (al-Masri), who ran the Tripoli branch of the Reform and Rehabilitation Institution — the prison network attached to the government-backed Special Deterrence Force (Rada) — was convicted by the Tripoli Criminal Court on 21 June 2026 and sentenced to seven years and four months for violating inmates' rights through torture, cruelty and degrading treatment (Attorney General's office statement, June 2026; Associated Press, June 2026). The conviction covers a fraction of the conduct in the ICC's January 2025 arrest warrant against him — murder, torture, rape and sexual violence, which remain ALLEGATIONs before The Hague — but a Libyan court, in the capital his force policed, jailed him.
  • One prosecution service operated across the line of division in the Derna file. After the September 2023 dam collapses killed more than 4,300 people (UN figure; over 8,000 missing), the Attorney General's office prosecuted the dam-management case in Derna itself, and on 28 July 2024 a Derna court convicted twelve officials and employees, with sentences of nine to twenty-seven years (Attorney General's office statement via AFP, 28 July 2024). The dead were eastern; the prosecutors were the national office seated in Tripoli; the court sat in Derna. Whatever else is divided, that file was handled as one country's law.
  • In April 2026 the two legislative chambers approved the first unified national budget since 2013 — 190 billion dinars, with 73 billion for salaries and 37 billion for subsidies across both zones, supervised by the Central Bank — ending, on paper, thirteen years of parallel fiscal orders in which each government spent from the same treasury by its own lights (Reuters, 11 April 2026; Central Bank of Libya statement, April 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The state itself has two sources, and each mints its own exceptions. The GNU governs under a mandate the House of Representatives reads as expired in December 2021; the east is governed by a government the GNU does not recognise, funded by parliamentary allocations — including nearly $11 billion over three years to the Development and Reconstruction Fund — that have never been matched by published accounts (BBC investigation, November 2026). Oversight itself is split: the US State Department's country reporting records that divisions in the Central Bank and the Audit Bureau between east and west limited transparency and oversight (OSAC country security report, April 2025). One law for all is not available to grade where there are two fountains of law; what can be graded is what each seat does inside its own zone and at the seams, and at the seams both seats built private channels.
  • The GNU's own cabinet endorsed the clearest private channel in the file. In 2023 the National Oil Corporation signed a production-sharing agreement with Arkenu Oil Company giving it a share of output from two of Libya's largest fields; a UN Panel of Experts report in March 2026 found the arrangement had moved more than $3 billion to bank accounts outside Libya. Arkenu states its production and proceeds were handled in accordance with its NOC agreement. Reporting and Libyan political actors have alleged connections between the deal and Ibrahim Dbeibah, the prime minister's nephew and adviser, and Saddam Haftar; a GNU official denied Ibrahim Dbeibah had any connection with Arkenu, pointing to its commercial registration, and no court has ruled on any individual's role — the personal links are ALLEGATIONs, denied, and recorded as such. What is documented is narrower and sufficient: the deal was endorsed by the GNU's own Council of Ministers, it ran outside the ordinary revenue path the unified budget was later built to restore, and it took a UN experts' report and public revolt — including from a military council in Dbeibah's own Misrata — to move the government against it (Reuters, 24 November 2026; UN Panel of Experts reporting, March 2026).
  • The fuel economy runs as a second, unwritten law in both zones. Libya spends nearly $1 billion a month importing fuel (SRSG Tetteh to the Security Council, 2026), much of it subsidised to a pump price of 0.15 dinars a litre and smuggled back out at multiples of that price — a rent distributed by connection and armed protection rather than by any published rule, documented for years by UN panels and by The Sentry's Libya's Kleptocratic Boom investigations, which name networks in both camps (The Sentry, 2024–2025). Neither seat has dismantled its own side's share of that economy.
  • The eastern seat's record on this test is worse, and is printed at full strength because it defines what "one law" is up against. Amnesty International's December 2022 briefing on the Tariq Ben Zeyad (TBZ) armed group — effectively led by Saddam Haftar — documented unlawful killings, torture, enforced disappearance, rape and forced displacement committed "with no fear of consequences," and called for criminal investigation of its commanders' responsibility; no TBZ commander has been prosecuted in the east (Amnesty International, We Are Your Masters, December 2022). The Development and Reconstruction Fund's head, Belqasim Haftar, denies corruption allegations against the fund, states it is independently audited and paid through the Central Bank, and has not produced the audit reports despite agreeing to requests for them (BBC, November 2026). A law that cannot reach the commander's son or open the fund's books is not one law in the east, whatever its text says.

Why: The grade is MIXED, and it is the most contested MIXED in this dossier. What holds it up is real: a prosecution service that arrests the prime minister's own education ministers, a court in the capital that jailed a state-force prison chief, a cross-division prosecution after Derna, and a unified budget that — for one year, on paper — put both governments under a single fiscal rule. What drags it down is structural: the source itself is doubled, each seat keeps an insider channel the written law does not govern (Arkenu and the fuel rents in the west; the unpublished fund accounts and TBZ's impunity in the east), and the GNU's one-law record is strongest exactly where an institution it does not command — the Attorney General — does the work. The eastern counter-seat, graded on the same evidence inside this test, would FAIL it: in the east the exceptions are not channels beside the law but the family's own institutions.

Helped: Libyan schoolchildren and textbook-procurement plaintiffs of the audit process, in whose name two education ministers were detained; Derna's survivors, who saw twelve officials convicted rather than none; public employees and subsidy recipients in both zones, whose salaries and subsidies were brought under one published budget in April 2026; inmates of the Mitiga prison network, whose jailer was convicted in a Libyan court. Hurt: Taxpayers and the national treasury, by the Arkenu channel's $3 billion-plus offshore movement (UN Panel of Experts figure; the company's compliance position recorded above) and by the continuing fuel-smuggling rent in both zones; eastern Libyans living under TBZ's documented impunity, for whom no prosecution has ever come; Derna's survivors again, on the other side of the ledger — the convictions stopped at dam management, and no senior political figure of either camp has faced a court for the decades of neglect behind the collapse, a responsibility The Sentry's investigation lays partly at the door of the Dabaiba family's Gaddafi-era public-works apparatus and partly at the eastern authorities who held the dams in 2023 — stated here as an investigative ALLEGATION against both houses, untested in any court.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The referees who graded hardest against the GNU kept their posts and their voices. Attorney General al-Sour, whose office arrested GNU ministers (Test 1), was neither removed nor publicly disciplined by the government he was prosecuting; the Audit Bureau published the textbook and water-sector findings that triggered the arrests, including findings embarrassing to the government that funds it (Audit Bureau statements, October 2025 and October 2026). The High National Elections Commission (HNEC) ran three phases of municipal elections across 2024–2026 — in some municipalities the first votes since 2014 — at turnouts of 68–72 per cent, and certified results in which independents outperformed established blocs, without documented GNU interference in the count (HNEC results statements, 2025–2026; SRSG Tetteh's briefing to the Security Council, 21 August 2025).
  • The courts in the capital ruled against a pillar of the security establishment the government depends on: the Tripoli Criminal Court's conviction of Rada's prison chief al-Masri (Test 1) was a judgment against the armed formation that controlled the capital's airport and main prison, delivered while that formation was still armed (Attorney General's office, June 2026).
  • Critical media in the west operates, names ministers, and survives its own exposés — the Sada newspaper's Audit Bureau documents on the Economy Ministry were published, and when its editor was detained the case became a national scandal rather than a quiet disappearance (below).

Evidence against:

  • The central bank's independence was ended twice in twenty-six months, once by the GNU's own camp and once by force in the GNU's own capital. In August 2024 the Presidential Council — the GNU's partner in the 2021 executive, aligned with and defended by the government — dismissed Governor Sadiq al-Kabir by decree, although the Libyan Political Agreement reserves changes to sovereign-institution heads to the two legislative chambers acting together; Kabir fled the country saying he feared militia attack, eastern forces shut down oil production in answer (output fell from about 1.2 million to roughly 350,000–450,000 barrels a day), and the crisis closed only when a UN-brokered consensus appointed Naji Issa in September–October 2024 (Presidential Council decree, 18 August 2024; UN-brokered agreement, 26 September 2024; Kabir's statements, September 2024). In 2026 the sequel ran the other way: Issa tendered his resignation in August, saying he could no longer continue, after protesting that both governments were overspending against the US-brokered unified spending framework; the House presidency accepted the resignation on 22 October — the same day the Central Bank reported unidentified men had entered its Tripoli headquarters and installed board member Fawzi Abu Khzam as interim governor, in a handover the bank first condemned, holding the Ministry of Defence among those responsible, and then, in a deleted-and-replaced statement, described as friendly; the House formalised the appointment on 2 November 2026 by 78 of 90 votes in a session at Sebha, without the High Council of State's endorsement, which the Agreement's procedure requires. Issa states he had retracted his resignation and that it was never lawfully accepted — the lawfulness of the succession is CONTESTED between Issa and the House, and is recorded as such. Africa Intelligence reported on 5 November 2026 that the US Treasury had suspended dollar transactions with the bank. Through all of it, the GNU — whose prime minister is also defence minister, in the capital where the bank was entered — issued no public objection and took no documented step (Central Bank statements, 22 October 2026; Reuters and Libya Herald reporting, November 2026). A referee was taken by armed entry in the seat's own city, and the seat's answer was silence.
  • The media referee was bruised, in a documented case with a named victim. On 11 July 2024 the Internal Security Agency arrested Ahmed al-Sanussi, owner of the Sada economic newspaper and host of the Flosna programme, a day after his outlet published Audit Bureau documents on corruption in the Ministry of Economy and Trade; he was held three days at an undisclosed location, without stated reason, following a complaint by the Economy Minister, and released after Libyan and international outcry (Committee to Protect Journalists, July 2024; UNSMIL statement, July 2024). The release shows the limit of the grip; the arrest shows its existence.
  • The GNU's hold on its own capital is a tenancy, not a grip — which cuts both ways on this test. Until September 2025 the Rada force, not the state, controlled Mitiga airport and the capital's main prison; the SSA ran Abu Salim as a fief until its commander was killed in May 2025 (Test 3). A government that cannot grip its referees because it does not fully hold its own ground is not the same as a government that holds and refrains — and the distinction matters to the grade: the autonomy of the west's courts and prosecutors is partly the autonomy of a fragmented field, not solely the restraint of the seat.
  • The eastern counter-seat grips as a system, and its record is printed at full strength. The LAAF's senior command is the commander's family: Saddam Haftar was appointed Deputy General Commander on 14 August 2025 to a post that did not exist until the House amended the army law days later to create it and to let him succeed his father; Khaled Haftar became Chief of Staff in the same week; Belqasim Haftar holds the reconstruction fund and its billions; the House ratified each step unanimously (Atlantic Council analysis, September 2025; House of Representatives sessions, August 2025). The GNS prime minister has served in an acting capacity since 2023 under that structure. In July and August 2025 the eastern authorities suspended municipal elections in 27 municipalities — including Benghazi, Sabha, Sirte and Tobruk — withdrawing voting materials on election morning in sixteen of them, a direct hand on the electoral referee that the SRSG publicly urged them to lift (SRSG Tetteh, Security Council briefing, 21 August 2025). The one institutional motion in the east's recent record — the House voting out Speaker Saleh after twelve years, under a new one-year term limit, in October 2026 — happened inside that system, was disputed by Saleh as unlawful, and was read by regional analysts as weakening the speakership within the ruling coalition rather than loosening the coalition's grip (Libya Tribune/Minbar reporting, October 2026); it is recorded as motion, not as independence.

Why: MIXED — the weakest MIXED in this dossier, held from FAILS by three referees who demonstrably kept their autonomy in the west: a prosecution service that jails the government's ministers, an audit bureau that publishes against its funder, and an elections commission that ran honest local counts. It is held from anything higher by the bank. The test names five referees; the central bank is the one that holds the country's money, and its independence was extinguished twice — by decree from the prime minister's own camp in 2024, and by armed entry in his capital in 2026, unopposed and uncommented by his government. A seat does not have to swing the club itself to fail the bank's part of this test; standing silent in its own capital while the referee is carried out is a position, and the record grades it as one. The eastern seat, on its own evidence inside this test, FAILS outright: there, the grip is not an episode but the constitution of the regime — command, parliament, government and reconstruction money arranged inside one family, with even municipal ballots suspended when they were not wanted.

Helped: Voters in the municipalities that did vote in 2024–2026, who got real counts and seated councils; readers of the west's critical press, whose outlets kept publishing; the Attorney General's and Audit Bureau's own institutional standing, strengthened by being allowed to bite. Hurt: Ahmed al-Sanussi, detained three days for publishing an audit report; Sadiq al-Kabir and the bank staff who fled in 2024, and Naji Issa in 2026 — two successive governors removed outside the agreed procedure, the second stating on the record that his resignation had been retracted; every Libyan holding dinars, whose currency's credibility is the bank's independence priced daily (official rate about 6.5 to the dollar against a parallel rate above 10.5 in November 2026 — a premium of roughly 64 per cent, Central Bank and market reporting, November 2026); voters in the 27 eastern and southern municipalities whose local elections were suspended by the authorities over them; and the High Council of State, written out of a sovereign appointment the founding agreement reserves to both chambers.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

Lets the ones who steady it move it, openly — adjusts rather than denies. When another option becomes available, do they at least consider it openly?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • May–September 2025 is the strongest adjustment sequence in the file, and it began in blood. On 12 May 2025 the Stabilization Support Authority's commander, Abdel Ghani al-Kikli ("Gheniwa"), was killed at the 444th Brigade's headquarters during a meeting convened amid rising tension between the GNU's brigades and the SSA — who ordered or intended the killing remains an open and contested question, with no official investigation findings published; what followed is documented: GNU-aligned forces seized every SSA base in Tripoli within hours, fighting with Rada killed at least eight civilians and wounded more than seventy (UNSMIL and OHCHR reporting, May 2025), protests demanding Dbeibah's resignation filled Martyrs' Square, and three ministers resigned. Dbeibah's documented answers were: a televised address acknowledging that armed groups had grown stronger than the state itself; decrees dissolving the SSA outright and disbanding the Directorate for Combating Illegal Migration; and then, when his forces moved on Rada's positions at Mitiga in September 2025, acceptance of a negotiated agreement under which Rada withdrew from the airport and handed its prisons to the judicial authorities — an option, taken openly, that avoided a second battle for the capital (GNU decrees, May 2025; UNSMIL statements, May and September 2025). The no-good-choice clause applies to this sequence and is applied by its mechanics below.
  • The Arkenu file (Test 1) shows the same motion under economic pressure: confronted with a UN experts' report, street opposition and a public challenge from a Misrata military council in his own base city, Dbeibah in April 2026 instructed the authorities to end the arrangement his own cabinet had endorsed, and by November 2026 his government had filed suit in a Tripoli court to annul the agreement — correcting through a court filing, in the open, rather than by decree or denial (Reuters, April and 24 November 2026).
  • The 4+4 agreement is this test's largest open item, and the other-option rule is applied to it directly. In August 2026 another option became available — a signed path whose stated endpoint is a unified executive authority and national elections within 24 months, which is to say the end of Dbeibah's own interim government. Dbeibah's documented response was to consider it openly and endorse it: GNU representatives signed on 30 August 2026, and in November 2026 he repeated his support for the UN roadmap in person to the Secretary-General. Notably, he did this against the stated position of his own camp's second chamber: the High Council of State voted on 5 October 2026 to reject the agreement's outcomes. The signature is a word, graded in Test 5 as in motion; for this test, the considering is the evidence, and it is documented (4+4 agreement text and UNSMIL statements, August–October 2026; GNU statements, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The founding refusal. In February 2022, when the House of Representatives named Fathi Bashagha prime minister, Dbeibah refused to hand over, announced he would surrender power only to an elected government, and called his supporters into the streets in his defence. His position is printed at full strength: the HoR's confidence procedures were themselves contested, Bashagha's mandate rested on the same expired transitional framework, and surrendering to a parliament-appointed government without elections would itself have broken the transition's stated purpose. The counter-record is also printed at full strength: the LPDF roadmap under which Dbeibah took office in March 2021 set his government's life to end at the December 2021 elections; when those elections collapsed, the refusal converted an interim premiership into an open-ended one, and the dual-government split that has defined Libya since dates from that refusal, not from the east's ambition alone. When the wobble was his own removal, the answer was not adjustment but entrenchment — and every subsequent wobble has been answered from inside the seat that refusal preserved.
  • July–August 2026: the electricity collapse. After roughly two years without scheduled load-shedding — a genuine GNU delivery, recorded in Test 5 — the grid failed in a heatwave, with blackouts of fourteen to twenty hours a day at temperatures near 47°C. Protesters in Tripoli, Zawiya and Misrata escalated within days from power cuts to demanding the government's fall, prosecutions for corruption and action on the collapsing dinar; protesters stormed the Mellitah gas complex and shut it and two fields, and government forces retook the complex by force. Dbeibah's documented adjustments were managerial: an emergency committee, public blame directed at the electricity company's leadership as corrupt and failing, and a promise of a 1,350-megawatt plant. The High Council of State held his government institutionally responsible and asked the prosecutor to investigate the sector; the "Kafa" ("Enough") protests of 18 August 2026 demanded the government's resignation. No minister fell, no inquiry with published findings followed, and the political demand — his departure — was met with force at the plant gate and silence at the seat (Libya Review, Libya Observer and Maghrebi reporting, July–August 2026; HCS statement, August 2026).
  • Two security files from the summer of 2026 sit open without documented answers from either seat: the 8 August 2026 drone strike that destroyed a power station and a thirteen-million-litre fuel tank at the Zawiya refinery — attribution CONTESTED, with eastern-aligned reporting directing suspicion at the GNU and the government publishing no investigation findings — and the 10 August 2026 car-bomb assassination in Benghazi of Fawzi al-Mansouri, the LNA's military-intelligence chief, unsolved and unclaimed (Libya Today News and regional reporting, August 2026). A wobble answered with no visible inquiry at all is recorded as such for both seats.
  • The eastern counter-seat's record on this test is mixed in its own right and printed at full strength. For it: Haftar accepted the October 2020 ceasefire after his Tripoli offensive failed, and has not renewed a general offensive in six years — an adjustment, though one made under military compulsion; the east also absorbed the October 2026 speakership change without violence. Against it: when the wobble came from below — municipal voters in 2025 — the answer was suspension (Test 2); when it came from within parliament in 2019, in the person of MP Siham Sergiwa, the answer is graded in Test 4.

Why: MIXED. The seat demonstrably adjusts: it dissolved a militia fief after the May 2025 bloodshed rather than defending it, chose a negotiated handover at Mitiga over a second capital battle, reversed its own endorsed oil deal through a court when the evidence and the street moved, and signed — against its own second chamber's vote — an agreement whose endpoint is its own replacement. It also demonstrably refuses: the one adjustment it has never made, in five years, is the adjustment of its own tenure, and the summer of 2026 showed the pattern at full length — managerial fixes and force at the plant, no political answer to a population sitting in the dark demanding the government's fall. The other-option rule is met on the 4+4 signature and failed on the 2022 refusal; both are the same man, and the grade holds both.

Helped: Tripoli's residents, spared a second militia war in September 2025 by the Mitiga negotiation; Abu Salim's residents, whose SSA fief was dissolved rather than re-legitimised; the national treasury and oil-revenue transparency, by the Arkenu reversal — if the court completes it; the transition process itself, by a sitting prime minister's signature on a path that ends his government. Hurt: The at-least-eight civilians killed and seventy-plus wounded in the May 2025 fighting that preceded the adjustments — adjustments bought after the blood, not before; Zawiya's and Misrata's protesters, whose documented demands in 2026 were answered with force at Mellitah and no political concession; Fathi Bashagha's supporters and, more durably, every Libyan governed since 2022 by the dual-state structure the refusal entrenched; the Zawiya refinery's workers and the Benghazi public, left with two major attacks and no published investigation from either seat.

No-good-choice clause — applied, by its mechanics: May 2025. The options actually on the table when al-Kikli was killed were three: move immediately on the SSA and Rada and risk a full militia war inside the capital; move on neither and leave armed sovereignty untouched, as every predecessor had; or move on the SSA, whose commander was already dead, and negotiate Rada down. Each carried documented harm — the first option's harm is measured in the eight civilian dead the partial version still produced; the second's harm was the standing one of a capital policed by fiefs. The trap's origin is graded honestly: the militia system predates Dbeibah (Rada and the SSA were built between 2016 and 2021 under the previous unity government and Presidential Council), but his government funded, formalised and governed through those same formations for four years, so the origin is shared, not clean. Inside the trap he was publicly honest about the state's weakness, and he took the least-harm door that was actually open — dissolution where dissolution was possible, negotiation where it was not. The sequence is therefore not held against him as a failure of this test; what is held is the four years of governing through the fiefs before the trap closed, graded in Tests 1 and 2. The 2024 central-bank crisis, by contrast, does not qualify for the clause's protection on the GNU side: the trap — a dismissed governor, a fleeing banker, an oil shutdown — was built by the Presidential Council's own decree, and a party does not earn the clause for a fire it lit; the eastern oil shutdown that answered it is likewise graded as a choice of leverage, not a necessity. Haftar's 2020 ceasefire qualifies in the moment — accepting the ceasefire was the least-harm door after the offensive failed — with its origin recorded in full: the offensive itself, launched in April 2019 during the UN Secretary-General's visit to Tripoli, was his construction (Test 5).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Including those who share their streets and the ones the count usually skips — the displaced, the detained, the foreign, the defeated.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Where Libyans were allowed to count themselves, the GNU's zone ran the count and published it. Across the 2024–2026 municipal phases, turnout reached about 71 per cent where voting was held — the highest since 2014 — women won 205 council seats and two mayoralties, and councils were seated in municipalities that had not voted in a decade (HNEC statements; SRSG briefings, 2025–2026). The one genuinely national system, the Central Bank payroll, pays some 2.3 million public employees in both zones without a loyalty test, and the April 2026 unified budget carried salaries and subsidies to the east on the same lines as the west (Central Bank reporting, 2026).
  • A jailer was counted against, in the open: al-Masri's June 2026 conviction (Tests 1–2) is also this test's evidence — the inmates of a state prison, among the least counted people in the country, saw their torturer sentenced by name in a Libyan court, even as the wider ICC file against him remains outstanding (Attorney General's office, June 2026).
  • The eastern seat's strongest counting evidence is Derna's housing: the Reconstruction Fund delivered free replacement apartments to flood survivors — some 2,000 units documented by November 2026 — and survivors interviewed by the BBC expressed documented gratitude for speed no western institution matched after any disaster (BBC, November 2026). It is printed at full strength, beside its other side in the ledger below.

Evidence against:

  • The February 2026 joint UNSMIL–OHCHR report on migrants, refugees and asylum seekers (covering January 2024–December 2025) is the heaviest single document in this dossier, and it covers both zones. It finds systematic violations — killings, torture, sexual violence, trafficking, arbitrary detention, collective expulsion and abandonment in the desert — operating as a business model run by traffickers and state-affiliated actors, with detention itself a revenue stream and impunity the norm. Its documented particulars include some 13,783 people intercepted at the Tunisian border and handed to Libyan border guards between June 2023 and December 2025 (IOM figures), 463 people deported to Niger in July 2024 and more than 1,400 in the first half of 2025, and, in the east's Kufra region in February 2025, two mass graves from which 93 bodies were exhumed by 19 February — some bearing gunshot wounds — in the same operation in which eastern security forces freed 76 migrants and arrested three suspects, a sequence printed whole: the east's forces both uncovered the graves and police the territory in which the graves were dug (UNSMIL–OHCHR joint report, 17 February 2026). The GNU's own Directorate for Combating Illegal Migration ran the western detention estate inside this system until its dissolution in May 2025; the abuses documented against its facilities predate and postdate individual commanders. Neither seat has prosecuted a senior figure of its own migration apparatus. In June 2026 the GNS formally banned the entry of Sudanese, Eritrean, Ethiopian and Somali nationals, and in October 2026 of Iranian and Afghan nationals — counting-out enacted as policy by the eastern government (GNS decisions, June and October 2026).
  • The east's count excludes its own citizens by category. The 27 suspended municipalities (Test 2) include Benghazi, the country's second city: several million eastern and southern Libyans were denied even a local vote by the authorities governing them, in the same years the west was seating councils at 71 per cent turnout. And the disappearance of MP Siham Sergiwa stands as the east's emblematic uncounted person: abducted from her Benghazi home on 17 July 2019 by masked gunmen after publicly criticising the Tripoli offensive — her husband shot, her son beaten, "the army is a red line" left graffitied on her house — she has never been found, no investigation has produced a public finding, and the LNA denies responsibility; the denial is recorded, and so is the seven-year absence of any answer (Amnesty International and UNSMIL reporting, 2019–2026; her fate remains unknown at grading).
  • The south is counted by neither seat. Fezzan's towns are policed by whichever armed formation holds them, its activists detained by security agencies answerable to Tripoli or Benghazi (Libyan Crimes Watch documented the July 2024 arrest of a Qatrun activist by the Internal Security Agency), and Kufra's Sudanese refugee population — swollen by Sudan's war to a size local reporting says exceeds the town's own residents — is administered as a smuggling problem rather than counted as a protected population (Minbar Libya reporting, 2025–2026).
  • Derna's other ledger: the dead were buried in mass graves marked by numbers, DNA identification of remains remained incomplete three years on, and the reconstruction that housed the living proceeded without published accounts (Test 1) — the survivors were counted as recipients, not as citizens with a right to the books (BBC and International Crisis Group reporting, 2024–2026).

Why: FAILS — for the graded seat, and the counter-seat's record on the same test is worse. The test asks whether everyone inside the territory counts, and the February 2026 UN report answers for both zones in the same sentence: the people least able to make themselves counted — migrants in detention, refugees in the desert borderlands, the expelled — are processed by a system in which state-affiliated actors profit and no senior figure of either government has answered. The GNU's for-shelf is genuine and is printed in full: real local elections, a universal payroll, a convicted jailer. It does not reach the test's centre, because the test's centre is precisely the people who cannot vote in those elections or draw that payroll. A seat that counts its own citizens carefully and counts the foreigner in its cells as revenue has drawn the circle where the test forbids drawing it. The eastern seat draws it tighter still — its own citizens' local ballots suspended, a sitting MP vanished unanswered for seven years — and that is recorded here without lifting the graded seat one point: another seat's worse circle is context, not credit.

Helped: Municipal voters across the west and the east's permitted towns, and the 205 women who took council seats through those counts; public employees in both zones, paid through one payroll; Derna's rehoused survivors; the 76 migrants freed in the Kufra raid of February 2025. Hurt: The migrants, refugees and asylum seekers of the UN's February 2026 report — detained, tortured, trafficked, expelled and abandoned under both authorities, including the 93 dead exhumed at Kufra and the thousands deported to Niger; Sudanese refugees at Kufra, administered as a security problem; the several million residents of the 27 suspended eastern and southern municipalities, uncounted even locally; Siham Sergiwa and her family, waiting seven years for an answer no authority has given; Derna's dead, numbered in mass graves, their identification unfinished; political detainees in both zones — thousands held arbitrarily on the UN's and the US State Department's reporting — whose names appear in no count either seat publishes.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Promises made in the open against the record that followed — the dated word against the dated act.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The salary word was kept, every month, through everything: the GNU paid the public payroll across the whole country — including the salaries of the eastern administration that denies its legitimacy — through currency crises, an oil shutdown and a bank seizure, and the April 2026 unified budget formalised the commitment at 73 billion dinars (Central Bank reporting, 2021–2026). For the 2.3 million households on that payroll, this is the promise that organises life, and it has not been broken.
  • The electricity word was kept for two years before it failed: after inheriting scheduled load-shedding as a fact of Libyan summers, the GNU delivered roughly two years without it before the July 2026 collapse (Test 3) — a kept service promise with an expiry the government did not choose to announce (Libya Review and regional reporting, 2024–2026).
  • One sentence of Dbeibah's has been kept to the letter for five years: "I will hand over power only to an elected government." He has not handed power to any unelected successor. The keeping is documented; what the keeping did is graded in the axes below, because this dossier's two axes are independent and this is the file's clearest crossing of them.
  • The 4+4 signature (30 August 2026) is a new dated word — elections within 24 months, a unified executive — recorded here as in motion, neither kept nor broken at grading; its deadline falls due in 2028 and the dossier series will grade it then.

Evidence against:

  • The pledge not to run. To be selected by the Libyan Political Dialogue Forum in February 2021, Dbeibah committed — verbally and in writing, under the Forum's roadmap rules — that he would not stand in the elections his government existed to organise. In November 2021 he filed as a presidential candidate. Stephanie Williams, the UN Secretary-General's former adviser on Libya who ran the process, stated the conclusion on the record in 2025: "He lied. He did lie." The elections then collapsed in December 2021 amid disputes over the electoral laws and candidate eligibility — a collapse with many authors, recorded as such — but the pledge's breach preceded the collapse and is not explained by it (LPDF roadmap and candidacy records, 2021; Williams, Brookings Global Reordering podcast, 2025).
  • The mandate itself. The single dated purpose for which the GNU was created was elections on 24 December 2021. Five years on there has been no national election of any kind under this government; the June 2022 election date Dbeibah proposed in February 2022 passed unheld; successive roadmaps — his own, the Bathily initiative, the Structured Dialogue of December 2025–June 2026 with its roughly 600 recommendations — have each been received with public welcome and none has been carried to a ballot by the government that alone could call one. The Structured Dialogue's participants, some 120 Libyans, delivered their recommendations in June 2026; the 4+4 track that superseded them was negotiated between the governments and the armed camps, not adopted from the Dialogue's text (UNSMIL reporting, 2025–2026).
  • The dinar word, in its practical form. No Libyan government promises an exchange rate in so many words; what the GNU promised, repeatedly, was economic stabilisation, and the record is two devaluations (April 2025 and January 2026), an official rate near 6.5 against a parallel rate above 10.5, a fiscal deficit the IMF put near 30 per cent of GDP for 2025, public debt at 146 per cent of GDP, and a Central Bank reporting a $4.7 billion foreign-exchange deficit in the first nine months of 2026 while recording, in the same period, oil revenues above $15 billion for the half-year — a combination the ousted Governor Issa attributed, in his August 2026 resignation, to both governments' overspending against the unified framework they had signed (IMF Article IV reporting, 2026; Central Bank statements, 2026; Issa's resignation as reported, August 2026). The stabilisation promise failed in the only place it is cashed: the market price of bread's inputs.
  • The eastern counter-seat's word record, at full strength. Against it stands the plainest broken word in this dossier: in February 2019, at Abu Dhabi, Haftar reached an understanding with Prime Minister Sarraj on elections and unified institutions; on 4 April 2019, with the UN Secretary-General in Tripoli for the national conference that understanding was meant to feed, he launched the offensive on the capital instead (UN and contemporaneous reporting, 2019). For it stand two kept positions of real weight: the October 2020 ceasefire has held for six years — no renewed general offensive, whatever leverage (oil blockades in 2022 and 2024) replaced it — and the east's reconstruction doctrine, stated by Belqasim Haftar to the BBC in terms that are themselves a word-position: "I have never made promises to the people. But I have provided them with what they need." The flats are documented (Test 4); the accounts that would let the provided be verified are not (Test 1). A seat that makes no promises cannot break them, and this dossier records that as the east's chosen shelter from this test — and records, beside it, that the one promise the east's commander did make in writing, at Abu Dhabi, was broken within six weeks by an army on the march.

Why: FAILS. The test is the dated word against the dated act, and this seat's two most specific, most solemn, most consequential commitments both failed on the record: the written pledge not to run, broken within nine months, and the election date that was the government's entire reason to exist, now five years past with no ballot held. The for-shelf is not decoration — the payroll never missed, the lights stayed on for two years, and the 4+4 signature may yet redeem the file — but a word test is graded on the words that bound the seat's own power first, because those are the words a ruler cannot blame on weather. Both of those words were his to keep, and he did not keep them. The eastern seat's word fails differently — one written understanding broken by an offensive, then a deliberate retirement into promiselessness — and is recorded as a fail of this test in its own column inside this dossier, not as a shelter.

The two axes, named: This file contains the series' textbook crossing #2 — kept his word, and the keeping hurt: "only to an elected government" has been honoured to the letter for five years, and the honouring is itself the mechanism by which an unelected interim government became permanent; the word is credited here, and the hurt — a transition frozen at the moment of its keeping — is carried in the hurt ledger in full, neither cancelling the other. It also contains crossing #1 — kept his word, and it helped: the payroll, paid to friend and opponent's employee alike, month after month. And the pledge-not-to-run is crossing #4 — broke his word, and it hurt: the breach bought one candidacy that never reached a ballot and cost the process the trust on which the December 2021 elections depended. No instance of crossing #3 (broke his word, and it helped) is documented for this seat; the east's promiselessness is not crossing #3 but an exit from the board the test measures, and is graded as such above.

Helped: The 2.3 million public employees and their households, east and west, whose salaries arrived through five years of division — the kept word that most Libyans can feel; electricity consumers, for the two years the grid held; Derna's survivors, by the east's delivered housing, which is credited here because this test's ledger follows acts, not camps. Hurt: The Libyan electorate entire — promised a vote on 24 December 2021, still waiting in November 2026, and asked in the meantime to treat the wait itself as fidelity; the LPDF process and every Libyan who organised for those elections in good faith; dinar holders and import-dependent households, by the stabilisation word's failure in prices; the victims of the April 2019 offensive, launched against a written understanding six weeks old; and, on the eastern side of the ledger, every Libyan who might have held the Reconstruction Fund to a promise — there being, by its head's own statement, none to hold it to.

Overall Summary

Abdul Hamid Dbeibah, Prime Minister of the Government of National Unity (the recognised seat; the eastern counter-seat of Khalifa Haftar graded inside each test): Gives by one law — MIXED · Holds without gripping — MIXED · Answers the wobble — MIXED · Counts everyone — FAILS · Keeps his word over time — FAILS.

The summary recaps the whys, because a grade without its why means nothing. Test 1 is MIXED because the west's prosecution service genuinely reaches the government's own ministers and jailed a state-force prison chief, and because a unified budget briefly put both governments under one fiscal rule — while each seat keeps an insider channel the law does not govern, Arkenu and the fuel rents in the west, unpublished fund accounts and brigade impunity in the east. Test 2 is MIXED, the file's weakest, because three referees kept real autonomy in the west while the central bank — the referee that holds the money — had its independence extinguished twice, once by the prime minister's own camp's decree and once by armed entry in his capital, unopposed by his government; the eastern seat's grip, by contrast, is constitutional to its regime and would fail outright. Test 3 is MIXED because the seat adjusted in blood and under pressure — dissolving a militia fief, negotiating at Mitiga, reversing its own oil deal through a court, signing an agreement whose endpoint is its own replacement — and refused the one adjustment that concerned its own tenure, in 2022 and again in the dark summer of 2026. Test 4 FAILS because the February 2026 UN report documents the uncounted — migrants detained, trafficked, expelled and buried in mass graves — as a business model running through state-affiliated actors in both zones, a finding no honest local election or universal payroll answers; the east's suspended municipalities and its vanished MP make its own circle smaller, which is context, not credit. Test 5 FAILS because the two words that bound the seat's own power — the written pledge not to run, and the election date that was its whole mandate — were both broken on the record, while the words that were kept (the payroll; "only to an elected government") are credited in full, the second of them as the series' clearest kept-word-that-hurt: fidelity to the sentence, five years of frozen transition as its price.

The no-good-choice clause was applied where its mechanics were met — the May 2025 militia sequence, where the least-harm door was taken inside a trap the seat had helped maintain — and refused where they were not: the 2024 bank seizure, a trap of the GNU camp's own lighting, earns no shelter. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the Tripoli court annuls Arkenu and where the money is answered for; whether the 4+4 path produces its unified executive and its 2028 ballot, or joins the shelf of welcomed roadmaps; whether the Central Bank's contested succession is regularised with the second chamber's consent or stands as the new method; whether any senior figure of either migration apparatus ever faces a court; and whether Siham Sergiwa is ever answered for. Libya's tragedy in this record is not that no one holds — it is that the holding is real but partial in the west and total but unaccountable in the east, and the two halves have learned to steady each other in division. The Sun, at the head of this series, holds on all five tests with no hands at all. Neither of Libya's seats is close; the distance is measured above, in receipts.
