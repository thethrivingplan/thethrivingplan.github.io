The Ruler's Test — Hibatullah Akhundzada, Supreme Leader of Afghanistan

Subject: Hibatullah Akhundzada (also spelled Haibatullah Akhundzada) — Supreme Leader of Afghanistan, in power since 15 August 2021, and leader of the Taliban since 25 May 2016. The seat graded is the supreme leadership, and the reason is stated plainly: executive power in fact sits there. The supreme leader, titled Amir al-Mu'minin (Commander of the Faithful), wields unlimited authority and is the ultimate source of all law; he appoints and dismisses the prime minister, the cabinet, judges, and provincial and local leaders, and any order he speaks becomes law (Supreme Leader of Afghanistan record; list of decrees record, 2021–2026). Prime Minister Hasan Akhund heads the cabinet in Kabul as the supreme leader's appointee. The government graded is the Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan. Its recognition status is stated plainly, with one correction to the brief that commissioned this dossier: the brief described the government as unrecognized internationally. That was true from August 2021 to July 2025, and it remains substantially true — but it is no longer completely true. On 3 July 2025 Russia formally recognized the Islamic Emirate, accepted the credentials of its ambassador, and remains, as of this grading, the only country to have done so (International Crisis Group, 4 July 2025; Hudson Institute, 2025–2026; Carnegie Endowment, July 2025). No other state has followed; several, including China, the United Arab Emirates, Pakistan, and Uzbekistan, have posted ambassadors in Kabul without formal recognition, and Afghanistan's United Nations seat is still held by the mission of the former republic — its chargé d'affaires, Naseer Ahmad Faiq, addressed the Security Council as Afghanistan's representative on 16 September 2026 (Permanent Mission of Afghanistan statement, 16 September 2026). The grading follows power as held, not recognition: a seat does not need a foreign stamp to be graded on what it does with the power it actually holds, and the single Russian recognition changes nothing in the record below. His mandate is stated plainly too: there is none from voters. Akhundzada was chosen by the Taliban's Leadership Council on 25 May 2016, days after his predecessor Akhtar Mansour was killed in a U.S. drone strike on 21 May 2016, as a consensus candidate — a Kandahar religious scholar and former head of the Taliban's insurgent judiciary, without a personal military faction of his own (Middle East Institute Taliban Tracker; CRS Afghanistan background reports). He led the movement in insurgency for five years, took state power by military victory on 15 August 2021 as the republic collapsed and President Ashraf Ghani fled, nullified both the 1964 and the 2004 constitutions by decree on 4 August 2022, and has ruled since without a constitution, an election, or an elected body of any kind. Under the mandate rule, a seat taken and held by force has no voter platform to grade — only the holding. He is graded on the holding: the in-power record from 15 August 2021 to November 2026, with the insurgency years as context only. Reporting on this seat is unusually thin and contested, and the dossier says so where it matters: Akhundzada is one of the most reclusive rulers in the world, based in Kandahar rather than the capital, with almost no digital footprint except two photographs and several audio recordings of speeches, no televised interviews, and communication conducted almost entirely through written decrees and recorded sermons (Wikipedia biographical record, cross-checked against MEI Taliban Tracker, 2026). Even his birth date is not firmly documented — the International Criminal Court's warrant gives 19 October 1967 in Nakhoni, Panjwayi District, Kandahar Province; other reporting has placed his age differently, and no authoritative civil record has been published. Rumors of his death circulated in 2020–2021 and were denied by the Taliban; a reported assassination attempt in 2026 circulates in secondary summaries as unconfirmed, and is recorded in this dossier only as an unconfirmed report, not as an event. Where the record is thin, this dossier grades what is documented — decrees, UN monitoring, and NGO and press documentation — and marks what is not documented, rather than filling the gaps. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Each test closes with the grade's reason in plain words and a ledger naming who his actions on that test helped and who they hurt. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on a platform the voters elected is never itself a failure — and this seat was never elected at all, taken and held by force, which is recorded as a fact about its mandate, not as a grade in itself: the Test grades the holding, never a platform that does not exist. Marks are lost only for how power is held — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), refusing to consider another option when one becomes available (Test 3), counting out part of the population (Test 4). Word-keeping and outcomes are graded as separate axes and printed independently; neither launders the other. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier says so and grades the best possible choice available in the moment; that is not held against him. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Some of his decrees, on their face, bind his own movement first. On 20 May 2022 he banned Taliban members from taking multiple wives (decrees list, May 2022). On 19 March 2023 he prohibited government officials from hiring relatives, applying the ban retroactively so that relatives already hired were to be dismissed (decrees list, March 2023). These are rules aimed at insiders' privileges, issued by the one man no insider can appeal.
  • The April 2022 narcotics ban was enforced hardest, in outcome, in the Taliban's own heartland. The ban of 3 April 2022 prohibited production of opium and all other narcotics nationwide; UNODC's surveys record cultivation falling from an estimated 232,000 hectares in 2022 to 10,200 hectares in 2025, with the south-western region — the Taliban's historic base — falling from 73% of national cultivation in 2022 to 13% in 2025, while the residual cultivation shifted to the north-east (UNODC Afghanistan Opium Survey 2025, 6 November 2025). Whatever else the ban did (Tests 4 and 5), it was not a law his own region was spared.
  • His first decree on women, of 3 December 2021, stated rights for the counted-out side of his own base's customs: it outlined women's civil rights including consent in marriage and property and inheritance rights, and instructed the Ministry of Hajj and Religious Affairs, the Ministry of Information and Culture, the Supreme Court, and provincial and district governors to implement them (decrees list, December 2021). Its limits are graded in Test 4; as a decree it was addressed to his own officials, against forced marriage as practised inside his own society.
  • The general amnesty announced in August 2021 was, as announced, a single rule for former enemies: former government officials and security personnel were not to be punished for their past service (see Test 5 for what the monitoring record shows happened under it; the announcement itself belongs on this shelf).

Evidence against:

  • In January 2026 he signed into written law the opposite of one law. The Criminal Procedure Code for Courts, signed in early January 2026 (dated 4 January 2026 in Rawadari's publication and 7 January in Taliban-source confirmations of its authenticity), circulated to provincial courts for immediate implementation without public announcement, and published by the Afghan human-rights organisation Rawadari on 21 January 2026 after it obtained the Pashto text, divides society in its Article 9 into four classes and grades punishment by the accused's status, not the offence: religious scholars are answered with advice; the elite with a court summons and advice; the middle class with imprisonment; the lowest class with imprisonment and corporal punishment, up to 39 lashes on the translations published (Rawadari, January 2026; Afghan Analysts Network, Amnesty International, Georgetown Institute for Women, Peace and Security, and Oxford Human Rights Hub analyses, 2026; Taliban sources confirming authenticity via HTN World, January 2026). A ruler's own class — the clerics — is placed by his signature beyond imprisonment and the lash. That is a second law for his own side, codified, and it is the single most direct Test 1 document in the file.
  • The same code grades the law by the person in a second way: it distinguishes "free" persons from "slaves" as a legal category in its punishment provisions, permits discretionary (ta'zir) punishment to be carried out not only by judges but by husbands and masters (Article 4(5) on the published translations), and provides that a husband's beating of his wife becomes criminal only where it causes a broken bone, an open wound, or visible bruising, with the burden of proof on the wife and a maximum of 15 days' imprisonment (Article 32, published translations; Law Street Journal and Scottish Legal News analyses, 2026). It does not recognise a right to defence counsel, to silence, or to compensation for wrongful punishment on the analyses cited.
  • Alongside the courts stands an enforcement arm that answers to no law shared with the judged: the Ministry for the Propagation of Virtue and Prevention of Vice, under the Law on the Propagation of Virtue and Prevention of Vice that Akhundzada approved on 24 August 2024 (35 articles; translated text, Afghanistan Justice / CEP, 2024–2025). UNAMA documented, for April–June 2026 alone, at least 389 arbitrary arrests and detentions and 65 incidents of ill-treatment by the ministry's personnel (UNAMA human rights update, April–June 2026, via UN News, July 2026). Supreme Court Observer's analysis records that the morality police can detain and punish on suspicion alone, without evidence, hearing, or avenue of appeal — a parallel structure operating outside the judicial system his own code governs (Supreme Court Observer, 2026).
  • The punishments themselves fall on the public, not the palace: UNAMA documented 414 cases of corporal punishment in the first half of 2025 alone (327 men, 83 women, three girls, and one boy), mostly for "moral" accusations, and the UN records 12 public executions since 2021, two of them in the August 2025–January 2026 reporting period, carried out in sports stadiums (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026: Afghanistan; OHCHR report to the Human Rights Council, March 2026). No member of the Kandahar leadership circle is documented as subject to any of these instruments.
  • The International Criminal Court's finding belongs here as the external referee's record of the source: on 8 July 2025 Pre-Trial Chamber II issued arrest warrants for Akhundzada and Chief Justice Abdul Hakim Haqqani, finding reasonable grounds to believe they committed, by ordering, inducing or soliciting, the crime against humanity of persecution on gender grounds against girls, women, and persons non-conforming with the Taliban's gender policy, and on political grounds against persons perceived as "allies of girls and women," from 15 August 2021 to at least 20 January 2025; the warrant was unsealed on 5 October 2026 (ICC press release, 8 July 2025; ICC warrant, public redacted version, 5 October 2026). This is a judicial finding of reasonable grounds, recorded as such — an ALLEGATION in procedural status, at large, untested at trial — not a conviction, and the dossier does not state it as one.

Why: A nepotism decree, a wives decree, and an opium ban enforced in his own heartland show the hand can bind insiders. But in January 2026 that same hand wrote the exception into the law itself: four classes, four punishments, his own class answered with advice. One law is not graded by the decrees that bind the rank and file; it is graded at the source, and at the source the 2026 code is a favorites machine with a signature on it. Helped: Taliban officials' relatives removed under the 2023 nepotism decree's intended beneficiaries — the public the decree said should not be governed by officials' families; farmers and communities in the south-west where the opium economy, and its armed economy, contracted; women whose marriage consent and property rights the December 2021 decree instructed officials to uphold, where it was applied. Hurt: Every Afghan placed by Article 9 in the middle and lowest classes, who faces prison and the lash for what earns a scholar advice; wives, for whom the code sets the injury threshold at broken bones and the proof burden on themselves; the 414 people corporally punished in the first half of 2025 on UNAMA's count and the families of the 12 publicly executed since 2021 on the UN's count; girls, women, and their perceived allies named in the ICC warrant's persecution charge.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The day-to-day government is not run from Kandahar's desk in every particular: a cabinet under Prime Minister Hasan Akhund administers from Kabul, ministries issue their own directives (the edicts record shows ministries, not only the supreme leader, as issuing authorities), and provincial governors govern provinces (Stop Gender Apartheid edicts record; decrees list, 2021–2026). The seat is absolute at the apex; it is not the only hand that moves.
  • On one documented occasion the machine's parts moved against the apex's own order and the order fell: the September–October 2025 internet shutdown and its reversal, graded in full in Test 3. Whatever its motive, the episode documents that the cabinet in Kabul retained enough operational autonomy to restore a service the supreme leader had ordered cut — the only documented instance in the file of a referee-like check functioning at all, and it is recorded here at its true, small weight.
  • Foreign and UN presences he dislikes continue to operate and to publish against him from inside the country: UNAMA's human-rights updates documenting his authorities' violations are compiled in Afghanistan and released quarterly, and UN agencies run a nationwide aid operation (UNAMA updates, 2025–2026; UN returnees overview, August 2026). He has restricted them — severely, on the against-shelf — but as of grading he has not expelled the monitors.

Evidence against:

  • The structure itself is the grip. There is no constitution (both predecessor constitutions nullified by his decree, 4 August 2022), no legislature, no elections and therefore no electoral commission to keep autonomous, and no separation of powers in any conventional sense: the supreme leader holds effectively unlimited authority, rules by decree from Kandahar, and is, by the movement's own account, accountable to Allah and not to any institution that can remove or overrule him (politalking Afghanistan 2026 snapshot, July 2026; BBC investigation, January 2026; Supreme Leader of Afghanistan record). More than 550 decrees and edicts issued between July 2022 and November 2023 alone were counted by the Middle East Institute's tracker, covering administration, the judiciary, and personal freedoms (MEI Taliban Tracker).
  • The judiciary is his instrument by design and, since January 2026, by text. He appoints the chief justice and judges; on 13 November 2022 he ordered judges to implement hudud and qisas corporal and capital punishments (decrees list, November 2022); and the January 2026 Criminal Procedure Code anchors the entire legal system in the decrees of the Amir, mandating obedience to reportedly over 200 existing orders, elevates confession and testimony over independent investigation, and — on the published translations — deems the killing of persons who "defend false beliefs contrary to Islam" permissible with the Imam's permission (Article 14, published translations; Supreme Court Observer analysis, 2026). A court system whose source of law is one man's spoken orders is not a referee; it is a transmission.
  • The media referee has been dismantled in the open. Live political talk shows have been banned since February 2025; in September 2025 the authorities banned live broadcasts of political shows and limited media interviews to a pre-approved list (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026); books authored by women were ordered removed from bookstores and university libraries regardless of subject, and the teaching of human rights and gender studies prohibited (OHCHR, March 2026). Afghanistan ranked 175th of 180 in RSF's World Press Freedom Index in both 2025 and 2026 (RSF, 2025; RSF via Khaama Press, May 2026). Reporters Without Borders' investigation of 4 November 2026 documented 77 journalists detained since August 2021, for periods from a week to two years, and identified Directorate 015 of the Taliban's intelligence service as a centre of surveillance and intimidation of media workers — the beatings, electric shocks, and mock execution described by journalists interviewed are ALLEGATIONS documented by RSF from testimony, recorded as such (RSF investigation, November 2026, via Asia Daily summary).
  • The one external referee with offices in the country was barred, by sex, from its own premises: since 7 September 2025 Taliban security forces have prevented Afghan women — including UN staff, contractors, and visitors — from entering UN premises across the country; the ban stood at 296 consecutive days on 30 June 2026 and remained in force in November 2026 (OHCHR, March 2026; UNAMA update via UN News, July 2026; UN News, November 2026). A government that decides which of the referee's staff may enter the referee's building, on pain of their sex, has answered the gripping question itself.
  • Power has visibly migrated toward the grip: reporting through 2025–2026 documents decision-making centralising in Kandahar around Akhundzada and a circle of loyal clerics, with Kabul ministers learning of edicts as they are issued — a pattern the BBC's year-long investigation, based on more than 100 interviews with current and former Taliban members, local sources, experts, and former diplomats, mapped as two power centres, Kandahar and Kabul, with the Kandahar centre prevailing (BBC investigation, January 2026). The Taliban leadership denies any split, including when asked directly by the BBC; the denial and the investigation are both on the record, and the centralisation of decree-issuing in Kandahar is documented independently of the rift claim by the decrees record itself.

Why: There is almost nothing to weigh. The for-shelf holds a cabinet that administers, one reversed shutdown, and monitors not yet expelled. Against it: no constitution, no elections, no legislature, a judiciary re-founded on his decrees in January 2026, a media ranked 175th of 180 with 77 journalists detained on RSF's count, and the UN's own women staff barred from UN buildings for over 296 days. The referees were not gripped one by one; the board was built without them. Helped: The Kandahar leadership circle, whose authority over every institution is now textual as well as practical; the Ministry for the Propagation of Virtue and Prevention of Vice and the intelligence directorates, which operate without judicial or media check; state and state-aligned media, broadcasting without an independent competitor. Hurt: Judges, whose law is now another man's decrees; journalists — 77 detained on RSF's count since 2021, and every newsroom working from pre-approved guest lists; Afghan women employed by the UN, barred from their own workplaces since September 2025; every Afghan with a case, a complaint, or a story, for whom no independent institution remains to take it to.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? And when another option became available, did he at least consider it openly?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The internet shutdown was reversed. On 15 September 2025 Akhundzada banned fibre-optic and Wi-Fi services in several provinces to "prevent immorality," with nationwide extension intended; on 29 September 2025 he ordered a full nationwide shutdown of internet and telecommunications, saying the internet was unnecessary and had been banned under Mullah Omar with good results; on 1 October 2025, three days later, service was restored, without any official explanation (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026; internet censorship record, 2025). The BBC's investigation reports what happened inside: cabinet ministers Abdul Ghani Baradar, Sirajuddin Haqqani, and Mullah Yaqoob gathered at the prime minister's office and persuaded Prime Minister Hasan Akhund to turn the internet back on, and Akhundzada backed down (BBC investigation, January 2026). The Taliban denies any internal split; whether the reversal was his reconsideration or his ministers' defiance is CONTESTED between the BBC's insider sources and the Taliban's denials — but the order he had made, he did not sustain, and the country got its connection back. Under the other-option rule, the restoration is recorded in full on this shelf whichever hand forced it.
  • Foreign pushback has twice been answered with negotiation rather than only with fire. After the deadliest Afghanistan–Pakistan clashes since 2021 in October 2025 — Pakistani strikes on Kabul and Paktika that the Taliban blamed on Pakistan and Pakistan did not officially acknowledge, followed by Taliban retaliatory attacks — the two sides agreed a 48-hour ceasefire on 15 October 2025 and then an immediate ceasefire in Doha on 19 October 2025, mediated by Qatar and Türkiye, under which Kabul agreed not to support groups attacking Pakistan and both sides agreed to refrain from targeting each other's forces, civilians, and infrastructure (CNN, 15 and 19 October 2025). The ceasefire has frayed since (against-shelf); the answering of the wobble with a table, in the moment, is documented.
  • He has, at long intervals, shown his face to the pushback in person: a first known meeting with a foreign leader, Qatar's prime minister, in Kandahar in May 2023 (CRS Afghanistan background report), and on 17 November 2026 a rare visit to Kabul, addressing thousands of scholars, lecturers, and officials at Kabul University, where he renewed the general amnesty, invited Afghans abroad — including those who had worked with American forces — to return, and offered government posts to qualified opponents on competence, saying appointments should not depend on language, ethnicity, or regional background (AFP via multiple outlets, 17 November 2026; Asia Daily and Afghan Review analyses, November 2026). Opposition figures rejected the offer as submission without power-sharing (Kabul Times News Network, November 2026); the offer itself, made publicly by a man who almost never appears, is recorded as an adjustment attempted, not as one accepted.
  • The opium ban's enforcement absorbed a documented economic shock without the ban being abandoned or selectively suspended for his own region (Test 1) — recorded here only for the narrow point that sustained farmer and trader pushback did not move the policy; whether that steadfastness is virtue or doubling down is graded by the policy's content in Tests 4 and 5, not here.

Evidence against:

  • The defining wobble of the tenure was answered by reversal of his own government's announced adjustment, within hours, with no explanation that has ever been completed. The Education Ministry had announced that all schools would reopen on 23 March 2022; girls arrived at school that morning and were sent home, the ministry stating secondary schools for girls would remain closed until a plan compliant with its reading of Islamic law and Afghan culture was drawn up (Human Rights Watch, 23 March 2022; CRS, April 2022). Analysts across the record attribute the last-minute reversal to Akhundzada personally, persuaded by hardline clerics at the Kandahar gathering then in session — an attribution recorded as the analysts' assessment (Barnett Rubin via Gandhara/RFE-RL, March 2022; Brookings, 2022), the Taliban's internal process being opaque. The pushback since has been total and documented: fatwas in favour of girls' education from Taliban-affiliated clerics, letters from clerics across Afghanistan and Pakistan, public argument from his own deputy Sirajuddin Haqqani in February 2023 that power must not be monopolised and the people's concerns respected, and five years of UN, donor, and regional pressure. The ban stands, into its fifth year, in November 2026. If another option became available — and on this file it became available repeatedly, from inside his own movement and his own faith's scholarship — the record shows no instance of his considering it openly. The option was refused a look, which is exactly what this test counts against.
  • The street's pushback is answered by the morality and intelligence apparatus, not by adjustment: UNAMA documented at least 389 arbitrary arrests and detentions by Virtue and Vice personnel in April–June 2026 alone, including at least 30 women arrested in Herat between 6 and 8 June 2026 for "improper hijab" (UNAMA via UN News, July 2026). Women's protests since 2021 have been dispersed and their organisers detained, on the continuous record of UN and NGO monitoring; no protest-driven policy adjustment appears in the record assembled here.
  • The Pakistan ceasefire he answered with in October 2025 was not held into a settlement: follow-up talks in Istanbul in November 2025 collapsed without resolving the core dispute — Pakistan's demand for action against the Pakistani Taliban (TTP), whose presence in Afghanistan Kabul denies — and border fighting resumed through 2026, including clashes at Angoor Adda on 27 October 2026 in which at least one civilian was killed and five members of one family wounded by a mortar round on the Pakistani side, on local officials' account, with neither side's claims independently verified (Defense and Tech reporting, October 2026; CNN, October 2025). Casualty claims from the October 2025 fighting itself were irreconcilable and are printed separately, never blended: Pakistan claimed more than 200 Taliban and militants killed against the Taliban's nine acknowledged losses; the Taliban claimed 58 Pakistani soldiers killed against Pakistan's 23 acknowledged (CNN, October 2025). The wobble was answered; it was not mastered, and the underlying question was denied rather than negotiated to ground.
  • Reported internal pushback is answered, on the leaked record, with warnings rather than engagement: in a speech at a Kandahar madrassa in January 2025, leaked to the BBC, Akhundzada warned that internal divisions would collapse the emirate — "As a result of these divisions, the emirate will collapse and end" (BBC investigation, January 2026). The speech's existence and content are the BBC's reporting from a leaked recording; the Taliban has denied the split the speech is taken to evidence. Both are printed; the dossier grades the denial-and-warning posture only as reported.

Why: The grade is MIXED because the record genuinely runs both ways, at different weights. He can retreat — the internet came back in three days, a ceasefire was signed in Doha within ten days of the worst border fighting in years, and in November 2026 he travelled to Kabul to renew an amnesty and offer opponents posts in person. But none of his adjustments is open: the internet returned with no explanation, the ceasefire's core dispute was denied rather than settled, and the Kabul offer carried no procedure, no posts list, and no power-sharing. And on the largest pushback of all — girls' education, pressed on him for five years by the UN, by donors, by regional states, by Afghan clerics, and by his own deputies — he has never once considered the other option openly; he reversed his own ministry's reopening on the morning it was to happen and has held the reversal ever since. Tactical retreat under internal pressure, structural refusal under every other kind. Helped: Every Afghan who uses a phone or the internet — restored on 1 October 2025 and still on at grading; border communities on both sides during the October 2025 ceasefire window; the exiles and former officials addressed by the November 2026 amnesty renewal, to the extent any act on it. Hurt: The girls sent home on 23 March 2022 and every cohort since, whose schooling is the price of the refusal (counted in full in Test 4); protesters and the women arrested in Herat in June 2026 and across the UNAMA record; border civilians in the resumed fighting of 2026, including the family hit at Angoor Adda on the local officials' account; Pakistanis and Afghans whose core dispute — the TTP file — was left denied and therefore armed.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include women and girls, the Hazara, Shia and Ismaili communities, Tajiks, Uzbeks and other non-Pashtun Afghans, former officials, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The end of the nationwide war is a counting fact, and it counts everyone. UNAMA recorded 3,774 civilian casualties (1,095 killed, 2,679 wounded) in the 21 months from 15 August 2021 to 30 May 2023 — a monthly rate substantially lower than any single year since systematic recording began in 2009, against 8,820 casualties in 2020 alone and 5,183 in the first six months of 2021 (UNAMA IED report, June 2023, via Afghanistan Analysts Network). Afghans of every community can travel, farm, and trade on roads that were front lines; EUAA's country reporting describes a "relative absence of armed conflict" since the takeover (EUAA Afghanistan Country Focus, 2025–2026). This was the structural result of one belligerent becoming the government, and it is credited here without discount — and without laundering the rest of the file.
  • Primary education for girls continues: girls attend school to grade six, and in November 2026 Akhundzada told officials in Maidan Wardak that education should reach every valley in the province (SATP terrorism update, 20 November 2026). The line his system draws is at puberty, not at childhood — the against-shelf carries what that line costs.
  • His November 2026 Kabul University speech stated the counting principle in terms: appointments on competence, not language, ethnicity, or regional background, with posts offered to qualified opponents including Afghans who worked with American forces (Asia Daily, November 2026). It is a statement, not yet a documented pattern of appointments; it is recorded as the seat's own stated standard, against which Test 5 can grade later filings.
  • The narcotics ban's public-health face counts the addicted among the counted: the April 2022 ban was accompanied by forced-treatment roundups and the conversion of facilities to rehabilitation — coercive in method, documented in UNODC and press reporting, and aimed at a population the previous order had largely left uncounted. The method's coercion is recorded; the shelf notes the population was, at least, seen.

Evidence against:

  • Half the population is counted out of public life by decree, in the UN's plain words: "The de facto authorities have, in effect, criminalized the presence of women and girls in public life" (UN Human Rights chief Volker Türk, presenting the OHCHR report, March 2026). More than 100 decrees curbing women's education, work, and movement have been issued since 2021 (UN Women via UN News, August 2026). The education figures are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended: UNESCO counted at least 1.4 million girls deliberately denied secondary education since 2021, and nearly 2.5 million deprived of the right to education including those already out of school before the bans (UNESCO, August 2024); UN agencies in August 2026 put 2.4 million girls excluded from secondary education (UN News, 12 August 2026); a November 2026 UN briefing put the figure at over 2.6 million girls denied secondary education, with female university enrolment at zero (UN News, November 2026). The December 2022 university ban, the December 2024 ban on women in medical institutes — applied so that in November 2025 medical graduation examinations were held without women for the second consecutive year (OHCHR, March 2026) — and the January 2026 termination of the reduced salaries of women civil servants ordered home since 2021, carried out with "minimal transparency, no due process, and no mitigation measures" (OHCHR, March 2026), complete the arc: schooled to twelve, then erased by instalments.
  • The counting-out reached the helpers: since 7 September 2025 Afghan women have been barred from UN premises nationwide (Test 2), women may not enter shops and clinics in documented provinces without a male guardian (mahram), and in Logar a woman was warned for buying groceries unaccompanied while the shopkeeper was slapped and taken to the Virtue and Vice office (UNAMA via UN News, July 2026). In April 2026 a new Code on Judicial Separation of Spouses made a woman's marriage dependent on her guardian's permission, implicitly sanctioned child marriage on UNAMA's reading, and imposed extreme obstacles on women seeking divorce (UNAMA, April–June 2026). Books by women were removed from libraries regardless of subject or the author's nationality (OHCHR, March 2026).
  • Religious minorities are counted out by name in his own code and on the ground. The January 2026 code, on the published translations, defines only Hanafi followers as Muslims and characterises other schools and sects as deviants (Article 2, published translations, January 2026). UNAMA documented the closure of at least 33 Ismaili jamatkhanas in Badakhshan (17 in Zebak, 14 in Ishkashim, two in Wakhan), worshippers ordered out on the ground that the buildings should function as mosques; the detention of 45 Shia men in Kabul for displaying Ashura flags; and Shia communities in Parwan and Logar instructed to confine Ashura observance to designated mosques (UNAMA quarterly report, October 2026). The UN reported that Taliban authorities used physical abuse and death threats to compel some 50 members of the Ismaili community in Badakhshan to convert to the Sunni faith (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026, citing the UN); Amnesty International's 2025 report records forced conversion of Ismailis, forced evictions disproportionately affecting Hazaras, and discrimination restricting marginalised groups' access to humanitarian aid (Amnesty International, Afghanistan 2025). Hazara and Shia pressure was raised again at the Security Council on 16 September 2026 by the former republic's mission — detention of religious and community figures, interference with observances, appropriation of land and property, with reports of targeted killings continuing; the specific recent killings cited there are recorded as reports, ALLEGATION as to perpetrators (Permanent Mission of Afghanistan statement, 16 September 2026).
  • Former officials are counted out of the amnesty's protection in the monitoring record (full counts in Test 5): killings, arrests, and torture of former security personnel documented by UNAMA in every quarter of 2025–2026, including of men forcibly returned from Iran (UNAMA, 2025–2026). LGBT Afghans face systematic persecution on Human Rights Watch's record, with the Virtue and Vice law criminalising same-sex relations (HRW, World Report 2026).
  • The poor were handed the opium ban's bill. Farmers' income from opium sales fell 48% in a single year, from US$260 million in 2024 to US$134 million in 2025, after the near-total collapse from pre-ban levels; UNODC records that many growers shifted to cereals while worsening drought left over 40% of farmland barren (UNODC, November 2025). The ban was his decree, enforced without a documented alternative-livelihood programme at anything near the lost scale.
  • The humanitarian ledger is the background to every count above, printed as separate sourced figures, never blended: 23 million people needing humanitarian assistance and 13.8 million facing acute food insecurity, with the 2026 Humanitarian Response Plan 29.9% funded, on the former republic mission's statement of UN figures (16 September 2026); 17.4 million projected to face acute food insecurity in 2026 on the FAO's figure via UNAMA reporting; humanitarian funding falling from $2 billion in 2023 to $1 billion in 2025 to $570 million so far in 2026, on the UN Humanitarian Coordinator's November 2026 briefing (UN News, November 2026); approximately 2.9 million Afghans returned, many involuntarily, from Iran and Pakistan in 2025, and nearly 1.15 million more between January and August 2026 (UNHCR, February 2026; Permanent Mission statement, September 2026); more than 400 health facilities closed in 2025 for lack of funds (HRW, World Report 2026), with 40% of primary healthcare facilities expected to close between 2025 and 2026 on the Coordinator's figure. These shocks are not all of his making — see the no-good-choice paragraph below — but the population absorbing them is governed by his decrees, and the decrees that bar women from aid work, UN premises, and medical training are his.

Why: The war's end is real, and it is the largest single benefit in this dossier — every community in Afghanistan lives under a fraction of the civilian-casualty rate of 2020. It does not answer this test. This test asks who is counted, and the documented answer is that the count stops at puberty for half the population — 1.4 million, 2.4 million, 2.6 million girls on the agencies' separate counts — stops at the jamatkhana door for Ismailis, at the Ashura flag for Shia, at the amnesty's edge for former officials, and at the farm gate for the poor who carried the opium ban. A peace that counts everyone as safe and then counts half of them out of school, work, care, and public life has done half the test. Helped: All Afghans, of every community, by the end of the nationwide war — the casualty fall from 8,820 in 2020 to a 21-month total of 3,774 after the takeover is the file's largest helped entry; boys and men, whose schooling, work, and movement continue; girls to grade six, whose primary schooling the system still runs; communities where the opium economy's armed trade contracted. Hurt: Afghan women and girls as a class — the 100-plus decrees, the education bans on every agency's separate count, the medical-training ban, the terminated civil-service salaries, the mahram rules; Ismaili communities in Badakhshan — at least 33 prayer halls closed, some 50 men compelled to convert on the UN's reporting; Shia and Hazara communities — Ashura restrictions, 45 men detained over flags in Kabul, forced evictions and aid discrimination on Amnesty's record; former officials and security personnel, counted in Test 5's figures; poppy farmers, whose income fell by half in a year with drought on top; LGBT Afghans, under systematic persecution on HRW's record.

The no-good-choice clause, applied by its mechanics: The returnee and aid shock of 2025–2026 is recorded as a situation with no good choice, and it is not held against him. The options actually open to any Afghan government in those years were these: absorb approximately 2.9 million returnees in 2025 and over a million more in 2026, expelled or pressured out by Iran and Pakistan, into an economy under sanctions, frozen reserves, and a humanitarian plan funded at 29.9% — or attempt to refuse entry to its own nationals at the border, which no government could lawfully or practically do. Foreign aid's collapse from $2 billion (2023) to $570 million (2026, at November) was decided in donor capitals, not Kandahar. The constraint is recorded, not a demerit. What remains gradable under the clause is the least-harm door inside the trap, and there the record is his: barring Afghan women from UN premises in September 2025, in the middle of the returnee emergency, restricted the aid operation's own staffing and reach — a choice among the options actually available, and not the least harmful one. The trap's origin is also partly gradable: the aid dependence itself predates him, but the policies that keep formal recognition — and with it normal development finance — out of reach are the decrees graded in Tests 1, 2, and 4, maintained through the emergency they worsened.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 15 August 2021, with dates. This seat took power by force and has no voter platform; under the mandate rule there is no elected programme to grade — only the words he and his movement actually gave: the Doha commitments of 2020, the assurances of August 2021, the amnesty, and his own decrees and Eid messages since.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The core promise to his own movement — kept in full. What Akhundzada promised the Taliban, across his Eid messages and decrees, was the implementation of his reading of Sharia as the whole order of the state: hudud punishments ordered to judges on 13 November 2022, the Virtue and Vice law approved 24 August 2024, the constitutions nullified, the January 2026 code anchoring the courts in his decrees. By November 2026 that order stands built. The keeping is recorded here without discount; its human content is graded in Tests 1 and 4 and in the crossings below, on the separate axis, exactly as the method requires.
  • The narcotics ban — a decree given and enforced for four years. The 3 April 2022 ban on opium and all narcotics has been held without suspension: cultivation down from 232,000 hectares (2022) to 10,200 hectares (2025), production down 32% in 2025 alone to 296 tons (UNODC, November 2025). UNODC also records the shadow: synthetic-drug production and trafficking, especially methamphetamine, continuing to increase since the ban, and cultivation shifting toward the north-east and across borders — the kept word's seam, recorded with it.
  • The Doha counterterrorism word, in part: the Taliban committed in the 29 February 2020 Doha Agreement to prevent any group using Afghan soil to threaten the United States and its allies. No attack on the U.S. or its allies from Afghan soil is documented in the record assembled here for 2021–2026, and the emirate has fought Islamic State Khorasan Province (ISKP) as an enemy throughout — ISKP remaining the main perpetrator of the suicide attacks that continue (UNAMA, 2023). The other half of this word is on the against-shelf, and it is the heavier half.
  • The amnesty, renewed in person: on 17 November 2026 he restated the general amnesty at Kabul University, urged Afghans who had worked with American forces to return under it, and offered qualified opponents government posts on competence (AFP and Asia Daily, November 2026). A word renewed is not a word kept; it is recorded here as the seat's current stated position, and graded against the monitoring record below.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The general amnesty — given August 2021, breached in every monitored quarter since. UNAMA's report of 22 August 2023 documented at least 800 violations against former officials and former security-force members between the takeover and 30 June 2023, including 218 extrajudicial killings, 424 arbitrary arrests and detentions, 144 cases of torture and ill-treatment, and 14 enforced disappearances (UNAMA, August 2023). The later counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended: 14 former personnel killed in October–December 2025, with 28 arbitrary arrests and at least seven torture cases in the same quarter (UNAMA via Pakistan TV summary, 2026); at least eight killings, 29 arbitrary arrests or detentions, and five torture cases in April–June 2026 (UNAMA, July 2026); four killings and at least 14 arbitrary arrests or detentions in July–September 2026 (UNAMA, October 2026); 12 killings, 13 arrests, and three torture cases in May–July 2026 on the former republic mission's citation of UNAMA (16 September 2026). The Taliban maintains the amnesty remains in force and rejects systematic abuse; UNAMA's documentation and the Taliban's position are both on the record, and the documentation is quarterly, named, and unretracted. In his November 2026 renewal he himself attached the condition that the amnesty holds provided returnees "did not commit crimes or stir unrest" — a condition whose application is decided by his own apparatus (Asia Daily, November 2026).
  • The schools word — given, dated, and reversed on the morning it fell due. Taliban spokesmen through early 2022 said girls' secondary schools would reopen "as soon as possible," and the Education Ministry announced reopening for 23 March 2022; the reversal that morning is graded in Test 3. The December 2022 university ban and December 2024 medical-institutes ban extended the breach. No new date has been given since; a word with the date removed is recorded as a word withdrawn.
  • The inclusive-government assurance — given in August 2021, never performed. The Taliban's August 2021 assurances of an inclusive Islamic government were followed on 7 September 2021 by an all-male caretaker cabinet of Taliban members, and five years on the government remains a Taliban government: no women in any cabinet or senior post, and a study of some 1,185 Taliban leaders cited in EU asylum reporting found Shia at no more than 0.6% of the sample (EUAA reporting via Shia Waves summary, 2026 — the sample is of Taliban leadership, not of the whole public workforce, and is printed as such). The November 2026 offer of posts to qualified opponents was made inside the existing structure, with no application process, posts list, or timetable announced (Asia Daily, November 2026), and was rejected by opposition figures as submission rather than sharing (Kabul Times News Network, November 2026).
  • Women's rights "within Sharia" — the August 2021 assurance, answered by 100-plus decrees. The movement's 2021 assurances that women would work and study within its reading of Islamic law were followed by the abolition of the Women's Affairs Ministry (replaced by the Virtue and Vice ministry), the bans graded in Test 4, and, in the ICC's procedural finding, persecution charges for the policy itself (ICC, July 2025). His 3 December 2021 decree on women's marriage consent and property stands on the record un-repealed — and alongside a 2026 spousal-separation code that UNAMA reads as sanctioning child marriage and subordinating a woman's marriage to her guardian's permission (UNAMA, 2026). The two documents are both his system's; the later practice is the one being lived.
  • The Doha word on terrorist safe haven — breached on the documented record, and contested in part. Al-Qaeda leader Ayman al-Zawahiri was killed by a U.S. drone strike on 31 July 2022 in a Kabul house linked to the Haqqani network — his presence in the capital a year after the takeover is a documented fact of the strike; the Taliban stated it had no knowledge of his presence and that the strike violated Doha, and both positions are printed (Just Security analysis, August 2022; U.S. government statements, 2022). UN sanctions monitoring has continued to report al-Qaeda maintaining a presence in Afghanistan, downsized and with limited resources, with conflicting assessments noted (EUAA Country Focus, 2025–2026). On the TTP: Pakistan attributes a surge of attacks to TTP sanctuaries in Afghanistan — 600 attacks in the year to October 2025 on ACLED's count via CNN — and Kabul denies sheltering the TTP; the sanctuary question is CONTESTED between the two governments, and the October 2025 Doha ceasefire's commitment that Kabul would not support groups attacking Pakistan (Taliban spokesman Zabihullah Mujahid's statement, 19 October 2025) has been followed by resumed border fighting through 2026 (Test 3). The U.S. State Department's assessment that the Taliban shows "bad faith" on Doha counterterrorism commitments is recorded as that government's assessment (Lead Inspector General report to Congress, January–March 2026).
  • The word to the world on engagement — the seat's own spokesman problem. Throughout, Taliban spokesmen have described restrictions as temporary, under review, or matters of sequencing — on girls' education above all — while the supreme leader's decrees made them permanent in instalments and the January 2026 code entrenched them. Where a movement's public word and its leader's decrees diverge for five years, this test grades the divergence itself: the word given outward was not the word kept inward, and the inward word is the one with police behind it.

Why: His word runs in two directions and both are documented. Inward — to his movement, on Sharia's implementation and the narcotics ban — it is kept exactly, for years, at real cost, and the for-shelf records it without discount. Outward — the amnesty, the schools date, the inclusive government, the women's-rights assurance, the Doha safe-haven commitment — every major word given to Afghans outside his movement or to foreign partners has been breached, withdrawn, or left contested by his own side's conduct, on quarterly UN documentation that has never paused. A ruler whose word is iron to his own camp and paper to everyone else has not kept his word over time; he has kept it over some people, which is Test 1's failure wearing Test 5's clothes. Helped: His movement and its clerical base, who received the order they were promised, built on schedule; the users, patients, and neighbouring states who benefit from a 96% fall in Afghan opium cultivation from its pre-ban level; Afghans who returned under the amnesty and were, in the event, left alone — the Contact Commission facilitated the return of more than 1,000 figures associated with the former regime over three years on its own claim (MP-IDSA assessment of the Taliban amnesty, August 2025). Hurt: Former officials and security personnel — 218 extrajudicial killings in the first monitored period alone on UNAMA's count, and killings in every quarter since, including of men returned by force from Iran; the girls promised a school door on 23 March 2022; the women assured of work and study in August 2021; Pakistanis killed in TTP attacks Islamabad attributes to Afghan sanctuary — attribution CONTESTED, the deaths not; every Afghan and foreign partner who priced the movement's outward word as reliable and planned on it.

The two axes, stated plainly (word × outcome): Crossing 1 — kept his word and it helped people: the April 2022 narcotics ban, given as a decree and enforced for four years — cultivation down from 232,000 to 10,200 hectares on UNODC's figures, with the addiction economy's contraction a documented public benefit (its harm to farmers is recorded in Test 4's ledger in full, and does not move the ban out of this crossing for the populations it helped). Crossing 2 — kept his word and it still hurt people: the Sharia order promised to his movement — the Virtue and Vice law of August 2024, the hudud order of November 2022, the January 2026 code — kept faithfully, decree by decree, and its keeping is the direct documented cause of the floggings, the closures, and the erasure graded in Tests 1 and 4; the keeping earns its credit on this test and the hurt stands in full beside it. Crossing 3 — went against his word and it helped people: the internet restoration of 1 October 2025 — his own stated position, given days earlier, was that the internet was unnecessary and rightly banned under Mullah Omar; the service was restored anyway, by his ministers' intervention on the BBC's account or by his own backing down, and the restoration helped every aid agency, business, student, and family it reconnected. The break is recorded here; the help is recorded in Test 3's ledger in full. Crossing 4 — broke his word and it hurt people: the general amnesty of August 2021 against UNAMA's quarterly record, and the schools reopening promised for 23 March 2022 — both breaks landed on the same broad population: Afghans who had served, studied under, or believed the assurances of the order his movement replaced and then gave.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Hibatullah Akhundzada has led the Taliban since 25 May 2016 and ruled Afghanistan since 15 August 2021 — a seat taken by military victory, held without a constitution, an election, or a single institution that can overrule him, and recognised, as of grading, by exactly one foreign state, Russia (July 2025); the dossier grades the power as held, and the power is absolute. On one law, the file ends in his own handwriting: after early decrees that did bind his insiders — relatives ordered dismissed, multiple wives banned, an opium ban enforced hardest in his own heartland — the Criminal Procedure Code he signed in January 2026 wrote four classes of Afghan into law with four different punishments, his own clerical class answerable by advice, the lowest by prison and the lash. Without gripping, there is nothing to grip with: the courts were re-founded on his decrees in that same code, the media stands 175th of 180 with 77 journalists detained on RSF's count, and the UN's Afghan women staff have been barred from UN buildings since September 2025. On the wobble he is genuinely mixed, and the mix is instructive: he retreats when his own cabinet moves — the internet he ordered cut on 29 September 2025 was back on 1 October — he signs ceasefires under fire, as in Doha in October 2025, and in November 2026 he came to Kabul in person to renew the amnesty and offer opponents posts on competence; but no adjustment is ever open or explained, the ceasefire's core dispute was denied into resumed border fighting, and on girls' education — pressed on him for five years by the UN, by donors, by Afghan and Pakistani clerics, and by his own deputies — if another option became available, he has never once considered it openly, having reversed his own ministry's reopening on the morning of 23 March 2022 and held the reversal since. On counting everyone, the tenure's largest fact is a benefit: the war's end cut civilian casualties from 8,820 in 2020 to 3,774 across the 21 months after the takeover, and every community shares it. Against it stands the count itself: more than 100 decrees against women and girls, with the agencies' separate figures — 1.4 million deliberately denied secondary schooling (UNESCO, 2024), 2.4 million excluded (UN agencies, August 2026), over 2.6 million denied (UN briefing, November 2026) — never blended in this dossier; at least 33 Ismaili prayer halls closed in Badakhshan and some 50 Ismaili men compelled to convert on UN reporting; former officials killed in every monitored quarter despite the amnesty; and poppy farmers' income halved in a year by a ban with no livelihood floor under it.
