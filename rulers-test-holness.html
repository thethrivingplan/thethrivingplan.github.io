The Ruler's Test — Andrew Holness, Prime Minister of Jamaica

Subject: Andrew Michael Holness — Prime Minister of Jamaica since 3 March 2016, and briefly before that from October 2011 to January 2012. Leader of the Jamaica Labour Party (JLP). On 3 September 2025 he won a third consecutive term — his fourth in total — when the JLP took 35 of 63 seats in the House of Representatives against 28 for the People's National Party (PNP), on a turnout of about 39.5 per cent; the Electoral Commission of Jamaica's final count and the OAS observation mission record a calm, orderly election which the opposition leader, Mark Golding, conceded (Electoral Commission of Jamaica; International IDEA/OAS reporting, September 2025). He remains prime minister at grading, fourteen months into that term and a year into the recovery from Hurricane Melissa. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machinery has investigated the prime minister himself, twice, in public. In February 2023 the Integrity Commission (IC) published an investigation into an alleged conflict of interest over government contracts awarded to Westcon Construction between 2006 and 2009, when Holness was minister of education; the Commission's Director of Corruption Prosecution (DCP) ruled that the investigation had failed to contradict or support the offences and that no criminal charges could be laid (Integrity Commission report and ruling, February 2023). He was investigated, and the independent prosecutor's office — not the prime minister — announced the outcome.
  • The second investigation ended the same way on its criminal track. The IC's 171-page report on his statutory declarations for 2019–2022 (tabled in Parliament 17 September 2024) recorded that its Director of Investigation could not conclude whether he was illicitly enriched, and referred the 2021 declaration to the DCP over four omitted bank accounts. The DCP ruled there was insufficient evidence that the omissions were intentional, and greater evidence supporting his explanation — dormant and childhood accounts, some opened by his parents from 1975 — than against it; no charges were laid (Integrity Commission report, August–September 2024; DCP ruling tabled with it; Jamaica Information Service, 17 September 2024).
  • The law has reached his own cabinet and his own Speaker. His education minister, Ruel Reid, resigned in March 2019 and was charged in October 2019; his fraud trial — five accused including Reid, former Caribbean Maritime University president Fritz Pinnock, Reid's wife and daughter, and a JLP councillor, all of whom have pleaded not guilty to ALLEGATIONs that more than J$25 million was siphoned from the ministry and the university between 2016 and 2019 — is actively running in the Kingston and St Andrew Parish Court as of November 2026, with more than 90 witnesses expected (Gleaner/Observer trial reporting, October–November 2026). His party's Speaker of the House, Marisa Dalrymple-Philibert, was charged by the IC in 2023 over an undeclared vehicle and resigned the Speakership; her case proceeded through the parish court (Integrity Commission report; court reporting, 2023–2024). The Petrojam scandal in his own energy ministry cost the minister, Andrew Wheatley, his portfolio in 2018; the IC's 2020 report found prima facie evidence of fraud against the refinery's former general manager and board chairman, who were prosecuted and — the outcome stated exactly — acquitted on all counts in December 2024 (Integrity Commission Petrojam report, 2020; CVM/press reporting of the verdict, 11 December 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The subject of the declarations investigation is litigating to end it. On 30 September 2024 Holness applied to the Supreme Court for 21 orders, including certiorari to quash the IC's reports, orders terminating all investigations into his financial affairs, and declarations striking down provisions of the Integrity Commission Act and the illicit-enrichment provisions of the Corruption Prevention Act as unconstitutional (claim SU2024CV03844; [2024] JMSC Civ 154). Litigation is a legal right, and the courts have entertained it — but the practical effect sought is that the one investigation into the investigator-in-chief's own finances be stopped by the investigated man. The courts have so far refused his procedural shortcuts: on 31 July 2025 Justice Jarrett dismissed his bid to strike out parts of the IC's affidavit evidence as "scandalous," refused the unredacted internal documents he sought, and struck parts of his own claim as an abuse of process; on 31 July 2026 the Court of Appeal dismissed his appeal, holding the trial judge's approach "cannot be faulted," and lifted the stay — the substantive judicial review remains undetermined as of November 2026 (Supreme Court ruling, 31 July 2025; Court of Appeal ruling, 31 July 2026; Irie FM/Gleaner reporting).
  • The underlying file remains open in a way it is open for no ordinary declarant: the Commission declined to certify his 2021 statutory declaration, referred his declarations to the Financial Investigations Division, and referred three companies connected to him — which had filed nil tax returns despite transactions the report put above J$470 million — for review; a J$1.9 million item of unexplained net-worth growth in 2022 was flagged in the report and is CONTESTED by Holness, who says the Commission's own figures contain an error (Integrity Commission report, September 2024; his statement to the House, 17 September 2024). His declarations for the period accordingly sat uncertified for years while he held the office that appoints and funds the certifier.
  • Members of his government pressed the watchdog publicly on his behalf: in September 2024 government lawmakers, including the justice minister, argued in the IC Oversight Committee that the Commission should be directed to certify the prime minister's declarations "forthwith" (Gleaner, 25 September 2024). The certification decision remained the Commission's — but the pressure on it, from the subject's own side, is on the record.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, the prosecution, INDECOM, media, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts rule against his government and against him personally, and the rulings take effect. In April 2024 the Full Court struck down part of his government's constitutional amendment on the Director of Public Prosecutions' tenure (below); in May 2025 a Full Court ruled that the series of states of public emergency declared between January 2018 and February 2023 was unconstitutional ([2025] JMFC Full 2, Campbell v Attorney General, 16 May 2025); and in July 2026 the Court of Appeal ruled against the prime minister in his own Integrity Commission litigation (Test 1). No judge in any of these matters is documented as punished, transferred, or defunded.
  • The electoral machinery is independent in outcome: the Electoral Commission ran the September 2025 election, the opposition gained 14 seats, its leader conceded, and the OAS mission described election day as calm and transparent (OAS/International IDEA, September 2025). INDECOM, the statutory investigator of the security forces, operates openly against the executive's own narrative: on 23 April 2026 it issued a public statement of "critical concern" rebuking his Minister of National Security, Dr Horace Chang, for dismissing body-worn cameras during planned operations — and the government had already announced the acquisition of over 1,000 body-worn cameras for the police force (INDECOM statement, 23 April 2026; Jamaica Information Service, December 2025). The central bank's operational independence was written into law under his government (Bank of Jamaica Act, 2020) and monetary policy has been set without documented executive interference. Jamaica's press is free, frequently hostile to him, and unmolested in the record: the Gleaner, Observer, and broadcasters have carried the IC investigation, the salary controversy, and the Melissa recovery critique — including a dancehall artist's hit song asking where the relief money went — without closure, prosecution, or licensing retaliation (press record, 2024–2026).

Evidence against:

  • For five years, his government policed whole communities under a rolling emergency regime that the courts later held the constitution did not authorize in that form. States of public emergency (SOEs) — warrantless search, detention without charge — were declared from January 2018 and renewed serially across parishes; the Public Defender reported in the SOE era that of nearly 4,000 people detained, only about four per cent were charged with any serious crime (Public Defender's report, cited in parliamentary debate, 2018). When the Senate refused the two-thirds vote needed to extend them — in November 2021, and again on 25 November 2022, when opposition senators declined to extend the 15 November 2022 declarations — the government answered not by accepting the check but by declaring fresh 14-day SOEs (6 and 28 December 2022), routing around the parliamentary threshold the constitution sets (Caribbean Times/CMC and International IDEA reporting, November–December 2022). In May 2025 the Full Court held the 2018–2023 declarations unconstitutional, including on the separation-of-powers ground that the executive had extended emergencies by a route reserved to Parliament ([2025] JMFC Full 2). The emergency instrument has since fallen out of routine use — but the documented pattern of 2018–2023 was governance by decree in the communities least able to answer back, ended by courts and the Senate, not by the executive's own restraint.
  • His government amended the constitution in a way whose practical effect ran to one incumbent officeholder. The Constitution (Amendment of Sections 96(1) and 121(1)) Act, 2023 raised the DPP's retirement age from 60 to 65 and, in section 2(2), allowed the incumbent DPP, Paula Llewellyn — then weeks from the expiry of her extension — to elect to remain without the usual recommendation of the prime minister and opposition leader. Opposition members challenged it as enacted for an improper purpose. The courts split, and the final position must be stated exactly: the Full Court upheld the retirement-age change but struck section 2(2) down in April 2024, and Llewellyn stepped aside; the Court of Appeal on 20 December 2024 reversed on that point, declaring section 2(2) valid and applicable to the incumbent; she resumed office and retired at 65 in September 2025 after 17 years ([2024] JMCA Civ 47; Court of Appeal costs ruling [2025] JMCA Civ 21). The amendment was lawful as finally determined — and it remains the case that a tenure rule of the constitution was rewritten, mid-stream, in terms that benefited the sitting prosecutor. The opposition's improper-purpose claim is recorded as its claim; the courts did not adopt it.
  • A contested appointment sits at the center of the legislature: his wife, Juliet Holness, a sitting JLP member of parliament, was elected Speaker of the House in September 2023 after her predecessor resigned under IC charge. The election was by the House and lawful; the opposition and commentators criticized the concentration of the premiership and the Speakership in one household as a structural conflict. The criticism is recorded as criticism; no rule was broken (parliamentary record and press commentary, September 2023).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Parliament, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • On pay, his first answer to the wobble was a public concession. When the May 2023 compensation review raised politicians' salaries by 200 per cent or more — his own from J$9.16 million toward J$28.5 million — the backlash was national; on 22 May 2023 he announced he would decline his increase entirely and remain at J$9.16 million, pairing the concession with accountability measures for the political directorate (Jamaica Information Service, 22 May 2023). The concession was personal, immediate, and made in a press conference he called.
  • On the emergencies, the adjustment came late but came: after the Senate's refusals and years of litigation, the SOE instrument was retired from routine use, and the murder decline that followed was achieved under ordinary and intelligence-led policing — the outcome the opposition and the Full Court had argued was possible (JCF statistics, 2024–2025; Test 4). He did not purge the Senate, the court, or the opposition for defeating his signature tool.
  • On Melissa, he has owned the shortfall in public rather than denying it: on the storm's first anniversary he said he was "not satisfied" with the pace of recovery and acknowledged that some families were still sleeping outside, and his government published a completion timetable — 90 per cent of assessed households paid by December 2026, the rest by January 2027 (Office of the Prime Minister briefing, 27 October 2026).
  • On the 2025 election itself: he campaigned, won narrowly, and described the result as a hard-fought mandate rather than disputing the closeness; the transfer of the reduced majority was orderly (press reporting, September 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The pay concession was reversed once the electoral pressure had passed. In February 2026 — five months after the election — the Office of the Prime Minister announced he would accept the full J$28.5 million salary after all, effective retroactively to September 2025, the month of the vote; the 2023 statement itself had reserved that a "new mandate" might allow reconsideration, which is printed here as his stated basis (Jamaica Observer, 20 February 2026). In October 2026, defending a J$40 million salary for the head of the new reconstruction authority while families remained under tarpaulins, he described his own J$28.6 million package as a "likkle salary" (Jamaica Star, 28 October 2026). The wobble was answered in 2023; the answer was withdrawn in 2026.
  • On the SOE ruling, the government's first response to the Full Court's May 2025 judgment was not acceptance but a stated intention to appeal — criticized by the claimant and the opposition as a knee-jerk reaction; the ruling stands unappealed-to-effect in the record at grading, and no SOE regime has been reimposed, so the defiance remained rhetorical (opposition and claimant statements, May 2025; the point is CONTESTED as to how far the appeal was pursued).
  • On the Integrity Commission, his answer to the wobble of investigation was litigation plus characterization: he has described the probe into his finances as flawed and unfair in Parliament and sought the courts' help to end it (Test 1), rather than submitting the disputed items and letting certification run its course. That is his right; the test records the choice.
  • The minimum-wage wobble of 2026 was handled openly but was still a retreat delivered by announcement: having promised J$18,500 as the first step in the 2025 campaign, he used the March 2026 budget debate to set J$17,000 instead, explaining the change by Hurricane Melissa's damage to businesses and households (Jamaica Information Service, 19–24 March 2026). Explained adjustment, not denial — graded here as the honest form of a step back, and under Test 5 as a promise partially unkept.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — inner-city communities under the SOEs and ZOSOs, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The headline counts moved for the whole flock, and most steeply for the poor. Officially measured poverty fell from 16.7 per cent in 2021 to 8.2 per cent in 2023 and 7.8 per cent in 2024 — the lowest since measurement began in 1989 — with food poverty at 2.7 per cent, also a record low (PIOJ/STATIN, Jamaica Survey of Living Conditions, reported 25 November 2025). Unemployment fell to all-time lows — 3.7 per cent in January 2025 (IMF Article IV statement, May 2025) and about 3.3 per cent through 2025 (STATIN). The minimum wage rose from J$6,200 a week in 2016 to J$16,000 in 2025 and J$17,000 on 1 July 2026.
  • The murder count — the count that falls overwhelmingly on inner-city communities — fell furthest of all: 1,141 murders in 2024 (down about 19 per cent), then a preliminary 673 in 2025, down about 42 per cent and the lowest since 1994, the first year below 1,000 since 2003 (Jamaica Constabulary Force figures via Jamaica Information Service, January 2026; PIOJ's final 2025 figure is 675). The decline continued into 2026 — 407 murders to 6 October 2026, down 24 per cent on the period (JCF). His opponents' voters were counted in the only way that finally matters: their ballots were counted, their party gained 14 seats, and their leader's concession was accepted without incident (September 2025).
  • The Melissa response was universalist in design: a single assessment system (113,565 household assessments completed by April 2026), a published grant scale under the ROOFS programme (J$75,000 minor / J$200,000 major / J$500,000 severe), roughly J$12 billion paid out by the first anniversary, and 2,500 modular houses purchased for families whose homes were destroyed (Ministry of Labour and Social Security and OPM figures via anniversary reporting, October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • As killings by criminals fell, killings by the state rose — in the same communities. INDECOM recorded 311 people fatally shot by the security forces in 2025, up 65 per cent from 189 in 2024 and the highest figure since 2010, with about half occurring in planned police operations; at least 231 more were recorded by early October 2026 (INDECOM statements, January and April 2026). INDECOM has said publicly it is troubled by the rise; the national security minister has accepted that one or two incidents may be questionable while arguing the trend will follow murders down (ministerial statements, 2026). Whether each shooting was lawful is for INDECOM and the courts case by case; the aggregate is the test's business: the state's own lethal-force count against the flock rose by two-thirds in the year the flock's murder count halved.
  • The SOE years counted inner-city residents as a category: nearly 4,000 detained in the early SOE period with about four per cent charged (Public Defender, 2018), under a regime the Full Court later held unconstitutional (Test 2). The Zones of Special Operations (ZOSO) framework from 2017 paired the security presence with social intervention — the design counted residents in — but the emergency detentions remain the documented counterweight.
  • The Melissa recovery, a year on, had not reached half the flock it assessed: of roughly 118,000 households assessed for repair grants, about half had been paid by the first anniversary; 136 people remained in emergency shelters on 28 October 2026, and thousands of families in St Elizabeth and Westmoreland — the parishes that took the Category 5 landfall (28 October 2025; at least 45 dead; damage assessed at US$12.2 billion, 56.7 per cent of GDP, by the ECLAC/PIOJ assessment) — remained under tarpaulins (ODPEM and anniversary reporting, October 2026). The World Bank projects the storm reversed poverty reduction, with the share living under its US$8.30 line rising toward 19.9 per cent in 2026 (World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, April 2026). Rural poverty was already the deepest before the storm — 11.0 per cent against 2.5 per cent in the Kingston metropolitan area in 2024 (PIOJ).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since 2016, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The fiscal word — kept, over a decade. He promised discipline and delivered the region's benchmark debt reduction: public debt fell from about 140 per cent of GDP in the early 2010s to 62.4 per cent at the end of FY2024/25, the lowest in 25 years, on sustained primary surpluses, with a legislated 60 per cent target that was on course to be met early before Melissa (World Bank, February 2026; IMF, May 2025). The storm blew the ratio back toward 68 per cent and the fiscal rules were suspended openly, through March 2027, with the target re-dated to FY2029/30 — a declared revision, not a quiet abandonment (Fiscal Commission/Fiscal Policy Paper reporting, 2026).
  • Work and wages — substantially kept. Unemployment at record lows (above); the income-tax threshold raised from J$592,800 in 2016 to J$1.8 million by 2025 and J$1,902,360 from April 2026; the minimum wage nearly tripled across his tenure (Test 4). His 2025 pledges — a phased doubling of the minimum wage to J$32,000 by 2030 and a gradual cut in the income-tax rate toward 15 per cent — are framed as term-length promises and cannot yet be graded as broken; the first wage step, however, came in below its announced figure (below).
  • Safety — kept late, then kept. For most of his tenure the promise of a safer Jamaica ran behind the record (murders above 1,400 as late as 2022 on JCF figures); the 2023–2025 declines (down 8, then 19, then about 42 per cent) delivered in his third election year the outcome he had promised since 2016 (JCF/JIS figures).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The 2016 "first 100 days" governance trio — none delivered in a decade. As opposition leader he promised that within the first 100 days of his administration he would move to institute a fixed election date, term limits for the prime minister, and impeachment of parliamentarians for gross misconduct (Gleaner, 8 February 2016; 2016 Throne Speech). Ten years on, none of the three exists in law; the constitutional reform process of 2023–2024 did not adopt them, they do not appear in the JLP's 2025 manifesto, and in September 2025 he won the third consecutive term that his own term-limit promise would have barred (Jamaica Elections 2025 debate record; manifesto analyses, 2025–2026). These were the promises that would have bound the promise-maker; they are the clearest unkept words in the file.
  • The republic — promised for 2025, retracted. In June 2022 his government announced Jamaica would become a republic by the September 2025 general election. The Constitution (Amendment) (Republic) Bill was tabled on 10 December 2024 and sent to a joint select committee; in March 2025 the timeline was retracted, in April 2025 he accepted the goal had been "dashed at this time" and blamed the opposition leader, and the bill lapsed on dissolution. The PNP's position — that it would not support the phased bill unless Jamaica's final court (CCJ accession replacing the Privy Council) was settled in the same phase, and its refusal to sign the Constitutional Reform Committee's report — is recorded as the other side of a genuine two-party deadlock; the promise, however, was his, and as of November 2026 no referendum has been held or scheduled and the parties remain unagreed on the final court (Republic Bill record; Ministry statements, June and October 2026). The related 2016 commitment to a referendum on the Caribbean Court of Justice was likewise never held.
  • The first wage step — short of the announced figure. The 2025 campaign promised J$18,500 in the first budget on the road to J$32,000; the delivered step was J$17,000 from 1 July 2026, explained by Melissa (Tests 3 and 4). An explained shortfall is still a shortfall against the number spoken.
  • His own pay — the 2023 word reversed. "I have declined my salary increase" (May 2023) held until February 2026, when acceptance of the full J$28.5 million was announced, retroactive to September 2025 (Test 3). The 2023 statement's own reservation — that a new mandate might allow reconsideration — is on the record; so is the fact that the reconsideration was announced after the election, not put to the voters during it.

The long arc, factually: Holness first held the office at 39, for eleven weeks in 2011–2012, as Andrew Golding's chosen successor, then lost the 2011 election and rebuilt the JLP in opposition around the 2016 governance pledges above. Winning in 2016, he governed as a fiscal consolidator and security hawk: debt down every year, the SOE as his signature crime instrument, and a steadily worsening murder count that peaked after COVID. The final third of the record reverses the security failure by ordinary policing while the courts struck down the emergency regime that preceded it, and adds a catastrophe — Melissa — that no promise contemplated. His word splits along a consistent line: on the economy, where the promises bound his government's budgets, they were kept with unusual fidelity; on the constitution, where the promises would have bound the prime minister himself, none of them were kept at all.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Andrew Holness presents the series with a genuinely two-sided file. The institutions of Jamaica demonstrably work against him and survive: the Integrity Commission investigated the prime minister twice and an independent prosecutor — not the prime minister — announced both criminal outcomes; the courts voided five years of his emergency declarations and ruled against him in his own litigation; the electoral commission counted his opponents into 14 new seats; INDECOM rebukes his security minister in public. Against that stands the other shelf: a prime minister whose own statutory declarations sat uncertified for years while he sued to terminate the investigation into them; five years of governance by emergency decree in poor communities, ended by the Senate and the courts rather than by his restraint, with thousands detained and few charged; a constitutional amendment whose benefit ran to one sitting prosecutor; police killings up 65 per cent in the very year murders halved; a hurricane recovery that, a year on, had paid half the households it assessed; and a decade-old trio of promises — fixed election dates, term limits, impeachment — that would have bound him personally and were never kept, alongside a republic promised for 2025 and quietly retracted. He kept his word where it bound his budgets and broke it where it would have bound his office. The record is correctable in the open: certification of his declarations, the conclusion of the Reid trial and of his own judicial review, the INDECOM trend, and the Melissa payment timetable his government has now published will each move these grades.
