The shot as a chain

A shot is five links in order. Power starts in the floor and ends in your fingertips; each link hands energy and direction to the next. When shots go wrong, work the chain from the floor up — don't rebuild the release for a balance problem.

LINK 1

Feet & balance

Feet about shoulder-width, shooting-side foot a half-step ahead, weight even, knees soft. You should be able to be nudged and not fall over.

WHAT WORKS Balance before everything: a shooter who lands where they took off is balanced; drifting forward, back, or sideways on landing is the tell that the base moved.

COACH NOTE — The 1-2 step vs. hop debate is smaller than it sounds. What matters is arriving balanced and on time with the catch. Pick the footwork that gets you set before the ball arrives, and keep it.
LINK 2

The set point

The ball rises from the catch to one repeatable spot — roughly above and in front of your shooting-side eye — where the shooting hand is under/behind the ball, wrist loaded.

WHAT WORKS One repeatable set point. Great shooters differ in where theirs sits; none of them have three.

LIGHT NOTE — If you can see the rim under the ball at your set point, your eyes and the ball aren't fighting for the same space.
LINK 3

The guide hand's real job

The guide (non-shooting) hand steadies the ball on the way up — then gets out of the way. It adds no power and no aim.

WHAT WORKS Guide hand on the side of the ball, fingers up, leaving before release. A guide thumb that stays and pushes is one of the most common causes of left–right misses (for a right-hander, a late thumb flick pushes the ball left).

COACH NOTE — Test it: shoot ten one-handed shots from close range. If your normal shot flies straighter than your one-handed shot, your guide hand is doing a job it shouldn't. If the one-handed shots spray, the problem is in the shooting hand itself.
LINK 4

Release & follow-through

The shooting arm extends, the wrist snaps, and the ball leaves off the index and middle fingers with backspin. Hold the finish — arm up, wrist flopped, fingers pointing at the rim — until the ball lands.

WHAT WORKS Backspin is not decoration: it softens the ball's bounce off rim and board, and a consistent spin is a visible signature of a consistent release.

LIGHT NOTE — Dropping your arm the instant the ball leaves changes nothing about that shot — but it rushes the next one. Hold the finish; it's free information about whether you rushed.
LINK 5

Arc

The ball should arrive falling, not flying flat. A good shot peaks well above the rim and drops in at roughly a 45–52° entry angle.

WHAT WORKS The physics in the next section: a steeper entry makes the rim a bigger target — up to a point. Too much arc costs control and energy.

The physics of arc, in plain words

The rim is 18 inches (45 cm) across; the ball is about 9.5 inches (24 cm) across. Shot flat, the ball sees the rim edge-on — the usable opening shrinks to a slit, and only a near-perfect shot fits. Shot so it falls into the rim, the ball sees the opening from above, closer to its true size. That is the whole secret of arc: the steeper the entry (up to a point), the bigger the target effectively is, and the more a slightly-off shot can still drop.

The trade-off, printed plainly: every extra bit of height costs release speed you must produce exactly right, gives wind and small errors more time to act outdoors, and makes distance control harder. Research on real shots puts the sweet spot at an entry angle around 45–52° — steep enough to open the rim up, flat enough to control. A rainbow that enters at 60°+ is not "more forgiving"; it is a harder shot to repeat.

WHAT REALLY HASN'T "More arc is always better." It isn't — past the sweet spot, makes go down, not up. Flat is bad and moon-ball is bad; the middle is the lesson.

COACH NOTE — A quick floor test for arc: stand at the free-throw line and have a partner hold a hand as high as they can reach midway. If your shot doesn't clear it comfortably, your entry angle is likely under 40° and the rim is a smaller target than it needs to be.

LIGHT NOTE — Experiment: hold speed steady and raise the angle 2° at a time. Watch the entry angle climb — and notice how the shot gets harder to repeat once you pass the low 50s.
COACH NOTE — The lesson to take to the court: at free-throw distance there is a family of angle/speed pairs that score, not one magic pair. Forgiving shots live in the middle of that family — entry near 45–52° — because a small error still drops. Shots at the edge of the family (very flat, or barely enough speed) only score when everything is perfect. Build your real shot in the middle of the family.
What this model leaves out — named, not hidden: no air resistance, no spin, no rim or backboard bounce physics, and your release is a perfect point. Real balls slow a little in the air and backspin changes how they meet the rim. The model is honest for its lesson — arc and entry angle — and is not a shooting simulator.

The free throw

The free throw is the only shot in basketball taken with no defender, no clock pressure on the motion, and the same distance every time — which makes it the purest test of routine in the sport.

WHAT WORKS A repeatable pre-shot routine. Same dribbles, same breath, same words, same look at the rim, every time. Why it works: under pressure, attention narrows and working memory shrinks. A routine moves the shot onto well-worn rails — the body runs a memorised sequence instead of solving the shot fresh while nervous. Studies of pre-performance routines across sports consistently find they protect performance under pressure; the routine's exact contents matter far less than that it never changes.

WHAT WORKS One external focus. Pick one target — the front of the rim, the back of the rim, a hook above it — and keep your eyes there. Research on attention in motor skills finds that focusing on the target (external) beats focusing on your own body parts (internal) for accuracy.

WHAT TO TRY Your exhale as the trigger. Many shooters start the motion on a slow breath out. It times the shot and settles the shoulders. Try it for two weeks of logged free throws and let the record judge.

WHAT REALLY HASN'T Thinking about the last miss, the score, or the crowd. Not a character flaw — a bandwidth problem. The mental game that works is smaller: routine, breath, target, shoot, next one. Elite free-throw shooters describe the line as boring on purpose.

COACH NOTE — Practice the routine, not just the shot: in training, step off the line between every free throw and run the full routine each time, exactly as in a game. Ten routine free throws teach more than fifty catch-and-fling ones — and log them as their own spot in your record, because game free throws and practice-line free throws are different skills until the routine joins them.

Faults & fixes — read the miss

A miss is information. Where the ball goes wrong, consistently, points at the link that broke. One wild miss means nothing; a pattern of ten is a diagnosis. Tap a miss pattern to open its story, its fix, and its drill.

Missing SHORT — hitting the front rim or falling shy Tap to open · Tap again to close

The story: the ball simply didn't get enough energy, or got it late. Usual causes: not enough legs (shooting with the arm alone), a pause between the dip and the rise that bleeds power, stopping the follow-through early, or tired legs late in a session.

The fix: shoot up before you shoot out — let the legs and arm rise as one motion, and finish tall on your toes with the follow-through held. Check your landing: landing well behind your takeoff point often means the power went backward instead of up.

The drill: one-hand form shots, one step from the rim, finishing on your toes — 3 sets of 10. Then step back one pace only when 8 of 10 drop.

LIGHT NOTE — Short late in practice is often just fatigue, not form. That's the session telling you your quality capacity is ending (see Practice Design).
Missing LONG — back rim, or over the rim entirely Tap to open · Tap again to close

The story: too much energy, or energy aimed too flat. Usual causes: pushing with the legs and throwing with the arm (double power), a flat trajectory that needs extra speed to arrive, or leaning/falling forward into the shot.

The fix: add arc before you subtract power — a flatter shot must be faster to reach, and faster is harder to control. Aim to drop the ball down into the rim: finish with your elbow at least at eye height and the ball peaking clearly above the board's top edge on mid-range shots.

The drill: "rainbow" shooting from the free-throw line — deliberately exaggerate the arc for 10 shots, then settle to your normal arc for 10. You are calibrating where your middle is.

Missing LEFT (right-handed shooter) Tap to open · Tap again to close

The story: something pushed or pulled the ball across your body. Usual causes: the guide-hand thumb flicking at release, the shooting elbow flaring out so the hand finishes across the face, or your whole body rotating (shoulders opening) during the shot. Mirror everything here if you shoot left-handed.

The fix: guide hand leaves early and finishes open, away from the ball — it should look like it's waving, not throwing. Shooting hand finishes pointing at the rim, not across your face.

The drill: guide-hand-off shooting — shoot with the guide hand hovering a centimetre off the ball (touching nothing at release) from close range, 20 shots. Awkward for a day, revealing for a season.

Missing RIGHT (right-handed shooter) Tap to open · Tap again to close

The story: the mirror problem: the ball starts off-line from the set point. Usual causes: the ball sitting too far to the shooting side at the set point so the arm pushes diagonally, the shooting hand under the side of the ball instead of behind it, or feet pointed one way while the shoulders aim another.

The fix: align the simple things first — toes, knees, and the ball's starting spot all on one line to the rim — and check the ball rests on your finger pads with the hand behind it, seams level, at the set point.

The drill: wall shooting — stand a metre from a wall, shoot your full motion so the ball rises and falls back into your hands, hitting the same wall spot. No rim to chase means the line of the shot is all you can watch. 3 × 15.

COACH NOTE — Before changing anything, film ten shots from the front (see Film It). Left–right patterns are the easiest to misread from memory and the easiest to see on video: guide thumb, elbow path, and finish direction are all right there.

Practice design — how practice actually builds a shot

The difficulty ramp WHAT WORKS

Good shooting practice climbs a ramp, in this order, and only moves up when the level below holds:

  1. Form shooting — close range, slow, one link at a time. Builds the motion.
  2. Spot shooting — set shots from game spots, at game spots' distances, keeping score by spot.
  3. Game speed — off the catch, off the dribble, off movement, at the speed a defender forces.
  4. Pressure — a consequence, a clock, a score to beat, a teammate watching. The shot you own is the one that survives this level.

WHAT REALLY HASN'T Shooting drills with no game pressure, sold as game training. Undefended, unhurried, uncounted reps build an undefended, unhurried shot. They have a place — level 1 and 2 of the ramp — but a practice that never climbs is rehearsing a game that doesn't exist.

Capacity: quality reps are finite

Here is a limit nobody can train away: a session's honest capacity is the number of reps you can shoot while your form holds. Past that point you are not practising your shot — you are practising the tired, broken version of it, and practice makes permanent whatever it repeats. In Your Record below you can mark where a session's quality ended; over weeks, that number is your real capacity, and growing it (with fitness and with shorter, more frequent sessions) beats pretending it isn't there.

Self-energy: legs are the battery

A shot is powered from the floor, so tired legs flatten arc and shorten range — every player has felt the fourth-quarter shot come up short. Two honest uses of that fact: (1) end sessions before the battery is empty, while form still holds; (2) as a labelled, occasional mode — a few shots taken under honest fatigue at the end of a workout (log them as Tired legs in the record, kept separate from your fresh numbers) teach you what your shot does when it's tired, which is exactly the shot a close game will ask for. The trade-off is printed with it: tired reps teach a tired motion if they become the habit, so this mode is a spice, not the meal.

Keeping score honestly

Memory lies kindly: we remember the makes. The record below counts makes by spot, because "I'm a good shooter" is a feeling and "I make 41% from the left wing and 63% from the right corner" is a fact you can practise from. Log attempts and makes exactly as they happened — a record padded to feel good is worse than no record, because every later decision reads from it.

COACH NOTE — Sample size honesty: 20 shots from a spot tells you almost nothing (a true 50% shooter will shoot anywhere from 30% to 70% across 20 shots by luck alone). Judge a spot at 100+ logged shots. The record shows your totals growing so you can see when a number has earned your trust.

Shot selection — what a good shot is

Form decides whether your shot goes in. Selection decides whether the shot was worth taking. The documented analytics picture, in plain words — points per shot, roughly, at high levels of the game:

ShotTypical returnWhy
Layups & shots at the rim~1.2 pts / shotClosest target, highest make rate, most fouls drawn
Free throws~1.5 pts / trip (2 shots)Unguarded, set distance — the most efficient "shot" in the game
Corner three~1.15 pts / shotShortest three (6.7 m on an NBA court) at three-point value
Above-the-break three~1.05 pts / shotLonger, but three points still pays for a ~35% make rate
Long two (just inside the line)~0.8 pts / shotNearly as hard as a three, worth one point less — the worst trade in the modern game

WHAT WORKS The core lesson survives every level check: step back, not in. A shot one step inside the three-point line gives up a point for almost no extra accuracy. And an open shot early beats a contested shot late — "good" is about openness and location, not about who takes it.

The limits, printed at the same size: these are league averages for elite players. Your numbers are yours — if your record says you make 55% of your mid-range shots and 22% of your threes, your good shot today is the mid-range one, and the analytics lesson for you is to practise the three until your own numbers move. Averages describe populations; your record describes you. Also: shot selection is a team act — the best shot is often the one your pass creates for a teammate.

Off-ball basics, in brief

  • Spacing: stand where your defender must choose between you and the rim — usually a pass away, outside the line. Two teammates in one spot guard each other.
  • Cut when ignored: if your defender turns their head to the ball, cut behind them to the rim. The easiest layups are earned standing still, then leaving.
  • Be shot-ready: feet set and hands showing before the pass arrives. Most "slow releases" are actually late preparation.

Drills — with honest transfer notes

A drill's value is what survives into a game. Each drill below carries its transfer note: what it honestly does and doesn't do.

Form ladder

One-hand shots from 1 m, then step back a pace after every 5 makes, out to the free-throw line.

Transfer: high for mechanics, low for games. This builds the motion the other drills will stress-test. Never skip it; never let it be the whole practice.

Five-spot scoring

10 shots from each of 5 spots (both corners, both wings, top). Log every spot separately in your record.

Transfer: high — same spots, same distances as games, and it feeds your makes-by-spot truth. Add movement between spots as you improve.

Beat your record

From your best spot: shoot sets of 10. Today's set must beat your logged average to "win" the day.

Transfer: the stake is your own record — standing with yourself. Real mild pressure, honestly labelled as practice pressure, not game pressure.

Catch-and-shoot on the move

A partner (or a chair and a self-toss) feeds you as you cut to a spot; shoot on arrival, game speed, alternate spots randomly.

Transfer: the highest on this page short of playing — random order, movement, and a pass to read. This is where blocked practice becomes game practice.

Plus–minus free throws

Shoot pairs: make = +1, miss = −1, in sets of 10 shots. Try to finish positive.

Transfer: builds the routine under a small, honest consequence. It imitates scoreboard pressure the way a photograph imitates weather — useful, partial, labelled as such.

Tired-legs finisher WHAT TO TRY

At the very end of a workout only: 10 shots, logged separately as Tired legs.

Transfer: teaches you your tired shot's tendencies (usually short — see Faults). Occasional by design; if tired reps become most of your reps, they become your form.

Film it — the real check

This page cannot see your shot. No text can. The real checks are a camera and a coach's eye, and here is exactly how to use the camera: film from the side (shooting-hand side, full body in frame) to read balance, landing drift, set point, and arc — and from the front to read the guide hand, elbow path, and left–right line. Ten shots from each angle tells you more than a month of guessing from memory. Show the same footage to any real coach and you have given them what they actually need.

Both shelves — where good shooters disagree

Contested topics, both sides at the same size. Neither side here is a straw man.

One-motion vs. two-motion shooting

THE ONE-MOTION SCHOOL

The ball flows from catch to release in one continuous rise — no pause at the set point. Advocates argue: energy from the legs transfers without a leak, the shot is quicker and extends range (smaller players especially can reach three-point distance), and one rhythm is simpler to repeat. Many of the longest-range shooters in the modern game shoot this way.

THE TWO-MOTION SCHOOL

The ball rises, settles briefly at a set point — often higher, above the head — then releases. Advocates argue: the set point gives a stable platform for mid-range shots, pull-ups, and shooting over defenders, and generations of great mid-range shooters were built this way. The pause, they say, is control, not a leak.

WHAT TO TRY The honest verdict: both schools have produced elite shooters, which means the motion count is not the deciding variable — repeatability and range are. If you struggle for distance, experiment toward one-motion. If your mid-range pull-up is your living, the two-motion platform may be your home. Two weeks each, logged, beats two years of arguing.

Does the elbow have to be perfectly in?

THE CLASSIC TEACHING

"Elbow under the ball, pointing at the rim" has been coached for a century. Its logic is clean: an elbow in line means the arm pushes in line, and the ball goes where the arm pushes. For beginners spraying shots sideways, it is often the fastest single fix.

THE REVISIONIST CASE

Slow-motion of elite shooters shows many elbows sitting slightly out — because shoulders, not geometry diagrams, decide what's comfortable, and forcing an elbow in can twist the wrist and shoulder into worse positions. What the film actually shows as universal is not elbow position but finish position: the shooting hand ends pointing at the rim.

The synthesis most evidence supports: coach the elbow as a range, not a point — roughly under the ball, never forced — and judge by the ball's line and your logged left–right pattern, not by a photograph of the "perfect" angle.

WHAT REALLY HASN'T "The perfect textbook form" — one shape for every body. Bodies differ in shoulder width, arm length, mobility, and strength history; the history of great shooting is a museum of different-looking shots that share the same few outcomes: balance, repeatability, backspin, arc. Copy the outcomes. A form copied joint-by-joint from a player built differently from you is a costume, not a shot.

Blocked vs. random practice — the finding that feels wrong

BLOCKED PRACTICE

50 shots from one spot, then 50 from the next. It feels great — makes climb within the session, confidence rises, and the shooter leaves believing the lesson landed. Motor-learning research calls this the performance effect: real, and mostly temporary.

RANDOM / VARIED PRACTICE

Spots, distances, and shot types shuffled, so no two shots in a row are the same. It feels worse — makes dip during the session. And the documented result, replicated across decades of motor-learning studies (the contextual-interference effect), is that it retains better: a week later, the random-practice group outperforms. Games are random practice by definition.

Printed as the evidence stands: use blocked practice early, to build a new motion; use random practice as soon as the motion exists, to keep it. If your practice always feels smooth, it is probably teaching less than it could.
