The Ruler's Test — Dickon Mitchell, Prime Minister of Grenada

Subject: Dickon Amiss Thomas Mitchell — Prime Minister of Grenada since 24 June 2022, and re-elected on 5 November 2026. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Grenada is a constitutional monarchy whose head of state, King Charles III, is represented by a Governor-General (Dame Cécile La Grenade) whose role is ceremonial, while executive power — command of the government, the budget, and the security services — sits with the prime minister. Premise check, November 2026: the brief's expectation is confirmed in its main line and corrected in one material particular. Mitchell, a lawyer who took over the National Democratic Congress (NDC) in 2021 when it held no seats at all, led it to victory on 23 June 2022 — 9 of 15 seats, 51.84% of the vote — defeating the long-serving Keith Mitchell (no relation) and his New National Party (NNP), which had held all 15 seats (2022 election results; Parliamentary Elections Office figures via regional reporting). He did not serve out a quiet first term to the constitutional deadline of October 2027. On 2 October 2026 he advised the Governor-General to dissolve Parliament, and on 4 October 2026 he announced a snap general election for 5 November 2026, almost a year early (Government Gazette No. 47, 2 October 2026; CMC reporting, October 2026). On 5 November 2026 the NDC won a landslide: 14 of 15 seats and about 61% of the valid vote (37,373 votes to the NNP's 22,618 on the Wikipedia tabulation of Parliamentary Elections Office figures; regional reporting of the same provisional count gives 37,369 to 22,567 — the two counts are printed separately here, not blended), on turnout of about 63.3–63.49%, down from 70.32% in 2022. The NNP, now led by Emmalin Pierre, kept only St. George North West, won by Adrian Joseph, who was sworn in as Opposition Leader on 17 November 2026; Pierre lost her own seat (CMC, 6 and 17 November 2026; OECS congratulations, November 2026). Mitchell was sworn in for a second term on 8 November 2026 and presented a new cabinet days later (CMC, 8 November 2026). He is therefore graded on a completed first term (24 June 2022 – 5 November 2026) — a full tenure the voters have just judged — plus the opening acts of the second term where they are already on the record (notably the Citizenship by Investment stance of 17 November 2026). The record graded is dominated by one event no ruler chose: Hurricane Beryl, which struck on 1 July 2024. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out the platform is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how it is done. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest single act of his first term applied a court's rule to everyone it covered, including people who had never voted for him. In March 2022 — before he took office — the High Court struck down the Pension Disqualification Act provisions that had denied pensions to public officers recruited from 1985; the outgoing Keith Mitchell government announced it would not appeal, estimating the obligation at EC$465 million up front and about EC$120 million a year for 37 years (Keith Mitchell national address, April 2022). Mitchell's incoming government began paying the outstanding pensions and gratuities in November 2022, and by the 2025 budget had paid EC$75.1 million in overdue pensions and gratuities to eligible retired public officers — retired teachers among them, the same group whose pay the previous government had docked during the 2018 industrial dispute (Government of Grenada budget reporting, March 2025; Grenadian Life analysis, 10 November 2026).
  • The January 2024 minimum-wage reform was written as one floor for the whole labour market, and widened who stood under it: the Minimum Wages Order 2023 (S.R.O. 42 of 2023), effective 1 January 2024, set a floor of EC$1,200 a month / EC$60 a day, raised domestic workers from EC$35 to EC$60 a day and elderly caregivers from EC$700 to EC$1,400 a month, and extended coverage to categories previously outside it — media workers, call-centre workers, construction helpers, sanitisation workers (Government Gazette notice; Labour Minister Claudette Joseph in Parliament, December 2023; regional reporting).
  • Where his own government's recovery money went wrong, the failure was named by his own finance minister in public, with an audit promised against the government's own contractors. In June 2025 Finance Minister Dennis Cornwall told reporters the Audit Department would investigate contractors who took Hurricane Beryl housing money and abandoned or botched the work — "persons who have collected government moneys and did not do what they should do with it… will have to account" — after the housing minister herself confirmed some Beryl-response houses were "a disaster" (CMC, 25 June 2025). The misconduct described is, at this stage, reported and under audit — ALLEGATION against the unnamed contractors, not a verdict — but the self-reporting is a fact about the government.
  • The oil and gas file was handled, on the government's documented account, by one published legal process applied to a foreign company with friends in the story: after establishing a Technical Working Group in 2022, engaging Global Petroleum Group (GPG) from 2023, serving notice, receiving GPG's 2 June 2026 representations, meeting it on 17 June 2026, and publishing the terminations in the official Gazette on 31 July 2026, the government terminated the 2016 memorandum (29 April 2026) and then the development licence and production sharing agreement (effective 31 July / announced 1–3 August 2026), with the full reasoning laid before a joint sitting of Parliament on 8 September 2026 (CMC, August–September 2026; Reuters, 3 August 2026). GPG disputes the termination's legality — see the against shelf.

Evidence against:

  • The pension settlement's second half created, on the opposition's and some lawyers' account, two classes of public officer under one roof. The Public Sector Employees Defined Contribution Pension Plan, effective 1 January 2025, requires a 3% contribution from the worker matched by 3% from government, with benefits riding on investment returns rather than a guaranteed share of salary. Lawyers involved in the original pension litigation argue — a contested legal opinion, recorded as such — that the new scheme is itself unconstitutional under section 92 of the Constitution (CMC, 31 December 2024). In the 2026 campaign, NNP leader Emmalin Pierre pledged to review the legislation so officers serving when the court ruled in 2022 could benefit from the 1958 defined-benefit plan, calling the new plan a "su su plan" and saying affected officers were "forced to join" it (Grenadian Life, 8 October 2026). The government answers that the plan extends a pension to the 3,000–4,500 workers who previously had none at all (CMC, October 2024 and December 2024) — both characterisations are printed because both are on the record; what is not disputed is that the two groups now retire under materially different rules, and which group a worker falls into turns on when and how they were hired.
  • The state's biggest revenue source is, by design, a second door into citizenship that money opens and wages cannot. The Citizenship by Investment (CBI) programme — run since 2024 by the Investment Migration Agency (IMA) — grants Grenadian citizenship for a contribution from US$235,000 to the National Transformation Fund or approved real-estate investment (IMA reporting; IMF 2025 Article IV staff report, January 2026). That is the programme's stated design, inherited from 2013 and continued and re-legislated under Mitchell, not an abuse of it; it is recorded here because Test 1 grades the source, and a citizenship that is sold to foreign investors while the diaspora's voting rights remained contested in court in October 2026 (Test 2) is two different relationships to the same passport.
  • Scrutiny of recovery spending ran ahead of answers. In the House Order Paper of 19 November 2025, Opposition Leader Emmalin Pierre tabled detailed questions — total spent on Build Back Better houses, lists of contractors, houses handed over by island, beneficiaries of the EC$15 million / 200-home mainland allocation, and the distribution of the CCRIF payout to farmers and fishermen — that were still questions, not answered accounts, sixteen months after Beryl (House of Representatives Order Paper No. 9 of 2025, 19 November 2025). A government that audits its own contractors (for shelf) and leaves the opposition's itemised questions standing unanswered in the same file has not yet run one transparent law over its largest spending programme.

Why this grade: The through-line of the term is a ruler who applied the big court ruling and the wage floor to everybody, and said so with money — and who simultaneously presided over a recovery programme whose beneficiary-level accounting was still being demanded in Parliament a year and a half on, and a pension reform whose fairness between two groups of officers is genuinely contested by the people it governs. Neither shelf cancels the other; that is what MIXED means here.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — retired public officers recruited after 1985, including retired teachers, who received EC$75.1 million in pensions and gratuities long denied them; the lowest-paid workers in newly covered categories (domestic workers, caregivers, media and call-centre workers) under the 2024 wage floor; homeowners whose Beryl contractors are now subject to audit. Hurt — public officers moved into the defined-contribution plan who expected the 1958 defined-benefit terms (their loss is contested by the government, which notes they previously faced retirement on NIS alone); Beryl-affected families whose rebuilds were abandoned or botched by contractors before the audit was announced; ordinary Grenadians who cannot buy the citizenship that investors can.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts ruled against his own side, in public, and the rulings stood. On 12 November 2024 the High Court dismissed a defamation suit brought by his own finance minister, Gregory Bowen, against the Grenada Broadcasting Network, reaffirming the media's right to report matters of public interest and ordering Bowen to pay EC$12,500 in costs (CIVICUS Monitor, November 2024). In October 2024 the same court system declared judicial corporal punishment unconstitutional (CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). No retaliation against either court or broadcaster appears in the record assembled here.
  • When his own snap election was challenged, he let the court decide it the day before polling — and the court, not he, had the last word. Former attorney general Cajeton Hood sued over voter registration and the constitutional basis of the dissolution; on 4 November 2026 Justice Agnes Actie dismissed the claim and upheld the legality of Mitchell's advice to the Governor-General, while noting that objections to an ongoing election belong to the post-election petition process (CMC, 4 November 2026; iWitness News via regional reporting, November 2026). The election proceeded under CARICOM (11-member mission) and OAS observation; the OAS congratulated the NDC while again recommending electoral reforms, as it had after the NNP's 15–0 win in 2018 (CARICOM mission statement, 31 October 2026; OAS reporting via post-election analysis, November 2026).
  • The institutional furniture of opposition survived a 14–1 result: the lone NNP member, Adrian Joseph, was sworn in as Opposition Leader on 17 November 2026 and immediately announced "relentless" scrutiny and a demand for a commission of enquiry into Project Polaris — in the government's own chamber, reported by the state-adjacent regional wire without interference (CMC, 17 November 2026). Before the landslide, opposition questions on Beryl spending were tabled and printed in the House Order Paper in the ordinary way (Order Paper, 19 November 2025).
  • The Eastern Caribbean Central Bank and the currency union were left alone: the EC dollar's 50-year peg continued untouched, and the IMF's Article IV process ran on its normal cycle with staff publishing recommendations the government has not adopted (IMF, 2025 Article IV, January 2026) — a referee publishing disagreement without consequence.

Evidence against:

  • The seat itself is heavily loaded onto one pair of shoulders. Mitchell has held, alongside the premiership, portfolios including National Security, Home Affairs, Public Administration, Information and Disaster Management, and Infrastructure and Physical Development (UN General Assembly listing, September 2025). In a state of about 115,000 people some concentration is structural; the documented breadth here — the PM as his own national-security, information and disaster minister through a hurricane recovery and a snap election — is nonetheless the kind of accumulation Test 2 exists to notice.
  • Media access narrowed in practice, on the media's own account. The Media Workers Association of Grenada criticised the government over press-freedom concerns after reporters and photographers were barred from a cabinet swearing-in — described by commentator Wayne Campbell as the first such barring in modern Grenadian history — and the weekly post-cabinet press briefing was dismantled in favour of information released on a "need to know basis" (Campbell commentary, February 2024, relaying MWAG's criticism; the underlying events date from the government's earlier period). Separately, CIVICUS records journalists' complaints through 2024–25 that officials failed to answer information requests and issued statements contradicting the Government Gazette on ministers' whereabouts (CIVICUS Monitor, January 2025). These are the press's documented complaints, printed as such; no law restricting the press was enacted, and the GBN ruling above went the press's way.
  • A 14–1 House is a mandate and a structural risk at once: 61% of the vote converted to 93% of seats under first-past-the-post, leaving one opposition voice in the elected chamber (Parliamentary Elections Office provisional results, November 2026; Kingston Mouth analysis, 6 November 2026). That arithmetic is the electoral system's, not Mitchell's manufacture — the same system gave the NNP 15–0 in 2013 and 2018 — but the check it weakens is weakened on his watch and to his benefit, and his government's conduct inside that near-empty chamber is what the second term will be graded on.
  • The diaspora-voting question was taken to court rather than settled in the open before the writ: Hood's October 2026 claim asked the High Court to determine who among Grenadians residing overseas is entitled to be registered and remain on the voters' list, and Mitchell announced the election date before that question was heard, with Hood publicly disappointed that the timetable left no room for a ruling first (CMC, 21 October 2026). The 4 November dismissal resolved the dissolution challenge; the underlying registration interpretation, on the record assembled here, was overtaken by the election rather than answered before it.

Why this grade: No referee was captured, punished or abolished on this record — courts ruled against his minister and for his election in the same tenure, observers watched, the opposition leader took his seat shooting. But the grip risks are real and documented: an unusually personal concentration of portfolios, a press corps reporting thinner access, and a chamber now holding one opponent. The institutions held; the holding was not always light.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the Grenada Broadcasting Network and the wider press, vindicated in court against a sitting minister; voters, whose election ran under two observer missions with a court-tested dissolution; the parliamentary opposition as an institution, which retained its leader's office and question rights. Hurt — journalists seeking routine government information, on their association's account; Grenadians abroad whose registration status went into the election unresolved; future scrutiny itself, which must now be carried by a single opposition MP in the elected House.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The defining wobble of the term was a Category 4 hurricane, and the answer was immediate, open, and built from pre-agreed instruments rather than improvisation or denial. Beryl struck on 1 July 2024, causing damage the IMF puts at over 16% of GDP (Mitchell's own early estimate was roughly a third of annual output — the two figures are printed separately, as sourced), damaging or destroying roughly 98% of buildings on Carriacou and Petite Martinique on the government's account, over 90% uninhabitable on regional reporting (IMF 2024 Article IV, February 2025; Government of Grenada via CMC, 2024; Caribbean Compass, 2025). The government triggered the world's first use of a sovereign bond "hurricane clause" — deferring over US$17 million in payments, including US$12.5 million on the 2030 bond — collected a US$44 million CCRIF payout on 16 July 2024 (Grenada's first), suspended the fiscal rules to fund recovery, and reported more than EC$100 million spent on response and recovery by the March 2025 budget (Reuters, August 2024; Ministry of Finance notice, September 2024; 2025 budget reporting). The no-good-choice clause applies here, by its mechanics: the options actually open in July 2024 were (a) hold the Fiscal Resilience Act's primary-balance rule and starve the recovery of two flattened islands, (b) borrow commercially at post-disaster prices and break the debt trajectory, or (c) suspend the rule openly, use the pre-arranged CCRIF insurance and the hurricane clause, and publish a return date. Each carried real harm; he took (c), the least-harm door, and was honest about the trap while in it — the suspension and the 2027 return were stated publicly and repeated to the IMF. Origin check, as the clause requires: the trap was not of his building — the hurricane clause itself was pioneered by a previous Grenadian government in the 2015 restructuring, and the fiscal space came substantially from CBI savings banked before the storm, which the IMF credits as "prudent savings of recent CBI revenues" (IMF, October 2025). He used inherited instruments well; he did not invent them, and this shelf does not pretend otherwise.
  • The United States radar request was the term's sharpest foreign pushback, and it was answered by slowing down in public rather than folding or slamming the door. When Washington asked in 2025 to install radar and technical personnel at Maurice Bishop International Airport — an airport named for the prime minister whose 1983 killing preceded a US invasion, in a region Washington was pressuring over Venezuela — Mitchell welcomed the "raging debate," told his "DM with the PM" programme no decision had been taken, made a statement to Parliament that Grenada would enter no agreement violating domestic, regional or international law, cited the civilian character of the airport and unresolved technical and safety questions, and let Washington's deadline pass without the requested answer (CMC / Caribbean Today, October 2025). Later reporting records Grenada as having formally declined, Mitchell confirming the request was "off the table" (regional reporting, 2026 — a weaker-sourced item, printed as reported). Trinidad and Tobago, by contrast, accepted a US radar in Tobago. The other option — cooperation — was visibly considered for months and declined with stated reasons; under the Test's rule, considering and declining openly is not against-evidence.
  • The GPG termination shows the same pattern against a corporate pushback: when GPG accused the government of blocking a US$350 million drilling campaign it said it was ready to launch, the government did not simply denounce it — it published its review, briefed a joint sitting of Parliament in detail (8 September 2026), and Mitchell stated arbitration remained open and that the two sides could "sit down and settle this matter" (CMC, August–September 2026). GPG's counter-claims — US$200 million invested, US$20 million advanced, a commercial discovery at Nutmeg 2 in 2017 — are GPG's, recorded as its ALLEGATION-level account against the government's finding of unmet obligations; the 2017 discovery was never appraised (Reuters, 3 August 2026).
  • On the CBI wobble of 2026, when another option became available he considered it openly rather than refusing to look: pressed by the EU's demand that the five Eastern Caribbean programmes close by 1 June 2028 or risk Schengen visa-free access, Mitchell told reporters on 17 November 2026 that Grenada intends to keep the programme to at least 2032, has engaged the US, UK and EU to demonstrate its vetting, is building a regional regulator (the Eastern Caribbean Citizenship by Investment Regulatory Authority), and — if closure is forced — asks the EU for a development package offsetting the lost revenue (post-swearing-in press conference reporting, 17 November 2026; European Commission letter of 25 June 2026 via Antigua and Barbuda's prime minister's office). Whether Brussels accepts any of this is unresolved; the considering, in the open, is the graded act.

Evidence against:

  • The pension wobble was answered by pressing on. Trade unions were, on the record, "cautious about the plan because of the approach adopted during the consultation process" from the start (Public Workers' Union position via CMC, October 2024); lawyers who won the 2022 ruling argued the replacement scheme was unconstitutional; the opposition made its alleged unfairness a 2026 campaign pledge. The plan nonetheless took effect on 1 January 2025 on the government's timetable, and by May 2026 the government was signing its administration and investment agreements under the theme "Securing Tomorrow, Today" (CMC, May 2026). Consultation happened — years of actuarial work and stakeholder engagement are documented — but on the central complaint, that serving officers were being moved off the terms the court had restored, the documented answer was implementation, not revision.
  • The recovery's second year wobbled, and the adjustment lagged the complaint. A year after Beryl, Carriacou residents were still reporting roofless homes under tarpaulins and a part-time desalination plant forcing them to buy water (Caribbean Compass interviews, spring 2025); the government's own finance minister was by June 2025 publicly warning its own housing contractors, and the housing minister conceding some rebuilds were "a disaster" (CMC, 25 June 2025). The audit announcement was an adjustment; it came eleven months after the storm, after the failures, not before them.
  • Parliament's pushback was sometimes answered by absence: Mitchell's radar statement to Parliament was delivered with opposition legislators boycotting the sitting (CMC, October 2025) — the boycott was the opposition's choice, recorded as such, but the term's most sensitive security question was thus debated in a chamber the other side had walked out of, and the record shows no government move to bring them back in before proceeding.

Why this grade: On the two existential wobbles — the hurricane and the superpower's radar — the record is genuinely strong: open reasoning, pre-agreed instruments used as designed, deadlines allowed to pass, alternatives considered in daylight. On the domestic wobbles — pensions and the recovery's failing contractors — the pattern reverses: consultation without revision, and correction after complaint rather than before. A ruler who answers the big wobbles openly and the small ones late earns MIXED, and the split is the finding.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the people of Carriacou and Petite Martinique, whose recovery was financed within weeks by insurance and deferred debt rather than awaited appropriations; Grenadian taxpayers as a whole, spared post-disaster commercial borrowing; Grenada's sovereignty position, which survived a US military request at a historically loaded airport without a rupture — Secretary of State Rubio publicly congratulated Mitchell on re-election on 6 November 2026 (US State Department statement). Hurt — GPG and its backers, whose 18-year position was terminated (they dispute its legality; arbitration may follow); public officers whose pension objection was heard and overridden; Beryl households whose contractor failures cost them a year.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the sister islands, the unestablished workers, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The sister islands were counted first, not last, in the term's crisis. Carriacou and Petite Martinique — together one constituency of fifteen, easily outvoted by the mainland — received the centre of the Beryl response: over EC$100 million in response and recovery spending reported by March 2025, building materials and tax relief for affected homeowners, property tax waived for 2025 on damaged properties, VAT obligations suspended for affected businesses, and a stated ambition, repeated in the October 2025 Nationally Determined Contribution, to rebuild them as the region's first 100% renewable, climate-resilient islands (2025 budget reporting; Government of Grenada relief measures, 2024; NDC 3.0, October 2025). A dedicated Minister for Carriacou and Petite Martinique Affairs sat in cabinet throughout. In November 2026 Carriacou's voters returned the government — anecdotal, but the taxi driver's verdict recorded by a visiting commentator was "this government has helped Carriacou people, man" (CaribMagPlus commentary, November 2026).
  • The workers the old system counted out were the stated centre of the labour reforms. Grenada ran a parallel public service of roughly 3,600 contract workers beside 3,300 established officers, with inferior terms and virtually no pension (Department of Public Administration via Catalyste+ case study, 2025). The government's regularisation programme moved to bring them in, and the defined-contribution pension — whatever its contested terms for established officers (Test 1) — was designed for, and first justified by, the 3,000-plus workers who previously retired with nothing beyond NIS (Mitchell, October 2024). The May 2023 NIS unemployment benefit — 50% of insurable income for up to 13 weeks — created a floor that had not existed (NIS via regional reporting, 2023).
  • The poor were counted in the term's ordinary arithmetic, not only its emergencies: the 2024 minimum-wage floor and its expanded categories (Test 1); free tuition at T.A. Marryshow Community College and the New Life Organisation, reported in the 2025 budget to have saved parents and guardians over EC$1 million, alongside a free school-meals programme (2025 budget reporting); and an unemployment rate the government puts at 7.5% by October 2025, down from 16.6% in 2021 — its figure, printed as its figure; the IMF records the same improving direction on its own estimates (Government of Grenada, 2025; IMF 2025 Article IV, January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • A year after Beryl, some of the counted were still living under tarps. Spring 2025 reporting from Carriacou records "way too many blue tarps on roofless homes," no rainwater collection without roofs, and a desalination plant running part-time (Caribbean Compass, 2025). The recovery's money totals are the government's; its completion rate was still being demanded, island by island, in Parliament in November 2025 (Order Paper, 19 November 2025). Counting a place in the budget and finishing its roofs are different acts, and on the documented record the second lagged the first.
  • The fiscal adjustment, such as it was, leaned on a revenue source that prices out the counted-in: CBI receipts of EC$381.6 million (2023), EC$553.7 million (2024) and EC$165.9 million (2025) on the Fiscal Resilience Oversight Committee's figures — a separate IMA-based accounting puts the 2025 net contribution to central government at about EC$153 million; the two figures measure different things and are printed separately, not blended (2025 FROC annual report via November 2026 reporting; IMA quarterly reporting via CEO World, November 2026). Mitchell himself puts CBI at about 12% of government revenue (2026 campaign reporting). A state substantially funded by selling passports to wealthy foreigners, while its own young people face still-elevated youth unemployment (about 22% on one 2026 labour analysis, down from 32% — a weaker-sourced estimate, printed as such), has a counting problem built into its model even when each individual policy is inclusive.
  • Grenadians' own mobility was hurt on his watch by partners' decisions he could not control — recorded here as hurt, not as his act: the United States added Grenada to its visa-bond programme effective 2 April 2026, requiring refundable deposits of US$10,000–20,000 from eligible visitor-visa applicants, and made the programme permanent on 3 August 2026 (US programme reporting via CMC, November 2026); and the EU's 2028 CBI deadline puts every Grenadian's Schengen visa-free access — not just investors' — in negotiation (European Commission, June 2026). The second term's central diplomatic file is whether ordinary Grenadians keep a door that the investor programme may close.
  • Project Polaris, the flagship promise to the sick, was still a construction file at term's end: a planned 250-bed climate-resilient hospital costing about US$250 million (17% of 2024 GDP), executed off-budget through a statutory vehicle, with a March 2026 groundbreaking reported by the project office, US$185 million said by campaign reporting to be secured, an OPEC Fund agreement (US$30 million in one account, US$60 million in another — printed separately, not blended), and Afreximbank talks reported stalled over contractual conditions (IMF 2025 Article IV Box 2, January 2026; BusinessDay, November 2026; CMC, 17 November 2026). The IMF flags the long-term operating costs and the need to bring statutory-entity investment under equivalent reporting and auditing (IMF, October 2025). The new Opposition Leader's first act was to demand a commission of enquiry into what was spent and what liabilities remain (CMC, 17 November 2026). Patients of the existing 198-bed General Hospital were counted in the plan; they were not yet counted in a building.

Why this grade: The direction of the term was toward the counted-out — sister islands first in the crisis, unestablished workers first in the pension design, the lowest-paid first in the wage order — and the money followed the words further than in most dossiers in this series. What holds the grade at MIXED is the gap between counting and completing: tarpaulins a year after the storm, a hospital still on paper, and a fiscal model whose engine is a door open only to the rich.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — residents of Carriacou and Petite Martinique (recovery spending, tax relief, materials); roughly 3,600 formerly parallel public workers brought toward regular terms and a pension; minimum-wage workers and students at TAMCC and New Life; the unemployed, through the 2023 NIS benefit. Hurt — Beryl households whose homes were still unroofed a year on; Grenadian travellers facing US visa bonds and threatened Schengen access; public patients awaiting the Polaris hospital; young jobseekers, whose unemployment remained roughly triple the headline rate on the available estimate.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign pledges and public statements vs. the record since 24 June 2022, with dates; the two axes — word kept, and whether it helped or hurt — graded independently.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The pensions owed — paid. The NDC's 2022 "transformational agenda" promised to honour the public officers' pension rights the courts had upheld. Payments began in November 2022, five months after taking office, and reached EC$75.1 million in overdue pensions and gratuities by the 2025 budget (Government of Grenada; Grenadian Life, November 2026). Crossing 1 on the two axes: word kept, and it helped — retired officers, teachers among them, received money owed for years.
  • The wage floor — raised, on the date given. Promised in the 2024 budget debate, delivered 1 January 2024 under S.R.O. 42 of 2023, with the expanded categories the Labour Minister named in Parliament (December 2023). Crossing 1: kept, and it helped the lowest-paid — while noting the floor (EC$1,200/month) remains below one estimate of a single adult's basic living cost (EC$1,400–1,500/month) in a 2026 labour analysis; a kept promise can still be an insufficient one, and the insufficiency is recorded here, not hidden by the keeping.
  • Free tuition and the unemployment benefit — delivered early. Free tuition at TAMCC and New Life and the NIS unemployment benefit (May 2023) both arrived in the term's first half (2025 budget reporting; NIS, 2023). Crossing 1: kept, and helped.
  • Fiscal discipline — the suspension was announced with its own expiry, and the expiry has been kept to in word and IMF-verified trajectory. The fiscal rules were suspended for Beryl in 2024 with a stated return; in 2026 Mitchell confirmed publicly: "We are returning in 2027 to the rules under the Fiscal Responsibility Act… Fiscal discipline is absolutely important" (Mitchell, 2026, via regional reporting). The IMF's January 2026 assessment records the commitment to restore the 1.5% of GDP primary-balance floor from 2027 and projects general government debt reaching the 60% target by 2033 — revised from its February 2025 projection of central government debt reaching target by 2030; the two projections measure different perimeters and dates and are printed separately (IMF, February 2025 and January 2026). The 2027 return is a promise whose keeping is not yet testable; it is recorded so the record can grade it later.
  • Developing the hydrocarbon patrimony "for the benefit of the Grenadian people" — the words were his government's mandate to its Technical Working Group (2022), and the termination of an 18-year non-performing licence is a defensible reading of them. No production resulted; the word graded here is the stewardship word, and the process (Test 1, Test 3) is the evidence. Crossing 3 is available on this file and is named: if the termination is read against the development promise, it is a word departed from in form that may yet help — the acreage is freed for a partner who will drill. That judgement is explicitly provisional on arbitration and on what happens to the blocks next.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The new hospital — the term's largest promise, undelivered within the term. Project Polaris was the flagship of the transformation agenda; by the November 2026 election it had a project office, a groundbreaking (March 2026, per the project office), partial financing and an IMF warning about operating costs — not a hospital. The previous General Hospital still carries the country's patients. Not a broken word with a date attached — no opening date was documented as promised and missed — but a first-term promise redeemed only as a construction file, and now the subject of the opposition's first demand for an enquiry (CMC, 17 November 2026).
  • The 100% renewable sister islands — stated as on track, not arrived. The October 2025 NDC 3.0 states Carriacou and Petite Martinique are "on track to become the region's first 100 percent renewable and climate-resilient islands," with the grid being upgraded and 400 kW of battery storage in place (NDC 3.0, October 2025). Against a 100% target, 400 kW of storage and an upgraded grid are a beginning; the word is graded as in motion, with the distance printed.
  • The pension word, read from the other side — Crossing 2, named. To established officers who heard the 2022 campaign as a promise to restore the 1958 settlement in full, the defined-contribution plan is the kept word that hurt: the state paid what the court ordered for the past, then legislated a cheaper, contribution-based future over union caution and a contested constitutionality opinion (Test 1). The keeping (arrears paid, a pension for the previously pensionless) earns its credit on this test; the hurt stands in the ledger in full, per the method — the two axes do not launder each other.
  • The early election itself — legal, and a word about timing that voters were not given in 2022. Nothing in the record shows a 2022 promise to serve a full term, so this is not recorded as a broken pledge; it is recorded as a unilateral change of the term's length, eleven months early, announced 4 October 2026 for 5 November 2026, which the courts upheld and the voters then ratified 14–1. A ratified change is not a kept schedule; both halves of that sentence are the record.

Why this grade: The checkable, dated promises of 2022 — pay the pensions, raise the floor, free the tuition, insure the unemployed — were kept, early and with figures attached, and the voters' 61% verdict is consistent with that record. The transformational promises — the hospital, the renewable islands, production from the sea — ended the term as files, groundbreakings and terminations rather than things a citizen can use. A word kept on the payable promises and still owing on the building promises is MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — retired public officers (EC$75.1 million paid); minimum-wage earners; students and parents (tuition, school meals); workers who lost jobs after May 2023 (NIS benefit). Hurt — established public officers whose expected pension terms were redesigned beneath them (Crossing 2); patients and health workers still waiting on Polaris; Carriacou and Petite Martinique residents whose renewable-island future remains a plan. Not documented: any Mitchell promise on the GPG acreage's next use, or a dated Polaris opening commitment — recorded as not documented rather than invented.

Overall Summary

Dickon Mitchell's first term was graded by his own voters on 5 November 2026, and they returned him 14–1 with 61% of the vote — the largest mandate in his party's history, won a year before he needed to ask. This dossier's grades are all MIXED, and the reasons differ by test, which is the point of printing them separately. On one law, he paid a court-ordered EC$75.1 million to retirees of every political stripe and wrote one wage floor for the whole market, while leaving Beryl's beneficiary-level accounts still being demanded in Parliament and a pension reform whose two-tier fairness is contested by the officers inside it. On his word, the dated promises were paid and the transformational ones are still files.
