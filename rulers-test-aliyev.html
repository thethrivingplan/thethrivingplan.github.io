The Ruler's Test — Ilham Aliyev, President of Azerbaijan

Subject: Ilham Aliyev — President of Azerbaijan since 31 October 2003, in his fifth term following the early presidential election of 7 February 2024 (official result 92.12%); next presidential election due 2031. He succeeded his father, Heydar Aliyev (president 1993–2003), after serving as his prime minister from August 2003 — the first dynastic succession in a former Soviet republic. His wife, Mehriban Aliyeva, has been First Vice-President since 21 February 2017, a post created by the 2016 constitutional referendum, and is first in the line of succession. He remains in office in November 2026, twenty-three years into his tenure. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The enforcement machinery has repeatedly struck mid-level insiders. In 2019–2020 the State Security Service and the Prosecutor General's Anti-Corruption Directorate arrested a series of presidentially appointed district executive heads — Aghstafa, Yevlakh, Neftchala, Bilasuvar (30 April 2020), Imishli (5 May 2020), Kurdamir (August 2020) and others — on bribery, embezzlement and abuse-of-power charges, alongside a deputy culture minister and senior border-service officers; Aliyev publicly tasked the new Prosecutor General, appointed 1 May 2020, with widening the campaign (Prosecutor General's Office statements; AzerNews and Caucasus Watch reporting, 2020). The charges were documented process; several cases ended in convictions.
  • In October 2025 the machinery reached the old regime's most powerful insider: Ramiz Mehdiyev, head of the Presidential Administration from 1994 to 2019, was placed under house arrest on charges of attempting to seize state power, high treason and legalisation of property obtained by criminal means (government-media reporting of the investigation, October 2025). The charges are ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated; Mehdiyev's camp and independent analysts describe the case variously as accountability and as an intra-elite purge — both readings are recorded here; the fact of the prosecution is documented.
  • Presidential pardons are issued regularly (801 convicts covered in May 2023; 154 in May 2024; 220 in May 2025), and the 2023 decree included eight people recognised by human rights defenders as political prisoners (Meydan TV pardon coverage, May 2023–2025).

Evidence against:

  • The law's weight falls overwhelmingly on critics, and has never reached the first family's documented finances. The OCCRP-led "Azerbaijani Laundromat" investigation (September 2017), based on leaked Danske Bank records, documented some US$2.9 billion moved through four UK-registered shell companies in 2012–2014; OCCRP reported that almost half — about US$1.45 billion — came from an account at the state-owned International Bank of Azerbaijan held by a shell company it linked to the Aliyev family, and that funds paid European politicians and bought luxury goods for prominent Azerbaijanis. This is investigative reporting, attributed as such; no Azerbaijani prosecution followed. The presidential spokesman's documented response was to call the investigation "absurd and biased"; the OCCRP website was blocked in Azerbaijan (OCCRP, September 2017; subsequent OCCRP reporting).
  • The ICIJ Pandora Papers (October 2021) documented, from leaked offshore records, that the Aliyev family and close associates were secretly involved in UK property deals worth more than £400 million; OCCRP's follow-up work put the family's London portfolio at nearly US$700 million held through dozens of offshore companies, and the UK's Register of Overseas Entities later publicly confirmed his daughters Leyla and Arzu Aliyeva as personal owners of six luxury apartments near Hyde Park (ICIJ, October 2021; OCCRP Pandora Papers reporting, 2021–2024). Aliyev's documented response, in a 2021 interview published on the presidential website, was that he was "not a poor man" when he became president, attributing the wealth to business success. No domestic authority has examined the holdings; Azerbaijani law-enforcement has opened no case.
  • The outcome measure sits near the bottom of the world: Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index scored Azerbaijan 22/100 in 2024 (154th of 180), improving to 30/100 in the 2025 index — still below its own 2017 best of 31 and far below its Caucasus neighbours (Transparency International, CPI 2024 and CPI 2025).
  • The pardon instrument itself is applied by category: the May 2024 decree — 154 convicts — was, per the head of the Defence Line human rights organisation, the first pardon in thirty years to include not a single recognised political prisoner; the May 2025 decree (220 convicts) again included none, per the chairman of the Coordination Center of Families of Political Prisoners (Turan and Meydan TV reporting, May 2024 and May 2025). The government's documented position is that there are no political prisoners in Azerbaijan and that all convictions rest on criminal acts alone — a position the pardon record, read against the prisoner lists in Test 4, does not support.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, media, the electoral authority, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Azerbaijan remains, formally, inside the Council of Europe and under the jurisdiction of the European Court of Human Rights, and its courts process the state's cases there; Aliyev's February 2024 threat to withdraw (Test 3) was not carried out, and no exit had occurred as of November 2026 (International IDEA democracy tracker, 2024–2025).
  • The Central Bank's governorship has changed by ordinary appointment without documented presidential interference in monetary decisions, and the state oil fund (SOFAZ) publishes audited figures; no documented case exists of the president directing a central-bank decision.

Evidence against:

  • The constitutional architecture itself was rebuilt around the seat. The referendum of 18 March 2009 abolished presidential term limits. The referendum of 26 September 2016 extended the term from five to seven years, created appointed vice-presidencies (the first of which, holding succession rights, went to the president's wife in February 2017), lowered the presidential age floor, and gave the president power to call early elections and dissolve parliament. The Venice Commission's preliminary opinion of 20 September 2016 found the changes "at odds with European constitutional heritage," giving the president "unprecedented" powers, weakening parliament and the judiciary, and criticised the procedure — no parliamentary debate, minimal public discussion — by which they were adopted (Venice Commission, 20 September 2016). The referendum proceeded unchanged.
  • Elections are administered inside that architecture. The OSCE/ODIHR final report on the 7 February 2024 early presidential election (27 February 2025) concluded that it "took place in a restrictive environment… marked by the stifling of critical voices and the absence of political alternatives," that the incumbent "was not meaningfully challenged," and — with the September 2024 parliamentary election report — raised serious concerns about whether ballots were counted and reported honestly, finding a "deliberate lack of safeguards against manipulation" (OSCE/ODIHR final reports, 27 February and 5 March 2025). ODIHR noted monitored broadcasters gave all challengers combined under two minutes of prime-time informational coverage during the campaign.
  • The judiciary's subordination produced the European system's most extreme documented case: Azerbaijan is the only state against which the European Court of Human Rights has delivered an Article 46(4) infringement judgment — the Grand Chamber found on 29 May 2019 that Azerbaijan had failed to act in good faith to implement its 2014 judgment ordering the release of opposition figure Ilgar Mammadov, who remained imprisoned for years after the judgment (ECtHR, Ilgar Mammadov v. Azerbaijan, infringement proceedings, 29 May 2019). Implementation has not normalised since: PACE's June 2026 resolution noted the high number of leading ECtHR judgments still pending implementation, and Azerbaijani civil-society organisations, citing Council of Europe data, stated in September 2026 that 68 percent of ECtHR judgments concerning Azerbaijan remain unimplemented (PACE, June 2026; joint statement of five Azerbaijani CSOs, 22 September 2026).
  • The media's autonomy was extinguished as a system. The 2022 Law on Media created a state registry independent outlets were required to join; from November 2023 the remaining independent outlets were dismantled by prosecution (Test 1 and Test 4): Abzas Media (arrests from 20 November 2023), Toplum TV (raided March 2024), Meydan TV (arrests 6 December 2024). The European Parliament's human-rights briefing of 4 May 2026 records that, as of May 2026, no independent media operates from within Azerbaijan. RFE/RL's Azerbaijani service and Voice of America broadcasts are banned in the country.
  • Parliament's weakness is stated by the Assembly that seats it: PACE Resolution 2527 (24 January 2024) listed among its grounds "the separation of powers, the weakness of its legislature vis-à-vis the executive, [and] the independence of the judiciary."

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, international bodies, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • One genuine, documented adjustment toward an adversary: on 7 December 2023, three months after the Karabakh offensive, Baku and Yerevan issued their first-ever unmediated joint statement — Azerbaijan released 32 Armenian servicemen for two Azerbaijani servicemen (exchanged 13 December 2023), Armenia withdrew its COP29 candidacy in Azerbaijan's favour, and both sides declared a "historical chance" for peace (joint statement of the Presidential Administration of Azerbaijan and the Office of the Prime Minister of Armenia, 7 December 2023). The peace track that followed produced an agreed treaty text in March 2025 and its initialling in Washington on 8 August 2025 (Test 5).
  • In the AZAL crash dispute with Russia he eventually accepted a negotiated close: after the 25 December 2024 crash of an Azerbaijan Airlines Embraer near Aktau (38 killed), Aliyev publicly demanded an apology, an admission of guilt, punishment of those responsible and compensation, rejecting Moscow's early "absurd theories"; President Putin apologised for the incident in Russian airspace, and following the two presidents' 9 October 2025 meeting in Dushanbe the foreign ministries announced a formal settlement covering all outstanding issues including compensation (presidential statements, December 2024 – July 2025; joint foreign-ministry statement reported by AnewZ, 2026). The adjustment here was Russia's more than his — but he took the settlement rather than the grievance.

Evidence against:

  • Every institutional pushback on his domestic record has been met with denial or escalation. When PACE resolved on 24 January 2024 not to ratify his parliament's delegation credentials — citing unfulfilled commitments on elections, separation of powers, judiciary and human rights — the delegation withdrew, and on 1 February 2024 Aliyev stated Azerbaijan would consider pulling out of the Council of Europe and the European Court of Human Rights if its voting rights were not reinstated (PACE Resolution 2527, 24 January 2024; International IDEA tracker, February 2024). Azerbaijan did not submit credentials for 2025. No commitment named in the resolution was implemented in response; repression intensified instead (Tests 1, 2, 4).
  • Election observation is answered the same way across two decades: ODIHR's 2025 follow-up assessment records that of its accumulated recommendations for Azerbaijan, two had been mostly implemented and eight partially addressed, with the rest outstanding — the same findings (restrictive environment, stifled voices, counting safeguards) recur in 2003, 2008, 2013, 2018 and 2024 without structural correction (OSCE/ODIHR final reports, 2003–2025). The official position, stated by officials and ruling-party deputies throughout, is that there are no political prisoners and that criticism reflects double standards — the premise of every finding is denied rather than answered.
  • The clearest doubling-down sequence is dated: the crackdown that began with the Abzas Media arrests of November 2023 accelerated through the February 2024 PACE sanction and the COP29 spotlight (November 2024), continued with the Meydan TV arrests days after COP29 closed (6 December 2024), and reached the country's principal opposition leader in November 2025 (Test 4). Pushback did not moderate the policy; it preceded its expansion.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Karabakh's Armenians, political prisoners and their families, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The material floor genuinely rose for the population inside the count. On official figures, poverty fell from about 49 percent in 2001 to about 5 percent by 2013 and has been held near that level; GDP per capita rose from roughly US$900 in 2003 to over US$7,500; state sovereign assets grew to roughly US$75 billion; his 2003 pledge that no displaced-persons tent camp would remain was fulfilled on the official record, and by his own November 2026 account nearly 100,000 Azerbaijanis have been resettled in the recovered Karabakh and East Zangezur districts over six years (World Bank and State Statistics Committee series; presidential statements, 2008–2026, including Jahangirbeyli village opening, 6 November 2026). Azerbaijan hosted COP29 in Baku in November 2024 and presided over the agreement of a US$300 billion annual climate-finance goal.
  • On Karabakh's Armenians, his government's stated position is on the record at full strength: that they are citizens of Azerbaijan entitled to equal rights with other minorities; that a reintegration plan covering rights, safety, cultural preservation and economic development was unveiled on 2 October 2023; that their departure was voluntary; and that Azerbaijanis expelled from Armenia and the occupied districts in the 1990s hold an equal claim to return (statements of the Presidential Administration and the president's foreign-policy adviser, 2023; Topchubashov Center analysis of the documented positions, 2023).

Evidence against:

  • The Karabakh Armenian population was counted out in fact. After a blockade of the Lachin Corridor lasting from December 2022 to September 2023 — which PACE condemned and which the International Court of Justice addressed in provisional measures in February 2023 — Azerbaijan launched its offensive of 19–20 September 2023; within days, 100,400 ethnic Armenians, 99 percent of the region's remaining Armenian population, fled to Armenia, and by November 2023 only a few dozen remained (UN mission reporting, October 2023; Freedom House, Why Are There No Armenians in Nagorno-Karabakh?, 2024). Freedom House's report, built on more than 300 survivor testimonies, concluded Azerbaijan conducted a deliberate campaign amounting to ethnic cleansing — a characterisation Azerbaijan rejects, maintaining the departure was voluntary and reintegration was offered. The characterisation is CONTESTED between those documented positions; the departure itself, its scale, and its permanence are not contested by any source. The ICJ's order of 17 November 2023 obliged Azerbaijan to ensure the safe, unimpeded return of those who fled and to protect the property records of residents; three years on, no significant return has occurred, and the initialled 2025 peace text contains no return mechanism (ICJ provisional measures, 17 November 2023; Caucasian Knot reporting on the order's third anniversary, November 2026).
  • The domestic count of the imprisoned has reached a twenty-year high. The Institute for Peace and Democracy counted 375 political prisoners in June 2025; a Columbia Global Freedom of Expression report counted over 392, including at least 25 journalists, in October 2025; PACE's June 2026 resolution recorded 328 in May 2026, and the Union for the Freedom of Political Prisoners counted 322 on 5 October 2026 (Institute for Peace and Democracy, June 2025; PACE resolution, June 2026; Fakt Yoxla compilation, October 2026). Named points on that curve: opposition leader Tofig Yagublu, sentenced to nine years in March 2025; Talysh historian Igbal Abilov, eighteen years for treason; peace researcher Bahruz Samadov, fifteen years for treason in June 2025 (European Parliament joint statement, 25 June 2025, and resolution of 18 December 2025 — the charges are ALLEGATIONs the defendants and their supporters reject as fabricated; no independent trial monitoring was permitted to test them).
  • The count reached the opposition's head. Ali Karimli, chairman of the Popular Front Party, was detained by the State Security Service on 29 November 2025 and charged under Article 278.1 with actions aimed at the violent seizure of power — an offence carrying a possible life sentence — in a case authorities link to the Mehdiyev investigation; he denies the charges as politically motivated. His pre-trial detention has been repeatedly extended, and Human Rights Watch documented punitive conditions — cut telephone access, confiscated newspapers, barred family parcels — imposed after a June 2026 interview in which he criticised his treatment (HRW, 5 August 2026; Amnesty International, 1 December 2025). More than twenty of his party's members were detained, remanded or travel-banned in the same wave.
  • Journalists are counted as smugglers. The three dismantled outlets were prosecuted under a common template centred on Article 206.3.2, currency smuggling, applied to the foreign grants that funded them: Abzas Media's seven journalists were sentenced on 20 June 2025 to terms of up to nine years (upheld on appeal, April 2026); nine Toplum TV defendants were sentenced on 27 July 2026 to twelve to fifteen years — the harshest media sentences of the crackdown; the Meydan TV case, opened with the arrests of 6 December 2024, remained on trial in November 2026, with Amnesty documenting denial of medical care to detained journalists, including Meydan TV editor-in-chief Aynur Elgunash, whose recommended kidney surgery a Baku court declined to enable by house arrest on 26 October 2026 (Amnesty International, November 2025 – October 2026; OBCT, July 2026). The outlets and the defendants call the charges fabricated — ALLEGATIONs in form, but the pattern, the timing against COP29, and the elimination of every independent newsroom in the country are documented facts independent of any single verdict. A man arrested in connection with Abzas Media's work, Ilgar Aliyev, died in prison in May 2026 (Amnesty International, November 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises and public commitments vs. the record since 2003, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The founding economic pledges were delivered on the official measures. His 2003 campaign promised 600,000 new jobs in five years and the elimination of the displaced-persons tent camps; by 2008 he announced 750,000 jobs created and the camps closed, and the poverty trajectory in Test 4 is the same record (US State Department cable reporting the pledge, 2006; presidential statements, 2008–2013). The numbers are the government's own and independent verification is limited — but the physical transformation (infrastructure, resettlement, sovereign reserves) is corroborated by the World Bank and IMF series.
  • The central territorial pledge was kept — by force. Across two decades Aliyev promised that Karabakh would return to Azerbaijani control and that Azerbaijan would not accept the status quo; the 44-day war of 2020 and the offensive of September 2023 delivered it, and the February 2024 election was the first held across the whole internationally recognised territory (ODIHR final report, 2025). The human cost of the keeping is graded in Test 4, not here; the word itself was kept.
  • The energy word to Europe is being kept. Under the July 2022 memorandum signed with the European Commission — whose president called Azerbaijan a "reliable partner" — Azerbaijan committed to doubling gas deliveries to the EU toward 20 bcm by 2027; deliveries have risen year on year since (European Commission memorandum, 18 July 2022; subsequent delivery reporting).
  • The peace text exists because he negotiated it. The treaty text was agreed in March 2025 and initialled in Washington on 8 August 2025 in the presence of Presidents Aliyev, Pashinyan and Trump — the closest the two states have come to a signed peace in three decades (White House summit reporting, August 2025).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The oldest word is the state's, given on accession. Azerbaijan joined the Council of Europe on 25 January 2001 under Opinion 222 (2000) commitments — free and fair elections, separation of powers, an independent judiciary, no political prisoners. PACE's Resolution 2527 of 24 January 2024 records that "more than twenty years after joining the Council of Europe Azerbaijan has not fulfilled major commitments stemming therefrom," and its June 2026 resolution found the position still deteriorating. Every one of those commitments was assumed by his father's state and reaffirmed throughout his tenure; none is met.
  • The 2003 pledge of a peaceful Karabakh settlement was superseded by war. His 2003 campaign pledged to resolve the conflict peacefully (Congressional Research Service, 2003). His documented defence — that three decades of negotiation under Armenian occupation left no alternative, and that four 1993 UN Security Council resolutions demanding withdrawal were never implemented — is printed at full strength; it explains the reversal without erasing it. The conflict was resolved by the offensives of 2020 and 2023.
  • The anti-corruption word runs against the family's documented record. Aliyev has pledged throughout his tenure that no official is untouchable and that corruption will be eradicated; the district-head arrests of Test 1 are real, but the Laundromat flows, the Pandora Papers holdings and a CPI score of 22 in 2024 (Test 1) measure the distance between the word and the record at the top, where no case has ever been opened.
  • The peace treaty remains unsigned on his condition. Fifteen months after initialling, the treaty is not signed: Aliyev states — as he did in Shusha on 8 November 2026, beside President Erdoğan — that the agreement "remains only initialed," because Azerbaijan expects Armenia first to amend its constitution to remove what Baku reads as a territorial claim. Armenia's speaker restated on 17 November 2026 that Yerevan is ready to sign immediately and does not accept the condition; Prime Minister Pashinyan's own timetable now runs to 2031 (statements of Presidents Aliyev and the Armenian leadership, October–November 2026). The condition is his stated position, consistently held; its effect is that the signature his Washington appearance promised has not happened.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Ilham Aliyev has held one seat for twenty-three years, inherited from his father and re-engineered — by the referendums of 2009 and 2016 — so that nothing in the constitution requires him ever to leave it, with his wife first in the line of succession. The tests measure what the seat does. It gives by two laws: prosecution is a working instrument against district governors, generals, journalists and opposition leaders alike, but it has never once turned toward the offshore wealth documented in the first family's name, and the pardon that frees ordinary convicts by the hundred has, since 2024, freed no political prisoner at all. It grips rather than holds: the electoral commission, the courts, the parliament and the media registry all answer upward, the last independent newsroom was prosecuted out of existence between COP29 and 2026, and Azerbaijan remains the only state ever found by the Strasbourg court to have defied a judgment in bad faith. It does not answer the wobble: twenty years of identical election findings have produced no structural correction, and the one sanction Europe applied — the loss of his delegation's credentials — was answered with a threat to leave the building. It counts by category: the Azerbaijani poor were counted in, measurably and over decades; the Karabakh Armenians were counted out in a single week of September 2023 and have not been counted back; and between 322 and 392 of his own citizens sit in prison tonight on lists his government insists do not exist. His word is the split the whole file turns on — kept, at real scale, on jobs, poverty, territory and gas; unkept on the commitments that would have limited the seat itself. The Sun in this system does not merely hold the planets. It has arranged that nothing else may shine.
