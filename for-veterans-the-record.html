For Veterans — The Record

The promise was made in plain words: serve, and the country will take care of you. This page holds that promise up against the record — what the system actually delivers, where it breaks, who is responsible at each break, and the doors that are still open. Both columns, at full size.

How this page works: the why chain

Everything here answers “why” — and then “why” again, under that — until there's nothing under it but the law, the money, or a person who decided. That's the bottom. And that's where the how shows itself: you don't invent the fix, you find it waiting at the bottom — and on this page, the how is always the next door. Where a chain stops early because the record runs out, it is labeled stopped — never dressed up as the bottom. Compressed, the whole method is three words: Why? → How? → Act.

1The promise

What a veteran is owed, in the system's own terms — before we check what happens.

When someone serves and comes home with injuries or conditions connected to that service, the law promises disability compensation — a monthly payment based on a rating from 0% to 100%. Around it sit the other earned doors: health care, education benefits under the GI Bill, home loans with no down payment, and — in Texas — tuition help and property-tax relief.

None of that is charity. It is the second half of an agreement. Which is why this page is built the way a legal record is built: the promise first, then the evidence of what actually happened, then who answers for the gap.

“A grateful nation pays its debts to veterans; it does not send them the invoice.” — Carol Whitmore, National Commander, Veterans of Foreign Wars (VFW), June 11, 2026

What is working — printed at full size

The backlog is at a record low

The claims backlog fell below 70,000 in July 2026 — the first time since February 2020 — and the average decision now takes 77.7 days, a record low. The VA processed a record 3.1 million disability claims in fiscal 2026. On housing: veteran homelessness hit a record low of 32,495 people on a single night in January 2025, down 56.1% since 2010, and the VA permanently housed nearly 48,000 homeless veterans in fiscal 2024. When a veteran reaches the system, the system often works.

Sources: VA press release, July 7, 2026; HUD 2025 Point-in-Time Count; VA Homeless Programs

Where it breaks

The doors veterans never walk through

  • VA health care is not a disability rating. Being treated at a VA hospital does not create or update a compensation claim — the two are separate systems, and nothing moves between them until a claim is filed. This one misconception quietly costs veterans years of back pay.
  • The GI Bill transfer trap. Benefits can be transferred to a spouse or child only while still serving. After separation, the option is gone forever — and many veterans learn this after it is too late. In the study's own interviews: “I didn't know that I had to be on active duty to transfer the benefit. I found out too late… It would've helped my kids.”
  • VA home loans go unused. An estimated 58,000+ VA loans went unused in 2024, and 55% of veterans in one survey wrongly believed a VA loan requires a down payment. It does not. Veterans rated even 10% disabled are exempt from the VA funding fee — labeled here as a lender's analysis and a credit-union study, not government counts.
  • The flattening line. Veteran homelessness fell to its record low but only 1.2% below the prior year, and 25 states saw increases. The trend suggests new veterans are becoming homeless nearly as fast as others are housed.

Sources: Census/VA/AIR study, 2024; Veterans United Home Loans analysis, Oct 2025; Navy Federal Credit Union study; HUD 2025

The heaviest number on this page

Suicide: the gap is outside the system

6,398 veterans died by suicide in 2023 — an average of 17.5 per day. The veteran suicide rate (35.2 per 100,000) was more than double the non-veteran adult rate. And 61% of the veterans who died had not received VA health care in the prior year — the deadliest gap is not inside the system; it is the veterans who never reach it.

The contested figure, both sides: the widely quoted “22 a day” comes from a 2012 VA report built on narrower data. America's Warrior Partnership argues the true count is higher still — up to roughly 24 suicides plus 20 “self-injury mortality” deaths (such as overdoses) per day — because death certificates misclassify. The VA's 17.5 is the official count; the challenge to its method is printed beside it, unresolved.

Source: VA 2025 National Veteran Suicide Prevention Annual Report (data through 2023); America's Warrior Partnership, Operation Deep Dive. If this is close to home: dial 988, then press 1.

The why chain — why do two in three veterans receive no disability compensation?
Why? Because compensation never starts on its own. It begins only when a claim is filed — and filing is left entirely to the veteran.
Why doesn't being treated at a VA hospital start it? Because health care and compensation were built as two separate systems with no door between them. Treatment records do not become a claim.
Why didn't anyone tell them? For many who separated before disability evaluation was built into out-processing, nobody was assigned to. The GI Bill study found the same shape in education: the top reason for non-use was not knowing deadlines and rules.
BedrockThe law's structure — two systems, and the burden of starting placed on the veteran — plus an information gap nobody was made responsible for closing.
The record runs out hereWhy any one veteran never filed is not recorded anywhere. This page doesn't guess at it.
The how — it stands at the bottom of the chainFile the Intent to File (Section 4) to lock the date, and take the claim to a free accredited door (Section 6). That is the whole fix for this chain, and it costs nothing.

3The live case: a bill, passed with no names attached

The Take Care of America's Veterans Act (H.R. 9237) — checked against the bill text, the VA's own estimates, and both sides' statements, November 13, 2026.

Open the live case — the bill, both ledgers, both casesTap to open
The accountability fact

Passed on Veterans Day — by voice vote

On November 11, 2026 — Veterans Day — the U.S. House passed H.R. 9237, a package of roughly 600 pages combining some 60 veterans bills, by voice vote: no roll call, no recorded names. The House majority's release called it “unanimously passed.” A voice vote leaves no record of who stood where — on a bill about veterans' earned benefits, there is no list of who voted for it. That absence is a documented fact, whatever a reader concludes about the bill itself.

DAV (Disabled American Veterans) condemned House leaders on November 12 for advancing it “during the lame-duck session without a recorded vote” and demanded a public revote, on the record. The VFW's headline was blunter: “House shirks accountability.”

Sources: AP coverage; DAV statement, Nov 12, 2026; VFW statement, Nov 2026; bill text, govinfo

The bill holds two ledgers at once. Both are real. Honest accounting prints both:

The expansion ledger

The centerpiece is the Major Richard Star Act: combat-injured veterans who were medically retired with under 20 years of service would receive both their military retirement pay and VA disability compensation — ending the current dollar-for-dollar offset that cancels one against the other.

  • ~50,000 to ~58,000 veterans would benefit (estimates split by outlet: ~50,000 Politico / Washington Examiner; ~58,000 AP)
  • Average boost of about $1,450 per month (Congressional Budget Office estimate)
  • VFW and DAV support the Star Act itself and most of the package

The payment ledger

Section 108 pays for the package in part by writing new disability rating criteria into law as a budget offset — the first time rating-schedule changes have been codified in statute to generate savings, per the VFW:

  • Tinnitus: the standalone 10% rating is eliminated; tinnitus becomes compensable only as a symptom of another service-connected condition
  • Sleep apnea: the automatic 50% rating for CPAP treatment ends; ratings shift to 0%/10% based on how well treatment works
  • Also in the bill: a near-tripling of the VA's streamlined refinancing (IRRRL) fee, per Sen. Reed
The $57 billion — checked

What the number is, and what it is not

The figure moving through veterans' posts online — “they took $57 billion from veterans” — traces to a real number with a blurred shape. The $57 billion is the VA's own estimate (from its 2022 regulatory analysis of these rating changes) of reduced disability compensation over 10 years, affecting up to 1.5 million veterans. DAV and the VFW both cite it.

  • It is not money taken from anyone's current check. Compensation already in effect is expressly protected — per the bill summaries, current ratings are protected for claims filed before October 1, 2026; the new criteria would apply to claims filed on or after that date, if the bill becomes law.
  • It is future compensation. The people who would pay are future claimants — including people still in uniform who have not filed yet.
  • It is an estimate of the rating changes' effect — the VA's figure, not a Congressional Budget Office score of the final bill.

Sources: VA 2022 regulatory analysis, as cited by DAV and VFW, Nov 2026; H.R. 9237 bill summaries

Update — November 12–13, 2026

The day after the voice vote, the House voted again — this time with names

On November 12, 2026 — the day after the voice vote — the House passed the standalone Major Richard Star Act (H.R. 2102) by a recorded vote of 392–18. Every member's name is on that record. It is the clean version of the same fix: it ends the retirement-pay offset (CBO estimate: about $13 billion over 10 years) without Section 108's rating cuts paying for it. It has been sent to the Senate, where it now sits beside H.R. 9237 — meaning the Senate holds two paths to the same Star Act fix: the package with the cuts inside it, and the standalone bill without them. Reporting on the passed texts describes the version inside H.R. 9237 as narrower than the standalone bill.

The Senate's own companion to the package, S. 4744, has been sitting on the Senate Legislative Calendar since June 11, 2026; no Senate floor vote on either bill had been scheduled as of November 13, 2026. Senate Veterans' Affairs Chairman Jerry Moran, a sponsor of the package, said November 11 he will work to “pass this legislation and send it to the President's desk as quickly as possible.” The VFW's answer, from National Commander-in-Chief Cory Geisler: the package “should be dead on arrival in the Senate.”

One more number for the record, labeled as what it is: in a September 2026 DAV survey of more than 4,200 veterans, family members, caregivers, and survivors, 71% opposed H.R. 9237 unless Section 108 was removed. The respondents were primarily DAV members — it is not a representative national poll, and it is printed here as a measure of DAV's own membership, nothing more.

Sources: House Clerk roll call and The Hill, Nov 12, 2026; Congress.gov (S. 4744, Calendar No. 433); Sen. Moran statement, Nov 11, 2026; VFW statement, Nov 11, 2026; DAV survey, Sept 2026

Both cases, in their own words

Supporters — House Veterans' Affairs Chairman Mike Bost and Senate counterpart Jerry Moran — argue the tinnitus and sleep-apnea ratings rest on outdated medicine, that the bill expands benefits overall, that the Star Act fix is long overdue, and that the package carries bipartisan content.

Opponents — the VFW and DAV — support the Star Act and most of the package. They oppose paying for one group of veterans' gains out of another group's future disability compensation, and they oppose the voice-vote procedure that left no names on record. DAV: the cuts would fall on “1.5 million disabled veterans over the next decade.”

Sources: House Veterans' Affairs Committee release; DAV and VFW statements, Nov 2026

The why chain — why did a veterans' bill pass with no names attached?
Why is there no list of who voted for it? Because the House passed it by voice vote — the chair declares the result from the sound of the room, and no member's name is recorded.
Why a voice vote, for a ~600-page package? The procedure was chosen by the House majority leadership that brought the bill to the floor. Voice votes are a real tool for uncontroversial measures; DAV and the VFW's objection is that this package — with the first-ever statutory rating cuts inside it — was not one.
The record runs out hereWhy leadership chose that procedure for this bill was not stated on the record. Motive is exactly the thing a voice vote leaves undocumented — so this page doesn't invent one.
Why does it matter, if the bill might pass anyway? Because a roll call is the only mechanism by which a veteran can hold a named person to account for a vote on veterans' benefits. No record, no accountability — that is not rhetoric, it is the mechanical consequence of the procedure.
BedrockA decision, made by named people in leadership roles, to use a procedure that leaves no names. The law allowed it; people chose it.
The how — it stands at the bottom of the chainThe bill still needs the Senate. The door is the one DAV is pointing at: contact your senators and demand a recorded vote (see the status box below). A recorded vote is the fix for this exact break, and it costs a phone call.
Status: not law — the fight is live · checked November 13, 2026

It has not passed the Senate

The House vote is not the end. As of November 13, 2026, the Senate has passed neither H.R. 9237 nor the standalone Star Act (H.R. 2102, passed by the House 392–18 on November 12). Senator Jack Reed (Appropriations Committee; West Point graduate) vowed on November 12 to block the package in the Senate. DAV is demanding a public revote with a recorded vote. Senate Veterans' Affairs Chairman Jerry Moran is pushing the other way — to pass the package quickly.

What a veteran can do this week: the VFW and DAV are both running action alerts on this bill — contact your two U.S. senators, and if the procedure is what moves you, say the specific thing DAV is asking for: a recorded vote, with names attached. And because the Senate now holds both bills, there is a second specific thing to say: the Star Act fix exists in a standalone bill (H.R. 2102) that pays for itself without Section 108. Your senators' phone numbers are on the U.S. Senate's public contact listings and through the Capitol switchboard.

Sources: Sen. Reed's release, Nov 12, 2026; Sen. Moran statement, Nov 11, 2026; House Clerk roll call, Nov 12, 2026; DAV and VFW action alerts, Nov 2026

4A claim, step by step

How a disability claim actually moves — including the two traps that quietly kill claims. Every step below can be done free, with free help (Section 6).

Open the claim, step by stepTap to open
  1. File an Intent to File first VA Form 21-0966

    A one-page notice that locks in your effective date — the date back pay is calculated from — for up to one year while you gather evidence. Filing this first, before anything else, is the single highest-value free move in the whole system. Every month of delay before it is filed is a month of back pay that can never be recovered.

  2. File the claim VA Form 21-526EZ

    On VA.gov, by mail, or with a free accredited representative. The “Fully Developed Claim” track means you submit all your evidence up front.

  3. Go to the C&P exam

    The VA schedules a Compensation & Pension exam with a VA or contract examiner. Missing a scheduled C&P exam is one of the most common reasons claims are denied or decided without the evidence. If you can't make it, reschedule — never just no-show.

  4. The rating decision

    The VA assigns each condition a percentage (0–100%, in 10% steps) or denies service connection. Current average decision time is about 76–78 days. Track your claim at VA.gov/claim-or-appeal-status.

  5. If denied or under-rated: a denial is not final

    You generally have one year from the decision letter to act while protecting your effective date. Three lanes, under the Appeals Modernization Act:

    • Supplemental Claim Form 20-0995 — with new and relevant evidence. VA goal: ~125 days.
    • Higher-Level Review Form 20-0996 — a senior reviewer re-reads the same evidence for errors; no new evidence allowed. VA goal: ~125 days.
    • Board of Veterans' Appeals Form 10182 — decided by a Veterans Law Judge. The slow lane: roughly 12 months on the Direct docket to 29–36+ months with a hearing.

Sources: VA.gov claim and decision-review pages; timeline figures are VA's published goals and 2026 practitioner estimates, as summarized in the research behind this page.

Why gaps happen — the documented reasonsTap to open

Why gaps happen — the documented reasons

  • Conditions never claimed. The VA rates only what is claimed. A condition that was never filed on pays 0% no matter how real it is — and VA health care treatment alone never files it for you.
  • Secondary conditions never connected. A condition caused or worsened by a service-connected one can itself be service-connected — for example, sleep apnea secondary to PTSD, or a left knee worn out by years of favoring a service-connected right knee. These are separate ratings, and nobody claims them for you.
  • A short or wrong C&P exam. The exam is often the whole evidence base for a rating. An examiner who spends minutes, measures range of motion without a goniometer (the angle-measuring tool examiners are supposed to use), or writes up the wrong condition can set the rating — and roughly 6 in 100 VA decisions contain an error by the VA's own measure.
  • Pyramiding — the rule that the same symptom can't be paid twice. VA regulation (38 CFR § 4.14) forbids rating the same symptom under two different conditions — “pyramiding.” The rule exists so one symptom isn't double-counted; its shadow side is that genuinely overlapping conditions can collapse into a single rating unless the distinct symptoms of each are documented separately.
  • Effective-date errors. Your back pay runs from your effective date — usually the later of when the VA received your claim or your Intent to File. A wrong effective date, or an Intent to File that was lost or never filed, quietly shrinks the check by months or years of back pay.

  • Hazlewood Act: up to 150 credit hours of tuition and most fees exempted at Texas public colleges and universities for eligible veterans (entered service from Texas, at least 181 days active duty, honorable discharge, Texas resident, federal education benefits exhausted or worth less). 7Spot the scam: the claim sharks

The one rule that protects you: if anyone asks for money to file your first claim, walk away. The same help is free by law at a VSO or your county office.

Open — spot the scamTap to open
Illegal — but the penalties were removed

Charging for an initial claim has always been against the law

Charging a fee to help with an initial VA claim is illegal for anyone. Only VA-accredited representatives may charge at all, and only for appeals-stage work under fee rules. But in 2006, Congress removed the criminal penalties — so enforcement is weak, and roughly 200 unaccredited “claim shark” companies now operate openly.

  • The typical charge: 5× the veteran's monthly benefit increase — commonly $5,000 to $20,000 taken out of a veteran's own award.
  • In 2026, a federal court found Veterans Guardian — one of the largest of these companies — liable; the veterans in the class action had paid over $250 million in fees.

Sources: 38 U.S.C. § 5904; federal court ruling against Veterans Guardian, 2026; figures as compiled in the research behind this page

In Congress now — both bills, both statuses

The fix is stuck; the legalize-it bill is moving against it

The GUARD VA Benefits Act would restore criminal penalties for charging veterans unaccredited fees. It is stuck in committee. A rival, industry-backed bill would instead legalize the fees. Neither had become law as of November 13, 2026. As with the bill in Section 3: the state of play is printed here so a veteran can watch it with open eyes and, if they choose, tell their representatives which side of it they stand on.

Before you sign anything with a claims company, ask:

  • Are you VA-accredited? (Checkable in the VA's accreditation search. If they dodge the question, that's your answer.)
  • Is this my first claim? (If yes, charging you anything for help with it is illegal.)
  • What does the free version cost? (A VSO or county service officer does this for $0 — that is the comparison every fee should be measured against.)
The why chain — why can companies charge veterans for what is free by law?
Why do the companies exist at all? Because the gap in Section 2 is real: millions of veterans never file, or file and get denied, and a frustrated veteran with a denied claim is a customer. The demand is manufactured by the system's own failures.
Why doesn't the law stop them? It does — charging for an initial claim is illegal. But a law with no penalty is a sign, not a fence: in 2006, Congress removed the criminal penalties, and enforcement has been weak ever since.
Why hasn't Congress put the penalties back? The GUARD VA Benefits Act, which would restore them, sits in committee — while an industry-backed rival bill, which would legalize the fees instead, competes with it. Money is on one side of that fight; the record shows which side, in the bills' own sponsors and backers.
The record runs out hereWhy each individual member of the committees has not moved the GUARD Act is not documented. This page prints the bills' status, not guesses about motives.
BedrockThe law as Congress left it in 2006 — a prohibition with its teeth removed — and the money that profits from leaving it that way.
The how — it stands at the bottom of the chainFor the individual veteran, the how is already standing: walk away from any fee for a first claim and take the same work to a free accredited door (Section 6). For the law itself, the how is the same door as Section 3: tell your representatives which of the two bills you stand behind.
