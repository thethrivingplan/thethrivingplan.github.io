The Ruler's Test — Mohammed bin Salman, Crown Prince and Prime Minister of Saudi Arabia

Subject: Mohammed bin Salman (MBS) — Crown Prince of Saudi Arabia since 21 June 2017; Prime Minister since 27 September 2022; Minister of Defense 2015–2022; chairman of the Public Investment Fund; author of Vision 2030 (announced 25 April 2016). He is the kingdom's de facto ruler, exercising power in the name of his father, King Salman. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The state's criminal machinery has convicted and punished men from inside the security establishment itself in the Khashoggi case: a Saudi court convicted eight defendants, and in final rulings in September 2020 five were sentenced to 20 years in prison and three to terms of 7–10 years (Saudi Press Agency; Reuters, 7 September 2020). The sentences followed the December 2019 verdicts in which five defendants were initially sentenced to death.
  • The November 2017 anti-corruption campaign did reach genuinely powerful insiders — serving ministers, the National Guard commander, and senior princes were among those detained, not only outsiders (attorney general's statements, November 2017; royal court statement, 30 January 2019).
  • The death penalty is applied to convicted murderers and to members of al-Qaeda and the Islamic State as well as to protesters and drug offenders; the 12 March 2022 mass execution of 81 men included men convicted of murder and of membership in militant organizations, per the Interior Ministry's published list (Saudi Press Agency, 12 March 2022).
  • Some prominent detainees were released through the courts or by sentence completion: women's-rights activist Loujain al-Hathloul was released in February 2021 after a court suspended part of her sentence (family statements; Reuters, February 2021), and Salma al-Shehab's 34-year sentence was reduced on review in September 2024 and she was released in February 2025 (Amnesty International and Reuters reporting, February 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The Khashoggi case is the defining asymmetry, and it is documented at the level of findings, not allegation. The U.S. Office of the Director of National Intelligence, in its declassified assessment of 11 February 2021, concluded: "We assess that Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince Muhammad bin Salman approved an operation in Istanbul, Turkey to capture or kill Saudi journalist Jamal Khashoggi," basing the assessment on his control of decision-making, "the direct involvement of a key adviser and members of Muhammad bin Salman's protective detail in the operation, and the Crown Prince's support for using violent measures to silence dissidents abroad, including Khashoggi." The UN Special Rapporteur Agnès Callamard's 2019 inquiry likewise found "credible evidence" warranting investigation of the crown prince's individual liability. Against those findings, the Saudi process: tried 11 unnamed defendants in closed proceedings; acquitted the two senior figures closest to the crown prince — deputy intelligence chief Ahmed al-Asiri and royal court adviser Saud al-Qahtani, the adviser the ODNI report names — with al-Qahtani not charged at all; initially described the killing as unpremeditated, "at the spur of the moment"; and commuted the five death sentences in 2020 after Khashoggi's sons announced they "pardoned" the killers — a pardon Callamard described as part of a "parody of justice," noting the convicted were never even named (ODNI assessment, February 2021; Callamard statements, 2019–2020; Saudi Press Agency verdict announcements, December 2019 and September 2020). Mohammed bin Salman's own statements are on the record: he has denied ordering the killing, and told CBS's 60 Minutes in September 2019, "I take full responsibility as a leader in Saudi Arabia, especially since it was committed by individuals working for the Saudi government… it happened under my watch." No proceeding in his own jurisdiction has tested the ODNI finding against him. In November 2022, after he became prime minister, the U.S. government determined he was entitled to head-of-government immunity, and the U.S. civil suit brought by Khashoggi's fiancée was dismissed in December 2022 — a jurisdictional outcome, not an adjudication of the finding (U.S. District Court for D.C., December 2022).
  • The Ritz-Carlton campaign ran outside any court. On 4 November 2017, a newly created anti-corruption committee chaired by the crown prince detained hundreds of princes, ministers, and businessmen — by the attorney general's account 159 were held at the Ritz-Carlton alone, and 381 people were ultimately summoned. There were no public charges, no trials, and no published evidence; most detainees were released after signing over assets in settlements the royal court valued at more than $106 billion (royal court statement, 30 January 2019). The finance minister later told Reuters only about $13 billion had been collected in 2018 — the recovered figure itself is CONTESTED between the government's headline claim and its own minister's accounting. The New York Times reported in March 2018 that 17 detainees required hospitalization for physical abuse and that a senior officer held in the campaign, Maj. Gen. Ali al-Qahtani, died in custody; the government denied coercion. A process in which the wealthiest men in the country buy their freedom in a hotel, without a judge, while (Test 4) peaceful Twitter users receive 34- and 45-year sentences from a terrorism court, is two laws, not one — whichever shelf the underlying corruption ALLEGATIONs belong on.
  • Senior royals are held without any process at all. Former Crown Prince Mohammed bin Nayef — the crown prince's predecessor and the kingdom's former interior minister — and Prince Ahmed bin Abdulaziz, the king's full brother, were arrested on 6 March 2020. Saudi officials speaking to Reuters cited suspicion of treason (an ALLEGATION of a coup plot); no public charge or trial has ever followed, and the U.S. State Department's 2020 human rights report recorded that the detentions were never announced by the government and that bin Nayef had no access to his personal doctor months later. NBC News reported in June 2021 that he remained detained and had allegedly suffered physical abuse; his lawyers raised concern for his wellbeing. He remains out of public view as of this grading (U.S. State Department Country Report, March 2021; Reuters and Wall Street Journal reporting, March 2020).
  • The pattern of who faces the law's full weight is consistent: the Specialized Criminal Court (the terrorism court) imposes decades-long sentences for peaceful speech (Test 4), while no member of the ruling circle has faced a public trial for anything since the crown prince's rise.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do courts, media, business, the religious establishment, and other branches of the royal family keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • A genuine codification program has made ordinary law more predictable: between 2021 and 2023 the kingdom enacted its first codified Personal Status Law (2022), Law of Evidence (2022), Civil Transactions Law (2023), and a new Companies Law — reforms the crown prince announced in February 2021 expressly to reduce judicial discretion and inconsistency. Commercial courts and a specialized commercial judiciary now operate, and the Saudi stock exchange applies disclosure rules to listed companies including Saudi Aramco after its 2019 listing (royal decrees and Saudi Press Agency, 2021–2023).
  • The private sector's share of GDP has grown under Vision 2030 (to roughly 47% by 2024, per the Vision 2030 Annual Report), and businesses outside the ruling circle's favor — including foreign firms — operate at scale; Saudi Aramco's December 2019 IPO was executed through public markets with published financials.
  • The religious establishment retains its formal institutions — the Council of Senior Scholars, the Grand Mufti, the two holy mosques' administration — and continues to issue rulings; what changed (below) is its independence, not its existence.

Evidence against:

  • The security state was rebuilt around one man. In June 2017 Mohammed bin Nayef was removed as crown prince and interior minister by royal decree and replaced in both roles by Mohammed bin Salman. In July 2017 a new Presidency of State Security was created, concentrating counterterrorism and domestic intelligence under the king's office. In November 2017 Prince Miteb bin Abdullah — son of the late King Abdullah and commander of the Saudi Arabian National Guard, the third armed force and the Abdullah branch's institutional base — was detained in the Ritz-Carlton campaign and removed; the National Guard passed to a loyalist. By the ODNI's own documented assessment, "since 2017, the Crown Prince has had absolute control of the Kingdom's security and intelligence organizations" (ODNI, February 2021). He additionally chairs the Public Investment Fund, the Council of Economic and Development Affairs, and since September 2022 serves as prime minister — a post previously held by the king himself.
  • The family no longer holds shares of power. The pre-2017 Saudi system distributed the great ministries among branches of the Al Saud as a balance mechanism. That distribution is gone: the defense ministry (his, 2015–2022, then his brother's), the interior ministry, the National Guard, the foreign ministry, and the energy ministry are all held by his appointees or immediate family, and the two most senior alternative figures — Mohammed bin Nayef and Prince Ahmed — are detained (Test 1). The Allegiance Council, the family body that formally approves succession, was presented with his appointment as crown prince as a decision already taken; Prince Ahmed was one of three members who did not support it (contemporaneous reporting, June 2017).
  • The religious establishment was brought to heel — in both directions. In April 2016 the religious police (the Committee for the Promotion of Virtue and the Prevention of Vice) were stripped of powers to pursue, question, or arrest. In September 2017, prominent independent clerics — among them Salman al-Ouda, one of the kingdom's most-followed religious figures — were arrested; prosecutors later sought the death penalty against al-Ouda on terrorism-related charges arising substantially from his public statements and silences, and he remains detained without a concluded trial as of this grading. The Council of Senior Scholars publicly endorsed the November 2017 purge, declaring that Islamic law "instructs us to fight corruption" — the establishment blessing the arrests of princes within days (New York Times reporting, November 2017). In October 2017 the crown prince announced the kingdom would "return to… a moderate Islam that is open to all religions" — a doctrinal direction set by the ruler, not by the scholars. Clerics who comply keep their pulpits; the Ministry of Islamic Affairs monitors sermons and has removed imams who departed from instructions. The establishment's autonomy now runs one way: it may ratify, not dissent.
  • Media ownership was consolidated by detention. In the November 2017 campaign, Waleed al-Ibrahim, founder of MBC — the Arab world's largest media group — was detained at the Ritz-Carlton; after his release in early 2018, the Saudi government took a controlling stake of roughly 60% in MBC (reported by the Wall Street Journal and Reuters, 2018). No independent national newspaper or broadcaster operates outside state or ruling-family ownership, and Saudi journalists work under cybercrime and counterterrorism laws applied to speech (Test 4).
  • The judiciary has no structural independence to grip or to hold with. Judges are appointed by the king on the recommendation of a Supreme Judicial Council whose membership is itself royally appointed; there is no elected legislature — the Shura Council is appointed in full — and the Specialized Criminal Court, created for terrorism cases, is the venue for dissidents, protesters, and clerics alike. The codification program on the for-shelf standardizes the law's content; it does not give any institution the power to say no to the crown prince, and no Saudi court is documented to have ruled against a core decision of his in the period graded.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, markets, allies, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Qatar blockade was ended without its demands being met. On 5 June 2017 Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, and Egypt severed ties with Qatar and closed its only land border, issuing 13 demands including closing Al Jazeera and downgrading ties with Iran. Qatar refused all of them. On 5 January 2021, at the Al-Ula summit, the blockade was lifted in a Kuwaiti- and U.S.-brokered reconciliation; Qatar conceded none of the 13 demands publicly, and the crown prince was photographed embracing the Qatari emir. A failed coercive policy was closed out rather than escalated indefinitely (Al-Ula Declaration, 5 January 2021; contemporaneous reporting).
  • The 2020 oil price war was reversed within weeks. In March 2020, after Russia refused OPEC+ cuts, Saudi Arabia slashed prices and raised output into a collapsing pandemic market; by 12 April 2020 it had negotiated the largest production cut in OPEC history (9.7 million barrels/day) with the same partners. The doubling-down lasted about a month (OPEC+ agreements, March–April 2020).
  • Yemen: the war was eventually wound toward truce. After seven years of intervention (Test 4), Saudi Arabia accepted the UN-brokered truce of 2 April 2022, which sharply reduced airstrikes and largely held after its formal expiry; in April 2023 the Saudi ambassador to Yemen traveled to Sanaa for direct talks with the Houthis, and in September 2023 a Houthi delegation was received in Riyadh — public negotiation with an enemy the kingdom had vowed to crush (UN announcements, 2022; Reuters, 2023).
  • The Iran file was reversed openly. On 10 March 2023, in a China-brokered agreement in Beijing, Saudi Arabia restored diplomatic relations with Iran after seven years of rupture — a public strategic adjustment undertaken while the Yemen talks proceeded (joint statement, Beijing, 10 March 2023).
  • Restraint after Abqaiq. After the September 2019 attacks on the Abqaiq and Khurais oil facilities — attributed by Saudi Arabia, the U.S., and European governments to Iran, which denied responsibility — the kingdom did not retaliate militarily against Iran, absorbing the temporary loss of half its oil output instead (contemporaneous reporting, September 2019; attribution CONTESTED by Iran).
  • NEOM was scaled to fiscal reality rather than defended to the point of crisis (Test 5): the 2024–2025 reductions, the September 2025 suspension of construction on The Line, and the Public Investment Fund's roughly $8 billion write-down on giga-projects were absorbed as policy adjustments, and the PIF governor publicly reframed the program around "disciplined, phased execution" (Bloomberg, April 2024; PIF statements, 2025–2026).
  • Lebanon's Prime Minister Saad Hariri, who announced his resignation from Riyadh on 4 November 2017 in circumstances France's president publicly treated as coerced, was permitted to leave after French intervention and rescinded the resignation on 5 December 2017 — the pressure was released rather than maintained (contemporaneous reporting, November–December 2017). Whether Hariri was detained is CONTESTED: he has said he was free; the sequence and the French intervention are documented fact.

Evidence against:

  • On Khashoggi, the first response was denial, sustained for weeks. Saudi officials denied any knowledge of Khashoggi's fate for more than two weeks after 2 October 2018, insisting he had left the consulate; the account then changed in stages — death in a "fistfight," then a "rogue operation," then premeditated killing by the team but not by the leadership. The final position — that the crown prince bears responsibility "as a leader" but ordered nothing — was reached only after Turkish evidence releases and the CIA's assessment made the earlier accounts untenable (statements of the Saudi public prosecutor and foreign ministry, October–November 2018; 60 Minutes, September 2019).
  • On Yemen, the wobble was denied for seven years. UN expert panels, the UN Group of Eminent Experts, and successive humanitarian appeals documented coalition airstrikes on civilian gatherings, markets, and a school bus (August 2018, 40 children killed, per the UN and contemporaneous reporting) and the blockade's role in the world's largest humanitarian crisis, while Saudi statements throughout attributed civilian harm to Houthi conduct and errors. The adjustment on the for-shelf came in 2022–2023, after the war's stated aims — restoring the Hadi government to Sanaa and destroying Houthi military capacity — had become unreachable (UN Group of Eminent Experts reports, 2018–2021; UNDP, 2021).
  • NEOM's downsizing was denied while it happened. When Bloomberg reported in April 2024 that The Line's 2030 scope had been cut from 170 km to 2.4 km, Saudi officials publicly denied any change to the vision; construction was nonetheless suspended in September 2025 and, per Semafor's May 2026 reporting, further work deferred past 2030 — the adjustment on the for-shelf was real, but it was never announced as one by the crown prince himself (Bloomberg, April 2024; official denials, 2024; Semafor, May 2026).
  • On the death penalty, criticism has produced no adjustment (Test 5): record execution years in 2024 and 2025 followed, rather than preceded, the leadership's stated intention to reduce executions.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — women, Shia citizens, migrant workers, Yemenis affected by the war, dissidents, his opponents — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Women were counted into the economy at historic scale. The driving ban was lifted on 24 June 2018; in August 2019 women over 21 gained the right to obtain passports and travel without a guardian's permission, to register births, marriages, and divorces, and to act as heads of household. Female labor-force participation rose from roughly 17–20% in 2016–2018 to 36% by end-2024 — exceeding Vision 2030's 30% target years early — and Saudi unemployment fell to 7.0% in Q4 2024, the Vision 2030 target met six years ahead of schedule (GASTAT labor statistics; Vision 2030 Annual Report 2024; IMF Article IV reporting, 2025). The kingdom's first female ambassador (to the United States, 2019) was appointed on his watch, and cinemas, concerts, and mixed public entertainment — closed since the early 1980s — reopened from April 2018.
  • Migrant workers received the first loosening of the kafala system. The Labor Reform Initiative, effective 14 March 2021, allowed migrant workers covered by the labor law — about 6.7 million people — to change jobs after one year or at contract end without employer consent, and to obtain exit/re-entry permits electronically without the employer's permission for the first time (Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development; Human Rights Watch analysis, March 2021).
  • Yemen, the later record: Saudi Arabia was for years among the largest single humanitarian donors to the UN Yemen appeals, accepted the April 2022 truce, and pursued direct settlement talks from 2023 (UN OCHA funding records; Test 3).
  • The social opening is broad for citizens who do not dissent: entertainment seasons, sporting events, and tourism visas (introduced September 2019) treat ordinary Saudis and visitors as participants rather than subjects of the religious police, who no longer patrol public morality (Test 2).

Evidence against:

  • Yemen is the largest count-out in the record. The Saudi-led coalition's intervention, launched 26 March 2015 with the crown prince as defense minister and its principal architect, produced what the UN Development Programme estimated as 377,000 deaths by end-2021 — 154,000 direct and the majority indirect, from hunger, disease, and collapsed services — with children under five accounting for roughly 259,000 of the total on UNDP's modeling. The UN Group of Eminent Experts found violations by all parties, including coalition airstrikes that it assessed may amount to war crimes; the coalition's air and sea restrictions on Hodeidah port, through which most of Yemen's food and fuel passed, were a documented driver of famine conditions. Responsibility is shared — the Houthis' siege of Taiz, aid obstruction, and child recruitment are documented by the same UN bodies — and the apportionment is CONTESTED by the parties; what is not contested is the aggregate toll or that the intervention was his signature policy (UNDP, "Assessing the Impact of War in Yemen," 2021; UN Group of Eminent Experts reports, 2018–2021).
  • The women who campaigned for the reforms were imprisoned as the reforms were announced. In May 2018, weeks before the driving ban lifted, Loujain al-Hathloul and other leading women's-rights activists were arrested; al-Hathloul was sentenced in December 2020 by the Specialized Criminal Court to five years and eight months, partly suspended, on charges tied to her advocacy, and released in February 2021 under a travel ban. She and other released activists reported torture and sexual harassment in detention — ALLEGATIONs the government denies and no independent investigation has tested. The 2022 Personal Status Law, while setting a minimum marriage age of 18, codified core elements of male guardianship: a woman still requires her guardian's consent to marry, and the law frames a wife's obedience as a legal duty — a structure Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch assess as entrenching discrimination in the very code presented as reform (Specialized Criminal Court verdict, December 2020; Amnesty International UPR submission, 2023; HRW, March 2022).
  • Peaceful speech is counted as terrorism. Salma al-Shehab, a Leeds University doctoral student and Shia citizen, was sentenced in August 2022 to 34 years in prison — increased from six years on the prosecution's appeal — for following and retweeting dissidents and women's-rights activists; Nourah al-Qahtani received 45 years the same month for tweets "tearing the social fabric." The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found both detentions arbitrary and discriminatory — in al-Shehab's case partly on grounds of her Shia identity. (Al-Shehab's sentence was later reduced and she was released in February 2025 — on the for-shelf; al-Qahtani remains imprisoned as of this grading.) A retired schoolteacher, Mohammed al-Ghamdi, was sentenced to death in July 2023 by the Specialized Criminal Court on charges resting substantially on tweets and YouTube videos from accounts with a handful of followers (Columbia Global Freedom of Expression case analysis of the UNWGAD opinion; DAWN court-document reporting, August 2022; HRW, 2023).
  • The Shia minority's protesters fill the execution lists. On 23 April 2019, 37 men were executed in one day, at least 33 of them Shia, many convicted in the Specialized Criminal Court after trials HRW assessed as unfair, including for protest-related offenses from the 2011–2012 Eastern Province demonstrations. On 12 March 2022, 81 men were executed in the largest mass execution in the kingdom's modern history; the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights stated her understanding that 41 were Shia Muslims who had participated in those same 2011–2012 protests. Some of those executed in these rounds were minors at the time of their alleged offenses, per UN and rights-group documentation of individual cases (OHCHR statement, 14 March 2022; HRW, March 2022 and UPR submission, 2023).
  • The execution count overall reached record levels. After a moratorium on drug-offense executions of roughly three years, they resumed in late 2022; AFP's tally of official announcements counted 338 executions in 2024 and 356 in 2025 — successive all-time records — with drug cases the majority in 2025 (243 per AFP) and foreign nationals, overwhelmingly migrant workers, bearing a disproportionate share: in 2025, for the first time, more foreigners were executed than Saudis, per the European Saudi Organisation for Human Rights' count (AFP tallies, January 2025 and January 2026; ESOHR/Reprieve, January 2026).
  • The kafala reform stops at the most vulnerable. The 2021 initiative excludes the roughly 3.6–3.7 million domestic workers, farmers, shepherds, and private drivers not covered by the labor law — the workers most exposed to abuse — and the exit permit itself was retained in modified form; passport confiscation and wage theft remain widely documented (HRW, March 2021; Business & Human Rights Resource Centre, 2021).
  • The Huwaitat tribe was counted out of its own land for NEOM. Members of the Huwaitat, whose territory lies inside the NEOM zone, faced forced eviction from 2020; Abdul Rahim al-Huwaiti, who posted videos refusing to leave his home in al-Khuraybah and predicting the state would kill him and plant weapons, was shot dead by security forces on 13 April 2020 — the government stated he had barricaded himself and opened fire, a characterization his family and rights groups dispute (CONTESTED as to the circumstances; the killing itself is documented by UN special procedures correspondence, AL SAU 11/2020). On 2 October 2022 the Specialized Criminal Court sentenced three Huwaitat men — Shadli, Ibrahim, and Ataullah al-Huwaiti — to death for resisting eviction, and UN experts recorded at least 47 tribe members arrested in connection with the project, with others receiving prison terms of 15 to 50 years (OHCHR communication UA GBR 11/2023, April 2023; ALQST reporting, 2022). The government's position is that residents were offered compensation and relocation; that offers were made is documented, as is the penalty for refusing them.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Signature promises and stated positions vs. the record, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The economic core of Vision 2030 (announced 25 April 2016) is measurably delivered in part. Female participation exceeded its 30% target (36%, 2024); the 7% Saudi unemployment target was reached in 2024, six years early, and the target was revised down to 5%; non-oil GDP grew 4.5% in 2024 and non-oil activities reached roughly half of GDP; homeownership rose from 47% (2016) to 65.4% (2024) toward the 70% target; the Public Investment Fund grew from roughly $150 billion (2016) to about $941 billion in assets under management (2024) (GASTAT; Vision 2030 Annual Report 2024; IMF, 2025). Tourism targets were exceeded early — the kingdom reported over 100 million tourist visits in 2023, seven years ahead of the original schedule, and raised the 2030 target to 150 million.
  • The promised global-sports position was achieved. Having made sports a stated pillar of the kingdom's repositioning, Saudi Arabia secured the 2034 FIFA World Cup — confirmed on 11 December 2024 as the sole bidder, acclaimed by FIFA's congress — along with Expo 2030 (awarded November 2023) and the 2029 Asian Winter Games (awarded 2022; later relocated, below). FIFA and the Saudi bid present hosting as a catalyst for change; rights organizations (Amnesty International and a 20-group joint statement, 11 December 2024) call the award a moment of "great danger" for migrant workers and residents. The promise to win the events was kept; the change claim is CONTESTED (AP, 11 December 2024; Amnesty International joint statement, 11 December 2024).
  • The social promises were substantially kept. "We will return to a moderate Islam" (24 October 2017) was followed by the driving ban's end, the religious police's defanging, cinemas and concerts, and the guardianship relaxations of 2019 — the daily-life transformation he described is visible in the documented record, whatever its limits (Test 4).
  • Aramco was listed, as promised, in December 2019 — the largest IPO in history at the time ($25.6 billion raised) — though on terms far below the original pitch (against-shelf).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The Line and NEOM shrank by roughly 99% against the announcement. NEOM was announced on 24 October 2017 as a $500 billion zone; The Line, announced in January 2021, was specified as 170 km long, housing 9 million people, with 1.5 million residents by 2030. By April 2024, Bloomberg reported the 2030 target cut to 2.4 km and fewer than 300,000 residents; in September 2025 construction was suspended, and by May 2026 Semafor reported further work deferred until after 2030 at the earliest, with NEOM as a whole expecting up to 100,000 residents by then. Cost estimates reported by the Wall Street Journal and others run into the trillions against the $500 billion announcement. The 2029 Asian Winter Games at NEOM's Trojena resort — awarded in October 2022 — were relocated by the Olympic Council of Asia to Almaty, Kazakhstan (announced February 2026) because the venue could not be ready. The crown prince has not publicly revised the promise; officials describe the changes as phasing (Bloomberg, April 2024; WSJ reporting, 2024–2025; OCA announcement, February 2026).
  • The Aramco pitch was halved and quartered. The 2016–2017 plan was to sell 5% of Aramco at a $2 trillion valuation, raising over $100 billion, with listings in New York or London. What occurred in December 2019 was a 1.5% sale on the domestic Tadawul exchange at a ~$1.7 trillion valuation, raising $25.6 billion, bought largely by domestic and regional investors (IPO prospectus and contemporaneous reporting, 2019).
  • The oil-dependence deadline passed unmet. In April 2016 the crown prince said Saudi Arabia could live without oil dependence by 2020 — "I think in the year 2020, if oil stops we can survive." Oil and its derivatives still supplied roughly 60% or more of government revenue into the mid-2020s on IMF figures; diversification is real (for-shelf) but the stated date was missed by a wide margin (Bloomberg interview, April 2016; IMF Article IV, 2025).
  • Executions rose to records after he said they would be reduced. In 2018 the crown prince stated the kingdom was working to minimize the death penalty, limiting it essentially to homicide cases; he repeated reform intentions in a 2021 interview. The record: 338 executions in 2024 and 356 in 2025, the two highest totals ever tallied, the majority in 2025 for non-lethal drug offenses — the category the reform statements implied would shrink (Time and CBS interviews, 2018; AFP tallies, 2025–2026).
  • Yemen's promised brevity. The March 2015 intervention (Operation Decisive Storm) was presented by Saudi officials as a short campaign to restore the recognized government; it ran seven years at full intensity before the 2022 truce, never restored that government to Sanaa, and ended in negotiation with the Houthis still holding the capital (Test 4).
  • A penal code promised for 2022 had still not been enacted as of early 2026 — one of the four codification laws announced in February 2021 (the Penal Code of Discretionary Sanctions) remained outstanding per ALQST's February 2026 reporting, leaving the discretionary sentencing under which the speech cases of Test 4 are imposed uncodified.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Mohammed bin Salman's record is the sharpest split in this series between transformation delivered and power unchecked. The transformation is documented and large: women driving and working at rates that beat his own targets, unemployment at its promised level six years early, a non-oil economy growing, a society visibly reopened — and, on the wobble test, real adjustments: the Qatar blockade closed without its demands, the oil price war reversed in a month, Yemen moved from bombardment to negotiation, Iran moved from rupture to embassies. The power is equally documented and equally large: a predecessor crown prince detained without charge since 2020, princes and billionaires settling for their freedom in a hotel without a court, media and clerical establishments absorbed, and the ODNI's finding — untested in any Saudi proceeding — that he approved the operation that killed Jamal Khashoggi, a finding he answers with responsibility "as a leader" and denial of the order. The count is where the record is heaviest: 377,000 dead in Yemen on the UN's estimate, 41 Shia protesters among 81 men executed in a single day in 2022, record execution years in 2024 and 2025 falling hardest on foreign workers, a tribe evicted at gunpoint for a city that has since been suspended at 2.4 kilometers, and decades-long sentences for retweets handed down by a terrorism court. His word splits by subject: on the economy he promised, he largely delivered; on The Line, on Aramco's scale, on living without oil by 2020, and on reducing executions, the record runs the other way. The tests measure the seat, not the vision — and the seat gives by favor, grips by design, and counts everyone who does not cost it anything.

Free. Always free.
