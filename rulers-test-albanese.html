The Ruler's Test — Anthony Albanese, Prime Minister of Australia

Subject: Anthony Albanese, 31st Prime Minister of Australia (in office since 23 May 2022); led Labor to victory at the 2022 election and to a landslide re-election on 3 May 2025 — the first Australian prime minister in 21 years to win a second consecutive term, with Labor taking 94 of 150 House of Representatives seats (AP, 3 May 2025; BBC reporting via GlobalPost, May 2025). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Both shelves are shown for every test — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders; no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He created the institution that grades his own side: the National Anti-Corruption Commission (NACC), established by his government's legislation and opened 1 July 2023, with jurisdiction over ministers, parliamentarians and public officials of all parties, including his own government (NACC records; The Conversation analysis, July 2025).
  • On the Qantas upgrades affair (below), the documented core is that the upgrades were entered on the parliamentary register of interests — i.e., declared under the same disclosure rules that apply to every member — and Albanese has stated he received no upgrades as prime minister (register of interests; Albanese statements, October–November 2024).
  • His government legislated five-year funding terms for the ABC and SBS and restored arm's-length, merit-based appointments to the ABC board, applying institutional rules designed to bind his own government as much as any other (Minister for Communications media release, December 2024; Parliamentary Library FlagPost, February 2025).
  • The May 2026 property-tax reforms were designed with grandfathering that protected existing investors of all political stripes rather than retrospectively punishing a disfavoured class, and applied prospectively from announced dates (Budget Paper No. 2, 2026–27; Act No. 49 of 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The gambling file. His own government's parliamentary inquiry, chaired by the late Labor MP Peta Murphy, reported in 2023 ("You Win Some, You Lose More") with 31 recommendations, including a phased full ban on online gambling advertising. The government's response arrived more than 1,000 days later (announced 2 April 2026; bill introduced 2 July 2026) and implemented only a partial package — a cap of three TV ads per hour, no full ban, no national regulator (Interactive Gambling Amendment (Gambling Reform) Bill 2026; Senate Environment and Communications Legislation Committee inquiry, 2026). Crossbench MPs Kate Chaney and David Pocock, the Greens, church leaders and anti-gambling campaigners all criticised the package as shaped by industry lobbying; Murphy's widower stated publicly that "the limits of the reforms reflect, in large part, the considerable lobbying power of the gambling industry, major sporting codes and media organisations" (AAP, 2026; Senate inquiry submissions, July 2026). The industry's counter-position is also on the record: the wagering peak body argues the measures go too far and will push punters offshore (Responsible Wagering Australia, via AAP, 2026). No finding of impropriety exists; the documented fact is a multi-year delay followed by a package materially weaker than his own inquiry's, in the policy area where Australia's gambling, sporting and media lobbies are strongest.
  • The Qantas relationship. In October 2024 a book by journalist Joe Aston alleged that Albanese received 22 Qantas flight upgrades between 2009 and 2022, some while he was transport minister — the minister responsible for regulating the airline — and alleged, on unnamed Qantas insiders' accounts, that he liaised directly with CEO Alan Joyce about travel. Status: ALLEGATION. Albanese and his office denied he ever called Joyce, or contacted anyone at Qantas by any means, to request an upgrade, and stated all travel was declared (Prime Minister's office statements, 31 October–4 November 2024). The opposition called for him to refer himself to the NACC; no referral and no adverse finding followed. What is documented beyond the allegation: Joyce testified to a Senate hearing in September 2023 that he and Albanese had been "good mates… for years," and Albanese's son received Chairman's Lounge membership, a fact Joyce declined to confirm or deny gifting (Senate hearing record, September 2023).
  • The Qatar Airways decision. In 2023 his government rejected Qatar Airways' application for additional flights into Australia — a decision that protected Qantas's market position, was criticised by the competition regulator's former leadership, consumer groups and the opposition as favouring the incumbent national carrier, and was initially given no published reasons (widely reported, 2023; Senate inquiry into bilateral air service agreements, 2023). The government later defended the decision on national-interest grounds. CONTESTED: whether the decision was favouritism or ordinary aviation policy remains disputed between the government and its critics.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do states, courts, the public service, the ABC and media, and his own party room keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Robodebt aftermath. The scheme itself (2015–2019) predates him and belonged to the Coalition governments of Abbott, Turnbull and Morrison. His government's record is the response: it established the Royal Commission in August 2022, released the final report the day it was received (7 July 2023), and in November 2023 agreed or agreed in principle to all 56 recommendations, funding implementation and boosting the Commonwealth Ombudsman's oversight (PM Transcript 45130, 7 July 2023; Government Response to the Robodebt Royal Commission, November 2023). Sixteen former public servants were investigated by the Australian Public Service Commission, and a former departmental secretary was found to have breached the APS Code of Conduct 12 times (APSC process, reported 2024).
  • The public service was rebuilt, not purged. His government abolished the average staffing level cap, converted outsourced labour back into more than 13,200 permanent public service roles, and invested in Services Australia frontline capacity — the institutional opposite of a loyalty purge (Budget Paper No. 4, 2026–27; Minister for Finance statements, 2025–26).
  • The courts' hardest ruling was obeyed. When the High Court ruled in NZYQ (November 2023) that indefinite immigration detention was unconstitutional, the government released the affected cohort — roughly 350 people — despite the severe political cost, rather than defying the Court (High Court, NZYQ v Minister for Immigration, 2023). When the High Court later unanimously rejected the government's novel argument for immunity from false-imprisonment liability for those detentions (Abdel-Hady v Commonwealth, 2026), the government accepted the ruling and its damages exposure (High Court judgment, 2026).
  • The ABC's independence was structurally strengthened (see Test 1): five-year funding terms moved toward legislation, indexation restored, board appointments returned to arm's-length merit selection (Parliamentary Library FlagPost, February 2025).
  • The states kept their spin. Major national reforms of his second term — hospital funding, schools funding, housing — were negotiated through National Cabinet with state and territory governments, including Coalition-led states, rather than imposed (National Cabinet agreements, 2025–26; Closing the Gap 2026 Implementation Plan).

Evidence against:

  • The watchdog was designed with a grip on it. During the NACC's creation, the government inserted a late amendment requiring "exceptional circumstances" before the Commission could hold public hearings — against the position of crossbench parliamentarians who had supported the bill, and, on later reporting, against the preference of his own attorney-general, with Albanese personally identified as a long-time sceptic of public hearings (The Conversation, July 2025; SMH reporting, 2026). In its first years the NACC held no public hearings. The Commission's first commissioner, Paul Brereton, resigned in 2026 after the NACC Inspector found he had engaged in "officer misconduct" in failing to properly recuse himself from the Robodebt-referral decision involving a former official who was his army-reserve colleague (NACC Inspector findings, reported 2025–26). The Inspector's intervention forced a reversal: from February 2025 the NACC has been investigating the six referred officials (NACC statements, 2025). The institution corrected itself — but the design choice that weakened its transparency was his government's, and the Robodebt decision it first produced was the one the Inspector found contaminated.
  • Parliament was used to outrun the courts on detention. After NZYQ, the government legislated a preventive-detention and monitoring regime (ankle bracelets, curfews) for the released cohort; the High Court struck the monitoring conditions down as unconstitutional punishment in 2024, and struck a revised version down again in 2026. Home Affairs Minister Tony Burke responded to the second ruling by announcing the government would accelerate removals to Nauru under its deportation agreement rather than accept the cohort's presence in the community (High Court rulings, 2024 and 2026; ministerial statements, 2026). Legislating around a court ruling is lawful; twice legislating punitive conditions the Court then voided is a documented pattern of the executive pressing against the judicial boundary.
  • Party room autonomy is real but managed. No ministerial colleague has mounted a leadership challenge and Albanese governs with an unusually disciplined caucus; however, in May 2025 two senior ministers lost their ministry places through factional ballot processes he presided over rather than by his personal selection — evidence cuts both ways: the party's internal machinery operated, and it operated to outcomes widely reported as not his first preference (reporting of the May 2025 ministry reshuffle).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, states, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Voice defeat was accepted in full, on the night. The referendum of 14 October 2023 — his signature first-term commitment, to constitutionally enshrine an Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Voice — was defeated with more than 60% voting No and every state except the ACT voting No. Albanese's response that night: "I absolutely respect the decision of the Australian people and the democratic process that has delivered it," and "as prime minister I will always accept responsibility for the decisions I have taken, and I do so tonight" (referendum-night address, 14 October 2023). There was no attempt to legislate the Voice anyway, no attack on the voters or the process, and no repetition of the referendum.
  • The stage-three tax-cut revision was announced as a change of position, in person. On 25 January 2024, at the National Press Club, Albanese announced his government would redesign the legislated stage-three tax cuts to give larger cuts to low- and middle-income earners and roughly halve the cut for incomes above $200,000 (from $9,075 to $4,529). He did not pretend continuity: the Treasurer stated "we have come to a different position… we are being upfront with people," and Albanese defended the change on changed-circumstances grounds — "Some would say that we should stay the course, even if it means going to the wrong destination. To them I say, we are choosing a better way forward" (National Press Club address, 25 January 2024). (The broken promise itself is graded in Test 5; the open owning of the change is graded here.)
  • The Bondi royal commission was eventually established. After the 14 December 2025 Bondi Beach terrorist attack killed 15 people at a Hanukkah event, Albanese initially resisted a federal royal commission, arguing it would take too long and appointing a faster security-agencies review under Dennis Richardson instead. After 25 days of sustained pressure from victims' families, Jewish community leaders and the opposition, he established the Royal Commission on Antisemitism and Social Cohesion in January 2026 under former High Court justice Virginia Bell, folding the Richardson review into it (Reuters, 8 January 2026; AP, February 2026).
  • The May 2026 tax changes were likewise owned in plain words. The morning after the Budget changed negative gearing and the capital gains discount — settings he had ruled out changing during the 2025 campaign — Albanese said: "Well, we have changed our position, and we're upfront about that" (post-Budget interviews, 13 May 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Bondi adjustment came late and was spun, not owned. Albanese did not concede his initial position was wrong; in a 7.30 interview he framed the 25 days as orderly consultation and process, while families had publicly pleaded for the commission a week earlier (PM Transcript 47138, January 2026; The Conversation's "View from The Hill," January 2026). The pattern — resist, hold until the position is untenable, then adjust while denying there was anything to adjust about — recurs in his record.
  • The $275 power-bill promise was walked away from, not answered. Labor's 2021–22 modelling claim that its energy plan would cut household bills by $275 by 2025 was repeated by Albanese with confidence ("I don't think… I know because we have done the modelling"). Bills rose instead; ABS data showed electricity costs up 32.2% in the year to January 2026 alone. Albanese distanced himself from the figure — "it's RepuTex's modelling… I'm not supporting that modelling anymore" — without a plain statement that the commitment had failed (2022 campaign statements; subsequent interviews; ABS CPI data, 2026; House of Representatives debates, 3 November 2025 and 2 March 2026).
  • Closing the Gap: determination rhetoric against a worsening ledger. The Productivity Commission data behind the February 2026 annual statement showed only four of 19 Closing the Gap targets on track — down from five the year before — with four targets, including Indigenous suicide reduction and adult incarceration, stalled or going backwards (Productivity Commission data; Commonwealth Closing the Gap 2025 Annual Report). Albanese's response was to reject the frame: "We must guard against talk of failure… I am not contemplating failure" (Closing the Gap statement, 12 February 2026). The investments announced alongside were real (see Test 4); the refusal to name the trajectory is the wobble un-answered.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — First Nations peoples after the Voice, migrants and asylum seekers, renters and the young, the coal regions — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • First Nations investment continued after the referendum loss. Rather than abandoning the field after October 2023, the government pursued what Albanese called "practical reconciliation": a 10-year, $4 billion joint investment to halve overcrowding in remote Northern Territory housing, $600 million in dedicated First Nations housing through the Housing Australia Future Fund, doubling the Remote Jobs and Economic Development Program to 6,000 jobs ($299 million), a $218.3 million First Nations family-violence plan, and expanded remote food-security subsidies (Closing the Gap 2025 Annual Report and 2026 Implementation Plan; PM Transcript 47214, February 2026).
  • Renters and welfare recipients received the largest structural increases in decades: the largest rise in Commonwealth Rent Assistance in over 30 years, permanent increases to working-age payment base rates, expanded single-parent payment eligibility, a legislated 20% cut to student debts in the second term, and expanded paid parental leave (Government Response to Robodebt Royal Commission, November 2023; second-term legislation, 2025).
  • Coal regions were given a transition institution rather than abandonment: the Net Zero Economy Authority was established to support workers and regions through energy transition, and the government continued taxpayer support to keep system reliability during closures rather than forcing abrupt shutdowns (Net Zero Economy Authority Act 2024).
  • The count extended outward: in September 2025 his government recognised the State of Palestine — a decision framed by the government as counting a people long outside the ledger (UN General Assembly period announcements, September 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The NZYQ cohort was counted out, at almost any cost. The roughly 350 non-citizens released after the High Court's NZYQ ruling — people who had served any criminal sentences, including stateless people and failed asylum seekers — were subjected to two rounds of punitive monitoring laws the High Court struck down (Test 2), and then to a deportation agreement with Nauru: an upfront payment exceeding $400 million plus about $70 million a year (officials told a Senate committee the deal could total roughly $2.5 billion over 30 years) to exile them on 30-year visas (Nauru agreement, August 2025; Senate committee evidence, September 2025). In 2026 the High Court dismissed a challenge to the deportations even where inadequate medical care in Nauru was said to put lives at risk (TCXM v Minister for Immigration), and the government proceeded (High Court, 2026; Human Rights Law Centre statement, 2026). Home Affairs Minister Tony Burke's stated rationale: "the best thing for people who have had their visa cancelled is to not be in this country" (ministerial statement, 2026). Human rights organisations' characterisation — exile of a small, unpopular cohort regardless of individual consequence — is on the record and undisputed in its factual core.
  • Offshore processing itself continued uninterrupted. The Nauru processing arrangements inherited from the previous government were maintained and then expanded into the deportation vehicle above; at no point in his tenure did the government move to end offshore processing (Home Affairs records, 2022–2026).
  • First Nations outcomes went backwards on his watch, whatever the inputs. Four of 19 Closing the Gap targets on track in 2026, down from five in 2025; the targets to reduce adult incarceration and children in out-of-home care worsening (Productivity Commission data, February 2026). And the structural half of his 2022 commitment — he had promised to implement the Uluru Statement from the Heart "in full": Voice, Treaty, Truth — was quietly shelved after the referendum: by the May 2026 budget, the Commonwealth's budget papers made no provision for treaty or truth-telling processes, a fact noted by critics across the spectrum (2026–27 Budget papers; Institute of Public Affairs media release, 13 May 2026 — an opponent's characterisation, with the underlying budget omission a matter of record).
  • Renters and the young are still waiting on the housing promise. The National Housing Accord target — 1.2 million new homes in five years from July 2024 — is badly off track: commencements ran at roughly 197,000 in the year to March 2026 against the 240,000-a-year pace required, the Housing Industry Association estimating the country "almost 30 per cent below target," and the National Housing Supply and Affordability Council projecting roughly 938,000 completions over the Accord period (ABS building data, 2026; HIA statements, 2026). Rental vacancy remained near record lows (~1.6%) with advertised rents up 5.4% in the year to January 2026 (Cotality data, 2026). The government's own May 2026 budget admitted its property-tax changes would further reduce near-term construction — a cost the government accepted, but a cost borne by the cohort already counted last.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Signature promises vs. the record, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The integrity commission exists. Promised before the 2022 election; legislated in 2022; operating from 1 July 2023 (NACC records). Its weakened public-hearing design is graded in Test 2; the promise to create it was kept.
  • Climate targets were legislated and then raised. The 43%-by-2030 and net-zero-by-2050 targets were written into law in September 2022 (Climate Change Act 2022). In September 2025 the government adopted a 2035 target of 62–70% below 2005 levels, within the Climate Change Authority's advised range of 65–75% at its lower end, with more than $8 billion in supporting measures (government announcement, 18 September 2025). Renewable generation has roughly doubled across his tenure to about half the electricity mix (Prime Minister's COP31 address, November 2026).
  • Tax cuts for every taxpayer were delivered — redesigned (Test 3), but delivered from 1 July 2024, with further legislated cuts in the second term (Treasury Laws Amendment (Personal Income Tax Cuts) legislation, 2024; 2026–27 Budget).
  • The second-term promises began landing: the 20% student-debt cut was legislated, the National Environmental Protection Agency and Centre for Disease Control established, and university fees reduced (second-term record, 2025–26).

Evidence against:

  • The $275 power-bill reduction never happened. Promised on RepuTex modelling throughout the 2021–22 campaign and defended by Albanese personally; by 2025–26 household electricity costs were substantially higher, with a 32.2% rise in the year to January 2026 alone (ABS CPI). The government substituted temporary energy bill relief payments — real assistance, but not the promised reduction (see Test 3).
  • "My word is my bond" — then the stage-three redesign. Albanese used the phrase "my word is my bond" in 2022 while committing to implement the legislated stage-three cuts unchanged; in January 2024 he changed them. The change was openly owned and benefited most taxpayers (Test 3), but the commitment broken had been stated in exactly the language of personal honour (2022 statements; National Press Club, 25 January 2024; Hansard, 29 May 2024).
  • Negative gearing and the capital gains discount: ruled out "for the 50th time," changed thirteen months later. During the 2025 election campaign (Cairns, 9 April 2025), asked to rule out changes to negative gearing and capital gains tax, Albanese answered: "Yes. How hard is it? For the 50th time." He repeated "no plans" through April 2025 and in August 2025 said the government would implement only "the tax policy we took to the election." The Budget of 12 May 2026 limited negative gearing to new builds for properties bought after Budget night and replaced the 50% CGT discount with indexation plus a 30% minimum tax from 1 July 2027 (Budget Paper No. 2, 2026–27; Act No. 49 of 2026; campaign transcripts, April 2025; Hansard, 25 May 2026). As with stage three, the change was admitted openly the next morning; as with stage three, the prior assurance had been absolute and recent.
  • The Uluru Statement "in full" became the Voice alone, then neither. The 2022 commitment was to implement the Uluru Statement from the Heart in full — Voice, Treaty, Truth. After the Voice referendum failed, no Commonwealth Makarrata (treaty/truth) body was established, and by 2026 the commitment had disappeared from the budget and the government's stated agenda (2026–27 Budget papers). Albanese has not formally renounced the commitment; he has simply stopped acting on two-thirds of it.
  • Climate ambition vs. fossil approvals remains the standing contradiction. While legislating targets, his government approved an extension of Woodside's North West Shelf gas project to 2070 (Environment Minister Murray Watt, 2025) — a project expected to produce on the order of 90 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent a year — and continued approving coal and gas projects under the EPBC Act. The Greens' characterisation ("sold out to coal and gas corporations," September 2025) and the Australian Conservation Foundation's ("timid") are opponents' words; the approvals themselves are matters of record, and they sit unresolved beside the targets. The government also faces a documented risk of missing the 43%-by-2030 target it legislated (SMH analysis, 18 November 2026).

Overall Summary

Anthony Albanese's record under the Test is the record of a holder who built real institutions and obeyed real rulings, but whose grip tightens around the unpopular few and whose word bends under pressure. He established the anti-corruption commission, accepted all 56 Robodebt recommendations, rebuilt the public service rather than purging it, strengthened the ABC's independence, and complied with the High Court's most politically costly ruling. Against that: the integrity watchdog was born with a transparency lock he insisted on, the gambling industry waited out his own inquiry and received a fraction of it, punitive laws for a few hundred released detainees were twice struck down and then routed around via a $2.5 billion Nauru exile deal, Closing the Gap went backwards to four targets on track out of nineteen, and three emphatic commitments — stage three unchanged, $275 off power bills, no changes to negative gearing or the CGT discount "for the 50th time" — were each reversed or unmet, though the reversals, to his credit, were announced in plain words rather than disguised. Grades: One Law — MIXED. Holds Without Gripping — MIXED. Answers the Wobble — MIXED. Counts Everyone — MIXED. Keeps His Word — MIXED.
