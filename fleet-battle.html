Finding ships — three honest ways to hunt

1 · Checkerboard parity

Fire only on one colour of an imaginary chessboard laid over the chart. Every ship of length 2 or more must cover at least one square of that colour, so no ship can hide from the pattern forever.

Trade-off: a guarantee, and dead simple to hold in your head — but it spends shots on cold water late in the game, when only one small ship is left.

2 · Probability heat

Count every way the surviving ships could still fit on the chart, and fire where the most fits overlap. The middle runs hot early; edges and corners run cold. This is the tool Coach draws for you — and the whole of how Pro plays.

Trade-off: fastest on average — nothing else is close — but the hot spot moves with every shot, and early on it can pull your fire into a cluster while a corner ship waits.

3 · Smallest-ship sweep

Space your hunting shots a full smallest-ship apart, in a loose grid — while the smallest ship afloat is 3 long, no shot needs a neighbour closer than 3 squares. Every shot questions the most new water it can.

Trade-off: the widest coverage per shot, and the cure for clustering — but a ship lying diagonally across your grid points can slip between them, where parity would have caught it.

Finishing a ship — the one place there's nearly one right play

Finish what you hit

Once a shot lands, sinking that ship before hunting again is as close to a single right answer as this game has: a wounded ship is certain points, and wandering off lets nothing improve. Two ways to walk it, though:

Cross pattern — with one hit and no direction, try its four neighbours. Safe, works from any single hit. Trade-off: up to three wasted shots finding the direction.

Walk the line — with two hits in a row, the ship lies on that line: extend both ends until it sinks. Trade-off: fastest finish there is — unless the two hits belong to two ships lying side by side, in which case the line is a lie and the cross was right.

Hiding your own fleet — three ways to place

1 · Spread wide

No two ships near each other, all over the chart. A found ship gives away nothing about the rest.

Trade-off: you must use the hot middle water too, where hunters look first.

2 · Hug the edges

Edges and corners are the coldest water on the probability map — fewer placements run through them, so hunters reach them last.

Trade-off: once an edge ship is found, its direction is half-given away — it can only run along the edge or straight out.

3 · One busy corner

Cluster the whole fleet in one corner and leave an ocean of nothing. A hunter can burn twenty shots in your empty water.

Trade-off: when the cluster is finally found, one good streak can unravel ship after ship. High risk, high delay.

How honest is the bot? — tap to open

The four levels are four different brains, not one brain with a handicap. Each was measured by playing 2,000 simulated games against random standard fleets on the 10 × 10 chart, counting the shots it needed to sink the whole fleet. Fewer is stronger. These are the measured numbers, run on this page's own bot code:

LevelHow it actually playsAvg shots to win
LearningFires at untried squares purely at random. No memory of a pattern, no plan — the spraying this game punishes.95.4
SteadyHunts on the checkerboard parity pattern; when it hits, it pokes the neighbouring squares until the ship sinks.55.3
SharpHunts spaced by the smallest surviving ship, and finishes ships by the probability map instead of guessing directions.51.2
ProPlays the full probability heat map on every single shot — the same map Coach can draw for you.46.1

For scale: there are 100 squares and 17 ship squares on a standard chart, so pure luck (Learning) needs almost the whole board. Pro still can't see your fleet — it only counts better. Placement, luck and salvo mode all move a single game; the averages are what separate the levels.

The documented game — tap to open

Fleet Battle is our own version — our name, our words, our art — of a proven, documented game: the hidden-fleet pencil-and-paper game soldiers were playing by the First World War, first sold in print as Salvo in 1931, and published as a plastic board game in 1967. The standard game here keeps its documented shape exactly: two fleets of five ships (5, 4, 3, 3, 2) hidden on a 10 × 10 chart, shots traded one at a time, hits and sinkings called honestly, first to sink the whole enemy fleet wins. Everything beyond that shape — salvo mode, other chart sizes, other fleets — is a labelled variant in the rule settings, never passed off as the standard game.
