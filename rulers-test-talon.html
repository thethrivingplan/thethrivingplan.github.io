The Ruler's Test — Patrice Talon, President of Benin (2016–2026)

Subject: Patrice Guillaume Athanase Talon — President of the Republic of Benin from 6 April 2016 to 24 May 2026, graded here on his completed tenure, following this series' handling of completed holdings. The premise in the original brief is confirmed in its main line and corrected in its details: Talon was term-limited, the presidential election was held on 12 April 2026, his finance minister Romuald Wadagni won it with 94.27% of the vote against Paul Hounkpè (5.73%), official turnout above 63%, and Talon handed over the presidency at Wadagni's inauguration on 24 May 2026 (Reuters, 24 May 2026; Constitutional Court final results). What the brief could not yet carry: the main opposition party, Les Démocrates, was kept off the 2026 presidential ballot by the sponsorship mechanism (Test 1); the ruling coalition took all 109 seats in the 11 January 2026 legislative election; a coup attempt on 7 December 2025 was defeated with Nigerian and ECOWAS help; and on 6 August 2026 Talon was elected president of the Senate — a chamber that did not exist until his own November 2025 constitutional revision created it, and whose 25 members are none of them elected (TM Africa, August 2026; CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). Talon, born in 1958 in Ouidah, is a cotton magnate ("the king of cotton") who had himself spent 2012–2015 in exile under his predecessor Thomas Boni Yayi, accused in an alleged poisoning plot, before a mediated settlement let him return and win the 2016 election with 65.4% in the run-off. Wadagni's holding since 24 May 2026 — six months at grading — is noted as context where it bears on a test, most sharply in Test 2: the instruments Talon built remain in use, and the two most prominent prisoners of his tenure, Reckya Madougou and Joël Aïvo, remain in prison as of November 2026 (Beninese press commentary appealing for clemency, 16 November 2026). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Casualty figures are kept in separate sourced counts and never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic printed on the Test card governs this dossier: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one — carrying out what Talon ran on (economic reform, institutional revision) is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how it was done — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4).

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The special court he built eventually consumed his own inner circle. In January 2025 the Court for the Repression of Economic Crimes and Terrorism (CRIET) sentenced Olivier Boko — a businessman long described as Talon's right hand and a possible successor — and Oswald Homeky, a former sports minister from his camp, to 20 years each for an alleged 2024 plot to overthrow him (contemporaneous reporting, January 2025). Whatever else that trial was (its fairness is disputed by the defendants and their supporters, and the plot itself remains an ALLEGATION as to its details), the machine did not stop at the president's own doorstep: the two most powerful men in his entourage after himself were prosecuted, convicted, and imprisoned by his own court.
  • The fiscal law was applied broadly rather than selectively. Under Talon and finance minister Wadagni, customs and tax enforcement widened the revenue base across the trading economy, the budget deficit was cut from about 6% of GDP in 2015 to 2.8% in 2025, and public debt was held to 53.2% of GDP, below the WAEMU ceiling (Beninese government transition document, June 2026 — government-stated figures; the deficit path is corroborated in direction by the World Bank's Benin Economic Update, 2024, which records growth of 6.3% in 2022 and 6.4% in 2023 on the back of these reforms). The cotton sector — his own industry — was reorganized under state-set rules rather than left as a private preserve, though opponents allege, as ALLEGATION, that the reorganization also served interests close to him; no court has so found.

Evidence against:

  • The electoral law was rewritten so that entry to the ballot ran through his own camp. The 2018 electoral code and party charter imposed a heavy financial deposit and a "certificate of conformity" from the Interior Ministry; in February 2019 the Constitutional Court — headed at the time by his former personal lawyer — upheld the certificate requirement, the electoral commission (CENA) then invalidated five of seven party lists, and the April 2019 legislative election was contested by exactly two parties, both loyal to Talon, which took all 83 seats (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2020; US State Department Human Rights Report 2019). Turnout fell to about 27% amid an opposition boycott, and the internet was shut down on election day.
  • The sponsorship mechanism then converted that exclusion into a standing veto over the presidency. The November 2019 constitutional revision — adopted by the opposition-free assembly without a referendum — required presidential candidates to be sponsored by 10% of MPs and mayors, officeholders who, after 2019, were overwhelmingly of Talon's camp. For the 2021 presidential election, 20 candidacies were filed and three approved: Talon and two minor figures (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022). For 2026 the mechanism worked to the single signature: Les Démocrates held exactly 28 of 109 seats, the threshold was 28 sponsorships, one deputy's sponsorship (Michel Sodjinou, who had publicly disputed his party's choice of candidate) was ruled invalid, leaving their candidate Renaud Agbodjo with 27 — and the CENA's rejection was upheld by the Constitutional Court on 27 October 2025 (CENA decision, 23 October 2025; Constitutional Court ruling, 27 October 2025; contemporaneous reporting).
  • CRIET's political docket tracked the opposition map. Reckya Madougou, a former justice minister and the Democrats' prospective 2021 candidate, was arrested in March 2021 after protesting her exclusion and sentenced by CRIET in December 2021 to 20 years for "complicity in terrorist acts." Law professor Joël Aïvo, another barred 2021 aspirant, was arrested four days after that election and sentenced by CRIET in December 2021 to 10 years for plotting against the state and money laundering. Sébastien Ajavon, who placed third in 2016, was sentenced in absentia in 2018 to 20 years on drug-trafficking charges he and his supporters describe as political, and remains in exile. The US State Department stated in December 2021 that the Madougou and Aïvo trials "raise grave concerns about political interference in Benin's criminal justice system." A CRIET judge who fled the country in 2021 stated, as reported, that the court received instructions in sensitive cases and that political pressure had compelled Madougou's detention — the government's position is that the court is independent; the dispute is recorded, not resolved.
  • The regional court found the pattern, and was refused. The African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights ruled in case after case — including Ajavon's, in March 2021 — that Benin and CRIET had violated fundamental rights, and called for repeal of the exclusionary amendments before the 2021 election. Benin withdrew from the court's individual-petition protocol in 2020 and did not comply (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022).
  • Speech was policed under a second, lower track: Amnesty International documented at least 17 detentions of journalists and activists under the 2018 Digital Code's provisions on "false information" and online "harassment" (Amnesty International documentation, compiled 2021).

Why: Test 1 grades the source — whether the law, at the point where it is written and first applied, is the same law for his side and the other side. For a full decade the heaviest legal instruments in Benin — ballot access, the sponsorship gate, the special court, the digital code — landed on the opposition, the press, and the exiled, while no figure of the ruling camp faced CRIET for anything except falling out with the president himself. The Boko–Homeky convictions are real evidence that the machine could bite inward, and they are outweighed: a law that reaches the ruler's friends only when they become his rivals is not one law for all; it is one law for the loyal. The grade is FAILS. Helped: Talon and his coalition, whose candidates faced cleared fields in 2019, 2021, and 2026; Wadagni, who inherited the cleared field. Hurt: the excluded parties and candidates (Les Démocrates and Renaud Agbodjo in 2026; every opposition list in 2019); the imprisoned (Reckya Madougou, Joël Aïvo) and the exiled (Sébastien Ajavon and others); voters, whose choices were pre-filtered before any ballot was cast.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Early in the tenure, a referee bit him and he submitted. In April 2017 parliament voted 60–22 for his constitutional revision — short of the required supermajority — and Talon accepted the defeat on television: "It's true that 60 members of parliament were willing to examine the proposal… unfortunately 22 were not favorable… I'll respect that. The constitutional revision initiated by president Talon is now over" (Reuters, April 2017). The revision he had campaigned on, including the single-term presidency, died by the rules and he let it die.
  • The opposition was readmitted to parliament once, and seated. In the January 2023 legislative elections Les Démocrates won 28 of 109 seats and took them, giving Benin an opposition bloc for the 2023–2026 legislature — the one legislature of the decade in which the ruling camp faced organized opposition on the floor (contemporaneous reporting, January 2023).
  • The security and judicial apparatus prosecuted the December 2025 mutineers through courts rather than by summary reprisal, and the state television, briefly seized, was retaken within hours (Reuters and CNN, 7 December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The Constitutional Court's decisive interventions ran one way, under a president of the court who had been Talon's personal lawyer: the February 2019 conformity-certificate ruling that enabled the exclusion of the entire opposition; the October 2025 ruling upholding the exclusion of the main opposition from the presidential ballot (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2020; Constitutional Court ruling, 27 October 2025).
  • The electoral commission's record is the exclusion record: only pro-Talon lists validated in 2019; 17 of 20 presidential candidacies refused in 2021; the main opposition ticket refused in 2025 over one contested sponsorship (CENA decisions, 2019, 2021, 2025). In July 2026, under his successor, the National Assembly removed the CENA's provisions from the electoral code altogether as its council's term ended (electoral-law reporting, July 2026) — recorded as successor context: the referee was not strengthened after him either.
  • Parliament was his camp's alone for most of the decade: all 83 seats in 2019–2023, and — after the 2024 electoral code raised the seat threshold to 20% in each constituency — all 109 seats again from January 2026, the Democrats' roughly 16% national vote converting to zero seats on 36.7% turnout (legislative results, January 2026; CIVICUS Monitor, 2026).
  • The media referee acted at scale against the press in his final year: on 13 November 2025 the High Authority for Audiovisual and Communication (HAAC) withdrew press cards from 130 journalists and suspended eight broadcasters in a "clean-up" the Media Foundation for West Africa criticized for its scale and abruptness (MFWA statement, November 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2026).
  • The constitutional architecture was rebuilt by his majority, without a referendum, in ways that entrenched his camp and then himself. The November 2019 revision (opposition-free assembly) created the vice-presidency and the sponsorship gate. The November 2025 revision (Law No. 2025-20, adopted 90–19 in the night of 14–15 November 2025, without referendum or public consultation — CIVICUS Monitor and Hivos Enabling Environment Snapshot, 2026) extended presidential, parliamentary, and local terms from five to seven years and created a Senate of 25 members, none elected: former officeholders sit by right and the rest are appointed by the president and the National Assembly's president. The Senate was installed on 30 July 2026, and on 6 August 2026 it elected Talon its president, 24 of 25 votes with one abstention (TM Africa, August 2026). The man who wrote the chamber into existence now presides over it.
  • The external referee was shed: Benin's 2020 withdrawal from the African Court's individual-petition protocol, and its non-compliance with that court's rulings, removed the one bench his government did not appoint (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022).

Why: A seat holds without gripping when the referees can say no and make it stick. Benin's referees said no to Talon exactly once on a matter he cared about — parliament, in 2017, before the exclusions — and he honored it; that is on the record and credited. Thereafter every referee that mattered was progressively brought inside his camp's perimeter: a court led by his former lawyer, an electoral commission whose validations cleared his field three elections running, a media regulator that struck 130 press cards in a day, two parliaments without an opposition, and finally an unelected upper house of his own design with himself in its chair. The grip did not relax when he left the presidency; it changed rooms. The grade is FAILS. Helped: Talon personally — a constitutionally entrenched platform after office, presiding over the Senate he created; his coalition, which holds every elected institution. Hurt: the Constitutional Court, CENA, and HAAC as independent referees; Les Démocrates and their voters, excluded by the referees' rulings; the 130 journalists who lost press cards in November 2025; and his successor's freedom of maneuver, which now runs under the eye of the predecessor's chamber.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The 2017 wobble — his own parliament killing his flagship reform — was answered with open acceptance, quoted in Test 2: no referendum end-run, no purge of the 22 who blocked him, the revision declared "over" (Reuters, April 2017).
  • The casualty wobble of April 2025 was corrected in public, upward. After the 17 April 2025 JNIM attack on military posts in the W National Park — the deadliest in Benin's history — authorities first reported 8 soldiers killed; six days later the government spokesman confirmed 54 at a press briefing. JNIM claimed 70; the two counts are printed separately here, as they were in the coverage, and the government's correction of its own figure is the point for this test: the state revised its number toward the truth, openly (International IDEA democracy tracker, April 2025; government briefing, 23 April 2025).
  • The December 2025 coup attempt was answered without breaking the calendar. On 7 December 2025, National Guard mutineers under Lt. Col. Pascal Tigri seized state television, attacked the presidential residence and senior officers' homes, and announced Talon's overthrow. Loyal forces, with Nigerian support and an ECOWAS standby deployment, restored order the same day; several people were killed, including at least one civilian, and 13–14 arrests were reported in the first days (counts vary by source) (Reuters, CNN, contemporaneous reporting, December 2025). The no-good-choice clause applies to the emergency hours and is applied here by its mechanics: the options actually open were to request regional military help, to face armed mutineers with loyal units alone, or to negotiate with men who had just attacked the presidency — each carrying real harm; the least-harm door, regional help under ECOWAS's constitutional-order mandate, was taken, and the constraint is recorded, not counted against him. What he did not do with the emergency is the stronger evidence: he did not postpone the April 2026 election, extend his term, or cancel the handover. The wobble that has ended a hundred presidencies was absorbed and the schedule held.
  • After the 2019 crisis he opened a partial door: the October 2019 "political dialogue" produced an amnesty law and the release of former president Boni Yayi from 52 days of de facto house arrest (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022). It was adjustment on his own terms — the exclusionary rules stayed — but the pushback was answered with something other than force alone.

Evidence against:

  • The street's pushback in 2019 and 2021 was answered with live ammunition and then with impunity. The African Court found that Beninese security forces used live ammunition on protesters in 2019 in violation of their rights; the newly seated all-government assembly then passed an amnesty covering the security forces, and no accounting followed (African Court rulings; human-rights documentation compiled 2021). The tolls are kept in separate sourced counts in Test 4.
  • When another option became available through the regional court — repeal of the exclusionary amendments before the 2021 election, as the African Court called for — he refused to look at it: the government ignored the call and held the election on the contested rules (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022). Under the addendum, considering another option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look is. No stated reasons were given; the ruling was simply not complied with.
  • The October 2025 sponsorship wobble was answered by letting the arithmetic stand. The main opposition's exclusion turned on one deputy's contested sponsorship out of 28; a government intent on a contested election could have accepted a cure period or a liberal reading. The exclusion stood, and the field was cleared (Test 1).
  • The post-coup answer to speech was prosecution, and it fell on people far from the mutiny. In the months after 7 December 2025, CRIET prosecuted opposition figures Alassane Tigri (a former minister and Democrats vice-president) and Candide Azannaï, and — on charges including "apology for crimes against state security" and "incitement to rebellion" — journalists and online commentators who had posted about the attempt: journalist Pascal Mitowadé was sentenced on 24 March 2026 to five years; Democrats activist Julien Kandé Kansou, arrested in June 2025 over criticism of a proposed internet tax, was sentenced on 17 March 2026 to two years (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). A coup attempt explains arrests of plotters; it does not explain five years for a journalist's social-media post, and the record shows no adjustment on this point.

Why: The wobble record splits cleanly in two, and the split is the grade. Wobbles that arrived through institutions he still respected — parliament in 2017, casualty facts in 2025, the constitutional calendar after a coup attempt — were answered openly, sometimes impressively. Wobbles that arrived as demands for pluralism — the street in 2019 and 2021, the African Court, the opposition's ballot access in 2025, speech after the coup — were answered with force, silence, or prosecution. A ruler who adjusts on facts and process but never on power is neither a denier nor an adjuster whole; the grade is MIXED. Helped: the constitutional calendar and everyone invested in it — voters, his successor, regional partners — when the coup attempt was absorbed without an extension; the public record, when the April 2025 toll was corrected upward in the open. Hurt: protesters in 2019 and 2021, whose pushback was met with live fire and an amnesty for the shooters; the journalists and activists prosecuted for speech in 2025–2026; the African Court's authority, refused without reasons.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's voters, the north, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The material ledger of the decade is real, and it was national in reach. GDP growth averaged above 6% in 2021–2023 (6.3% in 2022, 6.4% in 2023), above the 2010–2019 average and resilient through COVID-19, Nigeria's policy shocks, and the Niger border closure (World Bank, Benin Economic Update, 2024). Electricity access roughly doubled over the decade and per-capita income rose about 50% in CFA-franc terms (Beninese government transition document, June 2026 — government-stated figures). The Glo-Djigbé Industrial Zone began processing cotton and cashew at home instead of exporting them raw (World Bank, 2024). Infrastructure — roads, water, the Port of Cotonou's modernization — was built across regions, not only in the capital.
  • The north was not simply abandoned to the spillover: from 2022 the government maintained a standing military deployment (Operation Mirador) in the northern departments and kept the state's presence in the border zones as the Sahel war pressed south (contemporaneous reporting, 2022–2026).
  • The 2026 transfer itself counted every vote that was allowed to be cast: the result was proclaimed by the Constitutional Court, the loser conceded the arithmetic of a two-man race, and power changed hands on schedule without violence (Reuters, May 2026).

Evidence against:

  • At the ballot box, a large share of Benin was counted out three elections in a row. 2019: every opposition party excluded; turnout ~27% under boycott. 2021: 17 of 20 presidential candidacies refused, the two most prominent rivals in prison or exile by election day. January 2026: the opposition's ~16% of the national vote converted to zero of 109 seats under the 20% district threshold. April 2026: the main opposition party absent from the presidential ballot entirely (Tests 1–2). Whatever the growth figures say, the political accounting of the decade systematically excluded the citizens who did not support him.
  • Protesters in opposition strongholds were counted as a security problem, and some were killed. The counts are kept separate, as the sources give them. 2019: AFP reported a woman died in Cotonou in the May 2019 post-election clashes (medical sources, May 2019); two people were killed in Savè in June 2019 when police fired on protesters (AFP reporting, June 2019); Freedom House records "several deaths" across the 2019 suppression. 2021: AFP/Al Jazeera reported one dead and six wounded by gunfire in Savè on 8 April 2021 (local health official); Freedom House records two deaths in the pre-election suppression; a human-rights compilation of the period records four killed in the April 2021 demonstrations. No consolidated official toll was ever published, and the 2019 amnesty foreclosed prosecutions of security-force members.
  • The north bore the war's cost while the south held the growth. ACLED data show JNIM attacks in Benin caused more fatalities in the first months of 2025 (157 by late April) than in all of 2024 (103) (Armed Conflict Location & Event Data, via Critical Threats, April 2025). The single deadliest blow fell on northern soldiers: 54 killed on 17 April 2025 by the government's confirmed count; 70 by JNIM's claim — printed separately, never blended. Northern border communities lived the displacement, closed markets, and school closures that accompany an insurgency's arrival; the mutineers of December 2025 themselves cited the deteriorating northern security as a stated motive (their communiqué, 7 December 2025 — recorded as their stated reason, not as a finding).
  • Poverty narrowed slower than the economy grew. Roughly a third of Beninese remained in poverty at the handover (Beninese government transition document, June 2026), and his own successor's inaugural address conceded the gap in terms: "Our economy has progressed. That's a fact. But we all know that national growth only makes sense when it becomes visible in people's everyday lives" (Wadagni inaugural, via Reuters, 24 May 2026).

Why: Test 4 asks whose lives enter the accounting. Materially, Talon's Benin counted more people in — power, water, roads, and industrial jobs reached beyond the capital, and the growth was real. Politically and in blood, it counted people out: opposition voters were filtered from three consecutive elections before a vote could be counted, protesters in the wrong towns were met with live rounds and no accounting, and the northern communities absorbed an insurgency whose cost the capital's statistics do not carry. A decade that counts everyone in the GDP and a shrinking share of them in the democracy fails this test on the test's own terms. The grade is FAILS. Helped: urban and southern households reached by power, water, and roads; cotton and cashew producers tied into the Glo-Djigbé value chain; formal-sector traders and the treasury, on a decade of 6%-plus growth. Hurt: opposition voters in 2019, 2021, and 2026, counted out before counting began; protesters killed in Cotonou and Savè in 2019 and in Savè in 2021 (separate counts above); northern border communities and the families of the 54 soldiers of 17 April 2025; the roughly one-third of Beninese still poor at the handover.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 6 April 2016, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept):

  • The exit — his most consequential word, kept in full. Throughout his second term Talon stated he would respect the two-term limit and leave in 2026; he announced publicly in April 2025 that he would not be a candidate; his own November 2025 constitutional revision, which lengthened terms to seven years for his successors, kept the two-term limit and hardened it into a lifetime cap — the revised Article 42 provides that no one may serve more than two presidential terms in a lifetime — and did not reset his own count. On 24 May 2026 he handed the presidency to the elected successor and left the palace (Reuters, 24 May 2026; Law No. 2025-20, November 2025). In a region where, in this same decade, incumbents rewrote constitutions to stay, the word about leaving was given repeatedly, in advance, and kept to the day.
  • The reform attempt of 2017 was genuine. The single-term presidency he campaigned on was actually brought to parliament within his first year, and when it fell short he accepted the result rather than forcing it (Test 3) — the attempt and the acceptance are documented (Reuters; Freedom House, April 2017).
  • The economic program was substantially delivered. The Government Action Programs of 2016 and 2021 promised growth, fiscal consolidation, infrastructure, and industrialization; the record shows growth above 6% in the second term's first years, the deficit cut from ~6% of GDP (2015) to 2.8% (2025, government figures), debt held below the regional ceiling, the industrial zone built, and the port modernized (World Bank, 2024; government transition document, 2026). Under the mandate rule, carrying out the platform he was elected on is FOR-shelf evidence, and it is recorded here at full strength.

Evidence against (promises broken or receding):

  • The founding pledge was reversed. As a candidate in March 2016 Talon promised a single term: "I will first and foremost tackle constitutional reform," he said on his victory, arguing that one term of five years would reduce presidential "complacency" (Reuters, 26 March 2016). The reform failed in 2017 — and in 2021 he stood for, and won, a second term. His defense is documented and is printed here: the single term was a reform he sought and parliament refused, so the existing two-term constitution governed, and he broke no law. His critics' reading is also documented: the pledge was made personally and repeatedly on the campaign trail, and voters who chose him in 2016 as the one-term candidate were not consulted again before he ran. Both readings stand; the fact beneath them is not disputed — the man who promised to serve one term served two.
  • The referendum promise was dropped. On the campaign trail in 2016 Talon promised that constitutional change would go to the people in a referendum. The two revisions that actually reshaped the state — November 2019 and November 2025 — were adopted by parliament alone, the second without public consultation of any kind (campaign reporting, 2016–2017; CIVICUS Monitor and Hivos, 2026). The 2019 revision was adopted by an assembly from which the opposition had been excluded months earlier.
  • The democratic-deepening word against the record. He was elected in 2016 as the candidate who would modernize Benin's democracy, then one of West Africa's models. The decade that followed is graded in Tests 1, 2, and 4: three narrowed elections, a special court whose biggest cases were his rivals, and Freedom House's downgrade of Benin from Free to Partly Free during his tenure (Freedom in the World, 2021 onward). The word and the record diverge, and the divergence is the against-shelf.

The four crossings, named (word × outcome): 1. Kept his word and it helped: the 2026 exit. The promise to leave was kept, and the keeping helped — a peaceful, on-schedule transfer of the presidency in a region of coups and third-term revisions, with the successor inaugurated without violence. 2. Kept his word and it hurt: the institutional-reform program. He said he would rewrite the rules of the republic, and he did — sponsorship gates, a special court, seven-year terms, an unelected Senate — and the keeping itself narrowed political competition and excluded the citizens graded in Tests 1 and 4. The keeping earns credit here; the hurt is recorded in full there. 3. Went against his word and it helped: the single-term reversal, on his camp's documented claim. The continuity he bought by running in 2021 delivered the second-term economic program — the growth, consolidation, and industrialization figures on the FOR shelf are real. The claim that Benin is better off for the broken pledge than it would have been under a successor is his camp's argument, recorded as such; the output figures beneath it are documented. 4. Broke his word and it hurt: the same reversal's other face. The 2021 election the broken pledge produced was held on rules written after the opposition's exclusion, with his two most prominent rivals imprisoned by election day and protesters killed in the pre-election suppression (Tests 1 and 4).

Why: Test 5 turns, in this dossier, on two words pointing in opposite directions. The word about entering — one term, a referendum, a deepened democracy — was broken or receded. The word about leaving — two terms, out in 2026, no third-term revision for himself — was kept completely, publicly, and on schedule, and it is the rarer and heavier word in his region and decade. Neither launders the other; the axes are printed separately, as the crossings above show. The grade is MIXED. Helped: every Beninese with a stake in constitutional succession — the exit word, kept, is the foundation his successor's legitimacy stands on; beneficiaries of the delivered economic program (Test 4's FOR shelf). Hurt: the voters of 2016 who chose a one-term president and got two terms; the opposition figures and voters whose 2021 election was shaped by the reversal; public trust in campaign pledges as an institution.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Patrice Talon governed Benin for ten years as a builder with a cleared site. The building is real: a decade of growth above the regional average, a consolidated treasury, an industrial zone, modernized infrastructure, and — the rarest structure in the region — a presidency that ended on the day the constitution said it would, with the incumbent handing power to an elected successor and walking out. The clearing is also real, and it is what the grades record. The law was not one law (Test 1): the ballot-access rules, the sponsorship gate, the special court, and the digital code fell on the opposition, the press, and the exiled, and the regional court that said so was shed. The referees were gripped (Test 2): a Constitutional Court led by his former lawyer at the decisive moment, an electoral commission that cleared his field three times, a media regulator that struck 130 press cards in a day, two parliaments without an opposition — and, as the capstone, an unelected Senate of his own creation that he now presides over, so that the grip survived the handover it was built to frame. The wobble splits (Test 3): he accepted institutional defeat in 2017, corrected his own casualty figures upward in 2025, and absorbed a coup attempt in December 2025 without touching the calendar — and he answered the street with live fire and amnesty for the shooters, ignored the African Court without reasons, and prosecuted speech after the coup. The counting splits the same way and lands against him (Test 4): more Beninese counted into power, water, and growth; opposition voters counted out of three elections; protesters killed in counts that were never officially consolidated (2019: at least one woman in Cotonou per AFP, two in Savè per AFP, "several" per Freedom House; 2021: one per AFP's health-official source, two per Freedom House, four per a human-rights compilation — never blended, never officially totaled); 54 soldiers dead in a single northern day in April 2025 by the government's count, 70 by the attackers' claim. His word is the two-sided file of Test 5: the entry pledges (one term, referendum, deepened democracy) broken or receded; the exit pledge kept to the day. The crossings, named in full in Test 5: kept-and-helped (the 2026 handover), kept-and-hurt (the reform program delivered as promised, narrowing competition as delivered), broke-and-helped (the second term's economic continuity, on his camp's claim over documented figures), broke-and-hurt (the 2021 election the broken single-term pledge produced). The open files that will move these grades belong now to the successor's tenure and to time: whether Wadagni releases or retries the CRIET prisoners — Madougou and Aïvo were still in prison in November 2026 — whether the Senate Talon chairs becomes a check on the presidency or a perch above it, and whether the cleared field of 2026 is the last one Benin sees. The tenure is complete; the record, as always in this series, is correctable in the open.
