Mancala

The sowing game. Pick up a pit, drop its stones one by one around the board, and finish with more stones in your store than your opponent. No dice, no cards, no luck — just counting.

Free — always free. No ads, no account, nothing to buy. This page works offline: save it or add it to your home screen and the whole game travels with you, no connection needed.

The documented game: this is Kalah, the mancala game written down and published by William Champion Jr. in 1940 — the best-documented member of the mancala family, a family of sowing games played and recorded for centuries across Africa and Asia. The rules below follow Kalah as documented: six pits a side, four stones a pit, an extra turn when your last stone lands in your store, and a capture when it lands in an empty pit on your side. Where this page changes a rule, it says so in plain sight and calls it a variant.

    How to play — tap to open

    Your side is the row of six pits nearest you (South, at the bottom) and the big store on your right. North plays the top row, with the store on the left.

    On your turn: tap one of your pits. All its stones are picked up and dropped one by one, counter-clockwise, into each pit and store that follows — including your own store, skipping your opponent's store.

    Free turn: if your last stone lands in your store, you move again.

    Capture: if your last stone lands in an empty pit on your side, that stone and every stone in the pit directly across go into your store.

    Ending: when one side's six pits are all empty, the game ends and the other player sweeps every stone left on their side into their store. Most stones in the store wins.

    The one skill: counting. A pit whose stone count exactly equals its distance to your store lands its last stone in the store — a free turn. Pits are numbered by that distance in the lessons: the pit beside your store needs 1 stone, the next needs 2, and so on up to 6.

    Keyboard: Tab to a pit, Enter to sow it. Every pit button says its count and whose it is.

    Strategies the pros use — tap to open

    There is rarely one right way in Kalah. These are the documented lines of play, each with its price:

    1. The free-turn chain

    Open and play to land in your store again and again — each landing is another move. In the classic start, the pit with 4 stones that sits 4 steps from your store does it on move one. Trade-off: chaining empties your right-hand pits, and an empty pit facing a loaded enemy pit is a capture waiting to happen — yours to spring, if you get back to it first.

    2. The capture trap

    Leave one of your pits empty with a full pit across from it, then land a single stone there to take the whole pile. Pros set this up two moves early: first sow a pit so it creates the empty pit, then arrange a one-stone landing. Trade-off: the trap only pays if the across-pit stays loaded — a good opponent will sow it away or capture first.

    3. The store race (hoarding)

    Feed your store steadily and build the pit beside it, sowing it when its count can carry stones all the way around and back into the store. Trade-off: stones parked on your side at the end get swept by whoever still holds them — a hoard you never bank can become the other player's endgame gift.

    4. The starve-out finish

    When you are ahead, empty your own side to end the game on your terms. Trade-off: ending the game hands the opponent every stone left on their side — only pull this when your lead beats everything they could sweep.

    Coach mode points at whichever of these the board is actually offering, and says when a different line beats it.

    Are the bot levels honest? — tap to open the self-play check

    The four levels are built differently, and the difference was tested before this page shipped, on November 18, 2026, under standard rules (4 stones, captures on). The fair test: 24 openings were shuffled at random, and in each pairing both bots played both sides of the same opening — 48 games a pairing — so the powerful first move could not pick the winner. Here is what actually happened:

    MatchResult (48 games)
    Pro vs Sharp36 – 9 (3 draws)
    Pro vs Steady46 – 2
    Pro vs Learning47 – 0 (1 draw)
    Sharp vs Steady47 – 1
    Sharp vs Learning47 – 1
    Steady vs Learning37 – 8 (3 draws)
    Sharp vs Sharp (control — same bot both sides)23 – 23 (2 draws), as a fair test should read

    Learning picks loosely and overlooks free turns and captures on purpose, the way a new player does. Steady takes the best move it can see in one move. Sharp reads the board five moves deep. Pro reads eight moves deep with a sharper eye for captures and free turns, and solves the endgame exactly once twelve or fewer stones remain in the pits. One more honest finding: from the untouched starting position, Pro and Sharp split 8–8 over 16 games — between two strong players the first move is worth that much in Kalah, which is exactly why the check above shuffles the openings. If two levels ever tie in a fair check like this, the labels get fixed; during this game's build, Pro had to be made genuinely stronger twice before it earned its name.
