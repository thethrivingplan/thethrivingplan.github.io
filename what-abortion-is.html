What abortion is

Abortion is the ending of a pregnancy before birth, using medication or a procedure. This page explains what it is, how it is done, the situations it actually happens in, and how it is not used — with every major claim tied to its source.

General information only, not medical advice. Laws and availability vary by place and change over time. For a personal situation, a clinician is the right source.

What it is

In medicine, induced abortion ends a pregnancy deliberately, with medication or a procedure. That is the meaning used in this page, in law, and in the statistics below. ACOG; WHO

It is different from a spontaneous abortion — the medical term for a miscarriage, where a pregnancy ends on its own. The same medications and procedures are sometimes used to care for a miscarriage, because in both cases the task is to empty the uterus safely. ACOG

Pregnancy length is usually counted in weeks of gestation, starting from the first day of the last menstrual period. Most timing figures in this page use that count.

How it is done

Medication abortion

Two medicines in sequence, for early pregnancy. Mifepristone blocks progesterone, a hormone a pregnancy needs to continue. Misoprostol, taken 24–48 hours later, causes cramping and bleeding that empty the uterus. Misoprostol can also be used on its own. The FDA-approved regimen covers pregnancy through 10 weeks. ACOG; FDA via ACOG

Procedural abortion

In the first trimester, usually vacuum aspiration: the cervix is gently opened and a thin tube uses suction to remove the pregnancy tissue. It typically takes a few minutes in a clinic. Later in pregnancy, the usual procedure is dilation and evacuation (D&E), which combines suction and instruments, or, less commonly, an induction using medication. ACOG

How it is used — and how it is not used

It is used to

  • End a pregnancy a person does not wish to, or medically should not, continue — for the reasons in The situations below.
  • Treat some medical emergencies in pregnancy, where ending the pregnancy is part of keeping the pregnant person alive.
  • Complete care after a pregnancy has already died or cannot survive, in some cases.

It is not

  • Something that happens after birth. Abortion by definition ends a pregnancy. Ending a baby's life after birth is not abortion, in medicine or in law, and claims that it happens as abortion are false. KFF
  • Emergency contraception. The “morning-after pill” works before a pregnancy starts, mainly by delaying ovulation. It does not end an established pregnancy.
  • A miscarriage. A miscarriage is a pregnancy ending on its own; an induced abortion is a deliberate ending, even though follow-up care can look the same.
  • Sterilisation. It does not prevent future pregnancies — fertility normally returns quickly afterwards. WHO

The situations

The circumstances in which abortions actually happen, as patients and clinicians report them. Most decisions involve more than one reason at once. Guttmacher Institute

1. To save the pregnant person's life or health

In medical emergencies, ending the pregnancy is sometimes the treatment that keeps the mother alive — for example with severe preeclampsia (dangerously high blood pressure in pregnancy), heavy bleeding, infection when the fetus is dying, a pregnancy that is worsening a serious illness, or cancer treatment that cannot wait. KFF; ACOG

2. Lethal or severe fetal conditions

Some diagnoses mean the fetus cannot survive, or would die shortly after birth — for example anencephaly, where the brain and skull do not form. These diagnoses, often only found at the 18–20 week anatomy scan or later, account for a large share of the rare later abortions. KFF

3. Rape and incest

A small reported share: about 1% of US patients in survey data said the pregnancy resulted from rape, and under 0.5% said incest. Researchers note these are self-reported figures and may be undercounts. Guttmacher Institute, US patient surveys 1987 & 2004

Pregnancies from rape — how many, and how many are carried to term

How many: The standard study (Holmes et al., American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology, 1996; national sample of 4,008 US women) found about 5% of rapes of victims aged 12–45 result in pregnancy — an estimated 32,101 rape-related pregnancies a year in the US at that time.

What happened with those pregnancies, in that study: 50% ended in abortion; 32.2% were carried to term and the child kept; 5.9% were carried to term and placed for adoption; and 11.8% ended in miscarriage — so about 38% were brought to term. Also in that study, 32.4% of victims did not discover the pregnancy until the second trimester. Holmes et al., 1996

After the bans — a modeled estimate: Dickman et al. (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2024) estimated 64,565 pregnancies resulted from rape in the 14 states with total abortion bans during the first 4–18 months the bans were in effect (July 2022 – January 2024). Of those, 91% (58,979) were in states with no rape exception, and Texas alone accounted for an estimated 26,313. The researchers found few if any of these resulted in a legal in-state abortion — meaning, unless the person travelled or obtained pills another way, those pregnancies were carried to term.

Honest label: That 2024 figure is a modeled estimate, not a count, with a wide range — 95% confidence interval roughly 52,000–86,500 — built from federal victimization surveys. Criticism: the Heritage Foundation argued the method overstates the count, saying it conflates lifetime and per-incident pregnancy rates. Authors' reply: the authors stand by their method. Dickman et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2024; Heritage Foundation critique

4. The common, everyday reasons

In the Guttmacher Institute's survey of US patients, the most-cited reasons were: not the right time — about 74% said a baby would interfere with education, work, or care for others; not financially prepared — about 73%; partner-related problems or not wanting to be a single parent — about 48%; and needing to focus on other children. Most US patients already have at least one child. Guttmacher Institute, 2004 survey, published 2005

About later abortions Abortions at 21 weeks or later are about 1% of the total (see The numbers). Studies and reviews find they are most often tied to medical crises, lethal fetal diagnoses, or delays — not discovering the pregnancy until later, or taking time to reach care. CDC 2022; KFF

Common misconceptions and fear-based ideas

Claims people commonly hear, each answered with the checked fact. No mockery — just what the evidence says, and where it comes from.

Most abortions happen late, even at birth

False. In CDC data for 2022, 92.8% were at or before 13 weeks and about 1% were at 21 weeks or later. None occur after birth — abortion ends a pregnancy, by definition. CDC, Abortion Surveillance — United States, 2022

Abortion causes infertility

No evidence for that with safe, legal abortion. Major medical reviews find no link between safe abortion, done with a recommended method by a trained provider, and later infertility. The known fertility risks come from untreated infection or complications, which are rare in safe care. WHO; major medical reviews

Abortion causes breast cancer

No causal link found. After reviewing the evidence, including large studies using medical records rather than memory, the National Cancer Institute concluded induced abortion is not associated with an increase in breast cancer risk. National Cancer Institute; summarised by Susan G. Komen

Abortion causes mental illness

Not according to the largest review. The American Psychological Association's review found a single, first-trimester abortion of an unwanted pregnancy carried no greater mental-health risk than carrying that pregnancy to term. The strongest predictor of mental health afterwards was mental health beforehand. People can feel many things after either choice; that is different from abortion causing mental illness. American Psychological Association, 2008 review

The abortion pills are especially dangerous

Serious complications are rare. Across large studies, serious complications from medication abortion — such as infection or bleeding needing a transfusion or hospital care — occur in well under 1% of cases. Cramping and bleeding are expected parts of the process, not complications. Large clinical studies, reviewed by Guttmacher Institute & National Academies

People use it as casual birth control

The survey data point the other way. About half of US abortion patients — 51% in 2014 — reported they were using contraception in the month they conceived, most often condoms or the pill. No method is 100% effective, and methods can fail or be used imperfectly. Guttmacher Institute, Abortion Patient Surveys 2000 & 2014

Graphic descriptions show what a typical abortion looks like

Widely circulated graphic descriptions generally do not match the procedures as performed at the early gestations where most abortions occur. At 9 weeks, the embryo is about an inch long, and an early procedural abortion is aspiration — tissue removed by suction through a thin tube, in a few minutes. ACOG; CDC 2022 timing data

How often it happens — the numbers

Every number below names its source and year, because the US has no single complete count.

613,383

Abortions reported to the CDC in 2022 — a rate of 11.2 per 1,000 women aged 15–44. CDC reporting is voluntary and incomplete: not every state reports. CDC, 2022

≈ 1,032,000

Estimated abortions in the US in 2023 in the Guttmacher Institute's fuller count, which surveys providers directly. Its later revisions put 2023 slightly higher. Guttmacher Institute, Monthly Abortion Provision Study

92.8% by 13 weeks

When: CDC 2022 — 78.6% at or before 9 weeks, 92.8% at or before 13 weeks, and about 1% at 21 weeks or later. CDC, 2022

63% by medication

How: Medication abortion was 63% of US clinician-provided abortions in 2023; the rest were procedural. Guttmacher Institute, 2023

Most are already mothers

Who: In Guttmacher patient surveys, the majority of US patients already have at least one child, and most have low incomes. Guttmacher Institute, patient surveys

≈ 73 million a year, worldwide

The WHO estimates about 73 million induced abortions a year globally — roughly 3 in 10 (29%) of all pregnancies, and 6 in 10 unintended pregnancies. WHO

Honest label on the US counts US totals differ by who is counting and how. The CDC counts what states voluntarily report, so its total is lower. Guttmacher surveys providers directly, so its estimate is higher and closer to a full count — but it is still an estimate, and it is revised as more data arrives. Neither number is a complete, person-by-person census.

When people who wanted or needed one could not get one

Since the US Supreme Court's Dobbs decision in 2022 returned abortion law to the states, access has split sharply by state. Both halves of that picture are documented.

1. In ban states, in-state abortions fell to nearly zero

In states that enacted near-total bans or very early gestational limits after 2022, clinician-provided abortions inside those states fell to nearly zero. That part is not in dispute in either CDC-era or provider counts. Guttmacher Institute; KFF

2. The barriers for those who still tried

  • Travel: distance and cost to reach a state where it is legal, including transport, lodging, childcare, and time off work.
  • Capacity: clinic closures in ban states, and longer waits at clinics in legal states taking out-of-state patients.
  • Cost: the price of the procedure itself, which rises with gestation.
  • Rules: state waiting-period requirements that add a second trip or extra days.
  • Timing: discovering the pregnancy late, which narrows every option above.

Barriers summarised from KFF and Guttmacher tracking of post-Dobbs access.

3. Medical-need cases where care was delayed

News investigations by ProPublica, and reports from medical groups, have documented emergencies — miscarriages and dying pregnancies — in ban states where hospital care was delayed while staff weighed legal risk. In some of those documented cases, the patient died, and state maternal-mortality reviewers or medical experts consulted by ProPublica called deaths preventable. This is reported here as ProPublica and those reviewers documented it; the full scale is not known, because no complete national count of delayed-care cases exists. ProPublica investigations, 2024; medical groups

4. The other half: nationally, the total did not fall

Nationally, total US abortions did not fall after the bans — counts from both the Guttmacher Institute and the Society of Family Planning's #WeCount project rose slightly. The documented reasons are that many people travelled to legal states, and many used medication mailed under other states' shield laws — laws that protect providers in a legal state who prescribe and mail pills to patients in ban states. Interstate travel for abortion roughly doubled compared with before Dobbs. Guttmacher Institute; #WeCount via KFF

5. The measurable remainder: births above expectation

Studies found births rose a few percent above what would have been expected without the bans — in Texas after its 2021 ban, about 9,800 more births than expected in April–December 2022 (roughly 3%), and across 14 ban states studied by Johns Hopkins researchers, fertility was about 1.7% higher than expected, an estimated 22,180 additional births. Researchers are explicit about the limit here: this remainder — people who carried a pregnancy they would otherwise have ended — can be estimated from birth records, but never counted exactly, person by person. JAMA, Texas SB8 study 2023; Johns Hopkins / JAMA, 2025
