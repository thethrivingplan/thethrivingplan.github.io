The Ruler's Test — Azali Assoumani, President of the Union of the Comoros

Subject: Azali Assoumani — President of the Union of the Comoros since 26 May 2016 in the current tenure. The seat graded is the presidency, and it is the executive seat as actually held: the Comorian president is head of state and head of government, appoints and dismisses ministers and senior officials, commands the armed forces, and — since the constitutional revision of 2018 — presides over a system in which electoral disputes are judged by a chamber of a Supreme Court whose members he appoints. Azali, born in 1959, a former army colonel, first took power in the bloodless coup of 30 April 1999, stepped aside in January 2002 to contest the presidency as a civilian, and served an elected term from 2002 to 2006, at the end of which he handed power to his elected successor — the first peaceful transfer between elected presidents in Comorian history. He returned to office by winning the 2016 election under the 2001 constitution, with roughly 41 percent of the second-round vote. He is graded on the tenure since May 2016, weighted to recent years; his 1999–2006 record appears only as context where his own earlier word or conduct bears directly on a test. Three premise points are stated plainly at the top. First, the holder is confirmed: as of November 2026 Azali remains president — addressing the UN General Assembly in September 2026, attending COP31 in Antalya and the Francophonie summit in Cambodia in November 2026 — in the term that runs to 2029 (UN Web TV, September 2026; AKP, 14 November 2026). Second, a correction to the common shorthand about 2018: the referendum of 30 July 2018 did not abolish island rotation outright. It abolished the single-term rotation of the 2001 Fomboni system and the three vice-presidencies, allowed two consecutive five-year terms, and left a modified rotation in which one island may hold the presidency for two consecutive terms before the turn passes on; Grande Comore, which has held the presidency since 2016, exhausts its double turn in 2029, when the turn passes to Anjouan. Secondary sources that say rotation was simply "abolished" are noted where they appear; the dossier grades the precise position, which his own presidency reaffirmed in January 2025. Third, the January 2024 election produced two irreconcilable official counts — the electoral commission's and the Supreme Court's — and they are printed separately throughout this file, never blended. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic (printed on the Test card, above the five tests): "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one; the Test grades the holding, never the platform. Carrying out the program Azali ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. One boundary is recorded because it shapes the file: Azali was elected in 2016 under the 2001 constitution, whose platform included the single-term rotating presidency; the 2018 revision that reset his eligibility was adopted mid-term, by referendum, and was not itself the platform the voters had elected in 2016. Marks are lost only for how power is held: a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4).

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution at the center of the against-shelf was not invented from nothing. The "economic citizenship" program run under his predecessor Ahmed Abdallah Mohamed Sambi — Comorian passports sold to stateless Bidun in the Gulf under a 2008 law — was found by a parliamentary commission to have been illegal and its revenues embezzled, and the scheme's scale is documented independently of the prosecution: tens of thousands of passports issued beyond the number approved, with the proceeds disputed to this day (International IDEA democracy tracker, November 2022; contemporaneous reporting of the trial, November 2022). A state prosecuting a documented, billion-dollar diversion of public money by a former head of state is, on its face, applying the law upward — the direction one law is supposed to run.
  • The legal machinery has, in individual recent cases, touched figures inside the state's own apparatus. In the May 2025 file opened around the extradited online critic Abdousalam Ibrahim Mpingou ("Bachar"), the judicial investigation reached a former minister, Ali Ibouroi, a police commissioner, and an army captain, all placed in pre-trial detention alongside the critic himself (Civicus Monitor, 2025). The case is double-edged — it began as a prosecution of a government critic — but the detentions of officials are on the record.
  • The everyday corruption indicators are bad but not the region's worst, and they have not collapsed on his watch: Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index placed Comoros 163rd of 180 in the 2025 index — near the bottom, and a standing rebuke — while the state continued to publish budgets, hold donor conferences with public pledges, and operate a formal anti-corruption framework on paper (Transparency International CPI 2025, as tracked in 2026 index summaries).

Evidence against:

  • The Sambi case, inspected as process rather than as target, is the dossier's limiting case of two laws. Sambi was placed under house arrest on 19 May 2018 — the day after he publicly criticized Azali's suspension of the Constitutional Court and the planned referendum (Alkarama submission to the UN, 2023). The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention, in Opinion No. 65/2018, found his deprivation of liberty arbitrary and called for its remedy; the government did not release him (UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention, Opinion 65/2018). He spent more than four years in detention before trial — against a legal maximum of eight months' pre-trial detention, as his lawyers stated and contemporaneous reporting recorded — first on corruption charges that were then reclassified as "high treason," an offence his French lawyer Jean-Gilles Halimi states does not exist in Comorian law, a characterization the prosecution disputes (AFP reporting, November 2022). He was tried on 21–24 November 2022 before the State Security Court, a special court whose rulings cannot be appealed, after asking the presiding judge to recuse himself for having sat on the panel that indicted him; he refused to attend most of the trial, saying he could not be judged fairly; and on 28 November 2022 he was sentenced to life imprisonment (AFP and Reuters reporting, November 2022; International IDEA, November 2022). Alkarama's submission further reports that the investigating judge who charged him in August 2018 was a nephew of the president — recorded here as an ALLEGATION from an interested advocacy source, not a finding. The human rights NGO Alkarama and other observers assessed the proceedings as politically interfered with; the government maintains the trial was lawful (International IDEA, November 2022). Eight years on, Sambi remains imprisoned; in May 2026 six former prime ministers wrote to Azali warning that Sambi's health was "deteriorating alarmingly" and asking that he be allowed treatment abroad (letter of 26 May 2026, reported 2026).
  • The same severity, aimed downward and outward at the opposition, recurs across the tenure. Abdou Salami Abdou, the elected governor of Anjouan arrested in October 2018 after the island's uprising against the referendum, was sentenced in March 2022 to twelve years in prison for alleged participation in an insurrection (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2023). Achmet Saïd Mohamed, leader of the opposition Hury party, was barred from the January 2024 presidential election and arrested in Moroni five days before the vote; he spent more than a year in detention, undertook a hunger strike, and was provisionally released only on 3 May 2025 (Civicus Monitor, 2025). Presidential candidates were detained during the 2019 cycle as well: Soilihi Mohamed and Achmet Saïd Mohamed were briefly detained in March 2019, gendarmes interrogated a candidate's spouse, and the army was used to detain opposition figures during and after the campaign (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2023).
  • No comparable file exists on the other side of the map. Across ten years, the documented record assembled here contains no prosecution of a senior figure of the ruling CRC, no investigation of the presidential family's enrichment — an ALLEGATION raised by opposition figures and diaspora media, recorded as such, never examined by any Comorian institution — and no accounting proceeding on the passport scheme's other beneficiaries beyond Sambi's circle. The law that reaches a former president for life does not visibly reach anyone whose conviction would inconvenience the seat. That asymmetry, not any single case, is what the test measures.

Why this grade: The deciding evidence is the contrast the record itself draws. Where the accused is the president's principal rival, the state found a special court, an unappealable verdict, and a life sentence on a reclassified charge, and held him four years before trial in defiance of a UN body's finding. Where the accused would be one of the president's own, the record shows no file at all. A law whose weight depends on the defendant's distance from the seat is two laws, and the test fails.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the Comorian treasury's claim, in principle, on the passport scheme's diverted revenues, and citizens in the abstract sense that a former head of state faced a court at all. Hurt — Ahmed Abdallah Mohamed Sambi, imprisoned for life after a process the UN Working Group's findings shadow from its first day; Abdou Salami Abdou, twelve years; Achmet Saïd Mohamed, sixteen months detained across an election he was barred from contesting; the candidates, spouses, and party officials detained in 2018–2019; and every future defendant whose case will be measured against the precedent that the state's heaviest instrument is reserved for the seat's opponents.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The referees have ruled against his side in the open, recently, and the rulings stood. On 22 January 2025 the Supreme Court annulled the results in four constituencies in the parliamentary elections — Domoni I, Domoni III, Nioumakélé III, and Itsandra-Sud — all won by the ruling CRC, citing procedural irregularities including the arbitrary replacement of poll workers and ballot-box problems, and ordered re-runs; it was only the second such annulment in Comorian history (International IDEA, February 2025; Crisis Group CrisisWatch, January 2025). A court willing to void four of the president's party's victories is not, on that day, a gripped court.
  • The legislature pushed back against the executive's own bill and won. The 2025 revision of the Information Code reached parliament carrying a draft article 169 that would have let a judge compel journalists to reveal their sources in "exceptional cases"; after lobbying by the National Union of Comorian Journalists and rights defenders, parliament rejected the article on 26 December 2025, adopted the code without it by 31 of 33 votes, and wrote into the new article 153 that journalists "cannot be harassed by public authorities" over their sources (Civicus Monitor, January 2026; International Federation of Journalists, 2025). The code was promulgated in that form.
  • The press, though pressured, still operates and has slightly improved its measured standing: Comoros rose from 75th to 72nd of 180 in RSF's World Press Freedom Index between the 2025 and 2026 editions, and in 2025 press cards were issued to Comorian journalists for the first time, a formalization step RSF records without endorsing the wider environment (RSF country file, 2026 index).

Evidence against:

  • The tenure's founding institutional act was the removal of a referee before the president's own vote. In April 2018, weeks before the referendum that would reset his eligibility, Azali suspended the Constitutional Court — the body Freedom House describes as having been "considered impartial in deciding electoral matters" — and transferred its competencies to a new constitutional chamber of the Supreme Court, whose members are presidential appointees (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2020 and 2024). The legality of the referendum was thereafter judged by the replacement body. The opposition's charge that the dismissal rendered the referendum illegal is their characterization, printed as such; the sequence — referee suspended, then the vote that benefited the suspender — is not contested by anyone.
  • The electoral commission has been handled the same way. Facing dissent in parliament, Azali dismissed the CENI's top opposition representative (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2020). In January 2024 the commission he appoints produced the election's first official count: Azali re-elected with 62.97 percent — on a presidential turnout the CENI itself put at 16.30 percent, in an election where the same commission counted 189,497 ballots cast for island governors the same day but only 55,258 ballots cast for president (CENI figures via International IDEA, January 2024; AFP, 24 January 2024).
  • The Supreme Court then replaced the count rather than auditing it. On 24 January 2024 the court's constitutional and electoral section validated the election with different numbers — Azali at 57.2 percent, turnout at 56 percent, 191,297 presidential voters — dismissing the opposition's annulment suits as inadmissible. AFP's report of the ruling records the essential fact: "It was not immediately clear how the adjustment came about" (AFP, 24 January 2024). No public reconciliation of the two counts — a difference of some 136,000 presidential ballots — has ever been published. The referee did not recount the match; it rewrote the score.
  • The prosecution service moves with the seat. The public prosecutor who announced the closure of the knife-attack inquiry (Test 3) is the same office that prosecuted Sambi; the State Security Court that sentenced Sambi is a special jurisdiction revived under Azali whose decisions admit no appeal (AFP, November 2022). The judiciary's ordinary courts function — the four annulments above are real — but every jurisdiction that touches the president's tenure, rivals, or attackers sits inside his appointment power or outside ordinary appeal.
  • The media's autonomy is policed by summons and cell nights rather than by closure, which RSF describes plainly: in the Comoros "intimidation and arrests of journalists are still commonplace," and the journalists' union has denounced the systematization of "the night at the brigade" (RSF country file, 2026). Between 2 and 6 February 2023 four journalists were questioned by the gendarmerie's investigation squad on defamation and insult complaints filed by the state broadcaster ORTC and one of its executives, and were tried that June (RSF, June 2023). In January 2024, after the disputed count, the government imposed the first nationwide internet shutdown in the country's history, with WhatsApp calls and mobile messaging blocked, to prevent protesters from coordinating (NetBlocks, January 2024; Access Now, February 2025).

Why this grade: The deciding evidence is structural, and it predates every later exception. A president who suspends the constitutional court before his own referendum, moves electoral judgment to a chamber of his appointees, removes the electoral commission's opposition voice, and is then declared re-elected on a turnout figure his court multiplied by three and a half without public explanation, has gripped every referee that touches his tenure. The 2025 annulments and the parliament's source-protection victory show the grip is not total — institutions he does not need on the day can still rule against his party — but the test asks about the referees of his own power, and those he holds.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the CRC and its candidates, whose 2024 victory was validated on the revised count and whose parliamentary supermajority followed; the presidency itself, insulated from any forum it does not appoint. Hurt — the Constitutional Court's dismissed judges and the institution itself, abolished as a separate body; voters in the four annulled constituencies, whose first ballots were voided (a hurt later repaired by re-run, and recorded on both shelves accordingly); journalists who spent nights at the brigade for their reporting; and the Comorian public, which has never been given a single reconciled account of how many of them voted in 2024.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The sharpest foreign pushback of the tenure was answered with adjustment rather than escalation. When France launched Operation Wuambushu in April 2023 — mass expulsions of Comorian migrants from Mayotte and clearance of its shantytowns — Azali's government refused to receive deportees shipped unilaterally, suspended passenger traffic at the Anjouan port of Mutsamudu, and criticized the operation publicly as contrary to human rights; Azali then took the dispute to Paris in person for negotiation rather than letting it harden, while conceding openly that Comoros did not have "the means to stop the operation through force" (ISS Africa, 2023; France 24 and AFP reporting, April–May 2023). The grievance was pressed, the door to talks was used, and the relationship survived.
  • He has, late and under pressure, moved on the file that most stains the record. After the six former prime ministers' letter of May 2026 on Sambi's deteriorating health, and years of refusal, Azali stated publicly in November 2026 that discussions were under way and preparations had begun for the imprisoned former president's medical evacuation abroad (Comorian press reporting via Afreeque, November 2026). The adjustment is incomplete — Sambi remains imprisoned, and no evacuation had been completed at grading — but the direction changed in the open, and the shelf records the movement, not the completion.
  • Two institutional pushbacks were accepted without reprisal: the Supreme Court's annulment of four CRC constituency wins in January 2025 was implemented by re-run rather than defied (Test 2), and parliament's December 2025 rejection of the source-disclosure article was accepted, the code promulgated without it. The opposition leader whose detention disfigured the 2024 election, Achmet Saïd Mohamed, was provisionally released in May 2025 after a hunger strike — a release issued by the courts under pressure, not a presidential pardon, recorded here as the system bending rather than the president conceding (Civicus Monitor, 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The founding wobble was answered with troops. When Anjouan rose in October 2018 against the referendum that ended its expectation of the presidency, barricading the old town of Mutsamudu for almost a week, the government answered with the army: reinforcements were brought from another island, residents were confined without water or electricity at times, at least two civilians were killed on agency counts (news agency reporting, October 2018), and the island's governor was arrested and later sentenced to twelve years (Test 1). The African Union's attempt to mediate — High Representative Ramtane Lamamra's September 2018 mission — was refused on the substance: the government declined to revisit the referendum, calling it "ancient history," and declined the requested release of political prisoners, and the dialogue stalled (contemporaneous reporting, October 2018).
  • The 2019 election's verdict from the referees he did not appoint was rejected. Observer missions from the African Union, COMESA, and the East African Standby Force concluded the March 2019 vote was marred by irregularities and lacked credibility and transparency; the twelve opposition candidates rejected the results, and the government's answer was arrests — of candidates, of protesting women, and, after the 28 March 2019 gunfight at a Moroni military camp in which four people died (AP via VOA, March 2019) — of the third-placed candidate, former colonel Mohamed Soilihi. No recount, inquiry, or concession of any irregularity followed.
  • The 2024 wobble was answered with a curfew and a blackout. After the CENI's announcement, young protesters filled Moroni's streets; the government imposed a night curfew and the first nationwide internet shutdown in Comorian history, and the clashes left one dead — a young man who the head of El-Maarouf hospital's emergency department said likely died of a gunshot wound — and, on separate counts kept separate here, five wounded (Global Voices, February 2024) or at least twenty-five injured (Reuters, May 2024). The pushback was treated as a security problem to be silenced, and the underlying question — the two counts — was never answered at all.
  • The wobble that touched his own person was answered with a closed file. After the 13 September 2024 knife attack at a funeral in Salimani-Itsandra — in which Azali was slightly injured — the attacker, Ahmed Abdou, a 24-year-old soldier, was found dead in his cell the next morning before investigators had questioned him. The prosecutor announced an investigation; twelve days later, on 25 September 2024, the prosecutor's office closed it, stating there were no firearm or weapon wounds and "no reason to pursue the investigation"; the body had already been returned to the family, ruling out an autopsy, and the prosecutor's office did not answer AFP's questions about the separate inquiry into the attack itself (prosecutor's statement via Al-Watwan, September 2024; News Central, September 2024). Rights defenders and opposition leaders called the closure a denial of justice — one lawyer's words: "an investigation that never took place" (lawyer and human rights defender Gerard Youssouf, September 2024). Whatever happened in that cell, the state's answer to the most serious question of the tenure was to stop asking it within a fortnight.
  • The succession wobble was answered by denial rather than engagement. When his January 2025 words on Mohéli were reported by Reuters — "I will place my son to replace me as head of the state and the party" — the presidency's response was not to address the plan but to dispute the translation: he had meant "a child" in a cultural sense, all Comorians being his children (presidency statement via VOA Africa, 25 January 2025). The Reuters correspondent who reported the speech, Abdou Moustoifa, was taken into custody on 30 January 2025 and faced a prosecutor's charge of spreading misinformation — the wobble answered by prosecuting the messenger, literally (Civicus Monitor, 2025). Whether another option was considered when it became available is graded here on that record: the constitutional option — rotation to Anjouan in 2029, which the presidency's own statement invoked — was cited as a denial, not opened as a question, while the son's accumulation of office continued uninterrupted (Test 5).

The no-good-choice clause, applied — Operation Wuambushu, 2023: The options actually open to Azali when France announced the operation were these. (a) Accept the deportees as shipped: thousands of people, not all verifiably Comorian, dumped on Anjouan — the poorest island's port — by a unilateral French timetable, into a state where some 38 percent of people live below the lower-middle-income poverty line; the harm is borne by the deportees and by Anjouan, and the precedent rewards unilateralism. (b) Refuse and break with France: France is the former colonial power, a principal donor, and the administering power of Mayotte, which Comoros claims; a full rupture strands the migrants in limbo, forfeits the development relationship, and changes nothing on the water. (c) Refuse the unilateral operation while negotiating: port closure as leverage, public criticism, a presidential trip to Paris. He took (c), the least-harm door actually open, and was honest inside the trap about its hardest fact — that Comoros lacked the means to stop France by force. The constraint is therefore not counted against him on this file. The trap's origin is also recorded: the Mayotte question was created by the 1974 referendum split and fifty years of French administration, not by Azali; his own 2019 cooperation agreement with Paris on migration is part of the record (Test 5) but did not build the trap. What remains gradable, and is graded on the other shelves, is the sequel: expulsions from Mayotte continued at scale — some 16,000 to 16,500 people in the first half of 2026 alone (InfoMigrants figures reported 2026) — and no durable joint framework has replaced the unilateral one.

Why this grade: The pattern splits cleanly by whose ox is gored. Pushed by a foreign power, by his own court, or by his own parliament, Azali adjusts — negotiates, complies, promulgates. Pushed by the street, by election observers, by an island that feels cheated of its turn, or by questions about a death in his own custody, he answers with soldiers, curfews, blackouts, arrests, or a file closed in twelve days. A ruler who adjusts only when the pusher is stronger than him has not answered the wobble; he has priced it. MIXED, with the failing edge on every domestic count.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Comorian migrants in Mayotte's deportation queue in 2023, whose unilateral shipment was stalled and made a negotiation; Sambi, marginally and prospectively, by the 2026 medical-evacuation preparations; journalists, by the source-protection outcome he accepted. Hurt — the residents of Mutsamudu confined under military operation in October 2018, and the at least two civilians killed there (agency count); the Moroni protesters of January 2024, one dead on the hospital's count; Ahmed Abdou's family, who received a body and no findings; and the Comorian public each time a question about its own state was closed rather than answered.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Anjouan and Mohéli, the opposition's voters, the young, the migrants, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • On the world stage, he counted Comoros itself in. As chairperson of the African Union from February 2023 to February 2024 — the smallest state ever to hold the chair — Azali presided over the Union's admission as a permanent member of the G20 at the New Delhi summit of September 2023, the single largest enlargement of Comoros' diplomatic weight in its history (African Union, 2023; G20 New Delhi summit record). A country of under a million people was, for a year, the voice of a continent, and it used the chair to press small-island and climate claims he was still pressing at COP31 in November 2026.
  • The development accounting is real, if modest. The Plan Comores Émergent 2030, launched under his government, mobilized some USD 4.3 billion in announced pledges at the Paris donor conference of December 2019. Growth recovered past pre-pandemic levels — 3.0 percent in 2023, 3.4 percent in 2024, projected toward 4 percent by 2027 — and the poverty rate at the lower-middle-income line was estimated at 38.1 percent in 2024, projected to ease to 37.2 percent in 2025 (World Bank, Comoros Economic Update, June 2025). The 2027 Indian Ocean Island Games, which Comoros will host, have brought construction — including the Games Village whose progress he inspected in 2026 — and his government has visibly carried works to Anjouan, where the Secretary General of the Government's November 2026 tour inaugurated roads, a telecom point, and a renovated bridge (Comorian government reporting, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The constitutional settlement of 2018 counted two of the three islands out of the presidency's foreseeable future. Under the Fomboni system Azali himself had authored as junta leader in 2000–2001, the presidency rotated by single terms, so Anjouan and Mohéli each held a dated, guaranteed turn; under the revised system, Grande Comore took two consecutive terms (2019, 2024), the vice-presidencies that gave each island a seat at the top were abolished, and the other islands' next turn was deferred to 2029 at the earliest. Anjouan's October 2018 uprising — the only armed revolt of the tenure — was explicitly a counting revolt: young men barricaded Mutsamudu against "plans to end the rotating presidency" (contemporaneous reporting, October 2018). The island that felt itself counted out is the island that rose.
  • The 2024 election failed at counting in the most literal sense available to this test. On the electoral commission's own figures, 189,497 Comorians voted for island governors on 14 January 2024 and only 55,258 voted for president on the same day at the same polling stations; on the Supreme Court's figures, 191,297 voted for president. Both counts are official; they cannot both be true; no reconciliation has been published (CENI and Supreme Court figures, January 2024). Somewhere between the two versions, roughly 136,000 Comorians are counted or not counted depending on which state organ is speaking. A test named "counts everyone" cannot pass a seat under which the state cannot say how many of its citizens voted.
  • The parliamentary map followed. In the January 2025 legislative elections — boycotted by the Juwa party and other opposition parties over the 2024 count — the CRC took 31 of 33 Assembly seats; on Mohéli, four of the island's five constituencies had a single, unopposed ruling-party candidate, and the one opponent withdrew at midday on election day (International IDEA, 2025; IPU Parline). Invalid and blank ballots were 31.28 percent of all votes cast — nearly a third of those who did vote cast a ballot that counted for no one (official results, January 2025). Women hold 5 of 33 seats. The Assembly that resulted can amend the constitution on the ruling party's votes alone.
  • The young are counted as a security category. In November 2025 the Gen Z 269 movement protested in Mitsamihouli against corruption, the cost of living, and impunity; the demonstration was unauthorized, three people were arrested and brought before the Moroni court for disturbing public order, and in December 2025 youth protests in Fomboni, Mohéli — after a suspect's death in custody — were dispersed with further arrests (Civicus Monitor, January 2026). A generation whose median age is about twenty-one meets the state, in the documented record, mainly through its police.
  • The migrants at the state's own door were counted late. In November 2025, 73 migrants, including 15 children, who washed up at Salimani were held at Moroni's central police station without assistance from the authorities, as reported by the journalist Abdou Moustoifa — who was then summoned and interrogated for three hours over the report (Civicus Monitor, January 2026). The people were held; the counting of them was treated as the offence. The wider file runs through Test 3: some 16,000 to 16,500 people were expelled from Mayotte in the first half of 2026 alone, into a state whose poverty rate the World Bank puts at 38.1 percent and whose social protection it describes as weak (World Bank, June 2025).
  • The poor remain the uncounted majority in the outcome measures: poverty at 38.1 percent in 2024 on the Bank's estimate, "projected to decline only slightly" on modest growth and high inequality, with the Bank's judgment that without bold reform "poverty may deepen" (World Bank, June 2025). Ten years into the tenure, more than a third of Comorians live below the lower-middle-income line in a country whose presidency has spent the decade announcing emergence.

Why this grade: Every register of counting fails somewhere structural. The islands: two of three written out of the top seat for a decade, and the one that protested was occupied. The voters: two official counts, 136,000 ballots apart, never reconciled. The legislature: a third of ballots invalid, one island's seats unopposed. The young and the washed-up: met by police. Against that, the diplomatic counting-in of Comoros at the G20 and the slow poverty decline are real but they count the state, not its people. The test fails.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Comoros as a state, whose diplomatic standing rose further under Azali than under any predecessor; communities reached by Games-related and road construction, including in Anjouan; the treasury, by the donor pledges mobilized in 2019. Hurt — Anjouan and Mohéli, whose guaranteed turn at the presidency was replaced by a decade of Grande Comore; the opposition's voters, whose 2024 presidential ballots exist in one official count and vanish in the other; the 31 percent whose 2025 ballots were invalid; the Gen Z protesters of 2025; the 73 migrants held without assistance at Moroni's central police station; and the roughly 38 percent of Comorians still below the poverty line at the tenure's tenth year.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 26 May 2016, with dates; his earlier tenure's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The constitutional revision — announced, campaigned for, and delivered exactly as stated. From 2017 Azali said openly that he intended to revise the constitution, end the single-term rotation, and make the presidency renewable; he held the referendum on 30 July 2018, promulgated the revised text on 6 August 2018, stood in the early election it created in March 2019, and won it. Whatever the revision's merits — graded on Tests 1, 2, and 4 — as a matter of word the program was stated in advance and executed in full view. This is the file's clearest kept word, and its consequences are recorded in the ledger below without dilution.
  • The African Union chairmanship program — delivered. Taking the AU chair in February 2023, he committed to advancing the continent's place in global governance; in September 2023 the African Union took a permanent seat at the G20 under his chairmanship, and he handed the chair on in February 2024 on schedule, at the end of the term, without attempting to extend it (African Union, 2023–2024). A rotating seat he held for a year, he rotated.
  • The development mobilization — the conference happened and the pledges were made. The Plan Comores Émergent 2030 was launched as promised and the December 2019 Paris conference mobilized some USD 4.3 billion in announced partner pledges (donor conference record, December 2019). Delivery against those pledges is graded below; the mobilization itself was a kept commitment of process.
  • His earlier tenure's word, as context for the seat: in 2006, at the end of his elected term, Azali left office and handed power to the newly elected Sambi — a kept constitutional word that made him, at the time, the first Comorian ruler to transfer power peacefully to an elected successor. The man who later suspended a constitutional court had once been the man who went home on schedule. Both facts are his, and the file keeps both.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The succession word — spoken, denied, and contradicted by his own appointments. On 23 January 2025, speaking on Mohéli, Azali said, in Reuters' translation of his Comorian: "I will place my son to replace me as head of the state and the party" (Reuters, 23 January 2025). The presidency's statement the next day denied the meaning — "a child," culturally, all Comorians — and reaffirmed that the constitution provides rotation, with the next transition in 2029. The appointments tell their own sequence: his son Nour El Fath was made Secretary General of the Government with sweeping oversight of government affairs in mid-2024 (formalized by presidential decree of 14 April 2025), was elected to the National Assembly for Hambou in January 2025 with over 78 percent of the vote, and tours the islands inaugurating works in a head-of-government's posture (presidential decree 25-028/PR, April 2025; CENI results, January 2025; Comorian government reporting, November 2026). Under the constitution the presidency itself invoked, the 2029 turn belongs to Anjouan, and the son is of Grande Comore: he cannot succeed in 2029 unless the constitution is changed again or the rotation is set aside. As of grading, the president's spoken word, his office's denial, and his personnel decisions point in three different directions, and the only one being built, month by month, is the son's. The word at issue — that succession follows the constitution — is graded against the construction, not the press release.
  • The growth word — stated as a number, missed as a number. At his inauguration on 26 May 2024 Azali pledged to grow the economy at 5 percent per year and told Comorians, "I will not disappoint you" (Reuters, 26 May 2024). Growth in 2024 was 3.4 percent; the World Bank projects roughly 4 percent by 2027 and assesses that reaching upper-middle-income status would require sustained growth of 6.1 percent to 2050 (World Bank, June 2025). The flagship frame of the tenure — emergence by 2030 under the Plan Comores Émergent — stands at the decade's end with poverty at 38.1 percent and declining only marginally on the Bank's projection. The promise was quantitative; the shortfall is quantitative; the shelf records both numbers.
  • The inclusion word — offered at the inauguration, withdrawn in practice. In the same May 2024 speech he invited "civil society, the opposition and all political actors to put aside differences in favour of peace and democracy" (Reuters, 26 May 2024). Within the following twenty months: the Reuters correspondent who reported his succession speech was detained and charged with misinformation (January 2025); the Gen Z protesters of November 2025 were arrested; and in June 2026 the journalist Toufé Maecha, of La Gazette des Comores, was detained and charged with "disturbing public order" for an investigation into the imprisoned former president's health — reporting on the very file on which Azali was simultaneously announcing humanitarian preparations (RSF, 10 June 2026). The invitation and the record cannot both be the word kept.
  • The 2019 cooperation word on migration — reversed when it cost. Under the 2019 framework with France — €150 million in pledged development aid tied to cooperation against trafficking and on returns — Comoros cooperated, including, on a Moroni diplomat's account to ISS Africa, destroying kwassa-kwassa boats in Anjouan. In April 2023, facing Operation Wuambushu, the government repudiated the cooperation posture and refused the deportees (ISS Africa, 2023). The reversal is graded here as a broken commitment of record; that the break helped the people in the deportation queue is recorded in full in the crossings below and on Test 3, and neither fact cancels the other.

The four crossings (word × outcome), named: 1. Kept his word AND it helped people: the African Union chairmanship program — the continental commitments he stated in February 2023 were executed, the G20 seat was won, and the chair was surrendered on schedule in February 2024; the help is Comoros' and the continent's enlarged standing. His 2006 handover stands as the same crossing in his earlier tenure, recorded as context. 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt people: the constitutional revision. He said he would end the single-term rotation and reset the presidency's terms, and he did — the keeping is complete, and the hurt is on Tests 1, 2, and 4 in full: a suspended court, a twelve-year sentence for Anjouan's governor, an island's turn deferred a decade. The promise's content, not its keeping, is what those tests grade. 3. Went against his word AND it helped people: the Wuambushu reversal of April 2023. The standing commitment to cooperate with France on migration and returns was set aside — port closed, deportees refused — and the break shielded thousands of Comorians from a unilateral mass-expulsion timetable and converted a dumping exercise into a negotiation. The break is recorded on this test; the help is recorded on Test 3; both stand. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt people: the succession file. The constitutional reassurance — rotation, a 2029 transition, no hereditary seat, reaffirmed by his own presidency in January 2025 — is contradicted by the spoken sentence on Mohéli and by the son's staged accumulation of the Secretary Generalship, a parliamentary seat, and a head-of-government's public role; the hurt falls on Anjouan, whose 2029 turn the construction endangers, and on every Comorian whose constitution is again revealed to be negotiable by the family it constrains.

Why this grade: The kept words are real — a constitution rewritten exactly as announced, a continental chairmanship delivered and surrendered on time, a donor conference held. But the test weighs the words a ruler gives about the limits on his own power most heavily, because those are the words the whole system leans on. On those, the record is a spoken plan to place his son, an official denial that the sentence meant what it said, a journalist prosecuted for reporting it, and a son nonetheless installed step by step in the succession's doorway — while the growth number he set himself in May 2024 is missed by his own central bank's and the World Bank's figures. A word that holds only when it costs nothing has not been kept in the sense this test measures.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Comoros' diplomatic position (kept chairmanship program); the migrants shielded by the 2023 break with the cooperation framework; partners and investors who dealt with a president whose announced institutional program — the revision, the PCE, the Games — reliably happened. Hurt — Anjouan, twice over: its turn deferred by the kept word of 2018 and endangered by the broken reassurance of 2025; the Comorian poor, for whom the 5-percent and emergence words have so far produced 3.4 percent and 38.1 percent; and the public's stock of belief in official statements, spent each time a denial is issued for words the whole country heard.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Azali Assoumani is the most durable ruler in Comorian history — the man who took power in a 1999 coup, authored the Fomboni settlement that pacified the islands, left office on schedule in 2006, and then, returning in 2016, dismantled the settlement's central promise and rebuilt the state around his own continuity. The grades follow that arc. Test 1 fails on the asymmetry of the law: a life sentence for his predecessor and rival, delivered by an unappealable special court after four years of pre-trial detention the UN Working Group had already found arbitrary, against no comparable file ever opened on his own side — hurt: Sambi, Governor Abdou Salami Abdou, the detained candidates and party leaders; helped: only the treasury's claim on a genuinely documented passport fraud. Test 2 fails on the founding act and its sequel: the Constitutional Court suspended weeks before his own referendum, electoral judgment moved to his appointees' chamber, the CENI's opposition voice dismissed, and in 2024 a presidential count that his commission put at 55,258 ballots and his court at 191,297, with the gap never explained — against real counter-instances (four CRC results annulled in 2025, a parliament that struck down his government's source-disclosure article) that show the grip closing only where his tenure is touched. Test 3 is MIXED: abroad and uphill he adjusts — Wuambushu refused, negotiated, and survived, with the no-good-choice clause applied to that file in full — while at home every wobble from below was answered with troops in Mutsamudu, a curfew and the country's first internet blackout in 2024, and an inquiry into his attacker's death in custody closed in twelve days without findings. Test 4 fails on the counting itself: two islands written out of the presidency for a decade, two irreconcilable official counts of the same election, a third of 2025's ballots invalid, Mohéli's seats unopposed, the young and the shipwrecked met by police, and 38.1 percent of the country still poor. Test 5 fails on the words about his own limits: the growth number missed on the record's own figures, the inclusion invitation contradicted by detentions, and above all the succession — a spoken sentence placing his son, an official denial, a prosecuted reporter, and a son nonetheless assembled, office by office, at the constitution's door, with Anjouan's 2029 turn standing in his way. The crossings are named in Test 5 and stand as the file's honest complication: he kept his word and it helped (the AU chairmanship), kept his word and it hurt (the 2018 revision), broke his word and it helped (Wuambushu), and broke his word and it hurt (the succession reassurance). The open files that will move these grades are stated plainly: whether Sambi's announced medical evacuation is completed and his case ever revisited; whether any reconciled account of the 2024 count is ever published; whether the 2029 rotation to Anjouan proceeds on the constitution Azali's own office reaffirmed; and whether the son's construction reaches the presidency by ballot, by amendment, or not at all.
