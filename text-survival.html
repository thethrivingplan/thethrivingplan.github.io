Text Survival

You wake in the wild with nothing but the clothes you stand in. Day by day, choice by choice, you keep five meters alive — warmth, hunger, thirst, health, and energy — until rescue finds you, you walk out, or the wild wins. The game always tells you plainly which ending you reached, and why.

The documented basis, in our own words: this game stands in the text adventure form — stories you play by reading and choosing — whose documented ancestor is Colossal Cave Adventure (1976). It is also a survival game, a documented genre built on managing food, water, shelter, and health against the environment. How to play

Each day you read what the wild presents, then tap one choice to take it. That choice spends the day: its gains and costs land on your five meters, the weather rolls again, and the day counter advances. There is no typing and no timer — think as long as you like.

Three ways a run ends. Rescue — searchers find you, most likely when you stay findable and signal. Escape — you walk out to a road, a river settlement, or a lighthouse track, most likely when you explore and push your way out. Not making it — your Health reaches zero; the ending names the meter that failed you first, because that is the honest cause.

The six things you can do with a day: gather food and water, hunt or fish, build shelter and fire, rest and recover, explore, or signal for help. Every one trades something for something — the choice buttons say the trade in plain words before you commit.

Survival lessons — the real knowledge the game teaches

These are documented, real-world survival principles in plain words. The Teaching dial weaves them into play at the moment they matter; they also stand here in full for anyone who wants them straight.

The rule of threes

A rough guide rescuers teach: a person can last about 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in harsh cold or heat, 3 days without water, and 3 weeks without food. It is a priority list, not a stopwatch: shelter and water almost always outrank food. That is why this game's early days reward building and finding water before hunting.

Priorities change with the climate

ClimateFirst needWhy, in plain words
Cold forest (standard)Shelter and fire, then water, then foodCold and wet drain body heat fast — faster than hunger harms you. A dry shelter and a fire protect warmth while you solve the rest.
Desert (variant)Shade and water, then shelter for the nightThe sun and dry air pull water out of you all day. Rest in shade at midday, move at dawn and dusk, and never ration water by sipping tiny amounts — drink when you have it.
Coast (variant)Fresh water and shelter above the tide lineThe sea is everywhere and none of it is drinkable. Find fresh water first, and build above the highest wet sand — the tide comes in whether you are ready or not.

Water safety

Clear water is not automatically safe water. Boiling is the most reliable field method: a rolling boil makes water far safer to drink. If you cannot boil, let cloudy water settle and strain it through cloth first, and drink from moving water above any animal sign rather than from still pools. In this game, gathering water carelessly can cost Health; taking the time to boil it never does.

Signalling

Rescuers look for things that do not belong in nature: straight lines, bright colours, smoke by day, flame by night, and patterns of three — three fires, three whistle blasts, three flashes — the widely used distress pattern. A signal only works if someone can see it, so open ground beats deep woods, and a signal kept ready beats one built in a panic when you hear an aircraft.

The standing fork: stay put and signal, or walk out?

There is no single right answer, and this game never forces one. Both honest strategies stand here with their trade-offs:

Stay put and signal

For it: searchers start from where you were last known; staying keeps you findable, saves energy and water, and lets you build a shelter and signals that grow stronger each day.

Against it: if nobody knows you are missing, or no search comes, staying spends your meters waiting. A camp in a hidden hollow may never be seen.

Walk out

For it: you take the ending into your own hands; roads, rivers, and power lines lead to people, and exploring is the only way to find them.

Against it: walking burns energy, food, and water fast, injuries turn serious far from camp, and moving targets are harder for searchers to find.

In game terms: signalling raises your rescue chance; exploring raises your escape progress. Strong players often mix them — build a findable camp first, then explore in widening loops, always able to return.
