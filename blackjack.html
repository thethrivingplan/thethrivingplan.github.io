📖 How to play — plain words Tap to open · Tap again to close

The goal: finish closer to 21 than the dealer, without going over. Over 21 is a bust — that hand loses.

  • Card values: number cards are face value, face cards (J, Q, K) are 10, and an ace is 11 or 1 — whichever helps your hand. A hand with an ace counted as 11 is called soft.
  • Blackjack: an ace plus a 10-value card as your first two cards. It pays 3 to 2 — bet 100, win 150 plus your bet back.
  • The dealer plays the standard house way, printed at the top of the table: hits until 17 and stands on all 17s. The dealer has no choices to make — the rule decides.
  • Push: if you and the dealer finish on the same total, it’s a tie — your bet comes back, nobody wins.
  • Your doors: Hit (another card), Stand (keep your hand), Double down (double the bet, exactly one card), Split (a pair becomes two hands), and Surrender when it’s offered (fold, half your bet back). Every button says what it does, right on the button.
  • Insurance: when the dealer shows an ace, you may bet up to half your bet that the dealer has blackjack. It pays 2 to 1. It’s part of the real rules, so it’s here — and the honest note is that most players skip it.

Where the book is a close call — more than one honest way

Basic strategy above is the settled core, and the Coach teaches it as such. But a handful of spots are genuine judgment calls where documented schools honestly differ. There, the Coach names both ways with the trade-off instead of dressing one up as the only way:

  • Hard 16 against 9, 10, or ace — the closest call in the game. Book: hit, or surrender where offered. Other school: stand and refuse the bust card. Standing can never bust, but the dealer makes a hand so often that it loses a little more over time. Counters lean toward standing when the cards left are rich in big cards. The gap is small either way.
  • Hard 12 against a 2 or 3. Book: hit. Other school: stand and hope for a dealer bust. The dealer busts less often showing 2 or 3 than showing 4–6 — which is why the book stands at 4 and hits at 2.
  • Soft 18 against 9, 10, or ace. Book: hit — a soft hand cannot bust. Other school: stand on the 18 you have. Hitting can improve it or turn it into a worse hard hand; standing protects it but loses to most of those dealer finishes as it stands.
  • 11 against an ace. The schools genuinely split, and the answer flips with the soft-17 rule: double where the dealer hits soft 17, hit where the dealer stands. Tiny gap either way.
  • A pair of 9s against a 7. Book: stand on 18. Other school: split and press for two strong starts — bigger wins when it works, two bets lost when it doesn’t.
  • Even money on your own blackjack. Book: refuse it — 3-to-2 over time beats a guaranteed 1-to-1. The honest trade-off: certainty now versus a better average over hundreds of hands. Plain insurance (without your own blackjack) has no such defence — there is only one honest answer there: skip it.

Everywhere else on the chart there is genuinely only one honest answer, and the Coach says just that one. Where a spot above comes up at the table, the Coach names the alternative in the moment, tied to your actual hand.

Two things the chart never changes, whatever the settings: never take insurance as a strategy (it is a separate side bet, not a play on your hand), and a pair of 5s is never split — it plays as a hard 10: double against 2–9, hit against 10 and ace, exactly as the hard-hands chart plays 10.

🃏 Counting, explained — how it works, honestly labelled Tap to open · Tap again to close

First, the honest label: this panel teaches how card counting works in real casinos, as knowledge. The live count below tracks this page’s own table so you can watch the idea move — but read “This table, plainly” before drawing any conclusions from it.

What counting is: counters don’t memorize every card. They keep one running number in their head, called the running count, using the most common system (called Hi-Lo):

  • Every low card that comes out — 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 — counts as +1.
  • 7, 8, and 9 count as 0 — ignore them.
  • Every high card — 10, jack, queen, king, ace — counts as −1.

Why it shifts the edge: the count is really a picture of what is left in the shoe. Lots of low cards gone (a high count) means the cards remaining are rich in 10s and aces. That helps the player, for two plain reasons: blackjack — which pays extra — comes up more often, and the dealer, who must keep hitting stiff hands like 12 to 16, busts more often when big cards are left. A low or negative count means the shoe is rich in small cards, which helps the dealer instead: the dealer’s stiff hands get saved by little cards, and blackjacks get rarer.

The true count: a running count of +6 means more early in a big shoe than late in it, so counters divide the running count by how many decks are roughly left. That result is the true count — the number they actually judge by. In a real casino, a counter bets small while the count is low and bigger when the true count climbs, because that is when the maths tilts their way. It is a small tilt, earned over many hours, not a way to win any given hand — and casinos are free to shuffle early, use many decks, or ask counters to leave, which is why most real tables are hard to beat even for experts.

Dealer on soft 17

Soft 17 is ace + 6. Standard: the dealer stands. Variant: the dealer hits, which helps the house a little.

Decks in the shoe

Standard tables use six decks. Fewer decks make counting the cards that are gone a little easier; more decks make it harder.

Blackjack pays

Standard pays 3 to 2 (bet 100, win 150). The 6 to 5 variant pays less (bet 100, win 120) — it is here because real tables offer it, and it is labelled a variant every time it is in play.

Double down after a split

Standard: allowed — after splitting a pair, you may double either new hand. Variant: not allowed.

Surrender

Late surrender lets you fold a bad first hand for half your bet back. Standard here: offered. Turn it off for the stricter table.

Insurance

Offered when the dealer shows an ace. Standard here: offered.
