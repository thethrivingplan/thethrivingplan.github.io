The Quiet Room

Psychic training games — the documented exercises, run fairly, scored honestly. Zener cards from the 1930s laboratory work, a hidden-target test, and a precognition test where the target does not exist until after your guess is locked.

The honest part, first and at full size

Science has no settled proof that psychic abilities exist. The research record is contested — claimed results on one shelf, failed replications and sharp criticism on the other.

So this page does not promise to unlock anything, and it will never dress a score up. What it can do honestly is run the documented tests fairly — a real random draw, no peeking, no moving the target after the fact — and grade your record against chance with the math shown, every time.

Every score stands beside its chance line: what pure guessing would average. A run of luck is called a run of luck. A long record sitting on the chance line is called what it is. A record far above the line over many trials is called interesting — and still not proof, because proof needs more than one page and one player. This page neither preaches belief nor mocks it. It keeps score honestly and lets the record speak.

The exercises

Zener cards — the 1930s laboratory test

In the 1930s, at J. B. Rhine's parapsychology laboratory at Duke University, researcher Karl Zener designed a deck of 25 cards bearing five symbols — circle, cross, waves, square, star. One person would look at a card; another, who could not see it, would try to name it. That is the whole test, and it is the test here: the page holds a card, you name the symbol, then the card is shown. Five symbols means pure guessing averages 1 in 5 — 20%. The documented run is 25 trials.

Variant in play: 25-card deck — five of each symbol, dealt without putting cards back, as in the original laboratory. As the deck empties the odds shift; the score is still graded against the plain 20% line, and sessions played this way are marked “deck variant” in the record.

Hidden target — one of four

Target guessing is the other classic form: something is placed where you cannot see it, and you say where it is. Here a small brass key is hidden under one of four covered pictures before you guess — the other three hold a stone, a leaf, and a shell. Four spots means pure guessing averages 1 in 4 — 25%. Precognition — the guess comes first

Precognition is the claim of sensing something before it happens. Tested fairly, that means the target cannot exist yet when you guess — otherwise it is just the Zener test again. So this exercise runs in a strict order, printed here and logged on screen for every trial:

  1. Step 1 — You choose. Tap a symbol, then tap “Lock my guess.” Nothing has been drawn. There is no target anywhere in the page at this moment.
  2. Step 2 — The lock. Your guess is written into the trial log with a time stamp and cannot be changed.
  3. Step 3 — Only now does the page draw. The random draw happens after the lock, and the drawn symbol is written into the same log, below your guess.

Five symbols, so pure guessing averages 1 in 5 — 20% here too. If the order above were ever reversed, the test would be meaningless — that is why the order is printed and logged where you can watch it.

    Labelled: the practitioners' own method

    How the documented practitioners say to work

    The advice below is presented as what it is: the method described by the people who ran and practised these exercises, from the Rhine laboratory era onward. It is their documented approach, printed at full size — whether it works is exactly what your record is for.

    Quiet first

    The laboratory instructions asked for a calm, unhurried state: sit comfortably, let the noise of the day settle, and only then begin. Sessions were kept short enough that attention never had to be forced.

    Rhine's own reports treated the sitter's state as part of the apparatus. Tired, rushed, or irritated sitters were sent away to rest, because the laboratory believed — rightly or wrongly — that strain showed up in the scores. Whether or not the belief was sound, the practice is harmless and the sessions here are short for the same reason: 25 trials is a sitting, not a shift.

    Take the first impression

    The standing instruction in the literature: the first symbol that comes to mind is the answer to give. Do not deliberate, do not reason about what “should” come next, do not edit. The guess is the impression, spoken at once.

    Practitioners describe the impression as faint and easily talked over — a shape half-seen, a word on the edge of hearing. Their method is to report it before the reasoning mind builds a case for something else. In these exercises that means: tap the symbol that arrives first, even when — especially when — you cannot say why. Your second thoughts are recorded nowhere; the record only ever grades the guess you actually made.

    Don't chase

    After a miss, the documented advice is to let it go entirely. Do not try to “win it back,” do not change your approach mid-run, and never read a pattern into the last few cards — the next trial is a new trial, approached the same quiet way as the first.

    The old laboratory notebooks are full of sitters who began steering: picking the symbol they felt was “due,” or avoiding the one that had just appeared. The practitioners warned against exactly that, on their own terms — steering replaces the impression with a theory. The counterweight shelf below makes the same point from the other side: with a fair random draw, nothing is ever “due.” Both shelves agree on the behaviour, for opposite reasons. Follow it either way.

    Keep the sitting regular, keep the record always

    The practitioners kept dated notebooks: every sitting recorded, good or bad, none thrown away. A record with the bad days missing is not a record — it is an advertisement. This page keeps yours the same way: every finished session is written down, whatever the score.

    Labelled: the honest counterweight

    How the numbers can fool you — in plain words

    This shelf is the other half of the teaching, and it stands at the same size. Everything on it is ordinary, settled mathematics and psychology — the reasons a score can look like something it is not.

    Streaks happen all by themselves

    Guessing at 1-in-5, a run of three hits in a row will happen by pure luck about once in every 125 tries at starting one — and across a long record you will take thousands of tries, so streaks are guaranteed to show up. A streak feels like a door opening. Arithmetically, it is just what randomness looks like up close: clumpy, not smooth.

    Flip a coin a hundred times and you will almost certainly see five heads in a row somewhere in it. Nobody calls the coin psychic. The same clumpiness lives in these exercises, and it cuts both ways: a cold run of misses is equally meaningless. The page marks your best streak in the record because it is interesting — and prints it beside the totals, where its size can be judged honestly.

    Why the trial count is the whole game

    In 10 trials, luck swings wildly: scoring 4 of 10 at a 20% game (double the chance rate!) happens by luck roughly one time in eight. In 250 trials, that same doubling is essentially impossible by luck. Small numbers shout; large numbers tell the truth. That is why the record keeps totals across every session, and why no single session — good or bad — settles anything.

    The mathematics: each trial is a coin weighted toward “miss.” Your hit count over n trials wobbles around the chance expectation by roughly the square root of n — so the wobble grows slowly while the total grows fast, and the wobble as a share of the total shrinks. At 25 trials the luck-wobble is about ±2 hits either way, commonly. At 2,500 trials it is about ±20 hits on an expectation of 500 — a far tighter band around the line. Anyone claiming a real effect must beat the band, not the line.

    Regression to the mean

    After an unusually good session, the next session usually scores lower — not because anything was lost, but because the first score was part skill-or-chance and part luck, and the luck part does not repeat on schedule. Scores drift back toward their average. This is regression to the mean, and it explains a great deal of everyday disappointment: the remedy tried at the worst moment “works,” the star player “slumps,” the brilliant new method “fades.” Mostly, the extreme was partly luck, and luck reverts.

    Watch for it in your own record: if your best session is followed by ordinary ones, that is regression, not decline. The honest question is never “was that one session high?” but “where does the long total sit against the line?”

    Nothing is ever “due”

    A fair draw has no memory. If the star has not appeared in ten trials, its chance on the eleventh is still exactly 1 in 5 — the page does not keep a tally and does not owe you one. Believing otherwise is the gambler's fallacy, and it has emptied more pockets than any other idea on this page. (The one exception is the labelled deck variant: a physical-style deck of 25 really does empty, and there the odds genuinely shift — which is why that variant is labelled, and why card-counting it is a memory game, not a psychic one.)

    The file drawer

    One more quiet trap, in research and in life: results that look interesting get talked about; results that sit on the chance line get filed away and forgotten. A shelf of published “successes” can hide a warehouse of quiet null results. Your record on this page cannot be edited, session by session — every finished sitting counts, the dull ones included. That is deliberate. A record you can curate is a story, not a record.

    What would actually be convincing

    Stated plainly, so the target is honest: a hit rate clearly above the luck band, held over hundreds or thousands of trials, scored by a test whose fairness nobody has to take on trust — pre-registered methods, independent scorers, other laboratories repeating it. That is the bar science sets for every claim, and a century of parapsychology is contested precisely because its results have not cleared that bar in a way the wider field accepts. If your own long record ever sits far above the band, the honest next step is not celebration — it is a sterner test, under other eyes. This page will have done its job either way: it kept the score straight.

    How the draw works — no trust required

    Every draw on this page uses the browser's built-in cryptographic random source (crypto.getRandomValues) — the same machinery browsers use for security work — converted to a whole number with the leftover values thrown away so no symbol is favoured by rounding. There is no pattern to learn, no seed you could memorise, and no way for the page to steer a result: in the Zener and hidden-target exercises the target is fixed before your guess and cannot move; in precognition the draw literally does not happen until your guess is locked, and the on-screen log shows the order with time stamps.

    The exercises are the documented historical forms — Zener's five symbols and the 25-card run from the 1930s Rhine laboratory, four-way target guessing, and guess-first precognition — described here in our own words, with the symbols drawn by us. The names and the forms are a century old and belong to everyone; everything written and drawn on this page is original to it.
