The Ruler's Test — Mohamed Irfaan Ali, President of Guyana

Subject: Mohamed Irfaan Ali — President of the Co-operative Republic of Guyana since 2 August 2020; previously Minister of Housing and Water under President Bharrat Jagdeo. He took office after the disputed March 2020 election and its five-month recount, and was re-elected on 1 September 2025 with 55.31% of the vote and 36 of 65 National Assembly seats for his People's Progressive Party/Civic (PPP/C), and inaugurated for a second term on 7 September 2025. He remains in office at grading, presiding over the fastest-growing oil economy in the world: production averaged roughly 906,000 barrels a day in September 2026, a fifth production vessel began producing on 16 November 2026, and the Natural Resource Fund (NRF) held US$5.47 billion at the end of September 2026 (Ministry of Natural Resources and Bank of Guyana figures, compiled by 592Hub, November 2026). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The single largest legal process running against a political figure in Guyana is not his government's invention and has repeatedly cut against his government's prosecutors in court. Azruddin Mohamed — sanctioned by the US Treasury's OFAC on 11 June 2024 and indicted by a US federal grand jury in October 2025 — was arrested on the US extradition request on 31 October 2025 and granted bail; the Chief Magistrate hearing the committal case has three times refused prosecution applications to revoke that bail (most recently 3 November 2026), and the Director of Public Prosecutions has had to resort to judicial review of the magistrate's decisions rather than simply obtaining them (Guyana Inquirer, November 2026). A legal system wholly bent to the executive does not produce that record.
  • The state's most consequential act against the Mohamed family was tested in court and upheld on stated legal grounds: the Bank of Guyana's June 2024 revocation of the family's foreign-exchange and gold-trading licences was challenged by the Mohameds, and on 6 March 2026 the High Court dismissed their application, holding the central bank was entitled — indeed obliged — to weigh their OFAC designation under the licensing statute (CANA News, 6 March 2026). The government can fairly say the licence decision was a regulator's response to a US sanctions designation, judicially reviewed, not a decree.
  • The underlying US case is external and documented, not a Georgetown fabrication: OFAC's June 2024 designation states that Mohamed's Enterprise under-declared more than 10,000 kilograms of gold between 2019 and 2023, evading over US$50 million in duties; the 11-count indictment unsealed in the Southern District of Florida on 6 October 2025 alleges wire fraud, mail fraud, money laundering, conspiracy and customs offences between 2017 and June 2024. These remain ALLEGATIONs — no court in Guyana or the United States has ruled on them — and Mohamed denies wrongdoing, calling the case "not just a personal attack, this is political, this is a fear tactic" (US indictment and OFAC designation records; AP reporting, October 2025). Both the allegations and the denial are part of this record.
  • Ali himself has stood on the receiving end of the same machinery: in November 2018, under the previous government, the police Special Organised Crime Unit (SOCU) charged him with 19 counts of conspiracy to defraud over state land sales in the "Pradoville 2" scheme from his years as housing minister. The High Court stayed the proceedings in January 2019 — the DPP told the court she had not advised the charges — and the charges were later discontinued and never reached trial (court reporting, 2018–2019). The episode is contested in both directions: his supporters cite it as proof prosecutions in Guyana are wielded politically against whoever is out of power; his critics note the case died once he held power.

Evidence against:

  • Six years into his presidency, the documented pattern of who gets prosecuted runs overwhelmingly in one direction. SOCU charged former minister David Patterson of the AFC in January 2021 over a bridge feasibility-study procurement (the AFC condemned the charge as a "witch-hunt," noting a police legal adviser's prior conclusion of no evidence; Patterson maintains his innocence, and the charge is an ALLEGATION, unadjudicated here). The officials of the 2020 election administration remain before the courts six years after that election (Guyana Human Rights Association, November 2026). No comparably senior figure of Ali's own governing party has been prosecuted in the same period, despite documented procurement controversies inside his own administration — above all the Wales gas-to-energy contract (below, Test 5), where the state's response to a non-performing contractor was to pay more, not to prosecute anyone.
  • The Wales settlement is a favorites-at-the-source problem on the government's own announced facts. On 14 May 2026 the Office of the Prime Minister settled two disputes with the contractor Lindsayca, raising the US$759 million contract by about US$97 million (12.8%) and — critically — both sides surrendered their rights to historical liquidated damages, the contract's built-in penalty for the delay that had already pushed the project roughly two years past its original date. By August 2026 the Prime Minister was telling the public the government was still "deciding" whether to penalize the contractor at all, and by October–November 2026 the contractor was reported seeking a further US$102–170 million while reportedly being considered for Phase Two work (Office of the Prime Minister statement, 14 May 2026; Kaieteur News reporting, August–November 2026). Whatever the engineering merits — the government attributes much of the delay to defective soil conditions and to late site handover by ExxonMobil — a penalty regime waived for the state's largest contractor while tax and licence law is enforced to the letter against the president's chief political opponent is the shape of two laws, even where each individual step has a stated rationale.
  • The electoral playing field in 2025 was tilted by incumbency on the observers' record, not merely the opposition's: the EU Election Observation Mission's final report described "undue advantages of incumbency" and a "highly politicised" media landscape, and the Carter Center — which called the election itself the smoothest it had observed in Guyana — recorded concerns over state media bias and mishandling of state resources for campaigning (EU EOM Final Report, 2025; Carter Center, December 2025).
  • The sequence of local charges against the Leader of the Opposition in late 2026 is documented and is contested as to its meaning. After the extradition magistrate repeatedly refused to revoke Mohamed's bail, police detained and questioned him on 3 September 2026 over a drone and tinted vehicles near the president's private residence; on 13–14 October 2026 he was arrested after shots were fired outside the home of a senior police officer who is a witness in the extradition case (Reuters, 14 October 2026 — the shooting and Mohamed's responsibility are ALLEGATIONs); on 30 October 2026 he was charged under the Cybercrime Act with unauthorised disclosure of electronic data — prosecutors allege a police constable sold him Safe City surveillance footage — denied bail, and remanded; on 18 November 2026 the High Court denied him bail on that charge, and the Speaker refused WIN's request that he attend Parliament from detention (Guyana Inquirer; GHRA statement, 18 November 2026). The Guyana Human Rights Association calls the cumulative result a "fundamental miscarriage of justice" against an unconvicted constitutional officeholder; the government maintains each step is ordinary law enforcement against documented allegations. Both positions are recorded; what is not contested is the sequence and its timing.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, the prosecution, the central bank, and the media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts have ruled against the state's litigating positions in the most politically charged matter before them: the extradition magistrate's three refusals to revoke Mohamed's bail against the DPP's applications, and the DPP's need to seek judicial review of those refusals, are documented judicial independence in action (Guyana Inquirer, November 2026). The Caribbean Court of Justice — Guyana's final court, beyond the executive's reach — dismissed Mohamed's appeal on 29 July 2026 and allowed the extradition process to continue, a ruling against the opposition leader delivered by a court the government does not control (CCJ Media Release No. 30:2026, 30 July 2026).
  • The opposition's constitutional offices were eventually filled by the book: after the September 2025 election, opposition MPs elected Azruddin Mohamed Leader of the Opposition on 26 January 2026 (Reuters, 26 January 2026), and he sits in Parliament when not detained. The 2025 election itself was administered credibly — the EU mission found voting and tabulation "administered efficiently," and the Carter Center called it the smoothest of its six Guyana missions (EU EOM, 2025; Carter Center, 2025).
  • The central bank's licence revocation against the Mohameds survived judicial review on the merits (Test 1), and the NRF's balances are published monthly and quarterly by the Bank of Guyana — the figures used throughout this dossier come from those public reports.
  • A genuinely hostile independent press operated throughout his tenure: Kaieteur News and (until March 2026) Stabroek News published sustained, detailed criticism of his oil policy, contracts and ministers, and Stabroek's weekly cost-of-living column ran for years documenting hardship under his government. No journalist has been jailed by his government on this record.

Evidence against:

  • The two highest judicial offices have been held in an acting capacity for the whole of his presidency — and for twenty-one years in total. Guyana has had no substantive Chancellor since Justice Desiree Bernard in 2005, because Article 127 of the Constitution requires the President to obtain the agreement of the Leader of the Opposition. The failure is shared across five administrations, and the then-Opposition Leader Aubrey Norton refused even to respond to Ali's 2025 consultation, calling meaningful consultation with Ali impossible (Caribbean Times/CMC, 2025). But within that impasse, Ali's own 2025 moves were unilateral and contested: the long-serving acting Chancellor, Yonette Cummings-Edwards, went on leave and was not restored to the post, retiring instead; Ali elevated the acting Chief Justice, Roxane George, to acting Chancellor and appointed Navindra Singh acting Chief Justice — appointments Norton objected were made without the agreement the substantive posts require and elevated a judge he described as junior and government-linked (an interested party's characterization, recorded as such). At a 2026 Commonwealth judicial conference, Justice Abel named Guyana's two decades of acting appointments a live "pressure on judicial independence" (La Caribeña News, 2026; Guyana Bar Association calls for confirmation, January 2025).
  • The election of the Leader of the Opposition was delayed by the state's own timetable. Parliament first sat on 3 November 2025, two months after the election, and then did not convene the constitutionally required meeting of opposition MPs; the election happened only on 26 January 2026, hours before the budget presentation, after the US Ambassador publicly stated the post should be filled "as soon as possible" (Caribe Today/CMC, 13 January 2026). Ali dismissed suggestions his government was stalling as "absolutely ridiculous"; the Speaker, Manzoor Nadir, is a figure of the governing party's alliance, and the delay is a documented fact whatever its motive.
  • The media environment narrowed on his watch by financial rather than legal means — the mechanism is contested, the narrowing is not. Stabroek News, for 39 years the country's most trusted independent paper, printed its final edition in March 2026; its owners cited mounting financial pressures and outstanding government debt, and letter-writers and supporters put the state's unpaid advertising bill at over G$80 million (La Caribeña News, May 2026; Stabroek News letters, March 2026). The government has not conceded that its advertising practices were a cause. Reporters Without Borders placed Guyana 73rd overall on its 2025 index but 111th on the political indicator, called on Ali to "end hostility toward journalists," and cited the October 2024 targeting of publisher Leroy Smith among first-term cases; it recommended impartial rules for state advertising — a recommendation unimplemented on this record (RSF, 2025).
  • In November 2026 the president personally confirmed he had retained a Canada-based attorney, Selwyn Pieters, to represent him and government officials in seeking the removal of Facebook publications they consider defamatory, misleading or false — a sitting president engaging private counsel to police online criticism of his administration, reported by Demerara Waves and defended by Ali as specialist expertise (press reporting, 13 November 2026). It is lawful; it is also a grip fact about the information environment.
  • The fiscal constitution of the oil fund was rewritten in the executive's favor. His government replaced the 2019 Natural Resource Fund Act with its own Act in December 2021, passed on its one-seat majority over opposition objections that civic representation on the Public Accountability and Oversight Committee was being cut — critics at the Oil & Gas Governance Network called it a reduction of "people's power" in favor of political control, while the government said the 2019 Act had concentrated "overwhelming dominance" in the finance minister and promised a more arm's-length framework (OilNOW, 2020–2021; OGGN, 2021). The 2024 Fiscal Enactments (Amendment) Act then sharply raised the withdrawal ceiling — 100% of the first US$1 billion of prior-year deposits, on a sliding scale — so that by 2026 the state drew US$2.37 billion, close to the whole of each year's oil income as it arrives; the Macroeconomic Committee that advised on the ceiling was later removed by amendment (NRF Act 2021 and 2024 amendment; Bank of Guyana NRF reports). Opposition leader Norton called the fund, in the February 2025 budget debate, "a slush fund… a recipe for waste and illegal enrichment" — an opponent's rhetoric, recorded as such; the rule changes themselves are statute, not rhetoric.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Parliament, disasters, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His response to the worst disaster of his presidency was to hand the question to outsiders. After the ferry MV Barima capsized on 18 July 2026 with some 179 people aboard — 76 rescued, 73 bodies recovered, about 30 missing — Ali established a Commission of Inquiry chaired by a Belizean judge, Justice Godfrey Smith, staffed entirely by international maritime experts, with terms of reference covering negligence, the vessel's condition, regulatory compliance and the rescue operation, and a public pledge that "where failures, negligence or wrongdoing are identified, accountability must follow" (Department of Public Information, July 2026; commission sworn in 30 July 2026). The vessel's captain, chief mate and a Transport and Harbours official were charged with murder — charges that are ALLEGATIONs, and Prime Minister Phillips publicly said he did not believe there was deliberate intent to kill.
  • He redesigned his signature cash grant in response to public feedback and said so: the grant announced in October 2024 as G$200,000 per household was restructured, after complaints about household-definition disputes and family conflicts, into G$100,000 for every citizen aged 18 and over, verified by ID or passport — "We understand the importance of the feedback we received… This made it clear that changes were needed" (presidential statements, 2024).
  • On the Essequibo, his adjustments ran toward law rather than escalation. When armed men from the Venezuelan side shot and wounded six Guyanese soldiers on the Cuyuni River on 17 February 2025, and a Venezuelan patrol vessel entered Guyana's exclusive economic zone on 1 March 2025 and radioed the FPSO Prosperity that it was in "disputed international waters," Ali's response was a national address, activation of CARICOM and allied support, and a return to the International Court of Justice — which in 2025 ordered Venezuela not to hold elections in the Essequibo. Guyana completed oral hearings at the ICJ in May 2026 and Ali told the UN General Assembly that Guyana will abide by the ruling and urged Venezuela to do the same (CSIS analysis, 2025; AP, 1 March 2025; DPI, September 2026). Venezuela's position — that the waters are disputed and the ICJ lacks jurisdiction — is recorded; Venezuela's president called Ali "Zelensky of the Caribbean," which is also recorded.

Evidence against:

  • On the largest economic question before the country, pushback has produced no adjustment at all. As a candidate in February–March 2020 Ali promised his government would review and renegotiate the petroleum contracts — "everything was on the table" (his 2020 statements, compiled by Christopher Ram, 2026). In office he has refused even to ask: in a September 2025 Al Jazeera interview and again in August 2026 — after confirming that ExxonMobil had recovered its full US$55 billion cost bank and Guyana's profit-oil share had risen to 39.8% — he maintained that "sanctity of contract" forecloses renegotiation of the 2016 Stabroek agreement (OilNOW, September 2025; 592 Guardian, August 2026). Critics across the Guyanese spectrum (Ram; the Oil & Gas Governance Network) answer that Article 32.1 bars only requiring renegotiation without consent, that the 2016 agreement was itself a renegotiation of the 1999 one, and that asking costs nothing; the government's position is that contract stability protects investment and credibility, and that better terms were instead written into the new model PSA for future acreage (10% royalty, 65% cost-recovery ceiling, 10% corporate tax). The wobble is real and the refusal is documented; the two readings of it are both on the record.
  • The Barima inquiry itself is contested as a wobble-response: the GHRA and other critics objected that Ali established the commission unilaterally on 26 July 2026 and demanded it be replaced by a "truly representative" body built through consultation with survivors, families, Indigenous leaders and opposition parties (GHRA statement, November 2026). The commission's international composition is a fact; so is the absence of the consultation the critics asked for.
  • On the delayed election of the Opposition Leader, his response to a documented institutional failure was denial rather than adjustment — "absolutely ridiculous" — even though the meeting was in fact convened only after months and foreign prompting (Test 2).
  • On Wales, each missed deadline (original start-up end-2024; successive revisions) was met not with enforcement of the contract's penalty provisions but with their surrender in the May 2026 settlement, followed within months by further funding requests — the pattern critics name "the penalty that never comes" (592 Guardian investigation, September 2026; Kaieteur News, November 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Afro-Guyanese, Amerindian communities, the poor outside the oil economy, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The cash grant is universal by citizenship, not by party, region or race: every Guyanese 18 and over qualifies on an ID card. The 2026 round processed 620,255 grants by 17 November 2026 — in a country of under a million people — with the government seeking a supplementary G$1 billion to clear the remaining registrations rather than closing the list (Ministry of Finance statement via DPI, 18 November 2026).
  • The Indigenous share of the forest-carbon economy is a formal, above-proportion entitlement: under the December 2022 agreement selling 37.5 million ART TREES credits to Hess (later Chevron) for a minimum of US$750 million to 2030, 15% of revenues go directly to Indigenous villages for village-led sustainability plans — a share above the Indigenous population share (~12%), endorsed by resolution of the National Toshaos' Council in July 2022 after consultations across more than 200 villages, with cumulative programme receipts of US$353.5 million reported through March 2026 and village disbursements continuing (Government of Guyana fact sheet, December 2022; LCDS reporting compiled September 2026). The NTC chairman defended the deal on the record: "we only have 12% of the population, but we are getting 15%."
  • Tuition at the University of Guyana and government technical institutes was abolished from January 2025 as promised — a benefit that reaches every community's students alike.
  • The macro numbers his government cites are independently corroborated in direction: the IMF's 2026 Article IV work records real GDP growth of 19.4% in 2025, inflation of 3.3%, and unemployment down to 6.2% (IMF figures compiled August 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The state has stopped counting the poor. The most recent poverty estimates in circulation are the IDB's — 58% of the population below the US$6.85-a-day line and roughly a third in extreme poverty, on 2021 data — and the World Bank's roughly 48%; no census has been held since 2022 and no current official poverty measure for the oil-boom years exists, a gap Bloomberg's reporting described as a broken data compass. Government supporters contest the IDB figure as outdated and claim poverty nearer 18–20%; the contest itself is the finding — after six years of the fastest growth in the world, whether the flock is better off cannot be settled from the state's own counting, because the state has not published a count (IDB 2025 assessment; World Bank data; Bloomberg reporting, 2026). Stabroek News' weekly cost-of-living column documented, household by household until the paper's closure, families for whom the boom had not arrived.
  • Afro-Guyanese exclusion is claimed on the record by the community's own designated body and is contested by the government. IDPADA-G — the coordinating body for the UN Decade for People of African Descent — told the UN Permanent Forum in April 2025 that Afro-Guyanese face "systematic marginalization," citing land, state assets, employment and procurement; activists cite figures of roughly 30% of the population receiving about 10.4% of government contracts and 7.1% of contract value (IDPADA-G statement to the UN Forum, 2025; figures as cited by IDPADA-G and allied activists, November 2026 — presented as their documented claims, not as audited statistics). The concrete state action is documented: the government stopped IDPADA-G's subvention in 2022; the organization sued; a court ordered the outstanding 2022 subvention paid, and on 24 June 2026 the Chief Justice ruled IDPADA-G has no legal right to continued funding — a lawful outcome that nonetheless leaves the African-descent decade programme without its state-funded coordinating body, by the government's choice (High Court judgment, 24 June 2026).
  • Indigenous consent is contested by Indigenous people themselves. The Amerindian Peoples Association filed a formal complaint with ART alleging the carbon-credit scheme proceeded without proper consultation and free, prior and informed consent over titled village lands — directly contradicting the government's and the NTC's account of the same consultations (APA complaint to ART, 2023; both positions recorded). Land titling, funded in part from carbon revenues (US$13.3 million allocated), remains incomplete on this record.
  • The Barima's dead were drawn overwhelmingly from the hinterland and riverain communities the ferry served — the population furthest from the oil economy — and the inquiry's terms of reference themselves (vessel condition, maintenance, regulatory compliance) record that the state's maritime safety system had failed them before the ship sailed (COI terms of reference, July 2026).
  • His opponents' voters were counted at the ballot box — the 2025 count was internationally validated — but their chosen leader's ability to sit among them has been interrupted by detention since late October 2026 (Test 1), and on 11 November 2026 he was, on the trades union congress's account, blocked from attending a sitting of Parliament while in custody, a refusal the Speaker's office extended to virtual participation on 18 November (GTUC statements, November 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since August 2020, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Free university tuition — kept, on schedule. Promised in his first term; tuition at the University of Guyana and government technical and vocational institutions was abolished from January 2025 (presidential announcements, 2024–2025).
  • Cash grants — kept, and expanded in form. The October 2024 announcement was restructured (Test 3) and delivered; the 2026 round had processed 620,255 grants by November 2026 with supplementary funding sought to finish the list (Ministry of Finance, November 2026). Delivery was slow and queue-ridden in the first round — the promise was kept late and unevenly, but it was kept, and repeated.
  • Better terms for future oil contracts — kept as specified. The new model PSA (10% royalty against Stabroek's 2%, 65% cost-recovery ceiling against 75%, 10% corporate tax) exists and governs new acreage — precisely the forward-looking half of what he promised; it is the backward-looking half that was not done (below).
  • Essequibo — kept. He promised to defend the territory by law and diplomacy; the ICJ process was carried through hearings in May 2026 with a ruling expected by the end of 2026, and no inch was ceded or traded (Test 3).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Renegotiating the oil contract — not kept, and now refused. In February–March 2020, candidate Ali said the contracts would be reviewed and renegotiated and that everything was on the table. Six years on, with the contractor's US$55 billion cost bank fully recovered and the original risk rationale gone, President Ali states the government will not seek renegotiation, on sanctity-of-contract grounds his critics answer from the contract's own text (Test 3). This is the clearest word-against-record item in the file: the promise was specific, repeated, and central to the 2020 campaign's case that the 2016 deal — 2% royalty, 75% cost recovery — short-changed Guyana.
  • Half-price electricity — not delivered; the date has moved three years. The Wales gas-to-energy project was sold on start-up by the end of 2024 and a roughly 50% cut in electricity bills. On 15 August 2026 the Prime Minister told the public there will be no halving of bills until the plant reaches full capacity — now expected mid-2027, beginning with a single 57 MW turbine at the end of 2026 — while the programme's cost has risen from US$759 million toward a figure the Prime Minister himself put at about US$956 million for the works in Parliament in November 2026, and Kaieteur News estimates the two-year delay has cost the country some US$884 million more in fuel imports and emergency power (Kaieteur News, August–November 2026; parliamentary reporting, November 2026). The promise may yet be kept in 2027; on the record to November 2026 it is three years late and counting.
  • The long arc, factually: Ali entered national politics as a Jagdeo-era minister, was charged by SOCU in 2018 in the Pradoville matter (charges stayed, later discontinued, never tried — Test 1), won the presidency in the crisis election of 2020, and has governed since as the manager of an oil boom he did not discover but has chosen not to re-bargain. His "One Guyana" unity rhetoric runs against a documented record of deepening ethnic-political polarization around the boom's distribution — IDPADA-G's marginalization claims, the APA's consent complaint, and an opposition leadership in detention. His word splits along a consistent line: administrative promises within the state's gift (tuition, grants, future contract terms) are kept; the promise that required confronting the oil contract was not.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Mohamed Irfaan Ali governs the world's most dramatic oil boom with institutions that bend without — on this record — breaking. His courts have defied his prosecutors in open court over the opposition leader's bail; his final court sits outside his reach; his elections are internationally validated; his cash grant reaches citizens of every party; and his carbon deal reserves an above-share portion for Indigenous villages. Against that stand the refusals that define the tenure: a promise to renegotiate the oil contract abandoned and then ruled out of bounds by the man who made it; the two top judicial offices left acting for a sixth consecutive year while he rearranged them unilaterally; a constitutional office left vacant for months because the state's own timetable did not convene it; a flagship power project three years late with its penalties waived; an independent newspaper dead with the state's advertising debt among the cited causes; and a poverty count the state has simply stopped publishing, in a country where the last independent estimate put more than half the flock below the line. The tests measure the seat, not the boom — and on the evidence assembled here, the seat neither grips nor fully holds: it manages, it spends, it declines to ask, and it leaves the hardest questions — the contract, the count, the courts — for a later reckoning.
