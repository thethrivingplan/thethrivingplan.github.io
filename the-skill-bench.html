The Skill Bench

Six small tests of six real skills — reaction, timing, aim, memory, typing, and a steady hand. Every number here is a number this page actually measured on this device, against nobody but your own past self.

1 · Reaction

Wait for the pad to turn green, then tap it as fast as you can. Tap too soon and it is called what it is: a false start.

Tap here to start Then wait for green.

Honest measurement: your phone or computer adds its own delay between the screen lighting up and your tap registering — screen refresh, touch scanning, and browser timing all count. That lag differs by device and can be tens of milliseconds, so these numbers are honest for this device and best compared against your own earlier sets on the same device, never against another person's phone.

The skill

Reaction is mostly not tensing up. Rest your finger just above the pad, look at the whole pad rather than one corner, and let the colour change pull the tap out of you.

Coach

Don't anticipate. Guessing the moment feels faster and produces false starts — the clock only rewards the real signal. Soft focus: a relaxed, wide gaze catches a colour change sooner than a hard stare at one spot. Trade-off: hovering your finger closer shortens the tap's travel but raises false-start risk when you flinch; a slightly raised, relaxed finger is slower per tap and steadier across a set.

Play a full set and the Coach will read your rounds here.
Tap to open — strategies & trade-offs
  • Soft-focus watcher: eyes relaxed on the whole pad. Fewer false starts, slightly slower best time. Best for clean averages.
  • Hover-close sprinter: finger a hair above the surface. Fastest single taps, but flinches become false starts that void the round.
  • Rhythm breather: breathe out slowly while waiting to keep muscles loose. Costs nothing, helps most people on later rounds when tension creeps in.

2 · Stop the Needle

A needle sweeps the bar. Stop it inside the green zone. Five tries per set; your score is how far off the zone you land, in percent of the bar.

0%50%100%

The skill

The needle moves at a constant speed, so the zone arrives on a rhythm. Count the sweep — "one, two" — and tap on the count, not on a hunch.

Coach

Rhythm counting beats watching: your eyes report late, but a counted beat lands on time. Tap a fraction before the needle visually reaches the zone — that fraction is your own hand's delay, and it is roughly constant, so you can correct for it. Trade-off: correcting early fixes a consistent late bias but turns an already-centred stop into an early miss; only correct once your misses lean one way.

Play a set and the Coach will tell you whether your stops lean early or late.
Tap to open — strategies & trade-offs
  • Count the beat: count each pass and tap on the number. Very consistent; needs a couple of passes to lock the tempo.
  • Lead the target: aim your tap at the zone's near edge to cancel your hand delay. Great when you always land late; overshoots if your delay is small.
  • One-pass nerve: stop on the first pass before you overthink. Fast sets, wider scatter.

3 · Aim

Targets appear at random spots and sizes. Tap each one before it fades. Hits, misses, and accuracy are printed exactly as they happened.

Honest measurement: a tap on empty space counts as a missed shot, and a target that fades counts as a miss. Accuracy is hits divided by every shot you took plus every target you let fade — nothing is quietly dropped.

The skill

Keep your eyes near the centre of the area and let targets come to your attention. Chasing the last target with your eyes leaves you blind to the next one.

Coach

Centre-out scanning: hold your gaze near the middle; your peripheral vision spots a new target almost anywhere, and a short eye movement beats a long one. Trade-off: tapping the instant you spot a target is fast but sloppy on small targets; a half-beat to centre your tap raises accuracy and costs fades on Sharp. Your own hit times below say which side you are erring on.

Play a round and the Coach will read your accuracy and speed here.
Tap to open — strategies & trade-offs
  • Centre anchor: eyes parked mid-area, hand follows. Best all-round accuracy; slightly slower on corner targets.
  • Big-game hunter: big targets first when two overlap in your attention — they are the surest points. Can let small ones fade.
  • Full-commit taps: tap the moment a target registers. Highest speed, most empty-space misses.

4 · Echo

Watch the pads light and sound in sequence, then tap the same sequence back. Each round adds one step. How long a sequence you can hold is the score. Playing by ear or screen reader: each step is also announced in words as it plays.

Watch, listen, then tap the pads in the same order.

The skill

Don't memorise single pads — group them. "Red-blue-blue" is one chunk, not three facts. Chunking is how a sequence of twelve becomes four things to hold.

Coach

Chunking is the documented memory technique behind every long-sequence score: group the run into threes or fours and give each group a shape or a word ("stairs", "sandwich"). Trade-off: chunking holds far longer sequences, but a chunk recalled wrong loses you three steps at once — so say each chunk quietly as it plays, and if a group won't stick, split it smaller. Second tool: rhythm — hum the sequence as one tune; melody is stickier than a list.

Play a game and the Coach will note where your sequence broke.
Tap to open — strategies & trade-offs
  • Chunk into threes: the classic. Long reach; one bad chunk costs three steps.
  • Name the colours: speak the sequence as words. Strong for most people; slower on Sharp playback.
  • Sing it: lean on the tones as a melody. Excellent when colours blur together; weaker if you are tone-shy.
  • Trace it: move your finger over the pads as they light, without pressing. Muscle memory backs up visual memory; ties up your hand.

5 · Type Rush

Type the words shown, exactly. The clock starts on your first keystroke. Words per minute and accuracy are computed from your real keystrokes — every one counted, wrong ones included.

Honest measurement: words per minute counts only characters you typed correctly, at the standard five characters per word. Accuracy is correct keystrokes divided by all keystrokes, backspaces included in the count of effort — the numbers describe this passage on this keyboard, nothing more.

The skill

Accuracy before speed, always. A wrong keystroke costs the key, the backspace, and the retype — three actions for one letter. Slow down until the errors nearly stop, then speed finds you.

Coach

Home position: index fingers rest on F and J — feel the little bumps — and every finger owns its nearby keys and returns home. Look at the words, not your hands: your fingers learn the map only when your eyes stop driving. Trade-off: strict touch-typing feels slower for a week or two, then passes hunt-and-peck for good; glancing down is faster today and caps you forever.

Finish a passage and the Coach will read your speed and accuracy here.
Tap to open — strategies & trade-offs
  • Accuracy first: cap your speed at whatever keeps errors near zero. Lower WPM today, higher within a week.
  • Read a word ahead: eyes one word in front of your fingers smooths out pauses. Takes practice; early on it can tangle you.
  • Never chase a typo mid-word: finish the word, then decide. Stopping for every slip breaks rhythm; letting real errors stand costs accuracy — the balance is finishing the word.

6 · Steady Hand

Press on the green start circle and trace the winding path to the red finish without straying off it. Time and strays are printed as they happened.

Press and hold on the green circle to begin. Lift your finger or pointer and the run ends.

Honest measurement: a "stray" is counted each time your pointer leaves the path after being on it — not once per wobble — and the score shown is your raw time plus two seconds per stray, printed openly so you can see both numbers.

The skill

Slow is smooth, and smooth is fast. Move from the shoulder, not the fingertips, and look slightly ahead of your pointer, the way you look ahead when driving.

Coach

Slow is smooth: every correction you make at speed overshoots; halving your speed usually more than halves your strays. Breathe on the bends: most strays happen at direction changes — ease off before each bend, not during it. Trade-off: the two-second stray penalty means one careful extra second beats one stray every time; only hurry on the straights.

Finish a run and the Coach will read your time and strays here.
Tap to open — strategies & trade-offs
  • Tortoise: constant slow pace everywhere. Fewest strays; the clock is your only enemy.
  • Straight-line sprinter: brisk on straights, crawl the bends. Best scores once you know the path's rhythm; bends punish greed.
  • Two-anchor grip: rest your wrist or pinky edge on the desk/screen edge as a pivot. Steadier small movements; awkward on tall screens.

How This Works

Documented basis, in our own words. None of these games is new, and this page claims no invention: fairground and arcade skill testers have charged a coin to measure a steady hand and a fast tap for over a century; laboratory reaction timers have measured the gap between a light and a button-press since the 1800s; the sequence game — four coloured pads that light and sound in a growing order you must repeat — was first sold in 1978 and has been a memory benchmark ever since; typing speed has been honestly counted in words per minute since the typewriter era. The forms are documented and old. The name, words, art, and code on this page are our own.

Rules are settings. Every game's difficulty, rounds, and length are yours to change from the controls above it. When you play away from the standard bench settings, a banner on that game says so — a variant is a fine way to play, and it is labelled one so records stay honest about what produced them.

The record's shape. Every result is stored the same way: the game, the date and time, the settings in force, and the numbers the page measured. Export gives you that record as a plain JSON file. Forget wipes it in one tap — gone means gone.

No population numbers. This page never tells you that you are "faster than 80% of people" or any such thing — it has no sourced population data, so it prints none. Your only comparison is your own record, first attempts against latest, stated plainly on the shelf above.
