The Ruler's Test — Tshering Tobgay, Prime Minister of Bhutan

Subject: Dasho Tshering Tobgay — Prime Minister of Bhutan since 28 January 2024. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Bhutan is a constitutional monarchy in which the Druk Gyalpo (King), Jigme Khesar Namgyel Wangchuck, is head of state, while executive power is vested in the Lhengye Zhungtshog (Council of Ministers) headed by the prime minister (Constitution of Bhutan, 2008; Government of Bhutan overview, 2024). Tobgay, born in 1965, co-founded the People's Democratic Party (PDP) and previously served as prime minister from 2013 to 2018. He returned to office on a direct electoral mandate: in the two-round National Assembly election (primary 30 November 2023, general round 9 January 2024) the PDP won 30 of 47 seats with 54.98% of the general-round vote against the newly formed Bhutan Tendrel Party's (BTP) 17 seats, on turnout of 65.60% (Election Commission of Bhutan results via International IDEA, January 2024; Al Jazeera, 10 January 2024). On 28 January 2024 the King conferred the scarf of office on Tobgay, the Speaker, and nine ministers in Tashichhodzong, constituting the fourth democratically elected government. He is graded on roughly two years and ten months in office — a substantial record, not a completed tenure; his term runs to early 2029. The King's actual role is stated plainly where it bears on the tests, because in Bhutan it bears often: the King announced Gelephu Mindfulness City (GMC) in his National Day address of 17 December 2023 and established it by Royal Charter on 13 February 2024 — sixteen days after this cabinet was sworn in — chairs the GMC Authority's board, serves as Supreme Commander-in-Chief of the armed forces, appoints senior judges on the recommendation of the National Judicial Commission, and stands behind the Gyalsung national-service programme as its founder. Where a graded act is the King's rather than the prime minister's, this dossier says so and grades Tobgay on what was his: support, execution, silence, or pushback. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test, printed verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not a failure and is never graded as one; carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them, and conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Every test closes with a plain-words Why and a named helped/hurt ledger. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes, and Test 5 names which of the four crossings occurred. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Inside the national legal order, the anti-corruption machinery operates as a standing institution rather than a prime-ministerial weapon, and no documented case in this record shows it aimed at the opposition or spared for allies. The Anti-Corruption Commission (ACC) received 364 corruption reports in 2024–25 (up from 322 the year before), of which 39 qualified for investigation and others were routed to administrative action, inquiry, or sensitization; 485 individuals were implicated, the largest share private-sector employees (close to 350), followed by about 40 civil servants (ACC Annual Report 2024–25, reviewed by the National Council's Good Governance Committee, December 2025, via Bhutan Today). Bhutan scored 71 of 100 in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025, ranked 18th of 182 — the highest in South Asia — up from 68 held through 2018–2023 and 72 in 2024 (Transparency International, CPI 2025; ACC, 2026).
  • The tax reform that took effect on 1 January 2026 applies one schedule to everyone on its face, and its design is progressive rather than favoritist: the personal income tax keeps a Nu 300,000 tax-free threshold, lowers the lower-band rates, moves sole proprietors from a flat 30% business tax into the progressive personal schedule, cuts the corporate rate from 30% to 22%, and replaces a sales tax whose rates ran from zero to 100% by item with a single 5% Goods and Services Tax (Income Tax Act 2025 and GST Amendment Act 2025, passed July 2025; IMF Article IV staff report, January 2026; KPMG tax alert, October 2025).
  • The Nu 15 billion Economic Stimulus Programme (ESP), launched 18 May 2024, is structured as published sector windows — concessional credit, agriculture and livestock support, tourism and creative-industry windows — administered through the Bhutan Development Bank under an ESP Secretariat, not as discretionary grants from the prime minister's office; India's tranches are released against implementation milestones, with the fifth tranche of Nu 2.5 billion handed over in June 2026 bringing releases to Nu 12.5 billion of the Nu 15 billion (ESP programme record; Embassy of India, Thimphu, 2026).
  • The electoral source of the seat itself was one law for all parties: the Election Commission's two-round system eliminated the incumbent governing party (the DNT of his predecessor) in the primary round, and the same rules that seated Tobgay's PDP in 2024 had removed it from parliament entirely in 2018 (International IDEA election analysis, January 2024).

Evidence against:

  • Bhutan now contains a second legal order, and Tobgay's government builds and sells it. Gelephu Mindfulness City is a Special Administrative Region covering, on the GMC Authority's own figure, over 4,046 square kilometres — about 10% of the country — constituted not by an act of parliament but by Royal Charter granted by the King on 13 February 2024, a category the 2008 Constitution does not expressly provide for. Its Application of Laws Act 2024 (GMC Law No. 1 of 2024, in force 26 December 2024) gives the SAR its own executive, legislative, and judicial institutions, separate from the Royal Government and the national judiciary, and imports roughly 18 Singaporean enactments and 10 Abu Dhabi Global Market financial regulations wholesale as its commercial law (GMC legal-framework record, 2024–2026). The King chairs the GMC Authority's board. Tobgay did not author this — it is the King's project, announced before the election was even held — but his government executes it, his 13th Five-Year Plan names it the principal investment vehicle, and on 22 September 2026 he pitched it to investors at the UN General Assembly, describing himself, in his own words as reported, as "a salesman" for the project (UN General Assembly address, 22 September 2026; reporting of the address, September–October 2026). A Bhutanese business outside the SAR answers to Bhutanese law and Bhutanese courts; an investor inside it answers to imported law and separate courts. That is two laws at the source, whatever its economic merits.
  • The IMF flags the fiscal edge of the same fact: GMC's independent tax regime "could erode the national tax base, crowd out pro-growth public investment, and generate additional contingent liabilities" for the general government — the inside of the zone is fiscally privileged relative to the outside (IMF Article IV Consultation, January 2026).
  • At the border of belonging, one group is measured by a different test. When the first group of US-deported Lhotshampa (Nepali-speaking southern Bhutanese) arrived at Paro airport on 27 March 2025, officials interviewed them on whether they spoke Dzongkha and whether they knew anyone in the country, gave them small amounts of cash, drove them to the Indian border, and told them Bhutan was not their country (deportee and advocate accounts via CESLAM and US public-radio reporting, March–July 2025; the government's citizenship position is contested — see Test 4). No other returning population faces a language interview as the practical test of whether the law counts them.
  • The tourism levy, inherited rather than created by Tobgay but maintained unchanged by his government, prices belonging by passport: most international visitors pay a Sustainable Development Fee of USD 100 per night, Indian nationals pay Nu 1,200 (about USD 15) per night, and the first 15,000 Bangladeshi visitors a year pay USD 15 per day (Department of Tourism policy, as in force 2024–2026). This is recorded as a standing differentiation at the source, not as a Tobgay invention.

Why: On the national shelf, the record is genuinely one-law: an anti-corruption commission working to published numbers, a tax reform that flattens privilege rather than creating it, a stimulus run through windows and milestones, and no documented partisan targeting. But the largest single structure of his tenure is a charter-created enclave where a tenth of the country lives under different law, different courts, and a different tax regime — authored by the King, executed and marketed by the prime minister. One law holds where his government governs directly; it does not hold across the whole map he governs on behalf of, and he has never publicly treated that as a problem to be answered. That split is exactly what MIXED is for; if GMC's separate courts and tax regime consolidate into permanence, this grade moves to FAILS on the enclave evidence alone.

Helped: Ordinary taxpayers and small businesses, who gained a single-rate GST and a progressive income schedule (IMF, January 2026); farmers, SMEs, start-ups, and tourism operators with access to published ESP credit windows (Nu 3.012 billion approved across 2,313 applications by September 2025 — ESP implementation record); investors and future residents of GMC, who receive an imported, investor-oriented legal system. Hurt: Businesses and workers outside the SAR, who compete against a fiscally privileged zone inside their own country (IMF risk assessment, January 2026); the Lhotshampa deportees of 2025, measured by a language-and-belonging test applied to no one else (Test 4); current landholders in the Gelephu airport area — 552 landowners across roughly 1,400 acres — whose compensation, replacement-land, and relocation questions remain open while construction proceeds (The Diplomat, 5 October 2026, via Five Takes reporting).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The electoral referee demonstrably acts on its own. Freedom House's Freedom in the World 2026 report raised Bhutan's electoral-process scores to 4 of 4, noting the Election Commission's effective administration of local by-elections through 2025, including in remote areas (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026). The commission's two-round system has now removed the incumbent party at the primary stage in consecutive cycles — a referee no governing party controls.
  • Parliament checked the executive in public, and the executive submitted. In August 2026, after Home Minister Tshering resigned "on high moral grounds in relation to an ongoing personal matter" and the Energy Minister stepped down citing age, the PDP nominated its Health Minister for the vacant Speakership — and lost: on 19 August 2026 the Assembly elected the opposition BTP's Tempa Dorji as Speaker by 24 votes to 23, the first opposition Speaker in Bhutan's democratic history, and the result stood (Prime Minister's Office announcement, 11 August 2026; The Bhutanese and BBS reporting, August 2026). A government gripping its legislature does not lose its own chamber's chair to the opposition by one vote and accept it.
  • The upper house scrutinizes the executive's integrity machinery in the open: the National Council's Good Governance Committee publicly reviewed the ACC's 2024–25 annual report in December 2025, including pointed questioning from MPs of why the CPI score had jumped and whether the ACC's evidence supported it (Bhutan Today, December 2025). The Royal Monetary Authority publishes its own macroeconomic assessments in its own voice, including figures — public debt projected at 108% of GDP — that no government would choose as a headline (Ministry of Finance / RMA macroeconomic reporting, 2025).
  • The judiciary's independence is "largely respected" on the standing international assessment, scored 3 of 4 by Freedom House, with senior judges appointed by the King on the National Judicial Commission's recommendation rather than by the prime minister (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024 and 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The media referee is structurally held. The media regulator, BICMA, has its five members appointed directly by the government under the Information, Communications and Media Act 2018; the two largest outlets, Kuensel and the Bhutan Broadcasting Service, are state-owned; government agencies supply roughly 80% of newspaper advertising revenue, allocated, in RSF's words, based on outlets' proximity to those in power; and Bhutan has never enacted the right-to-information law its National Assembly passed in 2014 — the National Council never approved it, and no Tobgay initiative to revive it appears in the record (RSF World Press Freedom Index reporting, 2024–2026; Freedom House, 2024).
  • The measured result is a press that censors itself. Bhutan fell from 33rd in RSF's 2022 index to 90th in 2023, 147th in 2024, 152nd in 2025, and 150th in 2026 — still in RSF's "very serious" category — and a Bhutan Media Foundation survey records at least 84% of journalists practising self-censorship (RSF, 2022–2026; Bhutan Media Foundation). Most of that fall predates or coincides with the start of Tobgay's term; what is documented on his watch is the absence of reversal: criminal defamation remains on the books, Human Rights Watch's April 2024 UPR submission records citizens describing an atmosphere in which "controversial" subjects — the monarchy, inter-ethnic relations, the Lhotshampa file — cannot be publicly discussed, and CIVICUS rates Bhutan's civic space "obstructed" (HRW UPR submission, April 2024; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024–2025). None of this is a documented Tobgay order; all of it is a grip his government inherits, funds, and has not loosened.
  • The largest centralization of his tenure removes territory from the referees altogether. Inside GMC, lawmaking, executive power, and courts sit with an authority chaired by the King and separate from parliament and the national judiciary (Test 1). The Gyalsung national-service programme — a mandatory year for young Bhutanese, founded by the King and implemented from 2025 — likewise runs as a royal initiative delivered through the state rather than as a programme parliament designed. Tobgay's government administers both without any documented insistence that they pass through the elected institutions. The referees he does not grip are, in the SAR's case, referees the design does not include.

Why: Against the classic gripping tests — courts, elections, prosecution, central bank — the record is clean and in one case (the lost Speakership vote of August 2026) actively impressive: the institutions pushed, and he took the result. The grade is held at MIXED by two things: a media environment whose dependence on the state is structural, documented, and unreformed on his watch, and his willing administration of a royal enclave and a royal service programme that sit outside the referee system entirely. He does not squeeze the referees; he governs alongside structures that bypass them, and has not used his mandate to bring either the media framework or the SAR inside the ordinary checks.

Helped: The opposition BTP and its voters, whose Speaker candidate took the chair by democratic vote in August 2026; voters in remote districts, served by an election commission international monitors score at the top of its scale (Freedom House, 2026); the ACC, whose budget, reporting line to parliament, and public standing have been maintained. Hurt: Journalists and their readers — 84% self-censorship and a "very serious" RSF band mean the public is governed with less scrutiny than the constitution promises (RSF, 2026; Bhutan Media Foundation); citizens inside the GMC area, whose disputes will be heard by SAR institutions rather than the national courts they previously answered to; young Bhutanese conscripted into Gyalsung under a programme designed outside parliament — recorded here as a design fact, the programme's own merits being graded nowhere in this dossier.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The defining wobble of the term — the departure of roughly 9% of the population, on the government's own figure — was named, not denied. Tobgay has called the outmigration "an existential threat" in interviews; his government commissioned and then publicly worked from the World Bank's Migration Dynamics in Bhutan report (released 30 September 2025), which documents the push factors against his own administration in plain terms — low public wages, limited private-sector jobs, civil servants making up nearly half of all migrants, and almost 70% of 2024 civil-service resignations coming from education and health (World Bank, September–October 2025). Crucially, the adjustment chosen was attraction, not coercion: no exit restrictions, no service-bond crackdown appears in the record; the response was the ESP, civil-service and private-sector job creation, and the GMC jobs pitch. The counts are printed separately because they measure different things: the government-cited figure of about 66,000 citizens abroad (roughly 9% of the population, repeated by Tobgay in 2024), and the Australian Bureau of Statistics' count of 40,560 Bhutan-born residents in Australia alone at June 2025 (ABS, 2025; World Bank, 2025).
  • On the tax reform, pushback changed the policy before it launched. The GST's standard rate was cut from the 7% in the 2020 Act to 5% in the Amendment Act parliament passed in summer 2025, and lawmakers cut a proposed exemption list of 234 essential items down to a small core on stated anti-distortion reasoning — the argument happened in the open, in the chamber, and the government's original design is not what took effect on 1 January 2026 (GST Amendment Act 2025; KPMG, October 2025; IMF, January 2026). When the launch then produced documented confusion and double taxation on pre-GST stock, the Department of Revenue and Customs issued public clarifications and compliance warnings rather than denying the problem (DRC statements via Bhutanese press, January 2026).
  • On the rural life-insurance pledge (Test 5), when the finance ministry costed the promised fivefold increase at roughly Nu 600 million a year, the government said so in the chamber, funded a Nu 263 million review with a multi-agency steering committee, and moved to a targeted-subsidy redesign with a stated target of Nu 150,000 coverage by 2027 — a delay, openly explained, under direct parliamentary pressure from the opposition (Finance Minister Lekey Dorji, National Assembly question hour, June 2024, and budget statements, 2025). Considering a costlier constraint and declining the immediate version with stated reasons is, under the Test, adjustment — the broken timetable is graded in Test 5, not hidden here.
  • Between his two giant neighbours, the wobble is permanent, and the handling has been open motion rather than denial: border talks with China continued through the three-step roadmap agreed in 2021, with the 16th Expert Group Meeting held in Thimphu in October 2026 and the joint technical team meeting on delimitation following it, while the India relationship was simultaneously deepened in public — India's Nu 100 billion commitment to the 13th Plan, the Punatsangchhu-II inauguration during Prime Minister Modi's November 2025 visit, and two cross-border rail links advanced (Expert Group reporting, October–November 2026; Embassy of India, Thimphu, 2026). Neither partner's displeasure has been answered with a freeze or a public rupture.

Evidence against:

  • The deportee file was pushed back on — by the UN system, by refugee advocates, by US community organizations, by Nepal — and the government's position did not move. From the first group of ten on 27 March 2025 through continued arrivals into 2026, Bhutan's practice remained: interview, refuse recognition, transport to the Indian border (CESLAM reporting, March 2025; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026, listing the deportations among 2025's key developments). No review, no case-by-case mechanism, and no public reconsideration by Tobgay appears in the record assembled here. If another option became available — a verification process, a humanitarian stay, even a stated inquiry — the record shows no sign it was considered openly.
  • The press-freedom slide drew a decade of documented criticism (Test 2) and no documented answer: no right-to-information revival, no defamation reform, no BICMA restructuring in two years and ten months. Silence in the face of a measured, sourced wobble is a form of doubling down.
  • The GMC land wobble is live and unanswered in public. Current residents of the airport area question replacement-land fragmentation and whether they can remain in Sarpang; eleven landowners appealed replacement allocations in an earlier expansion round; and former residents expelled in the 1990s assert unresolved claims over land now inside the project (The Diplomat, 5 October 2026). Construction proceeds on schedule — airport earthworks about 65% complete in April 2026 (BBS, April 2026) — while the compensation argument is still open. Proceeding is not itself the failure; proceeding without a publicly answered settlement of the claims is the mark recorded here.

Why: Tobgay's instinct on economic wobbles is genuinely corrective — he names the emigration crisis against himself, publishes the unflattering numbers, lets parliament cut his tax rate, and explains a delayed pledge with its price tag attached. That is the FOR shelf, and it is substantial. But the wobbles that touch belonging and scrutiny — the deportees, the press framework, the Gelephu land claims — meet a closed door: no reconsideration documented in nearly three years. A ruler who adjusts on money and freezes on membership is a MIXED on this test, and the frozen files are the ones Tests 1 and 4 also grade.

Helped: Consumers and businesses, who got a 5% GST instead of 7% after open argument (GST Amendment Act 2025); rural households, whose insurance redesign at least moved toward targeting those who need it most rather than a flat promise the treasury could not carry (Finance Ministry statements, 2025); Bhutanese abroad, whose freedom to leave was never curtailed as a policy answer to the exodus. Hurt: The US-deported Lhotshampa, for whom no reconsideration came (Test 4); Gelephu-area landholders, building their lives around a project whose compensation terms are still contested while the earthworks pass 65% (BBS, April 2026; The Diplomat, October 2026); readers and journalists, whose documented constraints drew no policy response.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Nepali-speaking south, the minorities, the women, the young, the rural poor, and those outside the door — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • For citizens inside the count, the state's floor is broad and his government maintained it through a fiscal squeeze: free public healthcare and education remain universal, the 13th Plan expanded central grants to local governments under the constitutional fiscal-decentralization principle, and the ESP's windows were aimed at farmers, youth, students, SMEs, and the tourism sector rather than at the capital's insiders (13th Five-Year Plan; budget reporting, 2025; ESP record, 2024–2026).
  • The poverty trajectory, on the World Bank's numbers, is falling and forecast to keep falling: the Bank's April 2026 Development Update expects the national poverty rate to reach 5.1% in 2026 and 4.5% in 2027, while noting poverty "continues to decline amid uneven spatial progress" (World Bank, Bhutan Development Update, Spring 2026). The honest counterweight figures are printed with it: the 2022 Living Standards Survey measured national poverty at 12.4%, with rural poverty at 17.5% against roughly 5% urban, and 87% of the poor living in rural areas (NSB BLSS 2022; World Bank Poverty and Equity Assessment, 2025).
  • Growth under his government has been broad-based rather than enclave-only, on the IMF's account: recovery "supported by broadbased growth," real GDP growth of 6.8% in FY2024/25 on the IMF's fiscal-year basis and, printed separately and not blended, 8.52% in calendar 2025 on the National Statistics Bureau's basis, driven by Punatsangchhu-II's commissioning, tourism, and services (IMF, January 2026; NSB National Accounts 2025, released 2026; Kuensel reporting, August 2026). Tourism's recovery — 209,376 arrivals in 2025, up 44.3% from 145,065 in 2024, with SDF revenue of USD 43.31 million — spreads income to guides, drivers, homestays, and handicraft sellers across districts (Department of Tourism, Annual Tourism Snapshot 2025).

Evidence against:

  • In 2025 his government counted a whole people out, at the airport, in practice. The background is documented and contested, and both framings are printed: human-rights organizations, UNHCR-linked scholarship, and refugee advocates document the expulsion or coerced departure of an estimated 80,000–100,000 Nepali-speaking Lhotshampa from southern Bhutan in 1990–1993 after the 1985 Citizenship Act's retroactive tests and the 1989 removal of Nepali from schools; the Royal Government has maintained since 1990 that the camp population consisted largely of illegal immigrants who entered after 1958 and that departures were in substantial part voluntary (Bhutanese refugee-crisis record, with RGoB position and HRW/Amnesty/UNHCR documentation both cited). About 6,365 refugees remained in camps in Nepal as of 2022, and Bhutan has ratified neither statelessness convention. What happened on Tobgay's watch is not contested in its mechanics: beginning 27 March 2025, the United States deported resettled Lhotshampa refugees to Bhutan; Bhutanese officials interviewed the arrivals, declined to recognize them as citizens, gave them small cash amounts, and transported them to the Indian border, from where several reached Nepal — stateless for a second time. The counts, printed separately as sourced: ten people in the first group, of whom three were detained by Nepalese authorities after reaching the Beldangi camp (CESLAM, 31 March 2025); more than 50 individuals deported to Bhutan by July 2025, with at least 60 arrested in the US (community and programme counts, July 2025); arrests and deportations continuing into 2026, with at least 20 detained or deported from Pennsylvania alone by April 2026 (community leaders' count, April 2026). The advocacy characterization of the original expulsion as ethnic cleansing is an ALLEGATION-level framing by its proponents, recorded as such; the 2025 removals themselves are documented acts of his government, and no Tobgay statement accepting, reviewing, or regretting them appears in the record.
  • The south's land carries the same exclusion into his signature project. GMC is being built across the southern plains from which Lhotshampa families were expelled; former residents assert property claims the state has never settled, and the Bhutanese Refugee Representative Repatriation Committee's chairperson, Tilak Rai, appealed publicly in 2026 against developing what he called seized ancestral land without settlement (The Diplomat, 5 October 2026). The government markets the land to investors as a fresh start; the claimants are not in the prospectus.
  • Women are counted at the ballot box and barely in the chamber his party leads. The January 2024 election returned two women among 47 National Assembly members — 4.3%, down from about 15% in the previous legislature, the lowest since 2008 — with only six women candidates in the general round across both parties; the National Council holds three women among 25 (IPU Parline, 2024–2026; IPU, March 2026). The PDP fielded its share of that outcome, and no party-level corrective by Tobgay is documented; parliament's 2026 IPU-partnered workshops on women's representation are a chamber initiative, not a government deliverable.
  • Non-Buddhist minorities remain at the edge of the count. Freedom House scores religious freedom 2 of 4, recording that local authorities are known to harass non-Buddhists despite the constitutional guarantee (Freedom House, 2026; US State Department reporting). The young are counted as a problem more than as constituents: youth unemployment stood at 20.6% in the World Bank's April 2026 update (17.8% in Q3 2025 on the IMF's figure — printed separately, not blended), and roughly 9% of the population has voted with its feet (World Bank, April 2026; IMF, January 2026).

Why: This is the test the deportee file decides. A government can run free hospitals, falling poverty, and broad growth — his does — and still fail the counting test if, when the hardest cases arrive at its own airport, it interviews them for their language, hands them cash, and drives them to the border. The Lhotshampa were counted out in the 1990s by governments Tobgay did not lead; in 2025 they were counted out again, one planeload at a time, by his. The women's-representation and religious-minority files deepen the grade; they do not create it. FAILS is earned on documented acts of his own tenure, with the historical dispute printed beside them rather than resolved by this dossier.

Helped: The rural poor as a class, on a falling poverty trend and expanded local-government grants (World Bank, 2026; 13th Plan); tourism workers across 157 source markets' worth of arrivals in 2025; farmers and small enterprises reached by ESP windows. Hurt: The US-deported Lhotshampa — at minimum the documented first ten, and on programme counts more than 50 by July 2025 — rendered stateless again by refusal at Paro; the roughly 6,365 camp refugees in Nepal whose return file his government has not reopened; southern landholders and 1990s claimants inside the GMC footprint; women, who hold 2 of the 47 seats in the assembly his party controls; the young, one in five of whom is unemployed on the World Bank's 2026 figure.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 28 January 2024, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The Nu 15 billion stimulus — launched on the promised timetable. The PDP manifesto, A Contract with Bhutan: 13th Plan Plus 13 Pledges (Election Commission of Bhutan copy, November 2023), promised to "promptly mobilize Nu 15 billion immediately upon assuming government leadership." The ESP was launched on 18 May 2024, within four months of taking office, with a dedicated task force and secretariat as the manifesto specified; India's financing tranches reached Nu 12.5 billion by June 2026 (manifesto text; ESP record; Embassy of India, 2026). The launch promise was kept as written. Its delivery is graded separately below.
  • The tax transformation — delivered. The manifesto's economic programme promised revenue reform to fund the transformation; parliament passed the GST, excise, and income-tax package in July 2025 and it took effect on 1 January 2026, the most substantial tax overhaul in two decades (budget and IMF records, 2025–2026).
  • Hydropower acceleration — delivered in its flagship case. The 1,020 MW Punatsangchhu-II project, stalled for years before his term, was commissioned during it and inaugurated during Prime Minister Modi's November 2025 visit, lifting generation sharply — electricity generation rose from 11,673 MU in 2024 to 15,920 MU in 2025 (NSB, 2026) — and underpinning the growth figures in Test 4.
  • Tourism recovery toward the 300,000 target — in motion, not yet arrived. The manifesto set a plan to raise arrivals to 300,000 a year. Arrivals reached 209,376 in 2025 from 145,065 in 2024, and the government states the target of matching 2019's roughly 300,000 by end-2026 (Department of Tourism, 2025–2026). Recorded as direction, gradeable at term's end.
  • The USD 5 billion economy — on a stated path, unproven. The manifesto promised a USD 5 billion economy within five years and GDP per capita of USD 12,000 within ten. The Ministry of Finance put the economy at Nu 325 billion (about USD 3.8 billion) for 2025 with growth of 8.55% projected (MoF macroeconomic release, September 2025). The five-year promise falls due in 2029; it is recorded here so the record can grade it then.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The rural life-insurance increase — the clearest dated pledge, not delivered on time. Both governing and opposition parties pledged in 2023–24 to raise rural life-insurance coverage from Nu 30,000 to Nu 150,000. It was not in the FY2023–24 or FY2024–25 budgets; in June 2024 the finance minister told parliament it "won't be implemented right now" for fiscal reasons; by late 2025 the position was a funded review, a Financial Services Act in passage, and a target of reaching Nu 150,000 by 2027 (question-hour record, June 2024; Bhutan Today, December 2025). Rural families burying their dead in 2024, 2025, and 2026 received Nu 30,000 against a promised 150,000.
  • The stimulus's delivery gap. By September 2025 — sixteen months after launch — Nu 3.012 billion had been approved across 2,313 applications and Nu 1.709 billion actually disbursed: disbursement at roughly 57% of approvals, against a programme designed to run about two years (ESP implementation record, September 2025). The government has signalled reallocating underspent windows — an adjustment (Test 3) that is also an admission.
  • The exodus reversal — the manifesto's emotional core, unkept so far. Tobgay's manifesto message named the "mass exodus" as the crisis his government would reverse and promised to "entice those who have migrated overseas to return." Instead the outflow continued through his term: about 9% of the population was abroad by 2025 on the World Bank's figure, 10,167 Bhutanese students and dependants were granted Australian visas in July 2025–June 2026 alone (the second-highest year on record), and Australia moved Bhutan into its highest-risk student-visa category in January 2026 (World Bank, 2025–2026; Australian Department of Home Affairs data, 2026). Youth unemployment at 20.6% (World Bank, April 2026) is better than the 28.6% the manifesto cited for 2022 and far from the full-employment direction the contract implied.
  • The two axes, named as the method requires. Four crossings are documented in this file: (1) Kept his word AND it helped — the ESP launch and the tax reform, delivered as pledged, with the benefits recorded in Tests 1 and 4. (2) Kept his word AND it still hurt — the hydropower-led growth programme was pursued as promised, and the IMF records its shadow in the same breath: public debt around 108–118% of GDP (MoF projection 108%; IMF Article IV estimate about 118% for FY2024/25 — printed separately, not blended), the burden of which falls on the same taxpayers the growth is meant to serve. (3) Went against his word AND it helped — the rural-insurance timetable was broken, and the stated reason was protection of the wider budget on which the poor also depend; the help is recorded in full here, and the break is recorded in full above — neither launders the other. (4) Broke his word AND it hurt — the exodus-reversal pledge: not delivered, and the hurt is documented in emptied classrooms and clinics, with almost 70% of 2024 civil-service resignations from education and health (World Bank, September 2025). One further item is recorded as not documented: no public promise by Tobgay on the Lhotshampa or deportee question appears in the record assembled here, so no word-keeping grade is invented for it; its hurt is graded where it belongs, in Test 4.

Why: Tobgay's word is neither worthless nor bankable; it is a contract being honoured in its machinery and missed in its timelines. The big structural promises — stimulus launched, taxes rebuilt, hydropower flowing, tourism climbing — were made in writing and are visibly in motion, which under the mandate rule earns the FOR shelf in full. The dated, household-level promises — insurance at five times the cover, an exodus reversed — are the ones slipping, and they slip in the direction of the people with the least cushion. A prime minister two years and ten months into a five-year term, with his largest promises due in 2029, grades MIXED: the record so far is a government that builds what it said it would build, more slowly and more partially than it said it would deliver it.

Helped: Businesses and consumers who received the legislated tax system on the legislated date; electricity consumers and the treasury, from Punatsangchhu-II's generation; tourism operators riding arrivals up 44.3% in a year. Hurt: Rural households, who buried family members at the old Nu 30,000 cover through three years of a promised 150,000; ESP applicants among the 2,313 approved whose money had not arrived by September 2025 — Nu 1.3 billion approved and undisbursed at that date; schools and clinics that lost staff to an exodus the contract promised to reverse.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Tshering Tobgay holds the clearest mandate in this series' recent files — a two-round election, 30 of 47 seats, a written contract with thirteen pledges — and the record of his first two years and ten months is a study in where that mandate reaches and where it stops. Where the elected government governs directly, it governs evenly: an anti-corruption commission working to published numbers under a 71-point CPI score (Test 1's FOR shelf), a tax reform that replaced a hundred-rate sales tax with one 5% rate and a progressive income schedule, and a stimulus launched on schedule through public windows. Where the referees are concerned, he has taken real losses without reaching for the machinery — an opposition Speaker elected by one vote in his own chamber in August 2026 is the single cleanest data point — but the media framework stays structurally dependent on the state, self-censorship stands at 84% of journalists, and no right-to-information law has been revived (Test 2). His wobble answers split the same way: on money he adjusts in the open — the GST rate cut from 7% to 5% in parliament, the insurance delay explained with its Nu 600 million price tag, the emigration crisis named an existential threat against himself — while on belonging he does not move at all (Test 3). The failure that defines the file is Test 4: in March 2025 his government began receiving planeloads of deported Lhotshampa — people whose citizenship claim is contested and whose history is printed in full in this dossier — and answered each arrival with a language interview, a small cash payment, and a drive to the Indian border, more than 50 times by July 2025 on programme counts, continuing into 2026. Above all of it stands the structure he did not build but chose to sell: Gelephu Mindfulness City, the King's charter-created second legal order over a tenth of the country, pitched by the prime minister to the UN General Assembly as its salesman in September 2026, on southern land whose 1990s claimants remain uncounted. His word, finally, is MIXED in the precise sense the ledgers show: the machinery promises kept and helping, the hydropower promise kept with a debt shadow, the insurance promise broken with a fiscal reason that genuinely protected others, and the exodus promise broken with classrooms and clinics paying. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether GMC's separate courts and tax regime are ever brought inside one law, whether the Nu 150,000 insurance cover arrives in 2027, whether the deportee practice is ever reviewed, and whether the 2029 promises — USD 5 billion, 300,000 tourists — land where the contract said they would.
