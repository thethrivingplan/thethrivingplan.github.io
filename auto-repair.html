Job 1 · Under the hood & under the carOil and Filter ChangeDifficulty: Easy–ModerateFirst time: 45–60 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Work on a flat, solid surface with the engine off and cool enough to touch. If the car is lifted, it rests on jack stands or ramps rated for its weight — never on a jack alone, and never with any part of you under a car held only by a jack. Hot oil burns: let the engine sit at least 15 minutes after running. Keep oil off skin and out of drains and soil — it is collected and recycled, never poured out.

Tools needed

  • Wrench or socket that fits your drain plug (the bolt at the bottom of the oil pan)
  • Oil filter wrench
  • Drain pan that holds at least 6 quarts
  • Funnel, rags, gloves
  • Ramps, or a jack plus two jack stands

Parts & consumables

  • Engine oil — the weight and amount your manual names (commonly 4–6 quarts)
  • New oil filter
  • New drain-plug washer if your car uses one

Steps

  1. Warm the engine for 2–3 minutes so the oil flows, then switch off and wait 15 minutes.
  2. Raise the front safely (see “Ways to do it”) and set the parking brake; chock the rear wheels.
  3. Put the drain pan under the drain plug, loosen the plug, and let the oil drain fully — about 10 minutes.
  4. Remove the old filter. Check the old rubber ring came off with it — a doubled ring leaks.
  5. Smear a little fresh oil on the new filter's ring, spin it on by hand until snug, then about three-quarters of a turn more. Never crank it with the wrench.
  6. Refit the drain plug with its washer, snug — firm, not forced. Over-tightening strips the pan.
  7. Lower the car, add the manual's amount of oil through the filler cap, and check the dipstick — the marked stick that shows oil level. Add a little at a time until it sits between the marks.
  8. Start the engine for 30 seconds, check underneath for drips, switch off, wait two minutes, and check the dipstick again.
  9. Take the old oil and filter to a parts store or recycling point — most take them free.
Two honest ways to get under the car:
Ramps — drive up, done. Faster and very stable, but they cost money, need storage, and a low car may scrape. Jack and stands — cheaper and fits any car, but takes longer and every step matters: lift on the maker's lift point, stands under solid points, shake-test before going under.

Job 2 · ElectricalBattery Replacement & TestingDifficulty: EasyFirst time: 20–40 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Batteries hold acid and can spark. No smoking, no flames, eye protection on. Take rings and metal watches off. Negative cable off first, on last — the negative terminal is marked “−”. Why: if your wrench touches the car's metal body while loosening the positive first, it can short the battery. With the negative already off, that same slip does nothing.

The numbers (facts — your manual and a free parts-store test confirm them for your car)

  • A healthy, rested battery reads about 12.4–12.6 volts.
  • With the engine running, the charging system should read about 14 volts — much lower suggests the alternator (the engine-driven charger) is not charging.
  • Most parts stores will test a battery free, in the car or on the counter.

Tools needed

  • Wrench or socket for the cable clamps and hold-down (commonly 10 mm)
  • Multimeter (a simple electrical tester) if testing yourself
  • Wire brush, gloves, eye protection

Parts

  • Replacement battery of the group size and rating your manual or the old battery's label names — trade the old one in; the core charge is refunded and the old battery gets recycled

Steps

  1. Engine off, key out. Note that some cars need a radio code after a battery swap — check the manual first.
  2. Loosen and lift off the negative (−) cable first, then the positive (+). Keep them from touching the terminals.
  3. Remove the hold-down clamp, lift the old battery out — it is heavy, lift with your legs.
  4. Clean the cable ends and tray with the wire brush.
  5. Set the new battery in, refit the hold-down, connect positive first, negative last, snug each clamp so it cannot twist by hand.
  6. Start the car. If you have a multimeter, check for about 14 volts running, 12.4–12.6 rested after the car sits.
Two honest ways to test: a multimeter at home tells you voltage in a minute but not how the battery holds up under load; a free parts-store load test takes a short trip and gives the fuller answer. Voltage alone can look fine on a battery that still fails to start the car.

Job 3 · Brakes — read the limits noteBrake PadsDifficulty: ModerateFirst time: 1.5–2.5 hrs per axleTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
The car rests on jack stands — never just a jack. Brakes are the one system where a mistake hurts at speed: if anything in this guide does not match your car, stop and use a shop. This guide covers pads only. Opening brake lines, bleeding (pushing air out of the fluid lines), or touching the ABS unit (the anti-lock brake controller) is a step beyond this guide — see Honest Limits. Before driving afterwards, pump the brake pedal until it feels firm. The first press otherwise can go to the floor.

Tools needed

  • Jack, two jack stands, wheel chocks, lug wrench
  • Socket set, C-clamp or caliper piston tool
  • Brake cleaner, wire brush, gloves
  • Torque wrench — a wrench that clicks at a set tightness so bolts are neither loose nor stripped

Parts

  • New pads for that axle — always both sides together
  • Brake grease for the slide pins and pad backs, if the pad kit calls for it

Steps

  1. Loosen the lug nuts a quarter-turn while the wheel is still on the ground.
  2. Lift, set stands, remove the wheel.
  3. Unbolt the caliper (the clamp that squeezes the pads), slide it off, and hang it from a hook or rest it — never let it dangle by its hose.
  4. Note how the old pads sit, then remove them. Compare old and new shapes before fitting anything.
  5. Press the piston back slowly with the C-clamp. Watch the fluid reservoir under the hood — it can overflow as pistons go back.
  6. Clean and lightly grease the slide pins so the caliper can float as it should.
  7. Fit the new pads, refit the caliper, torque its bolts to the manual's figure.
  8. Refit the wheel, snug the lugs, lower the car, then torque the lugs in a star pattern to the manual's figure (commonly 80–100 ft-lb on many cars — it varies).
  9. Pump the pedal until firm before moving the car.
  10. Bedding-in: new pads need a gentle settling period — a series of moderate slows from about 40 mph with cooling time between, no hard stops for the first couple of hundred miles unless an emergency demands one. That mates the pad surface to the disc so braking stays smooth and even.

Job 4 · WheelsTire RotationDifficulty: Easy–ModerateFirst time: 45–75 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Jack stands for any corner in the air, chocks on the wheels staying down, flat solid ground, parking brake on. You will have two corners up at once for some patterns — use two pairs of stands, or a spare as a placeholder, and never rush a car in the air.

Tools

  • Jack, jack stands, lug wrench, torque wrench
  • Chalk or tape to mark where each tire came from

The patterns — by drivetrain (which wheels the engine drives)

  • Front-wheel drive: fronts go straight back; rears cross to the front.
  • Rear-wheel drive: rears go straight forward; fronts cross to the back.
  • All-wheel drive: cross both — fronts to opposite rears, rears to opposite fronts (an X pattern), unless your manual says otherwise.
  • Directional tires (tread made to roll one way, with an arrow on the sidewall): front-to-back on the same side only — never cross them.

Steps

  1. Check your manual for your car's pattern — it overrules this page.
  2. Loosen all lugs a quarter-turn on the ground.
  3. Lift and stand the car, move each tire to its new corner per the pattern, marking as you go.
  4. Hand-start every lug before tightening any — then snug them in a star pattern (opposite nuts in turn, like drawing a star) so the wheel seats flat.
  5. Lower the car and torque in the star pattern to the manual's figure — commonly 80–100 ft-lb on many cars; it varies, so check.
  6. Set pressures to the driver's door sticker figure (see Know Your Car) and re-check lug torque after about 50 miles.

Job 5 · RoadsideChanging a Flat / Fitting the SpareDifficulty: Easy — under pressureFirst time: 20–35 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Safe place first, before anything else: pull well off the road onto flat, firm ground, hazards (the flashing warning lights) on, parking brake set. If there is no safe place — a narrow shoulder beside fast traffic — driving slowly a short distance on the flat to somewhere safe is the better choice, and calling for help is always a right answer. Never work on the traffic side if you can avoid it, and never get under the car.

Tools — in the car

  • Spare wheel, jack, and lug wrench (usually under the trunk floor)
  • Wheel chock or a rock for the opposite wheel

Steps

  1. Hazards on, brake set, chock the wheel diagonally opposite the flat.
  2. Loosen the lug nuts a quarter-turn while the wheel is still on the ground — they are far harder once it spins in the air.
  3. Jack on the maker's lift point (shown in the manual and often marked by a notch by the wheel) until the flat clears the ground.
  4. Lugs off, flat off, spare on. Hand-tighten the lugs in a star pattern.
  5. Lower until the tire just touches and cannot spin, snug the lugs firmly, then lower fully and give each a final firm turn in the star pattern.
  6. Check the spare's pressure when you can — spares sit for years and go soft.

Temporary spare limits (fact — check your spare's sidewall for its own figures): the small “donut” spare is for getting to a tire shop: about 50 mph maximum and short distances only (often around 50 miles). It is not a fifth tire.

Job 6 · EngineSpark PlugsDifficulty: ModerateFirst time: 45–90 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Engine fully cold — plugs come out of an aluminium head that strips easily when hot, and a hot engine burns. Disconnect nothing with the engine running. Keep dirt out of the open plug holes: anything that falls in falls into the engine.

Tools

  • Spark plug socket (it has a rubber insert that grips the plug) and ratchet, plus an extension bar
  • Feeler gauge or gap tool — thin blades of known thickness for measuring the plug's gap
  • Torque wrench if your manual gives a figure

Parts

  • Plugs of the exact type your manual names, one per cylinder

Steps

  1. One at a time, start to finish — finish each plug completely before touching the next, so leads and coils can never get crossed or swapped.
  2. Remove that plug's coil or lead, blow or brush dirt away from the hole, then unscrew the old plug.
  3. Check the new plug's gap — the tiny space at its tip where the spark jumps — against the manual's figure with the gauge. Adjust by bending the outer arm only, gently.
  4. Start the new plug by hand, turning it with your fingers or the socket alone for the first several turns, so the threads can never cross. If it does not spin freely, back it out and start again — forcing it ruins the head.
  5. Snug it to the manual's torque, or the plug maker's turns-past-hand-tight instruction.
  6. Refit the coil or lead until it clicks or seats, then move to the next plug.

Job 7 · Engine — the easiest winEngine Air FilterDifficulty: EasyFirst time: 5–15 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Engine off. That is nearly the whole safety list — this is the friendliest first job in the book. Keep loose sleeves away from belts if you lean in right after switching off.

Tools

  • Often none — many filter boxes open with clips. Some need a screwdriver.

Parts

  • Filter that matches your car — take the old one to the store if unsure

Steps

  1. Find the air filter box: a large plastic box beside the engine with a fat tube running to the engine.
  2. Unclip or unscrew the lid and lift it just enough to slide the filter out — note which way up it sits.
  3. Wipe leaves and grit out of the empty box with a rag. Do not drop anything down the tube.
  4. Drop the new filter in the same way up, seat the lid evenly, and refit the clips or screws.

Teaching note: this filter cleans the air the engine breathes. A clogged one can cost power and fuel economy. Hold the old one up to the light — if you can barely see light through it, it was due.

Job 8 · Cabin comfortCabin Air FilterDifficulty: EasyFirst time: 10–25 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Engine off, and work gently — the glove box stops and clips are plastic and snap if forced. Nothing here can hurt you; plenty here can annoy you if rushed.

Tools

  • Usually none; occasionally a screwdriver

Parts

  • Cabin filter for your car — some are plain paper, some add charcoal for smells; either fits, charcoal costs a little more and dulls odours

Steps

  1. Empty the glove box. On most cars the filter hides behind it: squeeze the box's sides or unhook its stop so it drops down, revealing a narrow cover.
  2. Unclip the cover and slide the old filter out — note the airflow arrow printed on its edge and which way it points.
  3. Vacuum or shake out leaves from the slot.
  4. Slide the new filter in with its airflow arrow pointing the same way — the arrow follows the air's direction, usually down or toward the cabin as marked on the housing. Wrong way round, it still fits but filters poorly.
  5. Refit the cover and glove box, and test the fan on full — the air should smell fresher within a day.

Job 9 · Fluids — read the warningCoolant & Washer FluidDifficulty: Easy (top-up only)First time: 5–10 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST — AT FULL SIZE BECAUSE IT MATTERS
NEVER open a hot cooling system. A hot system is pressurised, and opening the radiator or tank cap can spray scalding coolant. Only check or open anything when the engine is cold — ideally untouched for hours. Coolant is also poisonous and sweet-tasting: wipe spills, keep it away from children and animals, and never leave an open container sitting out. This guide is checking and topping up only — draining and refilling the whole system is a bigger job with air-bleeding steps that vary by car.

Tools

  • None for a check; a funnel helps for topping up

Parts

  • Coolant of the type your manual names — pre-mixed is easiest
  • Washer fluid

Steps — coolant

  1. Engine cold. Find the see-through coolant tank (a translucent plastic bottle near the radiator with MIN and MAX marks). You check it without opening anything.
  2. Level between MIN and MAX when cold is right. Below MIN, top up slowly through that tank's cap — still only when cold — to MAX.
  3. Top up with the type the manual names. Coolant colours are not a reliable guide to type — green, orange, and pink do not map neatly onto formulas, and mixing types can shorten the coolant's life. The manual's named type is the safe answer; in a true roadside emergency, plain water gets you to help, with a proper refill to follow.
  4. If the level keeps dropping, that is a leak telling you something — a shop visit, not endless top-ups.

Steps — washer fluid (the easy one beside it)

  1. Find the washer bottle — its cap usually carries a windscreen-and-spray symbol.
  2. Open, fill to the neck with washer fluid, cap back on. Done.

Job 10 · VisibilityWiper BladesDifficulty: EasyFirst time: 5–15 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Put a towel on the glass where the wiper arm rests before you start. The arm is spring-loaded: with the blade off, a bare arm can snap back and crack the windscreen. The towel is the whole trick — never skip it.

Tools

  • None, usually

Parts

  • Blades in your car's sizes — the two sides are often different lengths, so buy by car model, not by grabbing two the same

Steps

  1. Lift the arm away from the glass and lay the towel under it.
  2. Find the small tab or clip where the blade meets the arm, press it, and slide the old blade off its hook.
  3. Slide the new blade on the same way until it clicks. Give it a gentle tug to confirm.
  4. Lower the arm gently back onto the glass, remove the towel, repeat on the other side.
  5. Test with the washers — a clean, quiet sweep with no streaks or chattering is the pass mark.
Two honest ways to buy: full blades cost more and take two minutes to fit; rubber refills (just the strip) cost less but are fiddly and fit fewer modern arms. For most people the full blade is the honest value.

Job 11 · LightsHeadlight BulbsDifficulty: Easy — unless access is buriedFirst time: 10–30 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Engine and lights off, and let a recently-used bulb cool. Never touch a halogen bulb's glass with bare fingers — skin oil creates a hot spot that shortens the bulb's life or cracks it. Hold it by its base, or with a clean glove or tissue. (This applies to halogen bulbs; sealed LED units are a different, usually shop, job.)

Tools

  • Often none; some cars need a screwdriver or the removal of the washer bottle neck or air box for access

Parts

  • Bulbs of the exact type named in the manual or printed on the old bulb — bought as a pair (see below)

Steps

  1. Open the hood and find the back of the headlight unit. On many cars you reach it from behind; your manual shows your car's route.
  2. Unplug the electrical connector, remove the dust cover if fitted, and unclip or twist the old bulb out — note its orientation.
  3. Fit the new bulb by its base, without touching the glass, in the same orientation until it seats and clips.
  4. Reconnect, refit the cover, and test both lights and both beams before closing the hood.
Replace in pairs — the honest trade-off: bulbs dim with age, so one new and one old means mismatched light and the old one usually fails soon after anyway. A pair costs more today and saves a second job within months. If money is tight, one bulb is legal and safe — just know the trade you are making.

A shop sign, plainly: some cars bury access so deeply that the bumper or the whole light unit must come off. If your manual's route involves body panels, a shop is not a defeat — it is the right call for that design.

Job 12 · Engine — inspectionSerpentine Belt InspectionDifficulty: Easy to inspectFirst time: 10 min to inspectTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Engine OFF for the close look, fingers clear. A running belt grabs fingers, sleeves, and hair faster than you can react. Look with the engine off; only listen with it running, from beside the car — never leaning over the belt.

What this belt is

The serpentine belt is the single long ribbed belt at the front of the engine. It drives the alternator, the water pump on many cars, the power steering pump, and the air-conditioning compressor. If it snaps, several systems stop at once — treat it with respect.

Tools

  • A torch (flashlight). Replacement, if it ever comes to it, needs a wrench for the tensioner — and on many cars that job is tight enough to be a fair shop job.

What to look and listen for

  • Cracks across the ribs, or chunks missing
  • Fraying at the edges, or cords showing
  • Glazing — a shiny, polished look on the ribbed side, which grips poorly
  • Squeal — a chirp or squeal on start-up or when the air-conditioning kicks in points at the belt or its tensioner

Looks can lie: modern belts are made of a rubber (EPDM) that rarely cracks as it wears — instead the grooves wear deeper, like a tire tread wearing down. A belt can look fine and still be worn out; a small groove-depth gauge, or a shop's quick check at service time, tells the truth.

Job 13 · Diagnostics — read before you buy partsReading OBD-II CodesDifficulty: Easy to read, skill to interpretFirst time: 10–20 minTap to open · Hold for more options
⚠ SAFETY FIRST
Reading codes is safe and harms nothing. The risk here is financial, not physical: a code is a clue, not a shopping list — read the teaching note below before buying any part.

What OBD-II is

OBD-II (On-Board Diagnostics, second generation) is the car's built-in fault-reporting system. On 1996-and-newer US cars (fact), every car has the same standard socket: under the dash, by the driver's knee, usually just left of the steering column.

Tools — two honest ways

  • A cheap code reader (a small handheld or a phone-linked plug, modest cost) — yours to keep, reads and shows codes at home
  • A free parts-store scan — most chains will plug in and read the codes free; they will also hope to sell you the part, which is exactly why the note below matters

Steps

  1. Find the socket under the dash by the driver's knee and plug the reader in.
  2. Turn the key to “on” (dash lit, engine off) unless the reader says otherwise, and run the scan.
  3. Write down every code exactly — letter and numbers — before doing anything else.
  4. Look each code up (below, and in the reader's book or a search) and think in systems, not parts.

Common codes in plain words

  • P0300–P0304 — misfire: one or more cylinders are not firing cleanly (P0300 = random, P0301–P0304 = that numbered cylinder). Causes range from worn plugs to coils to deeper engine trouble.
  • P0171 — system too lean: the engine is getting too much air or too little fuel for its mix — often an air leak or a tired sensor.
  • P0420 — catalyst efficiency below threshold: the catalytic converter (the exhaust cleaner) is not cleaning as well as it should. Expensive if it truly is the converter — so every cheaper cause gets checked first.
  • P0442 / P0455 — evaporative leak (small / large): fuel vapour is escaping from the sealed fuel system. Often just a loose or worn gas cap — tighten or replace the cap, drive a few days, and see if the light clears before spending anything.
  • P0128 — thermostat: the engine is warming up too slowly, usually a thermostat stuck open.
  • P0401 — EGR flow insufficient: the exhaust-gas recirculation valve (which routes a little exhaust back through the engine to cut emissions) is not flowing as it should, often clogged with carbon.
The teaching note that saves real money: a code names the system that noticed a problem — not the part to buy. A P0420 does not prove the converter is dead; a misfire code does not prove which of several parts caused it. Diagnose first, or pay a shop for diagnosis, before parts. And clearing a code without fixing the cause just hides it — the light returns, and on an emissions test the car will show it was recently cleared.

Honest Limits Printed at full size, on purpose

⛔ What NOT to do yourself

  • Airbag and seat-belt pretensioner work. These are small explosives designed to fire in a crash. They can fire during handling. Shop only, full stop.
  • High-voltage hybrid and EV systems. Orange cables mean hands off, full stop. Hybrid and electric-car high-voltage systems can kill, and they can hold charge after the car is off. Nothing on this page applies to them.
  • Brake hydraulics beyond pads, for beginners — brake lines, bleeding the fluid, and ABS units. Air or a leak in this system means no brakes. (Pads, as guided above, never open the fluid system — that line is the line.)
  • Refrigerant (air-conditioning gas) work. It needs recovery equipment, it is against the rules to vent it, and it can frostbite skin and eyes.
  • Anything that needs the car fully over your body without proper stands on solid ground, rated for the weight, with a shake-test first. No exceptions, no “just for a second”.
  • Any job where you cannot reach the maker's instructions for your exact car. If neither the manual nor a trustworthy guide covers your year and model, the honest move is a shop — not a guess.

How to know a shop is the right call

The job is on the list above · the guide and your car do not match · a bolt or fitting will not move and force would break it · you would need a tool you will use once that costs more than the shop's labour · the car is your only way to work tomorrow and the job might not finish · you simply do not feel sure. That last one counts. Uncertainty around brakes, fuel, or anything heavy is information, not weakness.

How not to get taken at a shop

  • Get the estimate in writing before work starts — parts, labour, and total.
  • Ask for the old parts back. Honest shops expect it; it keeps everyone honest.
  • Approve nothing extra by phone without the price in writing (a text or email counts). “While we're in there…” is where bills grow.
  • A second opinion is your right. You can pay for diagnosis, take the written findings, and decide slowly. A shop that pressures you to decide on the spot is telling you something.
  • Ask what happens if the fix does not fix it — a good shop answers plainly.

Know Your Car The dash, the rhythm, the sticker

Dashboard warning lights — in plain words

🔴 STOP NOW

  • Oil pressure (oil-can symbol): the engine is losing its lubrication. Stop and switch off as soon as it is safe — driving on can destroy the engine in minutes.
  • Temperature (thermometer in waves): the engine is overheating. Stop, switch off, and do not open anything hot (see Job 9).
  • Brake system (exclamation in a circle, or “BRAKE”): low fluid or a system fault. Test the pedal gently before moving at all; if it feels wrong, do not drive.
  • Charging (battery symbol) while driving: the car is running on battery alone and will stop when it runs out. Head for help now, electrics off where you can.

🟠 SCHEDULE SOON

  • Check engine, steady: the car noticed a fault (see Job 13). It is drivable gently, but book the diagnosis — do not ignore it for months.
  • Check engine, FLASHING: stop soon. A flashing light means an active misfire, and a misfire can overheat and damage the catalytic converter — slow down, avoid load, and get it seen promptly.
  • Tire pressure (horseshoe with an exclamation): one or more tires is low. Check all four at the next safe stop and fill to the door-sticker figure.
  • Maintenance reminders: the car's mileage-based nudge that a service is due.

🟢 INFORMATION

  • High beam, indicators, fog lights: simply telling you a system is on.
  • Door / boot open, washer fluid low, fuel low: the car asking for a small thing — do the small thing.
  • Traction / stability momentary flash on a slippery surface: the system is working. Lit steadily on a dry road, it moves to “schedule soon”.

The maintenance rhythm

General terms only — your manual is the real schedule for your car:

  • Oil change and tire rotation: roughly every 5,000–7,500 miles per many manuals — some cars and oils run longer; the manual decides.
  • Engine and cabin filters: roughly yearly, or by mileage in the manual — dusty roads shorten it.
  • Spark plugs and coolant: the manual's longer schedule — often many tens of thousands of miles on modern cars.
  • Every fuel stop or month: a walk-around — lights, tire look, anything leaking underneath.

Tire pressure — the sticker, not the tire

Use the number on the driver's door sticker (on the door frame, visible when the door is open) — not the number on the tire itself.

The number moulded on the tire is its maximum, not what your car wants. The door sticker is the maker's figure for your car's weight and handling. Check pressures cold — before driving, or after the car has sat — because driving warms the air and inflates the reading.
