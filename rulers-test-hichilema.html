The Ruler's Test — Hakainde Hichilema, President of Zambia

Subject: Hakainde Hichilema — President of the Republic of Zambia since 24 August 2021, and the seat graded is the presidency: head of state, head of government, and commander-in-chief, holding the appointment power over the judiciary's senior ranks (on Judicial Complaints Commission recommendation), the Electoral Commission's leadership, the Anti-Corruption Commission, and the police Inspector General. Hichilema, born 4 June 1962, is a businessman and commercial farmer who has led the United Party for National Development (UPND) since 2006. He contested five presidential elections before winning in 2021; in April 2017, as opposition leader, he was arrested on a treason charge arising from a motorcade incident and held for more than four months, much of it at Mukobeko maximum-security prison, before the charge was dropped and he was released on 16 August 2017 (Hichilema biography, Wikipedia, updated November 2026). He defeated incumbent Edgar Lungu in August 2021 with about 59 percent of the vote. General elections were held on 13 August 2026: the Electoral Commission of Zambia (ECZ) declared him re-elected on 18 August 2026, and he was sworn in for a second term on 1 September 2026 (Al Jazeera, August–September 2026; International Crisis Group, CrisisWatch, September 2026). Two tallies of the presidential result circulate in the record and are kept separate here, as this series keeps all counts separate: the tally reproduced by Wikipedia gives Hichilema 2,965,326 votes (60.49 percent) to Brian Mundubile's 1,856,222 (37.87 percent), on turnout of 57.23 percent; Zambian reporting citing ECZ final figures gives 3,010,542 votes (59.13 percent) to Mundubile's 1,872,799 (Breaking News Zambia, November 2026). The EU Election Observation Mission's final report records that the ECZ had still not published a complete set of presidential results for public access (EU EOM final report, presented November 2026). The UPND won 136 of the 226 constituency seats and 32 of the 40 new proportional-representation seats (Zambian Economist, October 2026). Under Article 106 of the Constitution, this second term is his last unless the Constitution is amended; a public debate on removing the term limit opened in October–November 2026 and is graded in Tests 3 and 5. He is graded on the full tenure since 2021, weighted to the last two years, because the 2026 election and its aftermath are where the record concentrates. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test is printed here because it governs the grading: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Each test closes with why the grade lands where it lands, and with a ledger naming who the actions on that test helped and who they hurt. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Counts from different sources are printed as separate counts and never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one — carrying out that platform is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how a thing is done (a second law for his own side, Test 1; gripping the referees, Test 2; counting out those who did not elect him, Test 4). Where the record shows a situation in which every available option carried real harm, the dossier says so and grades the choice as the best possible available in the moment, while still grading whether he built the trap and whether he was honest inside it. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes, and the Overall Summary names which of the four crossings occurred. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The asset-recovery campaign against the former first family ran substantially through courts, with judgments that can be read: on 17 September 2024 the High Court ordered the forfeiture of properties held by Tasila Lungu, the former president's daughter, under the Forfeiture of Proceeds of Crime Act, and when the State took possession she appealed — the Court of Appeal's ruling of 22 January 2025 addressed the propriety of the State seizing forfeited property without further court order and gave guidance constraining how it may be done (Tasila Lungu v The DPP [2025] ZMCA 1). A High Court likewise ordered the forfeiture of assets of Dalitso Lungu, the former president's son — 79 vehicles and 23 pieces of land and real estate, valued at more than USD 1.3 million (BBC reporting via Kennedy Gondwe's Muck Rack archive, 2025). Whatever else is said of these cases, their instruments were court orders, not decrees.
  • The prosecution service has at least once declined to proceed against a senior figure of the former ruling party for want of evidence: the Director of Public Prosecutions, Gilbert Phiri, dropped charges against former Patriotic Front deputy secretary-general Mumbi Phiri, citing lack of evidence (Zambia Monitor, 2025). A one-law system is visible in that refusal as much as in any conviction.
  • The two-term rule in Article 106 was applied to his predecessor: the Constitutional Court held on 10 December 2024 that Edgar Lungu had been twice elected and had twice held office and was "ineligible to participate in any future elections as a presidential candidate" (Chizombe v Lungu, Constitutional Court, 10 December 2024). The same clause is the one Hichilema's own supporters are now asking to reopen for him (Tests 3 and 5); as of grading it has been applied to the rival and not yet bent for the incumbent.
  • The economic programme ran on published rules rather than discretion: a completed IMF Extended Credit Facility, a debt-restructuring perimeter applied across creditor classes, and budgets presented to the National Assembly on schedule (IMF, Sixth Review staff report, February 2026; S&P Global Ratings, 20 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The coercive edge of the law has a documented direction, and it points one way. The arrests, summonses, seizures, and forfeitures of the tenure concentrate on the former first family and the former ruling party: Dalitso Lungu and his wife Matildah arrested in June 2024 on charges of possessing property suspected to be proceeds of crime (69 vehicles) and money laundering, days after authorities announced the seizure of some 20 properties linked to Dalitso, former first lady Esther Lungu, and Tasila Lungu (VOA reporting, June 2024); Esther Lungu repeatedly summoned and in and out of court through 2024, with her daughters also summoned before the Drug Enforcement Commission (CAJ News, 30 May 2024). Transparency International Zambia's president, Sampa Kalunga, publicly cautioned the agencies to investigate thoroughly "so that we do not see cases which only end up at either seizing properties or being withdrawn by the courts" (VOA, June 2024). The Patriotic Front's charge that the campaign is persecution of one family is an interested party's claim and is recorded as such; but no comparably senior figure of the ruling UPND appears anywhere in the documented record assembled here as prosecuted, forfeited, or even charged. One law at the source means the source — the choice of whom to investigate — and on the record the choices map onto the political map.
  • Speech offences abolished in form returned in practice against critics. Hichilema repealed criminal defamation of the president in December 2022 (Test 5), yet on 17 May 2024 Patriotic Front secretary-general Raphael Nakacinda was sentenced to 18 months in prison in a defamation case arising from remarks about the President — a conviction CIVICUS Monitor records as the defamation law "still in use despite abolition," the State's position being that the conviction rested on a different provision (CIVICUS Monitor, Zambia country report, 2024). A Zambian-American influencer, Ethel Chisono Edwards, was sentenced to 18 months for hate speech over insulting remarks about Hichilema (BBC reporting, 2025). A journalist, Thomas Allan Zgambo, was detained in August 2024 over alleged seditious material concerning a property connected to the President (CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). Socialist Party president Fred M'membe has been repeatedly arrested, summoned, and charged — including a libel charge over social media posts — across the tenure (Business Ghana/BBC reporting; Lusaka Times, March 2025). The pattern is not one prosecution but a standing weather system over one side of the street.
  • The law was litigated against a widow over a body, and the state lost on the merits of its own claim. After Edgar Lungu died in Pretoria on 5 June 2025, the Zambian government sued his family in South Africa to compel repatriation for a state funeral the family said he had expressly refused, with Hichilema's participation at its center (Government of the Republic of Zambia v Lungu Family & Others, Gauteng High Court, judgment 8 August 2025). In April 2026 the government took physical custody of the remains in Pretoria and a postmortem was conducted; the family states this was done without its knowledge or consent, under a poisoning docket the family calls fabricated — an ALLEGATION the government has not publicly answered in the record assembled here, while Attorney General Mulilo Kabesha confirmed the government had taken custody of the remains (The African Mirror, 22 April 2026; family statement via Makebi Zulu). On 23 June 2026 South Africa's Supreme Court of Appeal set aside the repatriation order, holding that the government had failed to establish any legal right — no binding agreement, no proven Zambian-law entitlement — to override the family (Lungu and Others v Government of the Republic of Zambia [2026] 3 All SA 238 (SCA)). A government confident of one law does not usually discover, a year into litigation, that its claim had no legal basis.
  • The gravest charge in the criminal code now sits on the man who finished second to him, in the same prison that once held Hichilema. Brian Mundubile and his running mate Makebi Zulu were arrested on 27 August 2026 and charged on 9 September 2026 with two counts of seditious practices over statements made on 14 August claiming the opposition had won and calling for declaration on parallel tabulation (QNC News, 23 November 2026; Crown TV Zambia, 24 November 2026). On 23 November 2026 the Director of Public Prosecutions issued a certificate charging 14 people, including Mundubile and Zulu, with treason, alleging a plot between 1 April and 14 August 2026 to overthrow the government, with overt acts including recruiting military personnel, funding training in Russia, and possessing weapons (Zambia Monitor via Legal Internationals, 23 November 2026). The treason allegations are ALLEGATIONs, untested in court, and the accused are presumed innocent; what is graded here is not their truth but the charging pattern: Human Rights Watch documented 18 people charged with treason in the election's aftermath (HRW, 1 November 2026) — a separate count from the DPP's 14-name certificate, kept separate as this series keeps counts. In 2017 the same charge, arising from far less, put Hichilema in Mukobeko; in 2026 it put his runner-up in the same prison, where Mundubile remains after 85 days in custody (Democratic Erosion, 23 November 2026; QNC News, 23 November 2026). The symmetry is the finding.

Why the grade lands here: The text of Zambian law grew more even-handed on his watch — repeals, an access-to-information statute, court-run forfeitures — and the DPP's refusal to prosecute Mumbi Phiri shows the machinery can say no. But Test 1 grades the source, and at the source the documented pattern is directional: investigation, seizure, sedition, and treason flow toward the former first family, the former ruling party, and critics of the President, while no senior figure of his own camp appears in the record as facing the same instruments. A law that only ever lands on one side is a second law in operation, whatever the statute book says. Helped: The public treasury and the credibility of asset recovery, where courts found grounds for forfeiture (the Tasila and Dalitso orders); ordinary litigants in the Mumbi Phiri case's demonstration that weak charges can be dropped. Hurt: The Lungu family — Esther, Tasila, Dalitso, and Matildah Lungu named — subjected to years of process, seizure, and a custody fight over the former president's remains; Patriotic Front and Tonse Alliance figures held under speech and treason charges; journalists and online critics (Nakacinda, Edwards, Zgambo, M'membe) imprisoned or prosecuted for words; and every Zambian whose willingness to criticize the government is priced by those examples.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Constitutional Court ruled against his government's process at the height of its flagship reform, and the government obeyed. On 27 June 2025 the Court declared the Constitution of Zambia (Amendment) Bill No. 7 unconstitutional for failure to comply with the wide public consultation required by Article 79; the President deferred the bill, appointed a 25-member Technical Committee on Constitutional Amendments, and took the revised text through nationwide consultation before Parliament passed it on 15 December 2025 (Constitutional Court judgment, 27 June 2025; Chalo Chatu, Constitution of Zambia (Amendment) Bill, 2025). A gripped court does not usually stop the president's central bill; a gripping president does not usually accept the stop.
  • Courts have ruled against the State inside the post-election cases themselves: on 21 November 2026 a three-judge panel of the Lusaka High Court dismissed all three preliminary objections the State had raised against Mundubile and Zulu's bail application in the sedition case, holding the High Court retained jurisdiction (QNC News, 23 November 2026). The judiciary's doors, once reopened, have not uniformly opened the government's way.
  • The State's own human rights body counted against the State in public: the Zambia Human Rights Commission said on 3 September 2026 that it was following the cases of 84 election-related detainees (Human Rights Watch, 1 November 2026, citing the Commission). The government also accepted a foreign appellate defeat without defiance: after the Supreme Court of Appeal ruled for the Lungu family on 23 June 2026, Attorney General Kabesha stated, "Although we disagree with today's judgment, we will not be taking this matter any further" (Lusaka Times, 23 June 2026).
  • The election itself was administered, on the day, to a standard the EU mission called efficient: polling staff "efficiently facilitating voting," a "competitive choice" offered to voters, candidate registration "inclusive, transparent and timely" (EU EOM Preliminary Statement, August 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Constitutional Court's bench was re-made in the middle of the case that decided whether his chief rival could run. On 23 September 2024 — two days before the Court was to sit on Edgar Lungu's eligibility — President Hichilema suspended three Constitutional Court judges, Justices Annie Sitali, Mungeni Mulenga, and Palan Mulonda, acting on a Judicial Complaints Commission recommendation arising from a fresh complaint by a private citizen, Moses Kalonde, after earlier complaints against the same judges over the same 2016 petition had been rejected (International IDEA Democracy Tracker, September 2024; Sishuwa Sishuwa analysis, 24 September 2024). The three were the judges who had dismissed Hichilema's 2016 election petition and had ruled in 2021 that Lungu was eligible to stand. They were removed from office on 20 October 2024 (International IDEA, October 2024 update). On 10 December 2024 the reconstituted Court — a seven-member panel — unanimously held Lungu ineligible for any future election, reversing the position the Court had taken when the suspended judges sat (Zambia Monitor, 10 December 2024; Chizombe v Lungu, Constitutional Court). The formal instruments (Articles 143–144, a JCC recommendation) were in order, and the presidency said so; the PF called the suspension illegal, which is an interested party's claim. What is documented and undisputed is the sequence: complaint revived, judges suspended 48 hours before the hearing, judges removed, case decided for the incumbent by the remaining bench. Analyst Sishuwa Sishuwa's reading — that the suspension secured the eligibility outcome — is an interpretation, labeled as such; the sequence it interprets is fact.
  • In August 2026 the courts were closed at the precise moment the presidential result could be challenged. Human Rights Watch documented that from 24 to 28 August 2026 the authorities suspended judicial services, closed and cordoned off court premises, and blocked legal challenges to the result; the police described the closure as an "intelligence-led and preventive measure" against planned unrest (HRW, 1 November 2026). The EU EOM's final report records the physical closure of access to the courts between 24 and 26 August as curtailing access to justice during two of the seven constitutionally provided days for filing a presidential election petition (EU EOM final report, November 2026). A referee whose door is locked for part of the only week a challenge may be filed has been gripped in the only way that matters.
  • The electoral referee's own conduct bent toward the incumbent at the count. The ECZ suspended the counting and announcement of results nationwide for six hours on 14 August 2026, citing violence against polling staff and alleged ballot-box theft — grounds the EU EOM recorded while noting their scale was "not sufficiently explained" (EU EOM final report; Zambia Monitor, November 2026). The mission found tabulation procedures deteriorated after counting resumed, with less public scrutiny; that the ECZ declared the result on 18 August without publishing a complete set of results; that the Commission imposed campaign timetables restricting other candidates to five-day provincial windows while exempting the President and Vice-President; and that state broadcaster ZNBC allocated 96.2 percent of its television political airtime and 90.5 percent of radio to the ruling party (EU EOM final report, November 2026). The government disputed aspects of the mission's findings when the report was presented (Zambia Monitor, November 2026) — the dispute is recorded; the underlying measurements are the mission's.
  • The police operated as a party to the contest across the whole tenure, not only in 2026. CIVICUS Monitor documents a standing pattern under the unreformed 1955 Public Order Act: opposition rallies denied or shut down — heavily armed police closing a Socialist Party rally in Kitwe on 8 June 2024 after permission had been granted; police disrupting a meeting between former president Lungu and Bishop Clement Mulenga on 18 May 2024 as an "illegal gathering"; a Tonse Alliance rally declared unlawful in January 2025 (CIVICUS Monitor, Zambia report; Zambia Monitor, January 2025). The EU EOM likewise cited "police bias" among the factors undermining fair competition in 2026. The Inspector General's account of the 14 August 2026 raid on Mundubile's home — officers "returned fire after gunfire and resistance" — sits against Mundubile's High Court petition stating he saw a soldier fatally shoot former minister Mutotwe Kafwaya at close range; the government's first public position had been to dismiss reports of the raid as disinformation, with the telecoms regulator texting every phone in Zambia to "beware of false reports of shootings and abductions," before the operation was admitted (HRW, 1 November 2026). Contradictory official accounts of a fatal raid are what a gripped information environment looks like.

Why the grade lands here: Every referee class named in this test shows the same hand at the decisive moment: the constitutional bench re-made mid-case in 2024; the courts physically closed inside the petition window in 2026; the electoral commission suspending the count, withholding the full results, and exempting the incumbent from its own campaign rules; the state broadcaster at 96 percent; the police licensing the opposition's existence rally by rally. Against that stand real counter-instances — the 2025 Bill 7 ruling obeyed, the November 2026 bail ruling against the State, the AG's acceptance of the SCA defeat — and they are why this file is argued rather than assumed. They show institutions that can still bite. They do not outweigh a record in which, each time the seat itself was at stake, the referees' independence was the variable that moved. Helped: The incumbent's re-election security and the UPND's control of the post-election process; officials spared a petition hearing in August 2026. Hurt: Justices Sitali, Mulenga, and Mulonda, removed mid-case; Zambian voters, whose constitutional route to challenge a presidential result was physically closed for part of its short window and whose full results remain unpublished; opposition candidates campaigned against under a timetable their referee wrote around the incumbent; and the detained, whose access to courts was suspended with the courts themselves (HRW, 1 November 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Bill 7 sequence is the strongest documented adjustment of the tenure. When the Constitutional Court held on 27 June 2025 that his constitutional amendment bill had been brought without the wide consultation Article 79 requires, Hichilema did not denounce the Court or force the vote: he deferred the bill, appointed a 25-member technical committee, ran nationwide consultations through late 2025, revised provisions, and returned the bill to Parliament, which passed it on 15 December 2025; he assented on 18 December 2025 (Constitutional Court, 27 June 2025; Chalo Chatu, Bill 7 record; Lusaka Brief explainer, 2026). Civil-society critics in the Oasis Forum tradition continued to object that the timetable was rushed and much of the original architecture survived — that objection is printed here as the critics' position, contested by the government — but the form of the answer was defer, consult, revise, and re-present, in the open.
  • The 2024 drought was met with an open declaration against interest: on 29 February 2024 the President declared the drought a national disaster and emergency and appealed for international assistance, rather than minimizing a crisis that exposed his government's preparedness (contemporaneous reporting, February 2024; UN OCHA situation reporting, 2024, which put the affected population at about 9.8 million people across 84 districts). Social cash transfer and cash-for-work were expanded into the affected districts.
  • Economic pushback produced course corrections, stated as such: the 2026 budget's slippage was answered with a 2027 budget, presented 6 November 2026, targeting a deficit of 3.0 percent of GDP against the 5.9 percent now expected for 2026, and fuel-tax measures imposed in the consolidation were reversed amid cost-of-living pressure (S&P Global Ratings, 20 November 2026).
  • A lost legal battle abroad was accepted in words and in fact: the Attorney General's June 2026 statement accepting the SCA's Lungu burial ruling, expressly declining the available appeal to South Africa's Constitutional Court (Lusaka Times, 23 June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The election wobble of August 2026 was answered first with denial and force. The initial official response to reports of the 14 August raid on the runner-up's home was dismissal as "disinformation," reinforced by a regulator's text message to every phone in the country; the operation was admitted only later, in shifting versions (HRW, 1 November 2026). Human Rights Watch's documented tally of the aftermath — kept here as HRW's count, from its 1 November 2026 statement: 14 apparent enforced disappearances, 61 people beaten, 71 arbitrarily detained, 18 charged with treason — has drawn, as of the reporting assembled here, no substantive official response; the Zambia Monitor noted no official government reply to the statement (Zambia Monitor via TMAfrica, 2 November 2026). The killing of Mutotwe Kafwaya during the raid remains, on HRW's account, without a credible official explanation or independent investigation — the Inspector General's "returned fire" account and Mundubile's eyewitness petition stand unreconciled, with the disputants named in Test 2.
  • Pushback from the church on the term-limit question was answered around, not answered. On 20 November 2026 the Zambia Conference of Catholic Bishops called on the President "to state clearly, publicly and without ambiguity that he will honour the limits set by the Constitution he swore to uphold," recalling his own words of 17 August 2021 — "Third term is not part of our language" (ZCCB Pastoral Statement, 20 November 2026). The government's reply, through spokesperson Elvis Nkandu, was that the debate came from individual MPs and youth groups, that there is no government policy proposal to reopen the question, and that a civil-society procession planned for 25 November 2026 to defend the limits addressed an "imaginary issue" (Zambian Eye, November 2026; Zambia Monitor, November 2026). The President himself, speaking at Choma the same day, said he respected the Constitution and would continue to do so, defended citizens' freedom to debate, and spoke of wanting the UPND to outlive his leadership — without giving the specific, unambiguous assurance the bishops requested (Zambian Observer analysis, November 2026). The presidency's separate line — that Zambians are free to debate term limits "but must not drag Hichilema into it" (Zambia Monitor headline, November 2026) — is the refusal in miniature: the one man whose answer would settle the question has positioned himself as a bystander to it.
  • Street-level pushback across the tenure was repeatedly met with the Public Order Act rather than engagement: the Kitwe rally shutdown of June 2024, the disrupted Lungu–Mulenga meeting of May 2024, four young protesters arrested in July 2024 for "idle and disorderly conduct" at a planned demonstration over load-shedding at ZESCO headquarters (CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). The wobble from below was policed, not answered.
  • The EU EOM's final report — 13 recommendations, eight of them priority — was presented to the President in November 2026 and met with public dispute of its findings by the government (Zambia Monitor, November 2026). Disputing an observer mission's measurements is a government's right; no commitment to the priority recommendations appears in the record assembled here.

The no-good-choice clause, applied and bounded: One August 2026 decision is graded under the clause. On 14 August the ECZ suspended the national count for six hours; its stated grounds — violence against polling staff and alleged ballot-box theft — were recorded by the EU EOM, which noted their scale was insufficiently explained. If tally staff were genuinely under attack, the options were to count on under threat, to suspend and secure the process, or to abandon the timetable entirely; each carried real harm, and a bounded pause to protect staff and materials is graded as the best possible choice available in the moment, not held against the seat. The clause is expressly not extended to the closure of the courts (guarding court premises while keeping them open was visibly available), nor to the raid on Mundubile's home and the disappearances HRW documented (targeted, warranted, court-produced process was available). The trap's origin is also graded: the conditions that made the count combustible — a tilted campaign environment, an unreformed assembly law, a referee whose credibility the opposition already disputed — were built across the preceding five years, and Tests 2 and 4 grade that building. Why the grade lands here: The record splits cleanly by what the pushback touched. Pushback about policy — a court's process ruling, a drought, fuel prices, a foreign judgment — was answered with adjustment, openly and sometimes against his own interest. Pushback that touched the seat — a disputed count, a dead former minister, a direct question about whether he will leave office in 2031 — was answered with denial, force, or a studied ambiguity that leaves the question open. Under the rule that extends this test, considering another option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence; the government has stated reasons on term limits (no policy proposal exists), and that is credited. But the bishops did not ask the government; they asked the man, and the man's specific answer is still outstanding. MIXED is where a genuine split record lands. Helped: Citizens consulted in the Bill 7 process and the drought-affected districts reached by expanded transfers; consumers, by the fuel-tax reversal; the Lungu family, by the accepted SCA outcome. Hurt: The families of the detained and disappeared of August 2026, who met silence where an accounting was owed; Mutotwe Kafwaya's family, whose habeas petition of 19 August 2026 was the instrument that forced the first official acknowledgment of his fate (HRW, 1 November 2026); and Zambians asking a plain question about 2031, who received a spokesman's procedural answer instead of the President's plain one.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's voters, the regions that did not elect him, the poor, and the detained — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The flagship social policy counts by formula, not by loyalty. Free education at primary and secondary level has run since January 2022, removing fees for every child in every constituency (Amnesty International UPR submission, January 2023, recording free primary and secondary education in place, while noting classroom and materials shortages). The Constituency Development Fund was raised in steps from K1.6 million per constituency to K40 million by 2026 (President's Choma address, 20 November 2026), allocated per constituency — opposition-held constituencies included — rather than per allegiance. The government states school feeding now operates in all 116 districts (UPND media statement, 13 May 2025 — a government claim, labeled as such).
  • The Bill 7 settlement structurally counted in groups that constituency voting had left out: 40 proportional-representation seats — 20 reserved for women, 15 for youth, 5 for persons with disabilities — allocated by presidential vote share, under which women hold at least 52 of the Assembly's seats in the new Parliament against 32 of 226 constituency seats won outright (Constitution (Amendment) Act No. 13 of 2025; Zambian Economist, October 2026). The reserved seats were shared with the opposition in proportion to its vote (NRPUP took 8 of 40), which is counting the other side's voters into the design rather than out of it.
  • The drought accounting followed the map of need: the February 2024 disaster declaration covered 84 districts, and expanded social cash transfer and relief were directed by district exposure, in a country where the drought's worst effects fell on farming regions including his own political heartland and far beyond it (UN OCHA, 2024).
  • The debt settlement's terms protected the counted-poor on paper and partly in fact: the IMF records that Zambia "has undertaken sustained fiscal consolidation while safeguarding social spending" through the completed programme (IMF, Sixth Review, February 2026), and the recovery that followed — growth the IMF forecasts at 5.6 percent for 2026, ratings restored to B- by Fitch (November 2025) and S&P (20 November 2026) — is a national asset, not a regional one.

Evidence against:

  • In August 2026, 1.87 million voters' choice was counted as a security threat. The documented aftermath of the count — HRW's counts, kept separate: 14 apparent enforced disappearances, 61 beaten, 71 arbitrarily detained, 18 charged with treason — fell entirely on one political camp: the runner-up and his running mate in Mukobeko, 43 opposition election monitors detained on 14 August and held incommunicado for eight days and beaten, on HRW's documentation, with police initially denying they were held at all; lawyers and journalists threatened, at least seven fleeing the country after being told they were on a "wanted list"; the passports of six critics abroad cancelled on 11 September 2026, among them academic Sishuwa Sishuwa; and reprisals against critics' families inside Zambia, including the detention of a diaspora critic's two siblings and harassment of their 76-year-old father to force removal of content, including a video demanding an inquiry into Kafwaya's killing (HRW, 1 November 2026). A count that ends with the losers in cells has counted them — as a problem.
  • The campaign itself discounted the opposition's voters before a ballot was cast: ZNBC's 96.2 percent television airtime share for the ruling party (EU EOM media monitoring, 2026) meant the state broadcaster Zambians collectively own addressed roughly a tenth of its political attention to the choice of nearly four in ten voters. The ECZ's campaign timetable exempted the President and Vice-President from the five-day provincial windows imposed on everyone else (EU EOM final report).
  • Parliament's arithmetic removed a grieving opponent: Tasila Lungu's Chawama seat was declared vacant in late 2025 for continued absence, while she was in South Africa litigating her father's burial against the government — the absence rule applied as written, and a fellow MP publicly called the government "hypocrites" for its treatment of her while she grieved (BBC reporting, 2025). The rule is the rule; its selection of this moment to enforce, against this member, is the counting question, and it is recorded as contested between the government's procedural position and the family's and opposition's account.
  • The adjustment years' burdens were distributed downward and the relief was imperfect: Amnesty International recorded through the term that schools lacked classrooms, books, and materials even as enrolment surged (UPR submission, January 2023), that persons with albinism continued to face violent attacks, and that discrimination and violence against LGBTI people persisted (Amnesty, 2023). The 2026 fiscal outturn — a deficit now expected at 5.9 percent of GDP against a 2.9 percent target, in the election year (S&P, 20 November 2026) — shows the consolidation's discipline relaxing exactly when the seat was being contested, with the correction deferred to the 2027 budget the same voters will pay for.

Why the grade lands here: This is the file's closest call, and the split is by domain. The counting that runs every day — school places without fees, a development fund allocated by constituency formula, reserved seats shared with the opposition by vote share, drought relief by district — includes everyone, including the people who voted against him; that is the strongest FOR shelf on this test in much of this series. The counting that ran in August 2026 excluded the opposition camp from the political community at the decisive moment, by cell, by airtime, and by closed court doors. Neither shelf cancels the other; MIXED records both, and the file that would move the grade is named in the summary. Helped: School-age children and their households in all 226 constituencies; women, youth, and persons with disabilities entering Parliament through the reserved seats, including on the opposition benches; drought-affected households reached by expanded transfers; future borrowers of a state whose credit rating returned to B-. Hurt: The opposition's voters and workers of August 2026 — the detained monitors, the disappeared, the beaten (HRW's counts) — and their families; diaspora critics whose passports were cancelled and whose relatives were visited at home; Tasila Lungu, who lost her seat while burying her father became a court case; and the poor, who carried the consolidation's costs first and are scheduled to carry the 2027 correction.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign pledges and public statements vs. the record since 24 August 2021, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Free education — promised, delivered, still running. The 2021 campaign's signature social pledge took effect in January 2022 and remains in force through the 2026 election he has just won partly on its strength (Amnesty International, January 2023; President's Choma address, 20 November 2026).
  • The two colonial-era repeals — promised, signed. On 23 December 2022 Hichilema assented to the Penal Code amendment abolishing the death penalty and repealing the offence of criminal defamation of the president, tweeting "#PromisesDelivered" (EEAS statement, December 2022; Death Penalty Information Center, December 2022; UN News, 6 January 2023). He had earlier commuted 30 death sentences in May 2022. The Access to Information Act followed, which the EU EOM lists among the positive legal steps of the period (EU EOM Preliminary Statement, 2026).
  • The debt promise — the largest economic word he gave, substantially kept. He promised in 2021 to resolve the debt crisis Zambia had defaulted into in 2020. The record: a 38-month IMF Extended Credit Facility completed with its sixth review in January–February 2026 (IMF, February 2026); agreements in principle covering about 94 percent of the USD 13.3 billion restructuring perimeter by S&P's count, about 96 percent by the Treasury's — two counts, kept separate — with more than 60 percent fully executed and the remainder expected to conclude in 2027 (S&P, 20 November 2026); formal exit from default; Fitch at B- since November 2025, Moody's raised to Caa1 on 30 October 2026, S&P to B-/B with stable outlook on 20 November 2026; and a staff-level agreement on 9 October 2026 for a successor 36-month ECF of USD 1.472 billion, awaiting IMF Board approval (Ministry of Finance statements, October–November 2026). Few rulers in this series carry a kept word of this size.
  • Constituency development and social protection — delivered in steps. CDF rose from K1.6 million to K40 million per constituency across the term (Choma address, 20 November 2026); partial NAPSA pension withdrawals, a long-standing worker demand he adopted, were implemented (UPND media statement, 13 May 2025 — a government claim, consistent with the public record of the 2023 implementation).
  • The term-limit word — intact, so far, in act. His statements are on the record with dates: 7 December 2018, no one is entitled to a third term; 17 August 2021, "Third term is not part of our language. It should not be anyone's language and Zambians have spoken about it" (both recalled in the ZCCB Pastoral Statement, 20 November 2026). Bill 7, his own constitutional vehicle, left Article 106 untouched (Lusaka Brief, 2026). As of grading he has not sought, accepted, or assented to any change in his own eligibility.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The Public Order Act — the clearest broken word on the democratic file. Repeal and replacement of the 1955 Act was promised in opposition, repeated in office — the New Heritage Party's September 2022 statement already records the promise and warns that previous administrations "promised similar things only to renege after finding the same bad laws expedient" (Zambian Observer, 9 September 2022). Five years on, the Act remains the operative law of assembly, and CIVICUS Monitor's finding is blunt: "Despite promises to reform the outdated 1955 Public Order Act with a progressive Public Gatherings Bill, the government has instead regressed, using security forces to suppress fundamental rights" (CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). The promised Public Gatherings Bill has not been enacted in the record assembled here.
  • Impartial enforcement — the 2021 promise the 2026 record contradicts. He came to office promising that law enforcement would be professional, that political cadres would no longer operate as a parallel authority, and that no one would be above the law. The EU EOM's 2026 findings — police bias, cadreism, misuse of state resources, detentions — and HRW's November 2026 documentation stand against the promise in the year it mattered most (EU EOM final report; HRW, 1 November 2026). The related promise's spirit is likewise contested: he repealed defamation of the president so no Zambian would be jailed for insulting him, and Zambians have since been jailed over insults to him under other provisions (Test 1: Nakacinda; Edwards) — the letter kept, the substance disputed, with the disputants named.
  • The cost-of-living word — receding through the term, and the fiscal word slipped in the election year. The 2021 campaign promised a cheaper life — mealie meal and fuel named specifically. Through most of the tenure prices rose, load-shedding reached up to 21 hours a day at the 2024 drought's trough (contemporaneous reporting, 2024), and the 2026 deficit is now expected at 5.9 percent of GDP against the 2.9 percent target his government set at the year's start, with consolidation deferred to 2027 (S&P, 20 November 2026). The drought was not of his making and is graded as circumstance in Test 3; the promise was nonetheless made in absolute terms, and on its own terms it has not been kept.
  • The unambiguous recommitment — not yet given. This is recorded as an open file, not a break: his anti-third-term words of 2018 and 2021 stand unretracted, and the government states there is no policy proposal to reopen the limit. But since October 2026 a petition to abolish term limits has been presented to the National Assembly (27–28 October 2026, Zambia Youth Organisation Consortium), some of his own MPs and youth groups have publicly called for reopening, the Catholic bishops have asked him personally for a clear public recommitment, and civil society has notified a procession for 25 November 2026 to defend the limits — while the presidency says Zambians may debate "but must not drag Hichilema into it" and police have advised the procession's organizers against proceeding (Zambia Monitor, November 2026). A word that a man declines to re-say, at the moment his own camp floats its repeal, is a word under a cloud he could dispel in one sentence. The grade will move when the sentence is said — or when it becomes clear it will not be.

Why the grade lands here: Hichilema's kept words are unusually concrete — a debt restructuring substantially completed, two repeals signed, a free-education system running, a development fund multiplied twenty-five-fold — and they are weighed at full value; under the mandate rule, delivering the platform he was elected on is this test's FOR shelf, not a neutral act. Against them stand the democracy words: the Public Order Act unrepealed and in active use against his opponents, impartial enforcement contradicted by the 2026 record, and the cost-of-living promise unmet on its own terms. The programme words were kept; the referee words were not. MIXED, with the term-limit file expressly open. Helped: Creditors and, more to the point, Zambian borrowers and taxpayers, by the restored credit standing; death-row prisoners whose sentences were commuted and every future accused, by abolition; schoolchildren and pensioners, by the delivered social words. Hurt: Opposition supporters who assembled under a law he promised to repeal and were dispersed or arrested under it; households that budgeted on the cost-of-living promise through the adjustment years; and — prospectively, if the open file resolves badly — every Zambian whose constitution's clearest safeguard depends on one man's willingness to repeat himself.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Hakainde Hichilema is the rare subject of this series who has sat on both sides of its central instruments: the treason charge that held him in Mukobeko in 2017 now holds the man who ran against him, in the same prison, on charges arising from the same kind of act — refusing to accept that he had lost. That symmetry organizes the whole file. On the first test, the law's text grew fairer on his watch while its application stayed directional — forfeitures, sedition, and treason flowing toward the former first family and his critics, with no senior figure of his own camp in the record as facing the same instruments; the year-long litigation against a widow over her husband's body, ending in a foreign appellate finding that the government had established no legal right at all, is the file in miniature. On the second, the referees moved each time the seat was at stake: three constitutional judges suspended 48 hours before his rival's eligibility hearing and removed, the reconstituted court then barring the rival; the courts closed inside the 2026 petition window; the count suspended and its full results still unpublished; the state broadcaster at 96 percent. On the third, the record splits by subject — genuine, open adjustment on Bill 7, the drought, and a lost foreign judgment; denial, force, and a spokesman's procedural answer where the seat or the succession was touched. On the fourth, the everyday counting is the strongest in this file — free education, a formula-driven development fund, reserved seats shared with the opposition — while the August 2026 counting treated the losers' camp as a security problem, and the grade holds both. On the fifth, the programme words were kept at a scale few rulers in this series match — the debt word above all — and the democracy words were not: the Public Order Act stands unrepealed over his opponents' rallies, and the cost-of-living promise is unmet on its own terms.

The four crossings, named as the method requires. Kept his word and it helped: free education, the death-penalty and defamation repeals, the debt restructuring — the keeping and the help are both documented. Kept his word and it still hurt: the consolidation word itself — he kept faith with the IMF programme and the restructuring terms through years when their incidence (fuel and meal prices, drought-era austerity) fell hardest on the poor, before the recovery arrived; the keeping earns its credit here and the hurt is recorded in full in Tests 4 and 5. Went against his word and it helped: the 2026 fuel-tax reversal — a departure from the consolidation path he had committed to, which lowered pump prices for households in the short term (S&P, 20 November 2026); the break is recorded, and so is the relief. Broke his word and it hurt: the Public Order Act promise, broken across five years, whose hurt fell on every opposition supporter dispersed, denied, or arrested under the unrepealed law.

The open files that will move these grades are named plainly: whether the treason case against Mundubile, Zulu, and their co-accused is proved in court or collapses — and whether, either way, the men are bailed and tried in the open (the sedition trial is set for 1 December 2026; a constitutional bail ruling in the treason case was pending at grading); whether an independent investigation of Mutotwe Kafwaya's killing is ever opened; whether the ECZ publishes the complete results; whether the Public Gatherings Bill is ever enacted; and whether the President says, in his own voice and without ambiguity, the sentence the bishops asked for on 20 November 2026. Zambia takes a seat on the UN Human Rights Council for 2027–2029 (Human Rights Watch, 1 November 2026). The record it carries into that seat is this one.
