The Ruler's Test — Anwar Ibrahim, Prime Minister of Malaysia

Subject: Anwar Ibrahim — Prime Minister of Malaysia since 24 November 2022, leading the Pakatan Harapan-led "unity government" (PH with Barisan Nasional, GPS, GRS and smaller parties) formed after the November 2022 election produced Malaysia's first hung parliament. He is simultaneously Minister of Finance. Previously Deputy Prime Minister (1993–1998) under Mahathir Mohamad; dismissed in 1998 and imprisoned on corruption and sodomy charges that he and international observers maintained were politically driven (the sodomy conviction was overturned in 2004; a second sodomy conviction in 2015 was followed by a full royal pardon in May 2018). He led the opposition for most of the two decades before taking office. Still in office at grading — he marked four years as prime minister on 24 November 2026; the next general election is due by February 2028. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machinery reached into his own office. His senior political secretary, Shamsul Iskandar Mohd Akin — one of his most trusted aides since December 2022 — resigned on 25 November 2025 after businessman Albert Tei alleged he had taken money and goods over Sabah mineral licences; Anwar accepted the resignation the same week and stated publicly that the MACC was free to investigate. The MACC arrested Shamsul and on 4–5 December 2025 charged him in Kuala Lumpur and Shah Alam with five counts of receiving bribes totalling over RM176,000; he pleaded not guilty and the case is pending (Reuters, 26 November 2025; The Edge, 4 December 2025 and 9 January 2026). The bribery itself is an ALLEGATION before the courts; the documented facts are the resignation, the arrest and the charges.
  • Enforcement also landed on his coalition's state allies: in June 2025 the MACC charged two Sabah assemblymen from the governing GRS bloc — and Tei, the whistleblower who paid them — over mineral prospecting licences; all pleaded not guilty (The Edge timeline, December 2025).
  • The largest corruption trial in the country's history ran to conviction under his government without documented interference: on 26 December 2025 the High Court convicted former prime minister Najib Razak on all four abuse-of-power and 21 money-laundering charges in the main 1MDB trial, sentencing him to a further 15 years and fines of RM11.39 billion plus recovery of RM2.08 billion; Najib has appealed (Reuters, 26–31 December 2025). The prosecution had been mounted under a previous government; it was neither slowed nor settled under Anwar's.
  • Prosecutorial discretion also ran in his chief opponent's favour, in the open: Muhyiddin Yassin, the former prime minister and Bersatu president charged in March 2023 with seven Jana Wibawa charges (four abuse-of-power charges were struck out by the High Court in August 2023, reinstated by the Court of Appeal in February 2024, and the trial began on 9 March 2026), was acquitted and discharged on 15 September 2026 on four charges involving RM200 million after the prosecution itself applied to discontinue them, citing evidence and litigation risks; he still faces three charges over RM25.3 million, on which prosecution witnesses testified in September 2026 that deposits into Bersatu's accounts were payments for projects, not donations (Bernama via Business Today, 15 September 2026; Scoop, 2 September 2026). The underlying bribery remains ALLEGATION, contested at trial.
  • The outcome measure moved the right way: Malaysia's Corruption Perceptions Index score rose from 47 in 2022 to 50 in 2023, held at 50 in 2024, and rose to 52 (54th of 182) in 2025 — still below his stated top-25 ambition, but the direction is documented (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released 10 February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The defining one-law event of his term is the discharge of his own deputy. On 4 September 2023 the Kuala Lumpur High Court granted Deputy Prime Minister Ahmad Zahid Hamidi a discharge not amounting to an acquittal (DNAA) on all 47 charges of criminal breach of trust, bribery and money laundering over Yayasan Akalbudi funds — after the prosecution had established a prima facie case and Zahid had been called to enter his defence. The prosecution applied to halt the case, citing further investigation; the judge granted a DNAA rather than the acquittal the defence sought. Zahid is UMNO's president, and UMNO's support is load-bearing in Anwar's coalition. The Malaysian Bar, Bersih and reform groups condemned the move and demanded the roadmap for separating the Attorney-General from the Public Prosecutor; Anwar stated repeatedly that he gave no instruction and that the decision was outgoing AG Idrus Harun's alone, and that he merely asked the AG for his reasons (contemporaneous reporting, September 2023; Anwar's parliamentary wrap-up, 19 September 2023). Whether he directed the outcome is CONTESTED — the opposition alleges a bargain, which he denies — but the structural fact is not contested: the beneficiary was his indispensable coalition partner, the decision was taken by an Attorney-General whose office his coalition had promised to split precisely to remove this conflict, and as of November 2026 that split has still not been enacted (see Test 5).
  • Clemency for the coalition's most famous convict accumulated in steps. Najib Razak's 12-year SRC sentence and RM210 million fine were halved by the Federal Territories Pardons Board in January 2024 (announced 2 February 2024) — a royal act of the previous Yang di-Pertuan Agong, taken by a board that includes federal ministers of Anwar's government. When the High Court on 22 December 2025 rejected Najib's bid to enforce an alleged royal "addendum" ordering house arrest — finding the addendum had not been decided by the Pardons Board — the rejection stood. But on 18 September 2026 the Pardons Board under Sultan Ibrahim granted Najib a conditional pardon to serve the remainder of his SRC sentence at home; declassified board minutes show the sitting Attorney-General and the Federal Territories Minister objecting in the meeting (Asia Daily, 22 November 2026). After an initial AGC attempt to suspend implementation pending clarification, the High Court gave effect to the order on 10 November 2026 and Najib went home, barred from politics and media under the conditions; the Malaysian Bar filed a judicial review against the order on 10 November 2026, which the AGC is opposing (Business Today, 24 November 2026). Clemency is constitutionally the King's act, not the prime minister's — that is stated plainly. The graded fact is narrower: at no point did Anwar's government use its place in the process to slow the sequence, and the political beneficiary is the former leader of his coalition's senior partner.
  • Lesser one-law debits are documented: the MACC named former prime minister Ismail Sabri Yaakob — of UMNO, his coalition partner — a suspect in a money-laundering investigation in 2025 after seizing some RM170 million in cash, yet no charge had been reported by November 2026 (MACC statements, 2025). And the September 2026 discontinuation of four of Muhyiddin's charges, whatever its litigation merits, means the two most consequential prosecutorial exits of the term both benefited the men at the top of the two largest Malay parties — his deputy's exit under his own AG, his rival's under the same office two years later.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the anti-corruption commission, the media, the central bank, state governments and coalition partners keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He signed away executive control of Parliament itself. The Parliamentary Services Act 2025 — restoring the administrative autonomy Parliament lost when the original 1963 Act was repealed in 1992 — was passed by the Dewan Rakyat on 5 March 2025 and the Dewan Negara on 20 March 2025, and came into force on 1 October 2026, transferring Parliament's staffing, finances and administration from the Prime Minister's Department to a Parliamentary Service Council that includes opposition MPs. Anwar described it, accurately on the documented record, as the executive relinquishing power: "The power that's there with the Prime Minister and the government is given to the House" (Bernama via Malay Mail, 15–16 November 2026).
  • The courts ruled against the state throughout his term and were obeyed: the Kota Kinabalu High Court held on 17 October 2025 that the federal government had acted unlawfully for nearly fifty years over Sabah's 40% revenue entitlement (see Test 3); the High Court dismissed Bersatu's challenge to the MACC's freezing of its accounts on 18 June 2026 on the merits, and the freeze stands as a court-tested act rather than an executive fiat (High Court judgment via contemporaneous reporting, June 2026); the Federal Court process in Muhyiddin's sedition referral ran its course in February 2026 without executive comment or pressure documented.
  • The media framework gained a genuine self-regulatory institution: the Malaysian Media Council Act was passed on 26 February 2025 after some five decades of campaigning by journalists, came into force on 14 June 2025, opened its complaints mechanism on 22 January 2026 — and in July 2026 Anwar directed that complaints against journalists from recognised media organisations be referred to the Council before any investigation or enforcement action (State Media Monitor, 2026; Malaysian Media Council records).
  • No central bank governor, judge, or state government has been removed or overruled on the documented record. Opposition-ruled states (Kedah, Kelantan, Terengganu, Perlis) govern without federal takeover; Sarawak's GPS government extracted recognition of its state oil company Petros as sole gas aggregator by negotiation (joint declaration, 21 May 2025); coalition partners defy him in public and survive — DAP's secretary-general resigned from his cabinet on a point of principle in November 2026 and Anwar's response was to ask him to stay (see Test 3).

Evidence against:

  • The judiciary's leadership transition of July 2025 was the term's clearest grip scare. Chief Justice Tengku Maimun Tuan Mat retired on 2 July 2025 without the six-month extension she could have been given — months after her April 2025 speech in Malta advocating removal of the prime minister's role in judicial appointments, a sequence one government politician publicly claimed was cause and effect (that causal claim is CONTESTED and denied by the government). The top posts then sat vacant for over two weeks amid 31 judicial vacancies, while a leaked Judicial Appointments Commission minute alleged that former AG Ahmad Terrirudin Mohd Salleh, by then a Federal Court judge, had interfered in judicial matters — an ALLEGATION, denied, never adjudicated in public. The Malaysian Bar marched on Putrajaya on 14 July 2025 demanding transparency and safeguards. The eventual appointee as Chief Justice (18 July 2025), Wan Ahmad Farid Wan Salleh, is a former UMNO deputy minister elevated from the Court of Appeal over more senior Federal Court judges — lawful under the Constitution, and Anwar denied any interference — but the appointment's optics were documented across the legal press as a self-inflicted wound to the perception of independence (Straits Times, 3 and 18 July 2025; IIUM Law Majalla analysis, 2025).
  • He kept and repeatedly renewed the anti-corruption chief he once demanded be suspended. As opposition leader in 2022, Anwar co-signed the call for MACC chief commissioner Azam Baki to be suspended over a shareholding scandal. As prime minister he extended Azam's contract three times (2023, 2024, 2025). When Bloomberg reported on 10–12 February 2026 that Azam had held 17.7 million shares (about RM800,000) in Velocity Capital Partner Bhd — above the limit for civil servants — and alleged a "corporate mafia" network inside the MACC (ALLEGATIONs; Azam and the MACC deny them, and Azam sued Bloomberg for defamation), Anwar refused suspension, citing due process, and the cabinet appointed a special committee chaired by the Attorney-General. The committee's findings had not been publicly released as of mid-2026 reporting; Azam served out his term and left office on 13 May 2026 when the government chose not to extend him a fourth time (The Edge Weekly, 2–8 March 2026; CEO Morning Brief, 11–12 May 2026).
  • The legal toolkit of control was widened as well as inherited: the December 2024 amendments to the Communications and Multimedia Act expanded the regulator's powers and penalties over online content despite civil-society criticism (ARTICLE 19/CIVICUS assessment, November 2026), and the Online Safety Act 2025, in force 1 January 2026, imposes proactive moderation duties on platforms. Malaysia's RSF press-freedom ranking under him reads 73rd (2023), 107th (2024), 88th (2025), 95th (2026) — an index, not a verdict, but the direction of travel across his term is documented, and RSF and CIJ attribute the 2026 fall in part to police investigations of journalists, including a raid on a Malaysiakini reporter's home days before the index was released, after his reporting on the migrant-worker recruitment system (RSF World Press Freedom Index 2026; CIJ statement, 30 April 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, coalition partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest protest of his term was answered with policy, not force. Ahead of the opposition-backed "Turun Anwar" rally of 26 July 2025 — the first major street protest of his premiership — Anwar announced on 23 July a package aimed at the grievances: a one-off RM100 SARA credit to every adult citizen (22 million people), the RON95 price cut to RM1.99, a toll-hike freeze and an extra public holiday. The rally proceeded peacefully at Dataran Merdeka under police facilitation, and no crackdown followed (Reuters and contemporaneous reporting, July 2025). Analysts read the package as an attempt to defuse the protest — that characterization is theirs — but the documented sequence is concession before confrontation.
  • His worst electoral result was accepted in terms. In the Sabah state election of 29 November 2025, his Pakatan Harapan won one of the 20 seats it contested (down from seven) and its ally GRS retained the state only by assembling a post-election majority; Anwar congratulated Hajiji Noor and said the federal government "fully respects the strong and clear message" of Sabah's voters, naming "prolonged injustice and neglect" as what voters had judged (Reuters, 30 November 2025; Malaysiakini, 30 November 2025).
  • On Sabah's 40% entitlement he moved — eventually — from resistance to acceptance of the principle. After the High Court's 17 October 2025 ruling, the AGC filed an appeal on 11 November 2025; Anwar told Parliament the government would not appeal the 40% entitlement itself, only perceived flaws in the judgment's reasoning, and in March 2026 he stated: "We are not appealing the 40 per cent case and that is final." The sequence is not clean — an appeal was nonetheless heard by the Court of Appeal on 29 October 2026 with judgment reserved (Sabah Guide case record, November 2026) — but the entitlement principle was conceded in public by the prime minister, and negotiations with Sabah were ordered opened.
  • Several documented adjustments came through institutions rather than around them: after cross-party criticism, the AG–PP separation bill was referred to a Parliamentary Special Select Committee in March 2026 and re-tabled in July 2026 with a strengthened design — public prosecutor candidates to be vetted through Parliament — which eight MPs across the aisle publicly endorsed (The Edge, 15 July 2026). The criticised regressive clauses of the 2024 citizenship amendments were decoupled and dropped after public backlash before the bill passed. After SUHAKAM and the Bar condemned the use of SOSMA detention in the GISBH case, Anwar directed a review and reassessment of SOSMA in February 2025 (Malaysian Bar statement, 20 February 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The adjustments repeatedly arrive late and partial. The SOSMA review produced no repeal or amendment by November 2026, and the Act's 28-day detention provision continued to be used, including a mass organised-crime operation in November 2026 (SUHAKAM, 25 November 2024; contemporaneous reporting). The diesel subsidy float of 10 June 2024 — the Peninsular price jumping from RM2.15 to RM3.35 overnight — was announced with minimal lead time and days of public confusion before the Budi Madani cash-aid mechanics settled, a sequencing failure documented across the coverage at the time.
  • On the judiciary crisis of July 2025, the wobble was met with silence first: more than two weeks of unexplained vacancy at the top of the courts, a Bar march, and only then appointments — with no public accounting for the delay or for the leaked JAC minute (Straits Times, July 2025).
  • On Azam Baki, he held his position against his own coalition's pressure for months: DAP ministers publicly called for a Royal Commission of Inquiry in February 2026, protesters gathered at MACC headquarters, and Anwar's answer was a closed committee whose findings were not public by the time Azam quietly left at term's end in May 2026 (The Edge Weekly, March 2026). The outcome — non-renewal — was the adjustment; the refusal to suspend or to publish was the doubling down, and both are on the record.
  • The most recent wobble is unresolved at grading. Transport Minister Anthony Loke — secretary-general of DAP, a senior coalition partner — announced in September 2026 that he would resign if Najib began house arrest, and resigned with immediate effect on 10 November 2026 when the court order took effect. Anwar confirmed receiving the letter on 12 November, said he wanted discussions, and on 16 November said publicly that no reshuffle was necessary and the government was stable; as of 22 November 2026 Loke had still not been granted a meeting and was telling reporters, "As far as I am concerned, I have already resigned," with the Transport Ministry in limbo (Asia Daily and Straits Times reporting, 22 November 2026). A partner's principled exit was met not with acceptance or refusal, but with deferral.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include East Malaysians, the poor, migrant workers, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • East Malaysia is counted in, measurably. Sabah's interim special grant rose from RM26.7 million (2021) to RM300 million and then RM600 million, paid in full for 2025 (Anwar's parliamentary briefing, October–November 2025); 13 MA63 claims were addressed on the government's count, including transfer of electricity-supply regulatory authority to Sabah and recognition of its technical departments. Sarawak's Petros was recognised as sole gas aggregator under the 21 May 2025 joint declaration, with 1.2 billion standard cubic feet per day allocated to the state's domestic roadmap — negotiated recognition of a state claim Putrajaya had resisted for decades, though the final commercial settlement remained under negotiation toward end-2026 with a Federal Court hearing set for January 2027 if talks fail (Prime Minister's Office statement, 21 May 2025; Asia Daily, November 2026).
  • The poor are counted through instruments that actually moved: the minimum wage rose to RM1,700 on 1 February 2025, with RM2,000 announced for June 2027 (KESUMA, Budget 2027 winding-up, 29 October 2026); the Gig Workers Act 2025 — passed 9 September 2025, in force 31 March 2026 — gave legal recognition and social-protection machinery to an estimated 1.64 million gig workers (East Asia Forum, 29 October 2026); the RM100 SARA credit of August 2025 went to every adult citizen, and total cash-aid allocations rose to RM15 billion. The mandatory death penalty was abolished in April 2023 with resentencing opened to the death-row population — a counting-in of the most easily discarded group in any system (International IDEA country report, April 2023).
  • Even migrant workers were partially counted in: mandatory EPF contributions for foreign workers began in October 2025, extending retirement savings to a workforce usually outside the ledger entirely (KESUMA compliance reporting, 2025–2026). Citizenship by operation of law for children born overseas to Malaysian mothers — a decades-old exclusion — was enacted in the October 2024 constitutional amendments.

Evidence against:

  • His opponents' voters are counted at a discount, by design. Federal constituency development allocations flow to government MPs; opposition MPs were offered allocations only under a memorandum of understanding whose draft conditions Perikatan Nasional rejected in September 2024 as improper. The impasse was allowed to stand: through 2026, constituencies that voted opposition remained without the development funding government constituencies receive — a documented, continuing asymmetry maintained by the side that, in opposition, had demanded equal allocations as a matter of principle (parliamentary record and contemporaneous reporting, 2024–2026).
  • Non-citizens were counted out of the flagship subsidy by explicit design: under BUDI95 (from 30 September 2025), citizens with a driving licence buy RON95 at RM1.99 per litre while non-citizens and large companies pay the unsubsidised price (RM2.60 at launch). The government defends this as the point of targeting — stated openly, not hidden — but on this test's measure it is a named exclusion of the migrant workforce from the term's signature benefit (Ministry of Finance launch materials, September 2025). The same workforce's recruitment system remained abusive enough that the journalist who documented it was investigated by police (CIJ, April 2026).
  • The counts that failed are also electoral facts he could not argue with: Sabah's voters — in one of Malaysia's poorest states — reduced his coalition to a single seat in November 2025; the Urban Renewal Bill drew sustained opposition from urban-poor and Malay groups who said it would displace the communities it named as beneficiaries, and it had not passed by late 2026 (contemporaneous reporting, 2025–2026). Both are recorded as the flock's own answer to how it feels counted.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record since November 2022, with dates; his long reformist arc where it bears on the tests.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • His own pay — kept, four years running. On 24 November 2022, his first day, Anwar announced he would draw no salary as Prime Minister or Finance Minister and that cabinet ministers would take a 20% pay cut until the economy recovered. He reaffirmed the position at the January 2025 cabinet retreat ("I do not take a salary… as long as the economy has not fully recovered"), and ministers were still publicly confirming in November 2026 that he works without a salary and the 20% cabinet cut remains in force (Bernama, January 2025; Housing and Local Government Minister's statement, 19 November 2026).
  • Subsidy targeting — delivered, late but real. The diesel float came on 10 June 2024; the RON95 mechanism he promised to detail by end-September 2025 launched as BUDI95 on 30 September 2025 at RM1.99 per litre for citizens, with projected savings of RM2.5–4 billion a year. The quota wobbled with oil prices (300 litres, cut to 200 in April 2026 during the price spike, restored to 300 on 1 September 2026) and he committed in October 2026 to holding RM1.99 even if the subsidy bill reached RM45 billion (Ministry of Finance statements, 2025–2026; Bernama via Paul Tan, 24 October 2026).
  • Institutional reforms delivered: mandatory death penalty abolished (April 2023); Malaysian Media Council enacted (February–June 2025); Parliamentary Services Act enacted and in force (October 2026). As ASEAN chair in 2025 he delivered the role's central test: Thailand and Cambodia agreed a ceasefire in Putrajaya on 28 July 2025 under his facilitation, followed by the Kuala Lumpur peace accord of 26 October 2025.
  • The term-limit promise was genuinely attempted: his government tabled the Constitution (Amendment) Bill 2026 to cap any prime minister at ten years — a limit that would bind his own coalition's future — and lost it in the Dewan Rakyat on 2 March 2026 by two votes, 146 to the required 148, with 44 abstentions and 32 absences including government MPs. Anwar's own four-year scorecard, published 24 November 2026, lists the failed bill openly among his administration's items (Reuters, 2 March 2026; Prime Minister's scorecard, 24 November 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or reversed):

  • The Sedition Act — reversed, on the record, twice. Pakatan Harapan's manifestos promised repeal of the Sedition Act 1948, and Anwar personally committed to it ("the commitment he and PH share to come good on their manifesto promise to repeal the Sedition Act… when he becomes prime minister" — Malay Mail, 24 October 2019). In office he first narrowed the promise — the Act was "needed" for cases touching royalty (18 July 2023) — and on 24 November 2026 told Parliament there is "no alternative to the Sedition Act for preserving racial harmony." Usage rose under him: police opened 125 investigation papers in 2023, 96 in 2024 and 142 in 2025 — the highest annual figure in the Home Minister's own parliamentary reply of 5 February 2026 — and the Act was used to charge the leader of the opposition, Muhyiddin Yassin, in August 2024 over a campaign speech about the previous King's invitation to form a government; that charge remained live at grading, its trial vacated in November 2026 while Muhyiddin sought its withdrawal following an audience with the Sultan of Pahang (Bernama, 3 November 2026).
  • The finance portfolio — not kept. PH's reform platform held that the prime minister should not also be finance minister; Anwar appointed himself Finance Minister on 2 December 2022 and holds both portfolios still, defending the arrangement as necessary for confidence and reform delivery.
  • The AG–PP separation — promised since 2018, unpassed in November 2026. The bill was tabled only on 23 February 2026, referred to a select committee in March, re-tabled in strengthened form in July, then deferred again — its second reading adjourned pending presentation to the Conference of Rulers (who were briefed on 14–15 October 2026), with the law minister conceding on 4 November 2026 that implementation could take a further year. The office whose fusion made the Zahid DNAA possible remains fused.
  • The term-limit retabling — promised, then missed. After the 2 March 2026 defeat, the government spokesman announced the bill would be retabled in the June sitting; it was not brought forward before that sitting ended on 16 July 2026 (The Edge, 16 July 2026), and had not passed by grading.
  • Political appointments — defended, not ended. PH promised to end patronage appointments to GLCs and statutory bodies; the think tank IDEAS counted 238 political appointments to federal statutory bodies and GLCs under his administration, and when a government backbencher proposed in Parliament (22 October 2024) that only professionals be appointed, Anwar defended appointing politicians who pass vetting — naming the vetting agencies — rather than ending the practice (C4 Center statement, 25 October 2024; IDEAS report, 2025). The appointment of Najib's daughter to the MATRADE board was among the cases critics cited.
  • SOSMA and the security-law shelf: the promised repeal/review of SOSMA produced a February 2025 review directive and no change to the Act by November 2026; the police oversight body that operates (IPCC) lacks the disciplinary powers of the IPCMC reformers — including his own coalition in opposition — had demanded.

The long arc, factually: Anwar Ibrahim spent 1998–2004 and 2015–2018 in prison under prosecutions he maintained were manufactured by the governments of the day, and built a quarter-century political identity on the claim that Malaysia's fused prosecutorial power and draconian statutes exist to be used against whoever holds them. In office, the record shows a man who has genuinely loosened some grips his predecessors tightened — Parliament's administration, the death penalty's mandatory form, the media council — while keeping his hands on the two levers that were used against him: a fused Attorney-General/Public Prosecutor and the Sedition Act, the latter now defended in his own words as irreplaceable. His word on money and on his own salary has been exact. His word on the architecture of power has been approximate, and the approximation always runs the same direction.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Anwar Ibrahim is the series' clearest case of a ruler graded against his own younger self — and the record lands mixed on every test because both men are in it. The reformer is documented: he surrendered the executive's control of Parliament, abolished the mandatory death penalty, built the media council his country's journalists had sought for fifty years, raised Sabah's grant twenty-fold and negotiated Sarawak's gas claim rather than crushing it, charged his own senior political secretary's case through the courts, and let a former prime minister be sentenced to fifteen more years without lifting a finger. The incumbent is equally documented: his deputy's 47 charges were discontinued after a prima facie case by the unreformed office he promised to split; the Sedition Act he promised to repeal recorded its heaviest year of investigations under him and was used against his chief rival; the anti-corruption chief he once demanded be suspended was renewed three times and protected from suspension under a cloud; and the two constitutional repairs that would bind his own power — the AG–PP split and the ten-year limit — stand tabled, deferred and defeated respectively, the second lost partly because his own side did not show up. He keeps his word exactly where it costs him money and approximately where it would cost him power. The seat, on this evidence, neither gives by pure favor nor grips in the old manner — it holds the old levers in reserve while building new ones it has not yet had to use, and the tests grade what is documented, not what is intended.
