The Ruler's Test — Kamla Persad-Bissessar, Prime Minister of Trinidad and Tobago

Subject: Kamla Persad-Bissessar SC — Prime Minister of Trinidad and Tobago since 1 May 2025. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Trinidad and Tobago's president is a largely ceremonial head of state, while executive power — the cabinet, the budget, the security services' political direction, and the state's appointments — sits with the prime minister. She holds the seat for the second time, non-consecutively: prime minister 26 May 2010 – 9 September 2015, then a decade as Leader of the Opposition, then returned to office when her United National Congress, leading a "Coalition of Interests," won the general election of 28 April 2025 with 26 of 41 House of Representatives seats and 54.04% of the popular vote, defeating the People's National Movement (13 seats) after ten years of PNM government; the Tobago People's Party won both Tobago seats (2025 general election results, Elections and Boundaries Commission; IPU Parline, 2025). The brief's premise was verified before grading: the seat did change hands in 2025, she is the current holder as of November 2026, and no further change has occurred — the PNM's Keith Rowley resigned as prime minister on 16 March 2025 and his successor Stuart Young held the office for 45 days before the election (IPU Parline, 2025). She is graded on roughly eighteen months in office. Her predecessor's completed tenure (Rowley, 2015–2025; Young, March–May 2025) appears as context where the record she inherited — the December 2024 state of emergency, the revoked Dragon gas licence, the fiscal position — bears directly on a test, and her own first premiership appears where a promise or a practice carries across both. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. No grade is naked: every grade must be earned on its own page, by the detailed why, with the receipts — the source citations — right there in the same section. Each test closes with why the grade lands where it lands, in plain words, and with a helped / hurt ledger: who her actions on that test helped, and who they hurt, named as groups and, where the record and the names rule allow, as people. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are separate axes and are graded and printed independently — a kept promise can still hurt people, and a broken one can still help them, and neither fact launders the other. The mandate rule: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one. The Test grades the holding, never the platform: carrying out what she ran on is evidence on the for-shelf of Test 5. Marks are lost only for how it is done — a second law for her own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect her (Test 4). The no-good-choice clause: where the record shows a situation in which there really was no good choice — or none that appeared to be at the time — the dossier says so, names the options that actually existed and the harm each carried, and grades it as the best possible choice available given the situation in the moment; that cannot be held against her. Options visible only in hindsight do not count as options refused. What remains gradable is whether she built the trap herself earlier, whether she was honest about it while in it, and whether she took the least-harm door that was actually open. The ethic: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Test 3 therefore grades not only whether she adjusts when pushed, but whether, when another option became available, she at least considered it openly; considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence — refusing to look is. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Her Attorney General is a cross-party appointment: John Jeremie SC served as Attorney General under the PNM's Patrick Manning, and she appointed him to the same office in her UNC government on 1 May 2025 (Persad-Bissessar administration cabinet record, 2025). The government's chief law officer is a man whose career was made on the other side of the aisle.
  • Her government conceded the state's own abuse in the Brent Thomas file rather than defend it because the abuse had been committed under its predecessor. In September 2025 Attorney General Jeremie issued a public apology to firearms dealer Brent Thomas, acknowledging the "gross abuse of power" Thomas endured when he was unlawfully detained in Barbados in October 2022 and forcibly returned without extradition proceedings; the state withdrew its appeal of the 2023 High Court ruling that found the actions unconstitutional and entered good-faith negotiations on damages (The 10-61 Dispatch, UNC review Part 4, 2026). The concession cost her government money and face; it was made anyway.
  • The Central Bank governor she removed (Test 2) was fought through the courts, not around them, and the fight ended in a settlement rather than a crushing. Dr Alvin Hilaire sued for wrongful dismissal, seeking some TT$9.87 million; on 2 November 2026, as his case resumed before Justice Ramsumair-Hinds and cross-examination of a sitting minister was about to begin, the state approached him and settled on terms his side described as favourable to him; Attorney General Jeremie stated the settlement saved taxpayers millions against the sum claimed (Caribbean Media Corporation, 2 November 2026; Trinidad Express, November 2026).
  • No opposition politician has been arrested, charged, or prosecuted on the documented record of her tenure. The opposition leader, Pennelope Beckles, operates openly; PNM parliamentarians debate, abstain, and vote against the government, including defeating its measures' margins in the Senate; former prime minister Stuart Young acts as counsel in a constitutional challenge against her government's flagship crime law (Trinidad Tribune, November 2026). Whatever else the against-shelf shows, the criminal law has not been pointed at her opponents.
  • Where the pension law below is concerned, the process point is recorded for accuracy: the bill was declared inconsistent with sections 4 and 5 of the Constitution and was therefore passed with the three-fifths special majorities section 13 requires — 27–0 in the House on 27 June 2025 (PNM abstaining) and 20–0 with 10 abstentions in the Senate on 30 June 2025 (Hansard, June 2025; Caribbean Today, July 2025). The form was followed; what Test 1 grades is the calibration, which is on the other shelf.

Evidence against:

  • Within eight weeks of taking office, her government passed a pension law backdated to fit one man. The Prime Minister's Pension (Amendment) Act 2025 introduced a minimum one-year term for a former prime minister to qualify for a pension and a tiered scale by length of service — and was made retroactive to 10 March 2025, one week before Stuart Young became prime minister on 17 March 2025. Young, who served some six weeks, is the only person the retroactivity touches: it stripped him of a pension of approximately TT$87,847 per month (Hansard, 27 June 2025; Trinidad Express, June 2025). The government defended the bill as fiscal responsibility — no lifetime pension for six weeks' service — and Attorney General Jeremie argued the bill names no one and is general in form (The Picong Party, constitutional analysis, 2025). Independent Senator Anthony Vieira answered in the debate that it was ad hominem legislation — law designed to target one person rather than establish a general principle (Trinidad Guardian, June 2025, via Trinbago Insider). A rule that is general in form and single-person in effect, backdated to a date chosen because of who it catches, is a second law wearing the first law's clothes.
  • The pension was followed within weeks by the removal of state security details from both living former prime ministers — Rowley and Young, both of the opposition party. Young stated publicly that his officers withdrew "suddenly" and without explanation; the government attributed the removals to "threat assessments" and called the timing coincidental, and has not published the revised assessments (Newsday, July 2025; Trinidad Express, July 2025). In July 2025 Rowley was also detained on arrival at V.C. Bird International Airport in Antigua and told his name appeared on a "watch list"; he was eventually allowed to proceed. Which list, issued by which authority, and why, has never been fully clarified; the Trinidad and Tobago government denied responsibility for the listing, and Rowley's own account treats the episode as connected to his treatment at home — the episode is recorded here as contested between those accounts, with the documented core undisputed: a former head of government was stopped, and no authority has explained it (Caribbean Media Corporation, July 2025).
  • She has repeatedly accused the opposition party of being a "narco-party" funded by "drug mafia money." No evidence was presented with the accusation, no financial disclosures, no referral to any investigative body, and the Elections and Boundaries Commission's records reflect no finding of illicit party financing against either major party (Trinidad Guardian, 2025, via Trinbago Insider). An accusation of criminal financing against the whole opposing party, made from the prime minister's office without a file, is the source itself applying two standards: proof for her side's claims is not required; the label alone does the work.
  • The chairmanship of the National Gas Company — the state company at the centre of the country's gas business — was given to Gerald Ramdeen, whose corruption charges in connection with an alleged kickback scheme involving former Attorney General Anand Ramlogan were discontinued in October 2022 after a key witness refused to testify: not an acquittal, not a verdict on the merits, a discontinuance (Trinidad Guardian, October 2022; Newsday, July 2025). The appointment is graded on the standard, not on Ramdeen's guilt, which no court has established and which remains an ALLEGATION in law: a government that prosecutes a "one law" message appointed to its most important energy chair a man whose own corruption case ended unresolved, replacing its own initial appointee to do so (Trinbago Insider, April 2026).
  • Between June and September 2025 her government terminated the Community-Based Environmental Protection and Enhancement Programme, the Unemployment Relief Programme, and the National Reforestation and Watershed Rehabilitation Programme — some 10,500 CEPEP workers and 360 contractors on 27 June 2025, 4,608 reforestation workers on 2 July 2025, and URP dismissals from 10 September 2025; the Central Statistical Office recorded 12,000 job losses between April and September 2025, and combined displacement estimates run from 16,000 to 20,000 (Newsday, June 2025; CSO data via Trinbago Insider, 2026). The government's stated grounds — that the programmes were patronage vehicles with ghost workers and unaudited renewals, including over 360 CEPEP contracts renewed in the election's final days without Cabinet approval — are documented and are recorded here at full strength (Trinbago Insider, 2026). What puts the file on this shelf is the incidence and the exemption: the programmes were concentrated in constituencies that historically supported the PNM, and Tobago — governed by her government's coalition partner — was exempted from the terminations entirely, with CEPEP and URP continuing there on THA funds; the exemption has not been publicly explained (Trinbago Insider, 2026). One programme law for Trinidad's opposition-leaning districts, another for the partner island, is the pattern Test 1 exists to catch. NATUC general secretary Michael Annisette — a public supporter of her administration — called for the decision to be rescinded and for consultation before dismissal, noting the Severance and Benefits Act's 45-day notice norm (Caribbean Media Corporation, July 2025).

Why the grade lands here: The for-shelf is real — a cross-party Attorney General, a conceded abuse case, a settled rather than crushed lawsuit, no prosecutions of opponents. But Test 1 grades the source, and at the source the documented sequence of her first five months runs one way: a pension law retroactively calibrated to the one living ex-prime minister of the other party, both ex-prime ministers' security withdrawn in the same weeks, an unevidenced criminal label applied to the opposition as a whole, an unresolved-case appointee raised to the top energy chair, and programme terminations whose burden fell on the other side's map while her partner's island was exempted unexplained. Each item has an official justification; the grade rests on the pattern the receipts make when they are laid in a row, and the pattern is two laws.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — taxpayers, on the government's own figures, by the pension saving and the Hilaire settlement; Brent Thomas, who received an apology and a damages negotiation instead of a continued state appeal; the principle of prosecutorial restraint toward opponents, which her government has so far observed. Hurt — Stuart Young, stripped of a pension by a law backdated to before his first day in the office it pensions; Keith Rowley and Stuart Young, both left without state security details on unpublished assessments; the PNM as an institution, carrying a "narco-party" label its accuser has never filed evidence for; and the 16,000–20,000 terminated programme workers (counted in full on Test 4's ledger as well), whose programme's documented corruption was answered by ending the workers' incomes rather than by prosecuting the documented corruption.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The police commissioner was replaced through the constitutional machinery, with the opposition's votes. Allister Guevarro was nominated by the Police Service Commission, notified by the President on 9 May 2025, and approved by a unanimous House of Representatives on 13 June 2025 — government and opposition together — taking office on 18 June 2025 after Commissioner Erla Harewood-Christopher's extended term expired on 15 May 2025 (Parliament of Trinidad and Tobago, motion record, 13 June 2025; Caribbean Media Corporation, June 2025).
  • The Senate's check on her government worked, and its working stood. Her Zones of Special Operations bill — which would have let the prime minister, with the police commissioner and chief of defence staff, designate zones subject to cordons, curfews, and warrantless searches — passed the House on 16 January 2026 but was defeated in the Senate on 27 January 2026, 15–14 with one abstention, after eight of the nine Independent senators and all six Opposition senators voted no (Caribbean Media Corporation, January 2026). The defeat was not circumvented by decree or by re-tabling under a new name in the same form; the government's fallback instruments — a further state of emergency, and later a differently drafted statute — went through Parliament and were voted on (Test 3).
  • The Chief Justice succession followed the constitutional form. Chief Justice Ivor Archie retired on 22 October 2025 after almost 18 years, having given notice the previous month and having stated it was always his intention to retire at 65; Justice Ronnie Boodoosingh, a judge since 2007 and a Court of Appeal judge since 2020, was appointed the same day under section 102 of the Constitution, after consultation with the prime minister and the leader of the opposition, by the Acting President (Office of the President, 22 October 2025; Judiciary of Trinidad and Tobago, media release, 22 October 2025). The speed of the succession is on the other shelf; the office was filled by the book's procedure, not by an acting arrangement left to linger.
  • The prosecution service acted against the government's own political interest, in public, and was not stopped. In May 2026 the police announced that, following legal advice from the Office of the Director of Public Prosecutions, warrants had been issued for Kaia Sealy — the paralysed survivor of the January 2026 police shooting whose case had become a national protest symbol against the government — charging her with three counts of shooting with intent to cause grievous bodily harm against police officers and with manslaughter in the death of her partner Joshua Samaroo (Caribbean Today, 28 May 2026; Trinidad and Tobago Police Service statement, May 2026). The charges are ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated, and Sealy's position is that she is the victim in the file; what belongs on this shelf is narrower: a prosecutorial decision acutely embarrassing to the government was taken and announced, and no interference with it appears in the record.
  • Critical media operate at full volume and in the open. Every item on this dossier's against-shelves was published by domestic outlets — the Guardian, the Express, Newsday, Wired868 — during her tenure, and the prime minister's disputes with the press (below) have been conducted in words, hers included, rather than by licence, raid, or closure. President Christine Kangaloo, appointed under the PNM, remains in office and has proclaimed her government's laws, including the ones she is reported to have had reservations about in public debate; the Elections and Boundaries Commission that ran the election she won was the commission she inherited, and it has since run the January 2026 Tobago House of Assembly election, whose 15–0 result against the PNM was certified without dispute from her government (Elections and Boundaries Commission, January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Central Bank governor was removed mid-term by Cabinet decision, and Parliament was refused the reasons. On 24 June 2025 Cabinet advised President Kangaloo to revoke Dr Alvin Hilaire's appointment as Governor of the Central Bank "with immediate effect"; his term ran to December 2026. Hilaire's attorneys' pre-action letter of 2 July 2025 records that a minister visited his office that day offering full compensation for the remainder of his term if he would resign, returned with a letter from the Attorney General when he asked for legal advice, and returned again by 7 p.m. with the revocation after he refused — an account the government has not answered in its particulars (pre-action protocol letter, 2 July 2025, via Caribbean Media Corporation). In Parliament, the Leader of Government Business invoked Standing Order 27(1)(g) to decline to answer the opposition's urgent question on the dismissal, on the ground that answering would be contrary to the public interest (Trinidad Guardian, June 2025). The prime minister had publicly complained weeks earlier that Hilaire was refusing to divulge information on the top users of foreign exchange (Caribbean Times, July 2025). A central bank governor who will not give the government what it asks, removed the same day he refuses a buy-out, with the reasons withheld from the legislature, is the clearest single grip on a referee in this dossier; the November 2026 settlement (Test 1) closed his case without any court vindicating the manner of the removal. His successor, Larry Howai, is a former finance minister of a previous UNC-led administration (Ministry of Finance media release, 25 June 2025).
  • The anti-corruption investigator was moved inside the political chain of command. In October 2025 the Anti-Corruption Investigation Bureau was transferred from the police service to the Office of the Attorney General — a Cabinet office held at the prime minister's pleasure. The Transparency Institute of Trinidad and Tobago described the move as a "regressive step," arguing that placing the anti-corruption body under the direct authority of a political appointee undermines its independence; the government described the transfer as an efficiency measure (Transparency Institute of Trinidad and Tobago statement, October 2025; Trinidad Express, October 2025).
  • Her successor crime statute reaches toward the police commissioner's independence, on a challenger's pleaded case. The Special Operations Act 2026 — proclaimed 6 October 2026, after the Senate passed it 16–15 on the casting vote of the Senate President when all nine Independent senators and all six Opposition senators voted against it (Trinidad Express, October 2026) — is now under constitutional challenge in Eversley v Attorney General, filed by activist Wendell Eversley with former prime minister Stuart Young SC leading his legal team. The claim pleads, among other things, that the Act "confers upon the Prime Minister an effective veto over the designation of Heads of Special Operations" and subjects the Commissioner's operational management to political executive involvement, and that it required a special majority it never received (fixed-date claim form, via Trinidad Tribune, November 2026). Those are a litigant's pleadings — ALLEGATIONs in a pending case, unadjudicated — recorded here because the Act's own text is public (Act No. 21 of 2026, Gazette, 6 October 2026) and because the vote that passed it is a fact: the referee bench of the upper house voted against it to a person, and it became law on a presiding officer's casting vote.
  • The referees she cannot remove, she has worked to discredit. Before the ZOSO vote she described the nine Independent senators — appointed by the President, the constitution's designated check in the upper house — as "bootlickers and brown-nosers" with "no courage to contest any election," and described President Kangaloo as a "low-level PNM functionary" (Trinidad Express, January 2026). When the Law Association of Trinidad and Tobago questioned the "hastened and truncated" process of the Chief Justice's elevation, she answered that she had "no regard for the eat-ah-food filth in the hierarchy" of the Association; the LATT replied that it remains "undeterred in carrying out its mandate" (Caribbean Media Corporation, 28 October 2025). No institution was closed or purged on this shelf; the pressure is rhetorical, sustained, and aimed at the legitimacy of the check itself.
  • The oversight instruments of policing have thinned on her watch. The Police Complaints Authority has never — in eight years, spanning both governments — received body-camera footage from the police service in any investigation; of roughly 1,000 body cameras purchased for the TTPS, about 180 were operational at Carnival 2026; and Opposition Leader Beckles stated in May 2026 that the Homeland Security Minister had cancelled the body-camera procurement — a claim recorded as hers, the government's procurement position being that a new purchase of 3,120 cameras is budgeted for 2026 (Trinbago Insider, 2026; Caribbean Today, 28 May 2026). The inheritance is shared; the trend line in her eighteen months is not toward a better-watched police.

Why the grade lands here: Both directions are documented at full strength, and neither cancels the other. The constitutional machinery seated her police commissioner unanimously, killed her flagship bill in the Senate and made the kill stand, filled the chief justiceship by the book, and let a prosecutor charge the government's most politically sensitive witness. Against that, she removed the central bank governor in a single day and refused Parliament his reasons, moved the anti-corruption bureau under her own Attorney General, and passed her replacement crime law over the unanimous objection of the independent bench on a casting vote — while publicly working to lower the standing of the senators and the lawyers' body that check her. That is a grip applied at some referees and not others, which is what MIXED means in this series.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the Senate's independent bench, whose ZOSO veto was exercised and respected as a legal fact; Kaia Sealy's prosecutors, in the narrow sense that the DPP's office was left free to take an unpopular decision; readers of a press that still publishes everything on this page. Hurt — Alvin Hilaire, removed from the Central Bank governorship eighteen months early without stated reasons; the Anti-Corruption Investigation Bureau's institutional independence, now reporting through a Cabinet office; the Independent senators and the Law Association's leadership, whose legitimacy the prime minister attacked in terms no predecessor in this series has used against the referees as a class; and police accountability in the Samaroo file, where the oversight body still has no camera footage to review.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, when another option became available, she at least considered it openly.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Tobago radar: an initial deflection, then an open owning of the secret. In late November 2025, asked about the United States military presence at Tobago's ANR Robinson International Airport, she first said the Americans were "helping us with something to do at the airport," to do with a roadway, and that "it is not a military force as such." Within days she acknowledged publicly that a US radar system was being installed, that she had known, and that she had chosen not to say so: "Since it is a sensitive national security issue… I did not think it was wise… to publicly broadcast to narco and human traffickers as well as other criminals that we were installing a radar." She stated the reason for the installation — the inherited radar system was "functioning sporadically and inefficiently," the Coast Guard could not access its data in real time, and she had requested a temporary US system until a permanent replacement could be obtained (Caribbean Media Corporation, 29 November 2025). The first answer was misleading and is recorded as such on the other shelf of Test 5; on this test, the correction was made by her, in public, with reasons, within the same news cycle's week.
  • The Senate's ZOSO defeat was answered inside the constitution. Having lost the bill on 27 January 2026, the government did not govern the zones anyway. Its next instruments were a state of emergency declared 2–3 March 2026 and extended by a House vote of 26–12 on 13 March 2026 (International Crisis Group, CrisisWatch, March 2026; Al Jazeera, 14 March 2026), and, after that emergency expired in September 2026, a new statute — the Special Operations Act — drafted to proceed by simple majority and passed, however narrowly, by Parliament (Trinidad Express, October 2026). The route is contested (Test 2); the form of the answer was legislative, not executive fiat.
  • The wage wobble was answered by conceding the number. Facing union pressure on the signature 10% promise, she directed the Chief Personnel Officer to submit a revised offer of 10%, and her finance minister announced it in the budget in her words: "Promise made, promise kept" (Budget statement, 13 October 2025, via Trinidad Express). The resulting memorandum with the Public Services Association was reached in November 2025, with new salaries effective January 2026 (Trinbago Insider Promise Tracker, 2026). The backpay half of that settlement then deadlocked (Test 5) — but the adjustment on the rate itself was made openly, against her own finance ministry's earlier 4% position.
  • The Venezuela confrontation was walked back when the facts changed. After a year of escalation — persona non grata in October 2025, the gas agreement suspended by Caracas, the January 2026 capture of Nicolás Maduro — she met Venezuela's interim president Delcy Rodríguez in New York in September 2026, on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly; a joint communiqué restored her to persona grata and agreed a cooperation agenda across trade, energy, security, and air connectivity (AZP News, September 2026). A position she had held at maximum volume was revised, in person, when the counterparty changed.
  • The no-good-choice clause, applied to the crime emergency itself. The situation she inherited had no clean door, and the dossier records the constraint rather than a demerit. The options that actually existed in May 2025 were these: Trinidad and Tobago closed 2024 with 626 murders, the worst year on record, and was already under a state of emergency declared by the outgoing PNM government in December 2024 (ICTJ, March 2026; The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 4, 2026). Door one — let emergency powers lapse and police by ordinary law — carried the harm that in fact materialized in the one ordinary-law month of her tenure: when the emergency expired on 31 January 2026, homicides returned within days, and the first quarter of 2026 tracked 29% above the same period of 2025 (Trinidad Express, via Trinbago Insider, 2026). Door two — govern by rolling emergency — carried the harm of suspended liberties and preventive detention without charge (below). She did not build this trap: the gang emergency, the December 2024 declaration, and the 2024 murder record all predate her government. Her choice of door two, repeatedly renewed through Parliament, is therefore graded as the best possible choice available given the situation in the moment, and is not held against her on this test. What remains gradable — her honesty about the trap and her treatment of the people who questioned the choice — is graded on the shelf below.

Evidence against:

  • The ZOSO defeat was answered first with an unverified accusation against the referees. Hours after the Senate vote on 27 January 2026, she posted that "two of the President's Senators approached a senior Government Senator seeking personal favours in exchange for support and for securing the remaining votes needed for passage. Those requests were rejected… They know who they are. They must live with that shame." No names, particulars, or referral to any authority followed. The Independent senators denied it — Senator Sophia Chote SC called the bill "draconian" and denied any approach — and the Law Association stated that making "grave accusations against parliamentarians, accusations that would constitute serious misconduct, without providing sufficient particulars to permit identification, investigation, or response, is inimical to the proper functioning of our democratic institutions," placing all nine senators under a cloud while allowing none to clear their name, and that if particulars could not be provided the accusations should be "publicly withdrawn" (Caribbean Media Corporation, January 2026). They were not withdrawn. The accusation is recorded as an ALLEGATION, denied by its targets, never particularized by its maker.
  • Another option was placed in front of her on ZOSO, and she refused to look at it. The Independent senators stated publicly and repeatedly that they supported the zones concept in principle; their objections were specific — a sunset clause forcing parliamentary review before renewal, and mandatory body cameras for officers exercising the enhanced powers; Senator Desiree Murray formally moved the body-camera amendment (Trinbago Insider, 2026). Considering those amendments and declining them with stated reasons would have cost this test nothing. Instead the amendments were not taken up, the bill died unamended, and the senators who offered them were answered with the "bootlickers" attack and the favours allegation above. The refusal to look, not the disagreement, is the against-evidence.
  • The street's biggest pushback of her tenure — the Samaroo killing — was answered by defending the apparatus and proposing to restrict the evidence. On 20 January 2026, after a chase ending in St Augustine, police fired at least 17 rounds into a crashed car, killing courier Joshua Samaroo, 31 — shot 19 times on the family's account of the reports — and leaving his partner Kaia Sealy paralysed; CCTV footage appeared to show Samaroo rolling down his window and putting his hands out before the firing (Trinidad Express, 2026; Trinbago Insider, 2026). Protests on 2 February 2026 demanded the resignations of the police commissioner and the Homeland Security Minister. No officer was suspended; the officers were placed on administrative duties; forensic reports remained outstanding months later (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 4, 2026). Minister Roger Alexander stated on television that "something needs to be done with those who do that in terms of the law," proposing legislation to criminalise the public sharing of CCTV videos that expose police abuses (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 4, 2026), and later, at a UNC news conference, that police officers "must also be recognised as victims," that judgments be left to the courts, and that he had no intention of stepping down (Trinidad Express, February 2026). The investigation files were to go to the DPP — process, eventually; the open adjustment the street asked for — suspension, disclosure, a resignation considered — was refused at every point.
  • The unions' October 2026 pushback was answered by attributing their motives. When at least twelve trade unions marched on 30 October 2026 over WASA retrenchments and the unresolved backpay, she stated ahead of the march that the protest "by these unions and the PNM is driven by racial and political hatred of the UNC and not by actual efforts to get benefits for their members," and afterwards described it as a "long touted hate protest" that had "bust," writing: "The protest has bust, back to work on Monday, enjoy the weekend, your Prime Minister loves you" (Caribbean Media Corporation, 2 November 2026). The unions' stated grievance — TT$3.3 billion in agreed backpay unpaid eight months after signature (Test 5) — was not answered in either statement. NATUC's Michael Annisette answered for the marchers: "this issue of wages and salaries has nothing to do with race" (Caribbean Media Corporation, 2 November 2026). The racial-hatred attribution is her characterization, recorded as contested by the people it describes.
  • The campaign rhetoric she was pushed back on hardest, she doubled down on. Her closing-rally words of 26 April 2025 — urging citizens facing home invaders to "load up your 'matic, light them up, empty the clip," quoting a Trinibad song — were condemned by human rights groups and the Council for Responsible Political Behaviour; she refused to apologise and insisted the public needed to defend themselves (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 4, 2026). The rhetoric was then codified: the Home Invasion (Self-Defence and Defence of Property) Act, proclaimed 20 January 2026, removes the duty to retreat and affirms deadly force in defence of property. That she ran on this is mandate, and Test 5 credits the keeping; what this shelf records is the answer to the wobble — when the country's human-rights bodies pushed back, the response was refusal, not consideration.

Why the grade lands here: She has adjusted, openly and more than once — the radar secret owned within days, the 10% conceded against her own ministry's position, the Venezuela confrontation reversed in person when the counterparty changed, and a lost bill answered with legislation rather than fiat. She has also, at the moments of sharpest institutional pushback, reached for the delegitimizing answer: an unparticularized bribery allegation against the senators who beat her bill, a ministerial proposal to criminalize the footage that exposed a police killing, and a racial-motive attribution against marching unions whose pay file she did not answer. The no-good-choice clause protects her emergency-powers decision itself; it does not protect the manner of those answers. Both shelves are heavy, and the grade is MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Tobagonians and the travelling public, told the truth about the radar within days of the first deflection; public servants, whose 10% rate she conceded rather than litigated; the country's external position, when the Venezuela thaw restored a working channel to its nearest neighbour and largest gas prospect. Hurt — the nine Independent senators, left under an unparticularized allegation of attempted bribery by the head of government; Joshua Samaroo's family and Kaia Sealy, who got process without disclosure and a minister proposing to criminalize the circulation of the footage of his death; and the marching unions of October 2026, whose documented pay grievance was answered as racial hatred.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Tobago, the minorities, the opposition's voters, the poor, and those who share the streets — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Tobago is counted in, measurably and first. Four days after the Tobago People's Party's 15–0 sweep of the Tobago House of Assembly election on 12 January 2026 (TPP 60.4% of the vote; Elections and Boundaries Commission, 2026), she personally piloted the Tobago House of Assembly (Amendment) Bill 2026 through the House of Representatives; it passed unanimously, 38–0, increasing the Assembly's secretaries from seven to twelve and its quorum from nine to twelve (Trinbago Insider, 2026). Tobago's allocation in the FY2026 budget was TT$3.724 billion — 6.3% of the national budget, above the Dispute Resolution Commission's recommended floor (Budget statement, October 2025, via Sheppard). The public-sector wage settlement expressly included Tobago House of Assembly workers (Caribbean Council, October 2025). The fuller constitutional self-government bill remains undelivered (Test 5) — a word file, graded there; on this test, the island that did not vote for her party nationally is funded above the floor and legislated for within the week of its own election.
  • The social delivery of her first budget ran to the counted-out as a class. The Children's Life Fund (Amendment) Act 2025, assented to 11 July 2025, substantially broadened state-funded overseas medical treatment for children with life-threatening conditions (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 3, 2026). The Couva Children's Hospital — built under her first government, idle for most of a decade under her predecessor — was recommissioned, with first paediatric surgeries on 28 November 2025 and over 500 surgeries performed by May 2026 (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 3, 2026). Some 18,000 laptops were delivered to Form 1 pupils between 27 November 2025 and 11 February 2026 (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 3, 2026). Private pensions became tax-exempt from 1 January 2026; VAT was removed from a wide range of basic food items from 17 October 2025; the price of super gasoline was cut by TT$1 per litre (Budget statement, October 2025).
  • A debt to the dead was paid that the previous accounting had left open. In January 2026 her government made ex gratia payments to the families of two of the Paria divers who died in the February 2022 underwater tragedy, and to survivor Christopher Boodram — families who had spent the intervening years in dispute with the state (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 3, 2026).
  • The murder decline's benefits landed where the dying was. Murders fell from 626 in 2024 to 369 in 2025 — a 41% reduction and the lowest annual figure since 2014 — and her July 2026 statement, citing police statistics, recorded declines in every police division between 2024 and 2026, with the largest reductions in the Port of Spain, Western, and Northern divisions, the poor urban corridors that carry the country's killing (TTPS figures via ICTJ, March 2026; prime ministerial statement, 21 July 2026). The method is graded on Tests 2 and 3 and its costs are on the shelf below; the benefit itself is real, and it accrued overwhelmingly to the poor.

Evidence against:

  • Venezuelan migrants and asylum seekers are counted out as policy. In late October 2025 her government halted planned releases of detained migrants and prepared a mass deportation exercise for at least 200 Venezuelans in detention; a Homeland Security Ministry memorandum recorded that "consideration is currently being given to the implementation of a mass deportation exercise" (AFP memorandum reporting, October 2025). Civil-society groups warned the deportations would breach the principle of non-refoulement, and Amnesty International's 2026 annual report documented that Trinidad and Tobago "continued to push back people seeking asylum, in violation of international human rights law" (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 5, 2026). In October 2025 she publicly invited CARICOM to "get together" and take some of the "illegal Venezuelans" sheltering in her country, adding that if any CARICOM state withdrew support for Trinidad's ambitions over her stance, "she did not care" (Sir Ronald Sanders, commentary, 24 October 2025). On 5 June 2025 she had already instructed the Coast Guard to use deadly force against unidentified Venezuelan vessels entering the country's waters (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 5, 2026). Trinidad and Tobago hosts one of the region's largest per-capita Venezuelan populations; the burden is real and is recorded — the counting-out is also real, and it is policy, not drift.
  • Two of her own citizens, killed at sea by the campaign she endorsed, were answered with a navigation instruction. On 14 October 2025 a US strike on a boat returning from Venezuela killed all six men aboard, among them Chad Joseph, 26, a father of three, and Rishi Samaroo, 41, who had been working on a farm in Venezuela and was returning to help care for his elderly mother — both of Las Cuevas, a fishing village (Army Times, 27 January 2026). She had endorsed the strike campaign in advance and in terms: "I have no sympathy for traffickers; the US military should kill them all, violently" (statement of September 2025, via Caribbean Media Corporation). The United States has produced no public evidence that either man was a trafficker; the families' position, pleaded in Burnley v. United States — filed 27 January 2026 in the Massachusetts federal court under the Death on the High Seas Act and the Alien Tort Statute — is that the men were fishermen and farm workers and that the killings were unlawful extrajudicial killings; the White House position is that the 14 October strike was a lawful operation against designated narco-terrorists (Army Times, 27 January 2026). Both positions are recorded; the men's status is contested and unadjudicated. What is not contested is her government's response to its own citizens' deaths: the prime minister advised local fishermen to "stay within the country's maritime boundaries" (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 5, 2026). No demand for the evidence against Joseph and Samaroo, no call for an inquiry, appears in the record assembled here. By mid-April 2026 the strike campaign she endorsed had killed at least 181 people across more than 50 vessels (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 5, 2026).
  • The emergency's weight fell, as it was designed to fall, on the poor young men of specific districts — and the release ledger shows how many were held without a case. During the July 2025 – January 2026 state of emergency, approximately 170 preventive detention orders were signed, at least 120 executed, and roughly 100 people held; when the emergency expired on 31 January 2026, dozens walked free from the Eastern Correctional and Rehabilitation Centre (Trinbago Insider, 2026). Under the March 2026 emergency, 373 people were arrested within ten days (Al Jazeera, March 2026). Fatal police shootings rose from 37 in 2024 to 55 in 2025 even as murders fell (Trinbago Insider, 2026). The Special Operations Act's first declared closed area, in November 2026, was Maloney Gardens, a public-housing development, placed under a 9 p.m. to 5 a.m. curfew (Trinidad Tribune, November 2026). The residents of the districts that gained most from the murder decline and the residents who carry the cordons are substantially the same people; both facts are on this test's ledger.
  • The terminated programme workers of Test 1 belong on this ledger too, at their human weight: 16,000–20,000 workers, drawn from the country's poorest households and concentrated in opposition-leaning constituencies, lost their incomes between June and September 2025, against a replacement National Recruitment Drive that drew over 110,000 applications and had hired 1,801 people — on three-month contracts — by late February 2026 (CSO data and EmployTT figures via Trinbago Insider, 2026).

Why the grade lands here: Tobago is counted in above the constitutional floor and within days of its own verdict; children, pensioners, patients, and pupils were delivered to at scale; the Paria families were paid; and the murder decline's beneficiaries are the poor of the killing corridors. Against that stand two groups counted out in the literal sense — Venezuelan asylum seekers pushed back against the documented non-refoulement standard, and two dead citizens of a Las Cuevas fishing village whose government answered their killing with advice to stay closer to shore — plus the detainee and programme-worker ledgers, whose costs fall on the same poor communities the benefits reached. A test that asks whether everyone is counted cannot pass a file with a policy-level exception, and cannot fail one with this much delivery in it. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Tobago's 53,000 electors and their Assembly, funded at 6.3% of the national budget and legislatively strengthened within the week; children needing overseas treatment and surgery, and their families; Form 1 pupils; private pensioners; the Paria divers' families and Christopher Boodram; and the residents of Port of Spain, Western, and Northern divisions, where the killing fell furthest. Hurt — Venezuelan detainees and asylum seekers, held for mass deportation and pushed back at the border; the families of Chad Joseph and Rishi Samaroo of Las Cuevas, who buried citizens their government would not ask a question for; the roughly 100 preventive detainees of the 2025–26 emergency, most released without charge when it expired; the residents of Maloney Gardens, first community under the new Act's curfew; and the 16,000–20,000 terminated programme workers, of whom 1,801 had been rehired on short contracts by February 2026.

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Manifesto pledges and public statements vs. the record since 1 May 2025, with dates; her predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The 10% — her signature promise, delivered on the rate. She campaigned on a 10% salary increase for public servants, against the PNM's standing 4% offer. Her finance minister announced the revised 10% offer in the budget of 13 October 2025 — "Promise made, promise kept because when UNC wins, public servants win" (Budget statement, October 2025) — and a memorandum of agreement with the Public Services Association was reached in November 2025 covering the 2014–2016 and 2017–2019 bargaining periods, with new salaries effective January 2026; a parallel 10% settlement with the National Union of Government and Federated Workers was signed on 1 April 2026 (Trinbago Insider Promise Tracker, 2026; Trinidad Guardian, 1 April 2026). Roughly 40,000 public-sector workers received the increase (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 3, 2026). The arrears half of this promise is on the other shelf; the rate itself was promised, resisted by her own officials at 4%, and paid.
  • The tax promises — kept in the letter. The property tax she pledged to scrap was repealed in her first budget; private pensions became tax-exempt from 1 January 2026, as promised; VAT came off basic food items from 17 October 2025; super gasoline fell TT$1 per litre on budget day (Budget statement, October 2025; Caribbean Council budget summary, October 2025). The new levies that accompanied them are on the other shelf as a word-adjacent file; the named repeals happened on the named dates.
  • Stand your ground — ran on it, enacted it. The home-invasion rhetoric of her campaign (Test 3) was a promise as much as a provocation, and it was kept in statutory form: the Home Invasion (Self-Defence and Defence of Property) Act 2025 was proclaimed into law on 20 January 2026, removing the duty to retreat (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 4, 2026).
  • The social promises — delivered on schedule. The Children's Life Fund expansion (assented 11 July 2025), the Couva Children's Hospital reopening (first surgeries 28 November 2025), and 18,000 Form 1 laptops (delivered 27 November 2025 – 11 February 2026) were each named on the campaign trail and each landed within her first year (The 10-61 Dispatch, Part 3, 2026). On election night she had listed the hospital, the laptops, and pensioners' protection among the victory's purposes (Writeups24, May 2025); all three files moved.
  • Pensioners — the next instalment is dated. The October 2026 budget announced a TT$250 monthly increase in the Senior Citizens' Pension effective January 2027 (Wired868, 21 November 2026). Recorded as promised-and-dated, not yet as paid.
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: Stuart Young's 45-day government had selected Nigeria's Oando Trading as preferred bidder for the Pointe-à-Pierre refinery lease in March 2025; her government repudiated that deal on taking office — "she has made it clear that her government will no longer go ahead with Oando and that they will not honour any deal signed by the PNM" (Knowledge Bylanes reporting, 2025). A predecessor's deal is not her word, and its cancellation is graded only as the starting condition of her own refinery promise, below.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • 50,000 jobs — the flagship number ran backwards. Her manifesto promised the creation of 50,000-plus jobs, and she repeated "over 50,000 jobs" among her victory-night commitments (Writeups24, May 2025; UNC Manifesto 2025). The documented first-year flow is the other way: 16,000–20,000 programme workers terminated June–September 2025 (Test 1), 12,000 job losses recorded by the CSO for April–September 2025, unemployment up to 4.8% by the third quarter of 2025, and the replacement National Recruitment Drive — launched 19 October 2025 promising 20,000 permanent positions — had hired 1,801 people, on three-month contracts, by late February 2026, against over 110,000 applications (Trinbago Insider, 2026). The TT$475 million Employment Fund allocated for the transition has produced no public accounting of disbursement (Trinbago Insider, 2026). Eighteen months in, the jobs promise is not late; on the official count it is negative.
  • The backpay — a settlement signed and then unsettled. The November 2025 agreement that delivered the 10% rate left TT$3.3 billion in backpay unresolved; eight months after signature the arrears structure remained deadlocked, the PSA's counter-proposal (80% cash, 20% deferred as interest-bearing bonds) went some two months without a Chief Personnel Officer response, and the PSA president described the file as "the first unsettled settlement in history" (Trinbago Insider Promise Tracker, 2026). In May 2026 the PSA rejected the CPO's "best and final offer" on the arrears, and the CPO gave the union four weeks to accept (Trinidad Guardian, 23–24 May 2026). The finance minister has since indicated all negotiations will be settled in Budget 2027 (Trinbago Insider, 2026) — a re-dating, recorded as such. The workers who marched on 30 October 2026 (Test 3) were marching on this file.
  • The refinery — promised reopened; at eighteen months, a shortlist. She campaigned on reopening the state refinery closed in 2018, and listed Petrotrin's reopening among her victory-night commitments (Writeups24, May 2025). The record since: a Restart Committee whose interim report of 4 December 2025 found a restart viable; a US$50 million engineering study; a March 2026 bidder list (Chevron, CNOOC, Oando, Indian Oil, Patriotic Energies); and, in the budget of 12 October 2026, her finance minister's statement that "we will restart the refinery" and that the government "is evaluating proposals from a shortlist of serious local and international investors" — with no operator selected, no final investment decision, and no restart date published (La Caribeña News, November 2026). On 6–8 November 2026 her energy minister said Cabinet could consider proposals "within days" and a formal request for proposals was expected before the end of 2026 (Hydrocarbon Processing, 9 November 2026; Apex News, 8 November 2026). The promise is alive and unkept; every date so far attached to it has been a date for a further process.
  • Dragon gas — her word, Washington's pen; recorded under the no-good-choice clause. She promised to secure Venezuelan gas for Trinidad's starved LNG and petrochemical plants. The constraint is documented and predates her: the Trump administration revoked the OFAC licences for the Dragon and Manakin-Cocuina fields in April 2025, under her predecessor, with wind-down by 27 May 2025 (US Congressional Research Service, IF12448, 2026). The licence her government obtained on 9 October 2025 — after Secretary of State Rubio signalled support to her directly — runs about six months and permits negotiation only; as her own energy minister confirmed, it "is for transactions and negotiations, not production and development" (The Oil Slick, October 2025). Venezuela then suspended the energy agreement in late October 2025; after Maduro's capture on 3 January 2026 the file revived — Shell reportedly approved by OFAC in mid-February 2026 to resume operations, a further licence expected in the third quarter of 2026, final investment decision targeted for year-end 2026 and first gas for the third quarter of 2027 (US Congressional Research Service, 2026; AInvest, 2026). The dossier records this as a promise whose keeping sits in another government's licensing office: the trap was not of her building, her pursuit of the weaker licence was the least-harm door open, and the word is graded as in motion under constraint, neither kept nor counted broken. What is hers is the framing: the October 2025 licence was announced as a restoration, when its own text allowed talk, not gas.
  • Tobago self-government — the administrative half kept, the constitutional half not. Her coalition's Tobago partner was promised self-government; what has been delivered is the THA (Amendment) Act of January 2026 (Test 4) — more secretaries, a larger quorum. The constitutional self-government bill that would grant Tobago legislative and fiscal autonomy has not been brought or passed under her government; the file's origin is also hers to carry — the previous version of that bill was defeated on 9 December 2024 when the UNC opposition she led voted against it (Trinbago Insider, 2026). Chief Secretary Augustine announced in January 2026 that Tobago would convene its own constituent assembly to draft its demand (Trinbago Insider, 2026) — the island routing around the centre, recorded as the current state of the promise.
  • The fiscal word — the deficit target her own budget set is not being met, on the IMF's count. Her FY2026 budget targeted an overall deficit of 2.2% of GDP; IMF staff, in the 2026 Article IV concluding statement of 10 February 2026, projected 5.0% of GDP for FY2026 and calculated that meeting her target would require additional measures of 2.8% of GDP (IMF, 10 February 2026). The same statement records the inherited position honestly — FY2025 deficit 5.5% of GDP, central government debt 67.8% of GDP, growth of 0.8% in 2025 — and that her budget "introduces important measures"; it also records S&P's affirmation of BBB− with outlook revised to negative (September 2025) and Moody's Ba2 maintained with outlook downgraded to negative (December 2025) (IMF, February 2026). A target announced and missed by more than double is a word file, whoever built the economy underneath it.
  • New levies under a no-new-burdens platform — contested, recorded both ways. Her platform promised relief from the cost of living; her first budget, alongside the repeals on the for-shelf, introduced a 0.25% levy on the assets of banks and insurers (from 1 January 2026, expected to raise TT$575 million a year), a landlord business surcharge of 2.5–3.5% of gross rent with a TT$2,500 registration fee, a TT$0.05 per kWh electricity surcharge on commercial and industrial users, a 5% tax on single-use plastics, doubled excise duties on alcohol and tobacco, and phased National Insurance contribution increases of three percentage points in each of January 2026 and January 2027, with the full-pension age rising gradually from 60 to 65 from 2028 (Budget statement, October 2025; Sheppard budget summary, 14 October 2025). Critics named them "stealth taxes" (TriniLulz, 2025); the government's framing is that the levies fall on banks, landlords, and luxuries to fund the relief — a framing the IMF partially endorsed in welcoming base-broadening measures (IMF, February 2026). The NIS increases carry their own crossing, named in the summary: they were not in her platform, they burden current workers, and the actuarial record — a fund the IMF and the budget both treat as unsustainable unreformed — is the documented reason they were imposed anyway.

Why the grade lands here: The promises she is most identified with split cleanly in two. The payable ones were paid: the 10% rate against her own officials' resistance, the property-tax repeal, the pension tax exemption, the fuel cut, the food VAT removal, the stand-your-ground statute, the hospital, the laptops, the Children's Life Fund — a first-year delivery list most governments in this series do not match. The structural ones have not landed: 50,000 jobs stands at a negative count on the official record, TT$3.3 billion of agreed backpay is deadlocked eight months after signature, the refinery has a shortlist instead of an operator, Tobago has secretaries instead of self-government, and her own deficit target is projected by the IMF to be missed by more than double. Dragon gas is excluded from the debit column under the no-good-choice clause — the pen is in Washington — and debited only for its framing. Kept where keeping was a decision; unkept where keeping required building. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — some 40,000 public servants on updated salaries; pensioners, by the tax exemption and the dated January 2027 increase; homeowners, by the property-tax repeal; motorists and food buyers, by the fuel and VAT measures; householders who wanted the stand-your-ground law she promised them. Hurt — the public servants again, on the other axis: the same workers are still owed TT$3.3 billion in backpay under a settlement their union calls unsettled; jobseekers — 110,000 applicants for a drive that had placed 1,801 by February 2026, and the terminated programme workers behind them; NIS contributors, paying three points more from January 2026 and three more from January 2027 toward a pension age of 65; and Tobago's self-government movement, which received administrative reform in place of the constitutional promise.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED.

Test 1 — FAILS, because the pattern at the source runs one way. A pension law backdated to 10 March 2025 to catch exactly one man — Stuart Young and his TT$87,847 a month; security details withdrawn from both living ex-prime ministers in the same weeks; a "narco-party" accusation against the opposition with no evidence ever filed; Gerald Ramdeen, whose corruption charges were discontinued without adjudication, raised to chair the National Gas Company; and 16,000–20,000 programme workers terminated on a map that exempted her partner's island without explanation. The for-shelf — Jeremie's cross-party appointment, the Brent Thomas apology, the Hilaire settlement, zero prosecutions of opponents — is genuine, and it does not outweigh a first-five-months sequence in which every discretionary incidence landed on the other side. Hurt: Young, Rowley, the PNM as an institution, the terminated workers. Helped: the treasury, and Thomas.

Test 2 — MIXED, because the referees both stood and were gripped. The Police Service Commission's nominee was approved unanimously; the Senate killed her ZOSO bill and the kill stood; the chief justiceship changed hands by the constitutional form; the DPP's office charged the government's most sensitive witness without interference. Against that: the Central Bank governor was revoked in a day, eighteen months early, with Parliament refused the reasons under Standing Order 27(1)(g); the Anti-Corruption Investigation Bureau now reports through her Attorney General; and her replacement crime act passed over all nine independent senators on a casting vote, with a pleaded — unadjudicated — claim that it hands her a veto over police operations chiefs. Hurt: Hilaire, the ACIB's independence, the independent bench's standing. Helped: the Senate's check, which was exercised and survived.

Test 3 — MIXED, because she adjusts in the open and also answers pushback by delegitimizing the pusher. The radar secret was owned, with reasons, within days; the 10% was conceded against her own ministry; the Venezuela confrontation was reversed in person in September 2026. But the ZOSO defeat drew an unparticularized bribery allegation against two unnamed senators, never withdrawn; the independents' offered amendments — a sunset clause, mandatory body cameras — were not considered, and refusing to look is what this test debits; the Samaroo killing drew a ministerial proposal to criminalize sharing the footage; and the October 2026 union march was answered as "racial and political hatred" while its TT$3.3 billion grievance went unaddressed. The emergency-powers choice itself is not debited: she inherited 626 murders and a running state of emergency, the one ordinary-law month saw homicides spike within days, and the clause grades that as the best possible choice available in the moment. Hurt: the nine senators under the cloud, the Samaroo family, the marching unions. Helped: the public told the truth about the radar; the workers whose rate she conceded.

Test 4 — MIXED, because the delivery is broad and the exceptions are policy. Tobago: funded at 6.3% of the budget, legislated for unanimously within four days of its sweep. Children, pensioners, pupils, patients, the Paria families: delivered to. The murder decline's gains accrued to the poor corridors that needed them. Counted out: Venezuelan asylum seekers, pushed back on Amnesty's documented record against the non-refoulement standard, with 200-plus detainees held for a mass deportation exercise; Chad Joseph and Rishi Samaroo of Las Cuevas, killed by a strike campaign she had endorsed in advance, whose families got navigation advice instead of a demand for the evidence; the roughly 100 preventive detainees released uncharged when the emergency lapsed; Maloney Gardens, first under the new curfew. Hurt and helped fall, in the poor districts, on many of the same households.

Test 5 — MIXED, because the payable promises were paid and the structural ones were not. Kept: the 10% rate, the property-tax repeal, tax-free private pensions, the fuel cut, food VAT removal, stand-your-ground in statute, the hospital, the laptops, the Children's Life Fund. Unkept or receding: 50,000 jobs (net negative on the CSO count; 1,801 placed of 20,000 promised by February 2026), the TT$3.3 billion backpay (deadlocked eight months after signature), the refinery (a shortlist and a promised RFP at eighteen months), Tobago self-government (administrative reform delivered, constitutional autonomy not), and her own 2.2%-of-GDP deficit target (IMF projection: 5.0%). Dragon gas is graded under constraint, not as a break: Washington revoked the licence before she took office and the replacement licence permits negotiation only.

The crossings, stated plainly, because the two axes do not launder each other. Kept her word and it helped: the 10% salary rate, the pension tax exemption, the laptops, the Couva Children's Hospital, the Children's Life Fund, the fuel and food relief. Kept her word and it still hurt: the crime promise — she said she would bring the killing down, and by the emergency method she ran on, murders fell 41% in a year; the same kept word held roughly 100 people in preventive detention without charge and suspended the country's ordinary liberties for some ten of fourteen months, and the hurt is recorded in full alongside the credit. The stand-your-ground statute is the same crossing in miniature: promised, delivered, and carrying the documented warnings of the country's human-rights bodies about lethal force in defence of property. Went against her word and it helped: the National Insurance increases — nowhere in her platform, burdensome to every contributor, and imposed on an actuarial record the IMF treats as a fund heading for exhaustion; the break is recorded on Test 5 and the rescue of future pensions is recorded here, in full, beside it. Broke her word and it hurt: the 50,000 jobs, whose first-year arithmetic is 16,000–20,000 terminations against 1,801 short-contract hires; and the backpay, promised in the same breath as the 10% and still unpaid eight months after the settlement that was supposed to settle it.

Kamla Persad-Bissessar returned to office after a decade out, with the country's worst murder year on record, a state of emergency already running, a revoked gas licence, and a deficit of 5.5% of GDP — an inheritance this dossier has been careful to price. Eighteen months on, the record shows a government that delivers what can be delivered by signature faster than almost any in this series, and reaches, at the same speed, for the instruments the Test exists to catch: a law fitted to one opponent, a referee removed in a day, an investigator moved in-house, a check answered with an accusation. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the Eversley challenge succeeds against the Special Operations Act; whether the Hilaire settlement is the end of the central bank file or its template; whether the backpay is paid in Budget 2027 as now promised; whether the refinery RFP produces an operator and a date; whether Dragon's final investment decision lands by the end of 2026; and whether the favours allegation against two unnamed senators is ever particularized, proven, or withdrawn.
