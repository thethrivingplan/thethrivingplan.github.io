The Loop Drive, stood on — a board that lifts on its own water

The
Hoverboard

A board you stand on, lifted and driven by the Loop Drive water loop. The deck tank feeds the pump, the pump feeds the Distributor, the Distributor feeds a vectorable nozzle array under the deck — and the water comes back. Your weight is the steering. The pump rides onboard. There is no tether and no jetski doing the work for you.

§1

How it lifts

Ride and glide first. Force second — same as the drive.

Lift is a jet pointed down

The pump takes water from the deck tank, the Glide Chamber shapes it into a smooth, directed stream, and the Distributor splits it across four vectorable nozzles under the deck. The jets point down. The thrust points up. The board rises on its own water.

  • All four nozzles even → the board holds level and hovers.
  • Nozzles vectored back → the board moves forward. Vectored forward → back.
  • One side up-weighted → the board banks and turns, or slides sideways without turning its nose.
  • Glide, not just blast: the Glide Chamber shapes the flow so the water does part of the work — the same principle as the Loop Drive sheet (LD-01).

What this design changes

Jet lift under a standing rider already flies — that part is not new, and this sheet does not claim it (see Fig. 2). What is new here is where the pump lives.

  • No tether. A Flyboard's pump sits in a jetski at the other end of a long hose. Here the pump rides onboard, in the deck, as the Loop Drive.
  • No second operator. Nobody on a jetski holds your throttle. Effort is in your feet and your wrist control (§3).
  • The water is worked, not spent and forgotten. In the lake form it returns to the lake it came from. In the anywhere form the skirt catches it and the same water goes around again.
FIG. 2 — THE MECHANISM UNDER THE BOARD ✓ PROVEN — NOT A GUESS

Water-jet lift — a standing rider on downward jets flies

How to read it: water forced down through nozzles under the rider's feet pushes the board — and the rider standing on it — up. Lean, and the thrust leans with you. That is the whole lift mechanism.

A pump forcing water down through nozzles under a board lifts the rider standing on it. That is proven, in public, every season:

  • JetLev (2003 onward) — the first water-jet pack: a rider lifted and flown on pumped water alone.
  • Flyboard (Franky Zapata, 2012) — boots on a board, two downward nozzles, riders hovering, diving, and flying many metres over lakes and harbours, run commercially as rentals worldwide.
  • The lift scales with flow. More water, moved faster, lifts more — the same trade every hydroflight device makes.

What is proven is the lift. What this sheet changes is the plumbing: the Flyboard's pump is a jetski, connected by a hose up to ~20 m long, with a second person (or a wireless throttle) working it. This design puts the pump onboard as the Loop Drive, closes the loop through the lake or through its own catch skirt, and puts the control in the rider's feet.

Honest label: Water-jet lift is PROVEN — JetLev and Flyboard devices fly riders today. Putting the Loop Drive onboard as the pump, and closing the loop through a catch skirt, is this design's own assembly. The mechanism is proven. This combination is the blueprint.

§2

The two forms

Same board. Same loop. Two ways to close it.

Form A — Open loop ✓ Jet lift proven

The Lake Form

An intake scoop under the board draws from the water below, and the exhaust returns to the same water — the loop closes through the lake. The deck tank is a buffer, not the whole supply: the scoop keeps it topped up as the board rides, the way the Loop Drive's intake tops up its tank.

It flies highest and longest of the two forms, because the lake is an endless tank — the limit is the battery, not the water. It rides over water only: lakes, calm harbours, wide rivers.

It needs no skirt and carries no catch weight. What goes down comes back up the scoop. The water is borrowed and returned in the same pass.

Form B — Closed loop

The Anywhere Form

The onboard tank carries the whole supply, and a wide catch skirt under the deck funnels the jetted water back to the tank, so the same water goes around — over concrete, grass, sand, or any surface that holds still under it.

Stated plainly: this form rides low. The skirt has to stay near the surface to catch — lift it high and the jets spray past the skirt, the tank drains in seconds, and the ride is over. This is a skim, not a flight: deck height is skirt height, a low hover just clear of the ground.

What it buys is the surface. No lake required. Yards, lots, flat ground — anywhere flat enough for the skirt to seal its catch.

ONE BOARD, BOTH FORMS: the skirt bolts on and off on universal-screw mounts. Skirt off + scoop open = lake form. Skirt on + scoop closed = anywhere form. Nothing else changes — same tank, same pump, same Distributor, same nozzles.

§3

Distributor & controls

The rider steers. The machine assists. The rider takes it back.

“The technology assists. It never substitutes. The person is the choice — the trigger, the lean, the call.” — The Takeover Line — printed on every Thriving Plan control
Primary control — always on

Weight shift steers the board

The rider's body is the main control, before any mode is chosen. Pressure sensors in the deck read where the rider's weight sits, and the Distributor answers it:

  • Lean forward → front nozzles ease, rear nozzles push — the board tips and travels forward.
  • Lean back → it settles, brakes, and travels back. Lean left or right → it banks and turns that way.
  • Stand centred and even → even split, level hover. The board holds still because you are holding still.
  • Crouch → the deck reads the drop and the Distributor softens lift — a controlled descent. Rise on your toes → lift firms.

No sticks to learn first. A person who can stand on a paddleboard already knows the language this board speaks.

Effort Control — Mode 1

MANUAL — foot-pressure nozzle vanes

The rider works the effort directly, with their feet. Pressure pads under the ball and heel of each foot drive the nozzle vanes — the small steering fins inside each nozzle that trim its jet.

  • Toe down → that corner's vane opens, jet firms, that corner lifts. Heel down → it eases.
  • Both feet firm → whole-board effort up — into the wind, up a rise, with a load. Both feet soft → effort down — with the wind, descending, gliding.
  • It is mechanical first: the pads move the vanes through the deck linkage, so the feel is in the foot, not in a menu.
Effort Control — Mode 2

AUTOMATED — balance assist

Sensors — tilt, height over the surface, wind, load — feed the Distributor, and it rebalances the four nozzles many times a second, the way a drone holds its hover.

  • It kills a tilt before the rider feels it — opposite corners up-weight automatically.
  • It meters effort against the medium — more into the wind, less with it, steady height over chop and slope.
  • It assists. It does not steer the trip. Where the board goes stays the rider's lean. Assist only keeps the board honest underneath them.

Instant human takeback — no menu, no delay

Both modes are fitted on every board. The rider chooses the mode with the wrist control, and switches at any time. Any firm weight shift or foot-pressure input overrides the assist immediately — the assist yields the instant the rider acts, and re-engages only when the rider's input settles. The machine assists. The person remains the decider, at any second, for any reason.

Dead-man line — fitted, always

The wrist line cuts the pump

A coiled line runs from the rider's wrist to a kill switch on the deck — the same dead-man principle as a jetski lanyard. The rider comes off, the line pulls, the pump cuts. No runaway board, no jets firing with nobody standing on them. The board settles onto the water or the ground and waits. Clip back in, press GO, ride again.

Wrist control

Effort and mode, in the hand

A small wrist unit carries three things and nothing else: the effort dial (whole-board power, for gloved or loaded riding where feet are busy), the mode switch (Manual / Automated assist), and the GO / settle button. Everything else is the rider's body. The dead-man line is anchored to this unit, so the hand that controls the board is the hand that keeps it alive.

Try the balance — lean the rider

Tap a lean direction. In Manual, your feet set the effort. In Automated, the Distributor reads the lean and rebalances — then take Manual back whenever you want.

§4

Tank, water & the soft fail

Backups on backups — and the first backup is the board itself.

The deck tank & its care

The tank lives inside the deck, low and centred, so its weight is ballast that steadies the board instead of a load that tips it. In lake form the scoop tops it up continuously; in anywhere form it is the whole supply and the level gauge is printed large on the wrist unit.

  • Filter stage on the intake and the return — grit and weed never reach the pump.
  • Dump & replace: a dump valve at the tank's lowest point, behind one universal-screw panel. Dirty water out, fresh water in, ride on.
  • Any water: fresh, salt, brackish — every wetted part is marine-grade, made to live in salt water (the Loop Drive's rule, LD-01 §4).

Nozzle guards — fitted, not optional

Every nozzle wears a guard grille, sized so fingers, toes, weed, and skirt edges cannot reach the jet core or the vane. The guards bolt on with the universal screw, come off for cleaning with the one driver, and the board does not ship — and is not ridden — without them. A guard is a working backup: it is never removed to save weight.

  • Skirt edge stays clear: in anywhere form the guards keep the catch skirt out of the jets.
  • Feet stay clear: the array sits under the deck, behind the guards — no jet ever points at the rider.
Redundancy Law — scaled to the board

Five layers stand between a failure and a fall — an odd count, on purpose

A failure has to defeat every layer before it counts as a failure. Five layers, never four: pairs can deadlock, the odd layer is the decider. And the companion rule rides with it: a backup that is known to work is never removed — not for weight, not for looks, not because a newer one exists.

LAYER 1

The deck floats. The deck is a sealed, buoyant board — pump dead over water, the board is a paddleboard, not a fall. The rider stands, sits, or paddles. This layer needs no power and cannot run out.

LAYER 2

Descent settles on the water cushion. If lift fades, the Distributor spends the remaining flow evenly, so the board comes down flat and slow onto the cushion of its own jets and the water under it — a settle, not a drop.

LAYER 3

The dead-man line cuts the pump. Rider off → line pulls → pump stops → no thrust with nobody aboard (§3).

LAYER 4

Nozzle guards hold the jets safe through every failure above — no exposed jet, no skirt or limb in the stream, in any state of the machine.

LAYER 5 — THE DECIDER

Reserve buoyancy + reserve power. A sealed reserve foam core keeps the deck afloat even swamped, and a small reserve cell runs the Distributor and the settle sequence even with the main pack flat. The last layer answers when all the pairs are spent.

Every part, five answers: what it is, what it's for, how to get it, what it costs, and why.

FIG. 3 — HOW IT'S USED: THE STANCE

Where the rider stands, which end is front

  • Front is marked in the deck itself — a raised arrow and the word FRONT moulded into the nose, readable by foot in the dark. The tail is squared; the nose is rounded. Shape and mark agree.
  • Feet go on the two pressure pads, shoulder-width, just behind the centre line, toes toward the nose. The pads are textured, raised at the heel, and coloured against the deck — stance is findable without looking down.
  • Tank hatch at the centre, between and ahead of the feet — fill and dump access without stepping off the stance.
  • Dead-man anchor at the rear centre — the wrist line clips there and never crosses the pads.
  • To trim a nozzle vane by hand: viewed from above, clockwise firms the jet, counter-clockwise eases it — the direction is moulded as an arrow on every nozzle collar: turn toward the arrow to firm.

§6

Hitting GO

So covered that just hitting go works.

  1. Pick the form. Over water: skirt off, scoop open — lake form. Over hard ground: skirt on, scoop closed — anywhere form, and keep it low.
  2. Check the tank. Lake form: let the scoop fill it. Anywhere form: fill it full at the hatch — the tank is the whole supply. Dirty? Dump and replace first (§4).
  3. Clip the dead-man line to your wrist, step onto the pads — nose forward, feet behind the centre line — and pick your mode: Manual for your feet, Automated for assist. You can switch mid-ride; your input always wins.
  4. Press GO and stand centred. The Distributor evens the split, the board rises level, and from there you ride your lean: shift to go, centre to hold, crouch to settle.
Makeshift path — separate from the top build

The proof-of-lift test rig — JUST GET THE JOB DONE

Before the top build, prove the lift on a stand. This path runs under the MAKESHIFT filter — whatever works, just get the job done — and it is deliberately not the board:

  • The rig: a fixed nozzle board — plywood or scrap deck stock, four fixed nozzles, guards fitted — bolted to a stand over a tank, tub, or the lake's edge. Nobody rides the rig. It lifts weights, not people.
  • The pump: an off-the-shelf high-flow pump — a pool, sump, or marine washdown-class pump from any hardware store or marketplace listing — plumbed straight to the nozzle board.
  • The test: sandbags in rider-weight steps on the board. It lifts the weight clear, holds it, and settles it flat when power fades — or it doesn't, and the sheet's numbers get corrected on the record before anyone stands on anything.
  • The kill cord is on the rig too — a pull-cord cut within arm's reach, from the first test. Habits built on the rig are the habits that ship on the board.

Market shape, as found November 27, 2026: off-the-shelf pump ~$80–250, nozzles and hose ~$60–120, scrap board and stand ~$0–50 — roughly $140–420 to answer the only question the rig exists to answer: does it lift?
