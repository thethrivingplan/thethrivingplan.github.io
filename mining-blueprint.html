Why? — What it is

Useful material is scattered thin through the whole crust of the earth — useless at that dilution. Mining exists because in a few lucky places the ground concentrated it, and somebody learned to find those places and free what's in them.

How? — What it means

Read the ground's advertisements, follow the deposit with the smallest hole that reaches it, and solve air, support, and water before they solve you. That sentence is the whole craft; the rest of this sheet is its unpacking.

Act. — What it could mean for you

The safe, legal small version is real and open to you: surface prospecting and rockhounding where permitted, with permission, under local rules. It is printed as a practice at the end of this sheet — rules included, not appended.

01 / FIRST IN THE DEPTH ORDER

Safety — The First Law

This section is first because in mining the order of knowledge is the order of survival. Mining has been among the deadliest work in every era it has existed — not because miners were careless, but because the underground is an environment that punishes three specific things, and it punishes them without warning.

Bad air

Underground air can quietly lose its oxygen or fill with gases you cannot see or smell — carbon dioxide pooling in low places, methane that explodes, carbon monoxide that poisons. Miners died of air that felt fine until they fell. This is why canaries went down coal mines: a small animal succumbs first, and that was the alarm.

Collapse

Every hole dug underground is a hole the ground is trying to close. Rock that stood for a million years can shift once its support is cut away — sometimes years later. Old workings are the worst: their timbers rot in the dark, invisibly, and the roof they held is still up there.

Dust in the lungs

Drilling and digging fill the air with fine rock dust. Breathed for years, silica dust scars the lungs — silicosis; coal dust does the same — black lung. It does not hurt while it happens. It collects. Modern mining law's dust limits and respirators exist because of what the dust did before them.

Abandoned mines: DON'T.

That is the whole instruction this sheet gives for entering an abandoned mine, and it is complete. Abandoned workings are documented killers — bad air pooled at the bottom, rotted supports, hidden vertical shafts under rotten floors, in ground nobody has inspected in decades. There are hundreds of thousands of abandoned mine openings in the United States alone, and people die in them every year, usually within sight of the entrance. No ore, no photograph, no adventure is down there that is worth it. Look from outside. Leave.

Real mining is licensed, engineered work. A working mine has surveyed plans, tested air, engineered supports, drainage, rescue arrangements, and law standing behind all of it. That is not bureaucracy on top of the craft — that is the craft, the part that lets miners come home. This sheet teaches the knowledge around mining: how deposits are found, why mines are shaped as they are, what problems every miner in history had to solve. It does not teach anyone to dig a shaft, and nothing on it should be read that way.

02 / THE UNLOCK

What Mining Is — and What It Opened

Mining, in one line: finding concentrated useful material in the ground, and freeing it. Both halves matter. A deposit you cannot find is geology; a deposit you cannot free is a rumor. And the first thing ever mined was not a metal at all.

STONE AGE · ~4,500+ YEARS AGO

Flint first

Flint — the stone that takes a razor edge — was mined before metal existed. At places like Grime's Graves in England, Stone Age miners sank shafts through chalk and followed seams of high-quality flint underground, by lamplight, with antler picks. Mining begins as a stone technology.

→
THE COPPER & BRONZE AGES

Copper, then tin

Copper minerals are the showiest in the ground — green and blue stains that advertise themselves. Mine copper and tin, smelt them together, and you get bronze: the first alloy, and the first age named for a metal. Two mine mouths feed it — copper and tin rarely live in the same place.

→
EVERY ERA

Gold

Gold barely reacts with anything, so it survives in the ground as itself — flakes and nuggets washed out of veins and settled in stream gravels. It was gathered and mined early not because it was useful for tools, but because it was beautiful, rare, and unchanging. It taught prospectors to read streams.

→
THE IRON AGE → NOW

Iron — the common one

Iron ore is everywhere compared to copper and tin, and rust-red rocks had been used as pigment for ages. What was missing was the hotter fire to free it. Once smelting caught up, the cheapest, toughest metal became the backbone of tools, rails, ships, and frames. The mine mouth was ready long before the furnace was.

The pattern to carry: every metal age on the tech tree starts at a mine mouth. The furnace gets the glory, but the furnace eats what the mine provides. This sheet ends where the miner's job ends — at the freed ore. What fire does to it next is the Bronze Working and Iron Working sheets, one door over on the tree.

03 / READING THE GROUND

Prospecting — Ores Advertise

Before anyone digs, someone looks. Prospecting is the reading half of mining, and its first principle is friendly: ores advertise. The minerals people want are mostly not the gray-brown of ordinary rock — they announce themselves in color, in weight, and in where water has carried them.

Documented

Color — the green stain

Copper minerals wear bright colors no common rock copies. Malachite is green — a green stain on rock in copper country is copper announcing itself. Azurite is deep blue. Iron rusts red and orange, and a rusty, iron-stained cap of rock — prospectors called it a gossan — often sits on top of richer sulfide ore below, like a sign over a shop.

Gold needs no color of its own: it is the yellow that does not tarnish, in a pan or a stream bed, still bright when everything around it has dulled.

Documented

Weight — the heft test

Metal ores are dense — noticeably heavier in the hand than the same size of ordinary rock. Prospectors have always hefted stones: a dull gray rock that feels wrong, too heavy, is worth a second look. Metal itself is heavier still, which is exactly why running water sorts it for free — see the streams, next.

The same density is the whole secret of the gold pan: swirl the gravel, the light rock washes away, and the heavy grains stay. The pan is a tiny mine that works by weight alone.

Documented

Streams — placer deposits

Rain and ice break rock; streams carry the pieces downhill and sort them by weight as they go. Dense gold flakes and nuggets drop out where the water slows — inside bends, behind boulders, in cracks in the bedrock — and concentrate there over centuries. That is a placer deposit: the stream did the mining's first shift. Finding placer gold in a stream also points uphill: somewhere above, the vein it weathered out of may still be in the rock.

Documented

Reading the ground, in plain words

Walk slowly and look at what the ground is already showing: fresh rock in road cuts and stream banks, the float — loose pieces — scattered downhill from where they broke off, and follow the float uphill to its source. Note where colors change, where rock type changes, where the heavy stones gather. Old prospectors worked exactly this way, and modern exploration still starts with boots and eyes before it brings in instruments.

One limit, printed plainly: a pretty rock is a clue, not a claim. What a deposit is worth takes sampling, assaying — laboratory testing — and law. Prospecting finds the advertisement; it doesn't print the price.

04 / FOLLOW THE DEPOSIT

Extraction Shapes — Surface, Pit, Shaft

Once a deposit is found, its shape and depth choose the mine. There is no style contest here: you follow the deposit, with the smallest opening that reaches it — because every extra ton of worthless rock you move is cost, time, and danger. Tap each shape to see it in the ground.

The three shapes are one idea at three depths. The deposit near the surface gets scraped and gathered. The deposit in a shallow body gets a pit — a stepped bowl dug down into it. The deposit running deep in a vein gets a shaft: a vertical hole to reach its level, then tunnels that follow the vein itself. Deeper always means the three problems of the next section get bigger — which is why nobody goes deep for fun or for free.

05 / EVERY MINE, EVERY ERA

Support, Air, Water — The Three Problems

Strip away the century and the machinery, and every underground mine in history had to solve the same three problems. They are the reason mining is engineered work — and understanding them is understanding what a mine actually is.

Documented

Support — hold the ground up

A tunnel is a space the rock wants to close. The classic answer is timbering: wooden posts and beams set as frames that carry the roof and walls, replaced and extended as the working advances. Timber also talks — it creaks and cracks under load before it fails, an audible warning miners learned to listen for. Modern mines use steel, bolts anchored into the rock, and sprayed concrete, but the job is unchanged since the first propped shaft: the opening must be held, deliberately, or the ground takes it back.

Documented

Air — keep it moving

Still underground air goes bad — oxygen used up, gases seeping in. The fix is ventilation: air must be made to flow through the workings in a planned circuit, fresh air in to the faces where people work, stale air out by a separate route. Old mines coaxed a draft with a furnace at the bottom of an upcast shaft — hot air rises, pulling fresh air in behind it. Modern mines use fans and airtight stoppings, and the air is tested, not trusted. The principle never changed: if the air isn't moving, nobody works.

Documented

Water — the third problem

Dig below the water table and the ground weeps into your workings — and floods them if you let it. Where the land allows, a sloped drainage tunnel (an adit) lets water run out by gravity. Where it doesn't, the water must be lifted: buckets, chain pumps, water-powered pumps — and then, in 1712, the Newcomen steam engine, built for exactly this job. The pump's long story starts at the mine mouth: steam power itself was born draining mines, and only later moved the world.

06 / THE SMALL, LEGAL VERSION

Starter Practice — Prospecting on the Surface

There is a real, safe, legal small version of this technology, and it is genuinely the first rung of the same ladder the old prospectors climbed: surface prospecting and rockhounding — looking, reading, and collecting loose material on the surface, where it is permitted. The permission and the rules are not a footnote to the practice. They are part of the practice, printed here at the same size.

  1. Pick ground you are allowed to be on

    Public land agencies (in the US, the Bureau of Land Management and the Forest Service) publish where recreational rockhounding is allowed; many state parks allow it and many do not; national parks generally prohibit collecting. Private land means the owner's permission, asked and given, before you pick up a single stone. When in doubt, ask the land manager — that's a rule of the craft, not a courtesy.

  2. Check for existing claims and local rules first

    Some ground that looks empty is somebody's active mining claim — collecting there is theft, and claim markers and agency records tell you where claims stand. Local rules also set what you may take: typically small amounts for personal use, hand tools only, no digging into banks or workings. Read the rules for the specific place before you go; they differ, and "I didn't know" has never been a defense anywhere.

  3. Work the surface only — eyes, feet, and a pan

    Walk stream beds and gravel bars. Heft the stones. Look for the advertisements: green and blue stains, rusty caps, rock that feels too heavy, bright flakes that don't tarnish. Pan gravel where panning is permitted and watch the heavy grains settle — you are running the oldest sorting machine in mining. Collect loose surface material only, within the local limits.

  4. Obey the first law while you do it

    Abandoned mines and old workings: don't — look from outside, leave, exactly as Section 01 says. Stay out of pits with unstable edges, tell someone where you're going, carry water, watch the weather in dry washes (flash floods are the surface's version of bad air). Fill in any small hole you were permitted to make, and leave the ground as you found it.

  5. Know where your ladder ends — for now

    What you find is a specimen and a story, not a mine. Selling, staking claims, digging, and anything underground are different legal worlds with their own doors. The next rung on this tree is different anyway: take your prettiest copper-stained rock home and look at it — the question it asks is what fire would do to it. That question is the Bronze Working sheet.

The next door

This sheet ends at the ore — honestly

Ore in a pile is potential, not metal. Turning it into copper, bronze, or iron takes fire, fuel, and a furnace — smelting — and that is its own technology with its own sheets: Bronze Working and Iron Working, elsewhere on the Tech Tree. Nothing here skips ahead and pretends the ore is already a tool. One door at a time, each one honest about where it ends.
