The Ruler's Test — Ismaïl Omar Guelleh, President of Djibouti

Subject: Ismaïl Omar Guelleh — President of Djibouti since 8 May 1999, now in a sixth term. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: in Djibouti the president is the executive. He appoints and dismisses the prime minister (Abdoulkader Kamil Mohamed, in post since 2013) and the government, commands the armed forces, and presides over a system in which, in the US State Department's words, "formal structures of representative government and electoral processes had little relevance to the real distribution and exercise of power." The holder named in the original brief is verified as still holding the seat in November 2026 — but the tenure has moved since the brief was written, and the movement is itself evidence. Guelleh, born 27 November 1947, was barred from the April 2026 election by the constitution's age limit of 75 — a limit he himself had inserted in 2010, when he abolished term limits. On 26 October 2025 the National Assembly voted 65–0 to remove the age limit; a second unanimous vote ratified it on 2 November; he promulgated it on 6 November and declared his candidacy on 8 November. The same revision removed the referendum requirement for adopting a future constitution and added a requirement that presidential candidates have resided continuously in the country for five years. On 10 April 2026 he won a sixth term: the Interior Ministry announced 97.8% against 2.2% for the sole challenger, Mohamed Farah Samatar of the small Unified Democratic Centre; the Constitutional Council confirmed a slightly different count — 97.01% to 2.99%, on 80.33% turnout. The two official figures disagree with each other, and the main opposition parties boycotted the poll, as they have boycotted every election since 2016. He was inaugurated on 9 May 2026 and named a new cabinet on 17 May. He is graded on the full tenure since 1999, weighted to the last decade where the record is densest, because the seat's defining acts span the whole period. Context that frames everything below: Djibouti has had two presidents since independence in 1977 — Hassan Gouled Aptidon and his nephew Guelleh — both of the Issa community, and the country of roughly 1.1 million people hosts the military bases of five foreign powers (the United States, France, China, Japan, Italy) on the Bab el-Mandeb strait. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic this Test applies is printed here in full: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one — Guelleh's 2026 platform was continuity, stability, and the Vision 2035 development program, and delivering continuity is credited where it is due, above all on Test 5. What is graded is the holding: whether the law is one law, whether the referees are gripped, whether everyone is counted, and whether the word survives contact with his own interest. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as two independent axes; neither launders the other, and the crossings are named where they occur.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The written law is even on its face. Djibouti's constitution and statutes provide for the equal treatment of all citizens; no statutory tier assigns rights by clan, party, or proximity to the palace (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2021). The second law documented below operates in the law's incidence, not its text — which is why this test grades incidence.
  • The one challenger permitted in 2026, Mohamed Farah Samatar, was registered, campaigned openly, and had his votes counted and published by both the Interior Ministry and the Constitutional Council (April 2026 results). A token opposition is not a level field, but the candidacy itself was not extinguished.
  • The state has repeatedly carried its largest disputes into open foreign courts rather than settling them by pure decree: it litigated against Abdourahman Boreh in London for years and against DP World before the London Court of International Arbitration, in public, under rules it did not control. What those courts found is on the against shelf; that the process ran at all is recorded here.
  • The outcome measure is stagnant rather than collapsed: Djibouti scores 31 of 100 on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index in both the 2024 and 2025 indexes (127th, then 124th of 180) — poor, and down from 36 in 2012, but not the region's floor (Transparency International, CPI 2024 and CPI 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The eligibility law has been rewritten twice, and both times the variable it solved for was one man's biography. In 2010, near the end of his second term, parliament abolished term limits, shortened terms from six years to five, and set a candidate age band of 40–75 (Congressional Research Service, February 2025). In 2025 the band's upper edge — the one provision now standing between Guelleh and another term — was removed, unanimously, twice in seven days, by the parliamentary route he chose over the referendum route the constitution also offered (International IDEA, October 2025; ConstitutionNet, 2025). A law that is re-cut each time it reaches the ruler is not one law; it is a wardrobe.
  • The same 2025 revision added a five-year continuous-residency requirement for presidential candidates — a rule whose incidence falls on exactly one class of person: the exiled opposition. Alexis Mohamed, a former presidential adviser and prospective candidate, told journalists he refrained from running because he could not obtain security guarantees for returning from exile (Africa Yearbook 2025, Brill). The head of the Djiboutian League for Human Rights called the package preparation for a presidency for life (reporting of the October–November 2025 revision).
  • The Boreh file is the one-law test in a single case, because it was fully litigated in a court the seat does not control. Abdourahman Boreh — former head of the tax authority, the businessman who had negotiated the Doraleh port deals, and by 2009 a fallen insider turned critic — was convicted in absentia in Djibouti in 2010 of terrorism and sentenced to 15 years, on the basis of intercepted-call transcripts that were wrongly dated: the calls preceded the grenade attack they were said to discuss. When Djibouti pursued his assets in London, the High Court discharged the freezing order after finding the court had been deliberately misled by the Republic's solicitor ([2015] EWHC 769 (Comm)); the solicitor was later struck off after the Solicitors Disciplinary Tribunal found he had deliberately misled the court. On 2 March 2016, Mr Justice Flaux dismissed the Republic's claims in their entirety, rejected every allegation of bribery and corruption against Boreh, and found that the proceedings formed part of a politically motivated campaign against him "orchestrated by the Government of Djibouti and by the President personally"; the judgment records that some of the Republic's witnesses, including the President, gave untruthful evidence, and Djibouti was ordered to pay costs on the indemnity basis ([2016] EWHC 405 (Comm)). These are findings of a court, not allegations. No figure who remained in the President's favor has faced a comparable process.
  • The law's incidence tracks alignment across the ordinary run of cases. Human Rights Watch documented the arrest of at least 15 opposition figures between 2020 and 2024 (Human Rights Watch reporting, summarized 2025). In January 2016, Omar Ali Ewado, a leader of the Djiboutian League for Human Rights, was convicted of inciting hatred and spreading false news after publishing the names of people allegedly killed by police at Buldhuqo the previous month (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2017). The same month, Kadar Abdi Ibrahim, co-director of the opposition newspaper Aurore, was convicted and his paper suspended after it published a photograph of a seven-year-old girl killed in the same dispersal (Alkarama Annual Report 2016). Journalists covering the detention of air force lieutenant Fouad Youssouf Ali in 2020 were arrested or forced into hiding (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2021).
  • Opacity sits where the money is newest. The Djibouti Sovereign Fund, created in 2020 as the state's flagship vehicle for attracting outside financing, was liquidated in April 2025, in circumstances Coface's country analysis ties to corruption allegations and a fragile business climate; no public accounting of the fund's assets appears in the record assembled here (Coface, Djibouti Country File, 2025–2026).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the strongest for-shelf items describe the law's text and its tolerances at the margin, while the against shelf describes its operation at the center: eligibility rules re-cut for the incumbent twice, a residency rule whose only real targets are exiles, and a foreign court's finding — after full litigation — that the state's legal machinery was run as a personal campaign against a man who had fallen out, with the President's own evidence found untruthful. One law for the connected, another for the fallen and the opposed, is the documented pattern.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the ruling network: the RPP/UMP circle, the presidential family's political household, and aligned commercial partners (among them China Merchants, which received the Doraleh container operation after DP World's expulsion). Hurt — Abdourahman Boreh (convicted in absentia at home, vindicated abroad, exiled); opposition figures arrested across 2020–2024; the rights defender and the editor convicted for publishing the names and the face of the Buldhuqo dead; exiled would-be candidates barred by the residency rule; and any ordinary defendant whose case touches the state's interest.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The forms of legality are observed even when the substance serves the incumbent. The 2025 revision was carried through the constitution's own amendment procedure — two Assembly votes — rather than imposed by decree (International IDEA, October 2025). He grips through the referees' forms more often than around them; the distinction matters and is recorded.
  • Referees have been let into the room at least once at full strength: the February 2013 legislative elections — the first the opposition contested in a decade — were observed by some 60 observers from the African Union, the Arab League, the United Nations, and the European Union, who declared them free and fair (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2014). The opposition disputed the count and the conditions, and that dispute is recorded on the against shelf of Test 3; the admission of the observers is a fact of this shelf.
  • The Constitutional Council's confirmed 2026 result (97.01%) differed from the Interior Ministry's announced result (97.8%), and both figures stand in the public record (Constitutional Council figures, 21 April 2026, as reported). A small divergence — but a divergence a fully scripted count would not have needed.
  • The National Human Rights Commission (CNDH) exists, receives complaints, and did investigate the deaths of August 2021 — though its conclusion, that law enforcement did not cause the deaths, is contested by civil society (US Department of State, 2021 Country Report on Human Rights Practices: Djibouti).
  • Foreign referees have ruled against his government repeatedly — the London High Court in 2015 and 2016, LCIA tribunals from 2018 through the final award of 29 September 2025 — and the state kept appearing in those forums rather than withdrawing from them. Its defiance of the outcomes is graded on Tests 1 and 5; its submission to the processes is a fact of this shelf.

Evidence against:

  • Parliament is not a check; it is an instrument. The UMP coalition took 58 of 65 seats in the February 2023 legislative elections, which most opposition parties boycotted, leaving zero opposition seats (ARIES/Strategic Intelligence Report, May 2026; ConstitutionNet, 2025). When the President's personal eligibility was before it in 2025, the Assembly voted 65–0, twice, in seven days.
  • The electoral referee was never rebuilt, though he signed for its rebuilding. The December 2014 framework agreement with the USN opposition promised democratic reforms, above all reform of the Independent National Electoral Commission (CENI); no CENI reform was undertaken before the 2016 elections, or since (Freedom House, via Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada research, 2019). Opposition parties cite the commission's partiality as a standing reason for the boycotts that empty every election (2021, 2023, 2026).
  • The judiciary does not constrain the seat. The US State Department's human rights reporting records politically motivated prosecutions as common and due process as systematically denied; V-Dem's judicial-constraints-on-the-executive index for Djibouti stands at 0.268 of 1 (V-Dem, via Country Index 2026). The Boreh litigation showed the domestic courts' other face: a 15-year in-absentia terrorism conviction built on transcripts misdated in the direction of guilt ([2016] EWHC 405 (Comm)).
  • The media referee is owned or exiled. Reporters Without Borders ranks Djibouti 168th of 180 in the 2025 World Press Freedom Index. The state owns the national broadcaster and the principal newspaper; the only independent outlet, La Voix de Djibouti, operates from exile in Europe, and its in-country journalists were arrested or driven into hiding in 2020 for covering the Fouad Youssouf Ali case (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2021). A BBC team was detained and deported in April 2016 after covering the Buldhuqo killings and the election (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2017).
  • The central bank's till is within the government's reach. The IMF's January 2026 mission recommended establishing a formal plan for the Treasury to settle its outstanding overdrafts at the Central Bank of Djibouti, and noted that gross reserves, though rising, remain below the requirements of the currency board itself (IMF, Press Release No. 26/005, 15 January 2026). A referee whose account the player overdraws is not holding the whistle.
  • The 2025 revision reached past the referees to the rulebook's own lock: it removed the requirement that a future constitution be adopted by referendum, so the next rewriting of the rules needs no one's vote outside the Assembly he controls (ConstitutionNet, 2025).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because every institution that could say no to the seat has been seated, staffed, starved, or rewritten by it: a parliament without a single opposition member voting unanimously for his eligibility; an electoral commission whose promised reform never came; a judiciary that foreign courts have caught convicting on misdated evidence; a press reduced to state outlets and an exile station; a central bank carrying Treasury overdrafts. The for-shelf items are real — procedure observed, observers once admitted, a two-point divergence in an official count — and they describe a grip that prefers legal dress, not a grip loosened.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the presidency itself, and every officeholder whose tenure depends on the referees staying seated. Hurt — voters in boycotted elections whose referee was never rebuilt; defendants and detainees before courts that do not constrain the executive; journalists inside the country; and the reading public of a state whose only independent newsroom broadcasts from abroad.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, when another option became available, he at least considered it openly: considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest domestic wobble of his tenure was answered with negotiation. After the disputed February 2013 legislative elections, the USN opposition refused its ten seats, built a shadow parliament, and sustained eighteen months of standoff and protest, during which more than 500 opponents were arrested (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2015). On 31 December 2014 the two sides signed a framework agreement; USN deputies took their seats in January 2015 — the first opposition presence in the legislature since multiparty politics began in 1992. The reform half of that bargain later failed, and the failure is graded on Test 5; the adjustment itself — a president who had never shared the building choosing to seat his opponents — happened, in the open.
  • The Ethiopia sea-access crisis was answered with escalating, public offers rather than threats. When Ethiopia's January 2024 memorandum with Somaliland put Djibouti's lifeline — over 90% of Ethiopian trade transits Djibouti's ports — under strategic threat, his government offered Addis Ababa "100 percent management" of the Port of Tadjoura with its new corridor (Foreign Minister Mahamoud Ali Youssouf to the BBC and VOA, August–September 2024). Djiboutian officials later disclosed that the offer had grown to a 60% equity stake in Tadjoura (Ports and Free Zones Authority chairman, April 2026). When Ethiopia sought an extraterritorial corridor and a naval base instead, Djibouti refused — openly, with sovereignty as the stated reason (reporting, September 2026). Each option was considered in public; the decline came with reasons attached.
  • An event-wobble was answered with administration. In 2020, COVID-19 was met with border controls, mass testing, and contact tracing that curbed the first wave; Djibouti had reported 61 deaths by year's end (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2021).
  • Regionally, pushback is routinely answered with diplomacy rather than force: Djibouti positioned itself as mediator in the Ethiopia–Somalia confrontation its own offer was designed to defuse, and his foreign minister's election as Chairperson of the African Union Commission in February 2025 marked the region's acceptance of that posture (African Union, February 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Buldhuqo, 21 December 2015. Security forces dispersed a religious commemoration in the Balbala suburb; the death toll is contested and has never been settled by an accounting both sides could check — the government stated that seven civilians died after armed individuals attacked the police (Foreign Minister's statement, December 2015); the opposition stated at least 19 were killed at a religious gathering (USN spokesman to Reuters, December 2015); CIVICUS puts the range at 19 to 37 dead. The documented sequel is a prosecutor's investigation with no accountability outcome in the record, and the conviction of the rights defender who published the alleged victims' names (Test 1). The wobble was answered with a counter-narrative and a prosecution of the messenger.
  • The Fouad Youssouf Ali file, 2020. A serving air force lieutenant fled to Ethiopia alleging corruption and clan discrimination, was deported back, and was charged with treason without a lawyer present; a video from his cell in Gabode prison alleging degrading treatment sparked protests in Djibouti City, Balbala, and Ali Sabieh (Human Rights Watch, 2020). His allegations of mistreatment are recorded here as ALLEGATIONs — he made them, Human Rights Watch called for an impartial investigation, and no investigation outcome appears in the documented record. What is documented is the answer the street received: police used excessive force, including live ammunition, against the June–July 2020 demonstrations, and journalists covering the case were arrested or forced into hiding (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2021). Fouad was sentenced to ten years in prison in 2021 (Genocide Watch country report, March 2026).
  • August 2021. When Afar–Issa violence in Ethiopia spilled into the capital, five people died in a week of unrest; civil society actors blamed police live ammunition for at least some of the deaths; the state's own human rights commission investigated and did not conclude that law enforcement caused them; the African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights issued a public call over the deaths (US Department of State, 2021 Country Report; ACHPR press release, 11 August 2021). No independent accounting followed.
  • The succession wobble, 2025 — the clearest refusal-to-look in the file. The pushback was his own repeated word (Test 5): the handover he had promised, with his party nominating a successor, was an option that had become available in the most literal sense — it was the standing plan he himself had announced. There is no record of him weighing it openly. The documented sequence is speed, not consideration: amendment adopted 26 October, ratified 2 November, promulgated 6 November, candidacy declared 8 November. Rights groups' objection — that this prepared a presidency for life — was not answered with reasons; it was answered with a timetable. Under this test's rule, considering the handover and declining it with stated reasons would not have counted against him. Refusing to look at it does.
  • The no-good-choice clause, applied — the Ethiopia sea-access file, 2024–2026. The record shows a decision in which every available option carried real harm, and it is graded here as the best possible choice available, given the situation in the moment; it is not held against him on any test. The options that actually existed: (1) grant Ethiopia an extraterritorial corridor and naval base — ceding sovereign control of coastline and unbalancing the five-power base arrangement on which Djibouti's security and rents rest; (2) refuse Ethiopia any expanded access — pushing a landlocked state of over 120 million people, the source of the overwhelming majority of Djibouti's port volume, toward Berbera, toward confrontation with Somalia, or toward coercion of Djibouti itself; (3) expand commercial access — full management, then a majority equity stake in Tadjoura — while refusing the sovereignty transfer. He took the third door, in the open, and stated his reasons. What remains gradable around the clause is graded: he did not build this trap (Ethiopia's landlocked geography and the Somaliland memorandum did), he was honest about the offers while inside it, and the door he took was the least-harm one actually open. The clause is expressly not applied to the succession file above: there, another option demonstrably existed — the one he had promised — so the constraint the clause exists to record is absent.

Why: The grade lands on MIXED because the record splits cleanly by direction. Facing outward — a disputed election settled by seating the opposition, a giant neighbor's sea-access demand answered with bigger and bigger public offers and a reasoned refusal at the sovereignty line, a pandemic administered — he adjusts, openly and sometimes generously. Facing inward — a massacre's contested toll, a whistleblowing officer's cell video, his own promised handover — the answer is counter-narrative, live ammunition, prosecution, and speed. The wobble test cannot average those into a pass; it records both, and the inward half is why the grade is not higher.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Ethiopian trade and Djibouti's port economy (kept whole through the sea-access crisis); the USN deputies seated in 2015; the public of the 2020 first wave. Hurt — the families of Buldhuqo, whose dead are still counted three different ways; Fouad Youssouf Ali (ten years) and the protesters and journalists of June–July 2020; the dead of August 2021 and their families, whose official accounting they dispute; and the electorate of 2026, whose promised succession was never even publicly weighed.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Afar and the other minorities, the regions beyond the capital, the young, the poor, refugees and migrants — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The refugee file is the strongest counting evidence in this dossier, and it is genuinely strong. The National Refugee Law promulgated by Guelleh on 5 January 2017 gives refugees official identification, the right to work, access to the justice system and to education and health services, and a path to naturalization (UNHCR, 2017). The open door behind the law has held through three decades of other people's wars: Djibouti hosts over 30,000 refugees and asylum seekers — Somalis, Ethiopians, Yemenis, Eritreans — in a population of about 1.1 million (Congressional Research Service, February 2025), and in September 2026 UNHCR recorded some 2,400 new Yemeni arrivals in a single week being received as the war across the strait escalated. These are people who will never vote for him, counted on paper and in practice.
  • Poverty is falling on the World Bank's measures: the share of Djiboutians living below $3.00 a day fell from 25.4% in 2017 to an estimated 19.3% in 2025, and the share below $4.20 a day from 43.7% to 33.8% over the same period (World Bank, Macro Poverty Outlook, October 2025).
  • The former rebels are inside the count. The FRUD — the Afar-led movement that fought the 1991–2001 civil war against this state — sits within the ruling UMP coalition (ConstitutionNet, 2025), and minority groups are represented at all levels of government (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2021).

Evidence against:

  • Representation stops below the paramount positions, and the line is ethnic. Freedom House records that "the president's majority Issa group holds paramount positions in the ruling party, the civil service, and the security forces," and the US State Department records that minority communities "suffer from discrimination that contributes to their social and economic marginalization." Afar grievances from the civil war — political exclusion, land access, representation — remain "largely unresolved" a quarter-century after the peace (Genocide Watch country report, March 2026).
  • The poverty map is the exclusion map. In the last full national survey (2017), extreme poverty in the regions ran at 45.0% against 13.6% in the capital, and 62.6% in rural areas (World Bank, Macro Poverty Outlook 2018). The growth model is headquartered in Djibouti City's port enclave; the regions the Afar predominate in are the ones the model reaches last.
  • The growth does not reach the young. Unemployment is measured at 25.9% (2024, Coface) to 36.4% (first quarter 2025, World Bank) depending on the instrument, and above 70% for young people (Coface), alongside headline growth of about 6.5% (IMF, January 2026). Coface's summary of the model is that growth "remains largely exclusive": capital-intensive port and free-zone projects generate few jobs. A generation is counted in the GDP and out of the payroll.
  • August 2021 showed the counting failing at the level of the dead: five people died in a week of Afar–Issa unrest in the capital; civil society blamed police live fire for at least some deaths; the state's commission cleared the police; residents told AFP more than a dozen had been killed, against the prosecutor's three (US Department of State, 2021 Country Report; AFP reporting, August 2021). The toll is contested because the count was never made in a way both communities could trust.
  • The people passing through are not protected. The US State Department ranked Djibouti Tier 3 — its lowest — in the 2024 Trafficking in Persons Report (Congressional Research Service, February 2025): migrants transiting Djibouti toward Yemen, among the most exposed people on earth, are failed by the state they pass through, on the state's own ally's assessment.
  • The aggregate after 27 years: Djibouti ranks 175th of 193 countries on the UN Development Programme's Human Development Index (UNDP, via Congressional Research Service, February 2025), despite a generation of growth rates most governments would envy.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the counting that works — refugees, headline poverty reduction — runs alongside a domestic accounting in which the Afar are represented everywhere except where power sits, the regions carry two to four times the capital's poverty, the young are unemployed at rates above 70% inside a booming port economy, and the dead of the capital's worst communal week were never counted to both communities' satisfaction. The mandate rule is applied here as written: he is not graded down for the port-and-bases model he was elected on; he is graded on the model's incidence, and its incidence counts the enclave in and the periphery out.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the more than 30,000 refugees and asylum seekers sheltered under the 2017 law, including the Yemenis received in 2026; port, logistics, and free-zone workers and the capital's connected households; the FRUD's political class, brought inside the coalition. Hurt — Afar communities, structurally; the rural regions (62.6% extreme poverty at last full survey); young Djiboutians, unemployed at over 70%; trafficking victims and transit migrants on the Yemen route; and the families of August 2021, still holding a contested count.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Platform and program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 1999, with dates; the two axes — his word, and who was helped or hurt — stated independently and crossed where they cross.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The hub program — built. Vision Djibouti 2035 promised to make the country the logistics and commercial hub of the region, and the physical program was delivered on his watch: the Addis Ababa–Djibouti electrified railway entered commercial service in 2018, the Doraleh Multipurpose Port opened in 2017, the Port of Tadjoura opened in 2017, and the Djibouti International Free Trade Zone — billed as Africa's largest — was inaugurated in 2018 (UNDP country reporting; World Bank reporting). The growth the program promised has been real: about 6.5% in both 2024 and 2025 (IMF, January 2026). Under the mandate rule this shelf is credited in full: it is the platform, carried out.
  • His word to the base-hosting powers — kept for decades. Five foreign militaries operate in Djibouti under agreements his government has honored without interruption; base rents exceed $125 million a year (Congressional Research Service, February 2025). Whatever that reliability purchases politically (see the crossings), the signature itself has been good.
  • The creditor word — partially repaired in the open. After external arrears reached about 6% of GDP in 2023, his government negotiated a reprofiling with China Eximbank — its largest creditor — including a four-year moratorium on the rail and water loans, and then consolidated: the fiscal deficit narrowed to about 0.7% of GDP in 2025, with a balanced budget adopted for 2026 (IMF, January 2026). The IMF still judges debt sustainability unrestored and urges the negotiations be finalized; the direction of the keeping is documented.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The last-term assurances — five of them, in sequence, each broken. In 2005 he was sworn in for a second term he said would be his last; he ran in 2011. The 2011 re-election was, in the Congressional Research Service's words, one he had vowed would be his "third and final time"; after it, he announced he would not seek a fourth term — and in December 2015 declared he would run in 2016 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2016). The 2021 fifth term was announced as his last. To the BBC in 2022 he said, of running in 2026: "No, no, no, three times no. You can remember that, I am over the age limit and I have to hand over" (BBC interview, 2022, via ConstitutionNet). As late as February 2025, the Congressional Research Service recorded that he had rejected the prospect of further constitutional changes. In October–November 2025 the constitution was changed — for his eligibility, by his Assembly, without the referendum he could have called — and in April 2026 he took a sixth term. The 2025 break is the most documented kind this test grades: the rule he broke was one he had written himself, and his own stated reason for obeying it ("I am over the age limit") was removed rather than answered.
  • The December 2014 framework agreement — the cheap half kept, the refereeing half not. The agreement that seated the USN opposition promised democratic reforms, above all reform of the electoral commission. The seats were delivered in January 2015; the CENI reform was not undertaken before the 2016 elections and has not been undertaken since, and the opposition spent 2015 protesting that the government was neglecting the deal's key commitments (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2016; Immigration and Refugee Board of Canada, 2019).
  • The state's word to DP World — a 50-year concession, terminated by decree, awards unpaid. Djibouti granted the Doraleh Container Terminal concession in 2006 and seized the terminal in February 2018, expelling DP World's staff. LCIA tribunals ruled the seizure unlawful repeatedly; awards totaling roughly $685 million stood unpaid when the final LCIA award issued on 29 September 2025 — which again confirmed the seizure was unlawful, while placing liability on the government rather than its port company, and dismissing the claim against the port company (LCIA award, 29 September 2025; both parties' statements). Djibouti then applied to its own high court to have the international adjudications ruled null and void (Port Strategy, 2025). The government's position is recorded in full: it says the concession was corruptly obtained and the seizure an act of sovereignty, and it hailed the September 2025 award as a victory because the claim against the port company failed. The bribery claim against DP World is an ALLEGATION that no tribunal in this record has upheld; the unlawfulness of the seizure is a finding several tribunals have made.

The four crossings, named: 1. Kept his word AND it helped people: the Vision 2035 build-out and the honored base agreements — infrastructure delivered, rents banked, poverty falling on the World Bank's measures. 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt people: the stability word. He has promised order and continuity for twenty-seven years and kept the promise; the keeping is itself the shield under which the domestic grip operates — his opponents' contention, recorded by the Congressional Research Service, is that the foreign bases have "shielded the president from greater Western criticism of abuses, allowing Guelleh to tighten his hold on power." The Doraleh stance is the same shape in miniature: he said the terminal would not be handed back, and it has not been — a word kept, at the price of roughly $685 million in unpaid awards carried on the state's books. 3.
