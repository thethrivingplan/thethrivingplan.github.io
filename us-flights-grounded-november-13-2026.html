The stop itself — what happened, and the timeline

FAA Air Traffic Control System Command Center Advisory 111, “NATIONAL GROUND STOP” — signed 3:54 p.m. ET, Nov. 13, 2026: all U.S. airports ground-stopped, all departing traffic held “until further notice,” inbound international flights handled case-by-case. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy publicly confirmed and announced it. The timeline runs from the Chicago-only stops about 40 minutes earlier — O’Hare at 3:10 p.m., Midway at 3:17 p.m., both for “EQUIPMENT / OUTAGE — COMMS ISSUES” — through the blanket phase (3:54–5:46 p.m.) and the phased release, to the formal cancellation at 8:19 p.m. ET: a total formal duration of 4 hours 25 minutes.

The workings, underneath

What a ground stop actually means: it holds departures. Aircraft already airborne were not ordered down — they continued and landed. More than 3,500 airplanes were flying when the stop was issued (Duffy / USA Today); by 8 p.m. ET fewer than 400 were in the sky. Reuters, citing Flightradar24, put 4,042 flights airborne destined for U.S. airports at issuance — a different count basis, not a contradiction of the order’s scope. The exceptions, even during the stop: inbound international flights case-by-case; critical medevac, life-saving cargo and law-enforcement flights; some transatlantic departures from Boston Logan shortly before 6 p.m.; Pacific-bound flights from San Francisco later in the evening. The ripple: at the FAA’s request, Eurocontrol suspended U.S.-bound departures from its 42 member countries, and Air Canada cancelled its U.S. flights Friday evening. And one scope question the record does not fully reconcile: the advisory says “ALL AIRPORTS,” while some reporting said the stop in practice applied to the contiguous U.S. and Alaska Air Group said flights within Alaska remained cleared to operate.

The numbers, and the official reason

The counts, read as snapshots: Friday cancellations rose from roughly 390 at about 5 p.m. to over 6,600 by late evening — by different trackers, at different hours, counting different things — with at least 827 flights diverted (Cirium via AP, 7:05 p.m. ET). There is no single toll, and the section says why. Then the official reason, in officials’ own words, in chronological order: the FAA’s statement — a nationwide ground stop “following communication issues impacting multiple Air Route Traffic Control Centers,” its information “preliminary and subject to change” — and Secretary Duffy’s evening-briefing account of aging routers giving inconsistent data until the system was shut down.

01 — WHAT HAPPENEDThe stop itself, from the FAA’s own advisory

FAA Air Traffic Control System Command Center Advisory 111, “NATIONAL GROUND STOP” — all U.S. airports ground-stopped, all departing traffic held “until further notice,” inbound international flights handled case-by-case. Ordered “BY ORDER OF THE FEDERAL AVIATION COMMAND CENTER.” Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy publicly confirmed and announced it.

“ATTENTION ALL AIRCRAFT OPERATORS, BY ORDER OF THE FEDERAL AVIATION COMMAND CENTER ALL AIRPORTS ARE GROUND STOPPED. ALL DEPARTING TRAFFIC SHOULD REMAIN GROUND STOPPED UNTIL FURTHER NOTICE. INBOUND INTERNATIONAL FLIGHTS WILL BE MANAGED ON A CASE BY CASE BASIS.”
— FAA ATCSCC Advisory 111, signed 3:54 p.m. ET (20:54 UTC), Nov. 13, 2026 · fly.faa.gov

What a ground stop actually means: it holds departures. Aircraft already airborne were not ordered down — they continued and landed. Duffy / USA Today: more than 3,500 airplanes were flying when the stop was issued; by 8 p.m. ET fewer than 400 were in the sky. Reuters, citing Flightradar24, put 4,042 flights airborne destined for U.S. airports at issuance — a different count basis, not a contradiction of the order’s scope.

Exceptions, even during the stop

  • Inbound international flights, case-by-case (advisory text)
  • Critical medevac, life-saving cargo and law-enforcement flights (Duffy on X, per USA Today: “exceptions were made…”)
  • Some transatlantic departures from Boston Logan shortly before 6 p.m. ET (Logan Airport via USA Today)
  • Pacific-bound flights from San Francisco cleared later in the evening

The ripple beyond the US

  • At the FAA’s request, Eurocontrol suspended U.S.-bound departures from its 42 member countries for flights departing 5 p.m.–midnight ET (Eurocontrol advisory via USA Today)
  • Air Canada cancelled its U.S. flights Friday evening; cascade effects hit Canada and Europe (Reuters)
  • No second or earlier nationwide stop was found — searches for a morning Nov. 13 stop return only this afternoon event

Alaska / Hawaii — unresolved: the advisory says “ALL AIRPORTS.” Some airline and travel reporting said the stop in practice applied to the contiguous U.S.; Alaska Air Group said flights within Alaska remained cleared to operate, and SFO–Hawaii flights were among the early phased releases. The record does not fully reconcile the two.

All times Eastern, Friday Nov. 13, 2026. The stop was preceded about 40 minutes earlier by Chicago-only ground stops for “EQUIPMENT / OUTAGE — COMMS ISSUES.”

3:17 p.m.

Midway ground stop, matching period and reason. Advisory 109, signed 3:31 p.m. (fly.faa.gov)

3:36 p.m.

FAA begins identifying “instability for our radar and our communications” in centers and TRACONs. (Duffy, evening briefing — USA Today; GeekWire)

Airlines begin receiving halt notifications. (The Air Current, via secondary summaries)

Advisory 111 signed — nationwide ground stop, all airports, until further notice.

Duffy posts on X: nationwide ground stop “following a communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers. FAA technicians are investigating.”

~5:00 p.m.

FAA posts on X: “temporary telecommunications outage — out of an abundance of caution all flights have been halted. Every flight has been accounted for…” (via USA Today)

5:46 p.m.

~6:55 p.m.

FAA NAS status board no longer shows a nationwide ground stop in force; airport delay programs remain. (AP-syndicated timeline) Why the board cleared while Advisory 120 still said “in effect” is unexplained in the sources — likely a display / phased-release artifact.

Duffy announces: “At 8:15 tonight Eastern we have opened the airspace.”

8:19 p.m.

Advisory 005 signed: “THE FAA COMMAND CENTER NATIONWIDE GROUND STOP HAS BEEN CANCELLED” (event 8:16–8:30 p.m.). (fly.faa.gov)

~8:30 p.m.

FAA website announcement noted by press. The 8:15 / 8:19 / ~8:30 figures are the announcement, the advisory signature, and the website posting — not three different lifts.

Note: Advisory 111’s listed windows (event to 11:00 p.m. ET / effective to 11:30 p.m. ET) were not the actual lift time — the text said “until further notice.”

Status late Friday night

The nationwide stop is over. As of 8:50–9:34 p.m. ET the FAA feed showed 0 ground stops and 4 ground delay programs: Boston (volume, avg 26 / max 103 min), LaGuardia (wind, avg 45 / max 166), San Diego (volume, avg 34 / max 81), San Francisco (wind, avg 138 / max 690). The underlying events are agreed.

04 — THE OFFICIAL REASONWhat officials said, in their own words

In chronological order. Note what is not here: no named failed component, vendor, or trigger for the overload; no technical incident report; no FAA confirmation of the CBS-reported network update (see Claim 3 below). Secretary Duffy’s router-overload account is a preliminary assessment at a press briefing, not a published FAA finding.

“This information is preliminary and subject to change. The FAA issued a nationwide ground stop for all U.S. airports following communication issues impacting multiple Air Route Traffic Control Centers. FAA technicians are investigating the issue. We will provide updates.”
— FAA General Statements, Nov. 13, 2026 · faa.gov (verified live during research)
“What we have is an incredibly old air traffic control system… They started to give us inconsistency in the data from those routers, and because of that we shut the system down… We have old switches, old routers. We use 1980s little copper lines. It’s an old system that went down, and went down everywhere. It got overloaded.”
— Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, evening White House briefing, ~8:30 p.m. ET (via USA Today / GeekWire). Duffy added that newer digital IP switches “did not experience these issues,” and that fixes deployed to reduce the overloading were, in his telling, an indication “it was a legacy network problem.”

The official account, step by step

  • 3:54 p.m. — Advisory 111: no technical reason given — only “extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety.”
  • 4:16 p.m. — Duffy on X: “a communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers.” Wording gap: the FAA statement says “multiple” ARTCCs; Duffy said “all.” Both are official; the FAA has not reconciled them publicly.
  • ~5:00 p.m. — FAA on X: “a temporary telecommunications outage,” flights halted “out of an abundance of caution.”
  • 5:46 p.m. — Advisory 120: reason field: “COMMUNICATIONS FAILURE.”
  • Afternoon — DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin: “It’s just a telecommunication outage as of right now”; “There is no prior indication as of yet that this was [a] nefarious attack against us”; “the public was never at danger.”
  • Evening briefing — Mullin: new equipment / patches being installed “was overwhelming the older system” (he did not name the equipment).
  • Evening briefing — the funding ask: both secretaries pressed Congress for modernization money — the administration’s ask is ~$30–30.1 billion on top of the $12.5 billion approved in 2025; Duffy: “The president’s asked for $30 billion. I want $50 billion.” Trump has declared the ATC failures “a national emergency” (Mullin).
  • Airlines offered no independent cause — their statements were operational: American expected “residual delays and cancellations tonight and into tomorrow”; United said “a significant number of Friday evening flights were canceled, others are still expected to operate.”

05 — SIDE BY SIDEOfficial account vs. what other circles claim

Each claim is marked against the record available Friday night: corroborated by documentation, plausible but unproven, or contradicted by the available record. Do not merge these into one narrative: the documented facts establish that a telecom failure forced the stop. Why the telecom system failed was not established in any source available Friday night.

Corroborated by documentation

1. A telecom / communications failure at the ARTCCs forced a safety ground stop

Who says it: FAA (advisory, statement, X); Duffy; Mullin

Advisory 111 / 120 text on fly.faa.gov; FAA statement verified live; consistent airline, Eurocontrol, NATCA and flight-tracking data.

Plausible but unproven

2. Aging analog switches / routers overloaded with data and gave inconsistent data; new IP equipment held up; fixes reducing the overload restored service

Who says it: Duffy (evening briefing); Mullin (“new technology overwhelming the older system”)

Stated by cabinet secretaries and consistent with the documented history of FAA telecom failures — but no FAA technical report, failed component, or trigger for the overload has been published, and officials have not confirmed the CBS update / reboot account that would explain the overload.

Plausible but unproven

3. An L3Harris-operated network update rebooted and crashed the backbone; a router problem started at Chicago Midway

Who says it: CBS News (multiple unnamed sources); WSJ / Reuters (airline officials on the L3Harris network); CNN (airline source on Midway routers); Bloomberg (FBI examining a software update)

Multiple independent-seeming anonymous-source reports converging on the telecom backbone. The FAA has not confirmed any of it; L3Harris did not respond to requests for comment; source independence is not established. Context: the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure (FTI), operated by L3Harris, is the documented backbone linking 4,400+ FAA facilities, with a track record of failures (Philadelphia TRACON, Sept. 21, 2026, ~9,500 flights delayed / cancelled per Reuters; Dallas, Sept. 2025; Newark / Philadelphia, spring 2025) — which supports the category without proving this mechanism.

Contradicted so far — not definitively excluded

4. Cyberattack / foreign attack, including Iran

Who says it: rampant social-media speculation (Columbus Dispatch); colleagues texting Soufan Group research director Colin P. Clarke asking “This is the Iranians, right?” (USA Today) — his description of the anxiety, not an attribution

Evidence offered: none documented. Against: Mullin — “zero evidence,” no “footprints,” “mechanically driven” appearance; two law-enforcement officials told CNN preliminary indications were not a cyberattack; Bloomberg, citing a person familiar: the FBI does not believe it was a cyberattack or criminal activity. No agency (DHS, FBI, CISA, Pentagon) has attributed the outage to Iran or any foreign actor. Caveat: Mullin also said “nothing completely certain right now,” and no final investigative finding exists. Aviation security consultant Jeffrey Price told USA Today early on that “everything’s on the table” while stressing he did not know the cause.

Timing only — unproven, undercut by technical analysis

5. The SMART AI rollout (Nov. 10) caused the outage

Who says it: a Slashdot comment minutes after the stop that spread widely (traced by Brendon Beebe, Substack); CNN analyst Mary Schiavo called the timing “just too much of a coincidence” (opinion); raised by Rep. Jesús García (D-IL)

Basis offered: timing + scope only — the FAA announced Nov. 10 that SMART (Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories) would be used “in the airspace of all 22 centers,” and Friday’s failure hit the ARTCCs. Supporting process points: the expansion came ahead of an expected 90-day evaluation; on Oct. 9, 25 lawmakers had asked the FAA to pause SMART and asked how it behaves under degraded-data conditions, without a public answer; NATCA had said it was not involved in SMART’s design, testing or implementation (pre-Friday statement). Against: the FAA’s own Nov. 10 description makes SMART an advisory planning layer — specialists in Washington review its output and send scheduling recommendations for local leadership to consider; it does not control aircraft, radios, or radar. CBS’s Chris Van Cleave reported SMART is not plugged directly into frequencies / comms. It had run since Sept. 21 and already covered Atlanta / Jacksonville / Miami with no reported SMART outage. WIRED noted the rollout and explicitly said there was no evidence linking it to the stop. Denied by vendor Air Space Intelligence ($875M / 12-year contract — an interested party) and by Duffy (“no possible way”) — but Mullin earlier said officials were “looking at” SMART (including via CISA) and “can’t rule out anything at this point,” which keeps it formally open.

Contradicted by the available record

6. The Microsoft 365 outage (Nov. 12–13) is connected

Who says it: social-media users and commentators connecting two outages ~24 hours apart; Mullin noted “a lot of people are making that jump”

Microsoft 365 (Outlook / Teams / Exchange / Copilot) had a global outage from the morning of Thursday Nov. 12, which Microsoft attributed to a technical issue tied to a network capacity upgrade and a later network change, with no indication of malicious activity. Mullin: “no connection between this and Microsoft… separate issues.” Microsoft: “Microsoft has no indication that the FAA’s nationwide ground stop is related to any Microsoft services.” A DuaNews fact-check (Nov. 13) found no confirmed connection, and no federal agency has confirmed one. In all reporting reviewed, the FAA network at issue is a private L3Harris-operated network, not a Microsoft service.

Contradicted

7. The National Weather Service suffered a simultaneous nationwide radar / telecom outage

Who says it: initially Reuters; social posts; CNN at one point (per Gizmodo)

NWS posted on X before 6:15 p.m. ET: “there is no radar outage and [radar.weather.gov] is fully operational! Outdated content from isolated webpages stating otherwise has been circulating, but NWS radars are operating normally.” Gizmodo called the Baltimore / Washington NWS office twice; the spokesperson confirmed no radar problem. The rumor appears to trace to an outdated Anchorage, Alaska NWS webpage that the Wayback Machine shows has been active for years.

Unproven — inconsistent with the operational record

8. The stop was manufactured / exaggerated to pressure Congress for $30–50B

Who says it: a large share of commenters on the White House’s Facebook video of the briefing; similar skepticism in other social threads

Basis offered: timing — the administration had formally asked Congress for ~$30.1B the day before, and Duffy used the Friday-night briefing to raise the ask to $50B. No evidence of fabrication is offered, and the operational record (FAA advisories, 827+ diversions, mass airline cancellations, controller accounts) is inconsistent with a fabricated stop. The funding debate itself — what the 2025 $12.5B bought, whether $30–50B more is warranted — is a legitimate open political question, separate from fabrication. Lawmakers’ own line was different: Sen. Tammy Duckworth — “demanding answers… will warrant a thorough and complete investigation”; Sen. Maria Cantwell — the committee will review primary / backup frequency technology; Rep. Ted Lieu called for hearings and systems without a single point of failure; Senate Commerce Chair Ted Cruz’s committee said it “highlights the need to prioritize modernizing (ARTCCs)… No new ARTCC has been built since LBJ was in office.”

Contradicted / unsupported

9. Nov. 13 is the start of a predicted Nov. 17 “Black November” / 90-day shutdown

Who says it: posts on 4chan (Nov. 11), Telegram, Reddit and conspiracy forums, surveyed by Mick Dalla-Vee (Substack, Nov. 13)

The prediction’s date is Nov. 17, not Nov. 13 — and the actual scheduled nationwide event on Nov. 17 is a FEMA / FCC nationwide test of the Emergency Alert System. Nothing in the Nov. 13 record connects the FAA outage to this prediction.

06 — THE FRONT LINEWhat the unions and operators actually said

NATCA — controllers

President Nick Daniels (CNN, Erin Burnett OutFront, Nov. 13): controllers were dealing with intermittent frequencies and radar, “handling something that’s never been seen before,” actively searching for working frequencies; the FAA spread aircraft out and reduced capacity to maintain safety. NATCA’s public statement: controllers are on the job, “the safety of the flying public remains our highest priority”; a post-event post credits members for landing aircraft safely when equipment fails and argues for better pay and conditions. NATCA offered no cause for the outage in the material reviewed.

ALPA — pilots

Nov. 13: monitoring the ground stop, in constant contact with the FAA and NATCA, pilots ready to resume once safe — “Safety comes first.” No cause offered. Together, these accounts corroborate the operational picture — intermittent frequencies and radar, no playbook, capacity cut for safety — without endorsing any root-cause theory.

07 — CAN’T VERIFYWhat is simply not established yet

  • Root cause / trigger of the router overload. No FAA technical incident report, failed component, or vendor finding has been published. Duffy’s account and CBS’s update / reboot account may describe parts of the same failure, but their relationship is unestablished.
  • Whether the L3Harris / FTI network was the failed system — reported via anonymous sources; unconfirmed by the FAA; L3Harris had not commented in any source reviewed.
  • “All” vs “multiple” ARTCCs (Duffy vs FAA statement), and 21 vs 22 centers (some FAA facility pages list 21 en-route centers + combined facilities; officials consistently said 22 on Nov. 13).
  • Alaska / Hawaii scope — how “ALL AIRPORTS” was applied to in-state Alaska flights, which Alaska Air Group said remained cleared.
  • A single authoritative flight / passenger toll — tracker snapshots diverge by time, tracker and counting universe; the FAA published no count of its own.
  • Why the NAS status board stopped showing the stop ~6:55 p.m. ET while Advisory 120 still said it remained in effect.
  • Any NTSB / Inspector General investigation — none had been announced in sources available at publication; congressional inquiries were calls and statements, not yet hearings.
  • Saturday / Sunday final impact — only early cancellation listings (~800 Saturday per FlightAware at ~8 p.m. ET; ~1,500 Sunday per Flightradar24) were available; finals will differ.
