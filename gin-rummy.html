Melds & deadwood mathTap to open or close

What it is. A meld is a set of 3–4 cards of the same rank (7♠ 7♥ 7♦) or a run of 3+ cards in one suit (4♣ 5♣ 6♣). Aces are low only — Q♠ K♠ A♠ is not a run. Every card not in a meld is deadwood, and deadwood is what you pay with.

What it costs. Ace = 1, 2–10 = face value, J/Q/K = 10 each. Three loose face cards are 30 deadwood — more than most hands are lost by. The table above counts your deadwood live, using the best possible meld arrangement of the cards you hold, so the number you see is the real number.

What it means. A card is worth keeping only if it is pulling its weight: it is in a meld, one card from a meld (a pair, or two to a run), or cheap. A lone K with no partner and no run neighbours is 10 points of rent every turn. Pairs are a promise, not a meld — two 9s are still 18 deadwood until the third 9 or a run arrives.

How to act. Early, keep flexible cards (middle ranks like 5–8 touch the most runs). Late, dump expensive loners first. When you draw, ask: does this card lower my deadwood after my best discard, not before?

Knock or play for gin? The risk, pricedTap to open or close

Knocking ends the hand when your deadwood is at or under the knock value (10 by default — it is a setting, below). You score the difference between the bot's deadwood and yours. Gin is deadwood 0: the difference plus a 25-point bonus, and the defender cannot lay off a single card. Big Gin — all 11 cards melded before you discard — pays the difference plus 50.

The price of waiting. Every extra turn you chase gin, the bot gets a turn too. Here is the knock decision in plain numbers, knocking with 6 deadwood:

The gin gamble, honestly. Say you sit on 4 deadwood with two realistic gin outs in a 30-card stock. Each draw is roughly a 2-in-30 shot; over three more turns you gin perhaps 1 time in 5 — and in the other 4, the bot may knock first and catch you holding those same 4 plus whatever you picked up. Gin's +25 is real money, but a knock banked now can never be undercut by a hand that never gets played. Rule of thumb the good players use: knock early with 7 or less; chase gin only when your deadwood is already tiny and the stock is still fat. The Coach dial prices this for your exact hand, live.

Reading the discardsTap to open or close

The discard pile is the only honest newspaper at the table. Three reads, in order of strength:

1. What the bot picks up is close to gospel. Nobody takes a face-up card for fun. If the bot takes the 8♣, it wants 8s, or 7♣/9♣, or both. From that moment, your 8♦ is a loaded card: discard it only when you must. The Coach names these cards for you as they happen.

2. What the bot throws early is usually safe. A first-turn discard of 3♦ says the bot is (probably) not building diamonds near the 3, and its mates 3♠ 3♥ 3♣ just got safer to throw yourself. Probably — a sharp player occasionally throws bait. This page's Sharp bot does not bluff its discards; that limit is stated, not hidden.

3. What the bot passes tells a story too. If the bot refuses a 5♥ upcard, hearts around the 5 are a little safer. If the bot keeps refusing low cards and the stock shrinks, it is usually already close — knock sooner rather than later.

Dead cards. When two of a rank are already in the discard pile, the other two can never make a set for anyone — and a run needing a card already buried is dead too. Counting burials is how Sharp plays, and the discard strip above keeps the whole pile visible so you can count with it.

Undercut & laying off — the defender's rightsTap to open or close

Laying off. When a player knocks (not gin), the defender may attach their own deadwood cards onto the knocker's melds — a fourth 9 onto a set of 9s, a 3♣ or 7♣ onto a 4♣ 5♣ 6♣ run — before deadwood is counted. Laying off is automatic and optimal on this page; you never lose points to a missed layoff.

Undercut. If, after laying off, the defender's deadwood is equal to or less than the knocker's, the defender wins the hand: they score the difference plus a 25-point undercut bonus. Ties go to the defender — that is why knocking with 10 late in a hand is braver than it looks, and knocking with 1–3 is nearly undercut-proof.

Against gin there is no defence. No laying off, no undercut — gin's deadwood is 0 and cannot be tied. That immunity is half of what the +25 bonus is paying for.

Drawn hands & the stock. If the stock runs down to two cards and nobody has knocked, the hand is a draw: no score, and the same dealer deals again.

Rules & settings

The rules are settings: change them and the table plays by your numbers from the next deal. Changes save on this device only.

The documented rules this table plays

  • Standard 52-card deck. Dealer deals 10 cards each; the next card turns face up to start the discard pile, the rest is the stock.
  • The non-dealer is offered the upcard first; if they pass, the dealer may take it; if both pass, the non-dealer draws first.
  • On your turn: draw the top of the stock or take the top discard, then discard one card. You may not discard a card you just took from the discard pile on that same turn.
  • Knock when your deadwood — after your discard — is at or under the knock value. Gin is deadwood 0 (bonus 25, no laying off against it). Big Gin is all 11 cards melded (bonus 50).
  • Defender lays off onto the knocker's melds (never against gin), then deadwood is compared. Equal or lower defender deadwood = undercut, defender scores the difference + 25.
  • The loser of a hand deals the next. First to the target score wins the match; if the loser never scored a point all match, the winner adds a 100-point shutout bonus.

Your settings

What each bot really does — honestly

  • Casual keeps cards that lower its deadwood, but forgets the discard pile, sometimes keeps a pretty card it should throw, and knocks on a coin flip when it can. It does not read your discards at all.
  • Steady always takes a discard that improves its hand, always throws its worst card, and knocks with 7 or less (or anything legal when the stock runs low). It notices what it needs, not what you need.
  • Sharp does everything Steady does, plus: it remembers every discard, avoids feeding ranks and suits you have picked up, prefers throwing cards whose mates are already buried, and prices the knock-vs-gin choice by deadwood and stock left. It does not peek at your hand, and it never bluffs a discard.

No bot sees your cards. Every bot decision is made from its own hand and the public discard pile — the same information you get.
