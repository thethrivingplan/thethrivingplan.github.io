Start here: the order that keeps you alive

Survival teaching agrees on the order, because the body sets it. In harsh weather, exposure can kill in hours. Dehydration takes days. Hunger takes weeks. So the working order is:

  1. Stop the emergency in front of you — heavy bleeding, immediate danger.
  2. Shelter and warmth — get out of wind and wet, insulate yourself from the ground.
  3. Water — find it, make it safer, carry it.
  4. Being found — signal early, while you have energy.
  5. Food — last, and mostly a morale question in the first days.

First move, always: STOP

S — Sit downSit before you act. A moving, panicking person walks away from help and into worse trouble.
T — ThinkWhat do I actually know? Where was I last sure of my position? What do I have with me?
O — ObserveLook and listen: water sounds, roads, rooftops, aircraft, weather coming in, daylight left.
P — PlanOne small plan, in order: shelter, water, signal. Then do only the first step of it.
Why it works: panic burns the two things you cannot replace — energy and daylight. Sitting down is a physical brake. Most rescues happen close to where a person stopped; most tragedies involve someone who kept walking.
Coach drill: next time you're on any trail, practise STOP at a random moment. Sit for two full minutes and name five things you can see, hear, or smell that would matter if you were lost. That noticing is the skill.

🔥 Fire Priority: warmth, light, signalling, boiling

Fire gives you warmth, dry clothes, boiled water, light, a signal, and a reason to sit still. But the honest first lesson is unglamorous:

The honest ladder: 1. A lighter or matches you actually carry — carrying them is the real skill; they work tired, cold, and one-handed. 2. A ferro rod — a metal rod that throws hot sparks when scraped; works wet, lasts for thousands of strikes, but only lights well-prepared tinder. 3. The bow drill — fire from friction alone, the hard documented method below. Learn the ladder from the top. Nobody was ever rescued because they made fire the hardest way.

The three sizes of wood — in plain words

  • Tinder — the fine, fluffy stuff that catches a spark or a small flame: dry grass, shredded bark, feather sticks (a stick shaved into curls), cotton balls. It must be dry and fine. Tinder burns for seconds.
  • Kindling — pencil-thin to thumb-thick dry sticks. They catch from the tinder and build real flame. Gather more than you think you need — then double it.
  • Fuel — wrist-thick and bigger logs. They catch from the kindling and burn for a long time. Fuel never catches from a spark, and trying is the classic beginner failure.

Step by step — any method

  1. Prepare everything before any flame exists. Tinder nest, kindling sorted by size, fuel stacked within reach. A new flame is a baby: it cannot wait while you go shopping.
  2. Choose the spot: bare ground or rock, away from roots, dry leaves, and low branches; sheltered from wind but not under your shelter. Clear a circle down to mineral soil.
  3. Build the lay first (two drawn below), leaving a gap to place the tinder.
  4. Light the tinder, shelter it from wind with your body, and feed kindling smallest-first, leaving gaps — fire eats oxygen as fast as wood.
  5. Add fuel only when kindling is burning strongly. Build up gradually; a log dropped on a young fire smothers it.
The bow drill — the hard way, in fullTap to open ▾

A bow drill makes an ember from friction: a wooden spindle spins against a flat hearth board, and the hot wood dust it grinds out collects in a notch and glows. The parts: spindle (a straight, dry stick about thumb-thick), hearth board (flat, dry, softer wood), bow (a curved stick with cord tied end to end), bearing block (a hard piece held in the top hand to press the spindle down), and a tinder nest ready and waiting.

  1. Carve a shallow dent in the hearth board near one edge; spin the spindle in it briefly to seat it, then cut a V-notch from the dent to the board's edge so dust can fall through.
  2. Put something flat and dry under the notch to catch the dust pile.
  3. Loop the bow cord once around the spindle. Kneel, pin the hearth board under one foot, brace your top-hand wrist against that shin — a wobbling spindle is the number-one failure.
  4. Saw the bow in long, steady strokes, pressing down moderately. Speed and pressure together make heat; frantic short strokes make neither.
  5. When dark dust piles up and smokes on its own, give a final faster run, then stop. The pile should hold a glowing ember.
  6. Tip the ember into the tinder nest, fold the nest loosely around it, and blow long and gentle until it bursts into flame. Then build the fire around it as above.
How it fails: damp or mismatched woods (spindle and board should be dry, similar softness); spindle wobbling; stopping the moment smoke appears — smoke is the halfway mark, not the finish; blowing the ember apart with hard puffs instead of long, soft breaths. Expect your first successes to take many tries at home. That is exactly what the practice log below is for.
Wet-weather fireTap to open ▾
  • Split wood is dry inside. A soaked log's heart is often dry — split it and shave feather sticks from the inner wood.
  • Look for standing dead wood and the dry undersides of leaning trunks; wood lying on wet ground wicks water.
  • Build the fire on a platform of sticks or bark so it isn't sitting in water.
  • Use more tinder and smaller kindling than usual, and shield the young flame with your body or a pack.
  • Once lit, dry tomorrow's tinder and kindling beside the fire — and carry a pinch of dry tinder in a sealed bag on your person, always.
Fire safety and putting it out properlyTap to open ▾
  • Never leave a fire unattended; never build one you cannot put out; obey local fire bans and rules — they exist because conditions make fires escape.
  • Keep it small. A small fire does every survival job and uses a fraction of the wood.
  • Putting it out: let it burn down, spread the coals, douse with water, stir, douse again, and feel with the back of your hand for heat. Repeat until it is cold to the touch. Ashes can hide live coals for hours — "looks out" is not out.
  • Never sleep with a fire inside a closed shelter: smoke and carbon monoxide kill quietly. Fire goes outside; warmth comes in through shelter design and insulation.

💧 Water Priority: second, right after shelter

Finding it

  • Downhill and down-valley: water runs down. Follow the land's folds; listen — moving water is audible a long way off.
  • Green vegetation in a dry landscape, animal tracks converging, and insects (especially bees) all point toward water.
  • Rain is the cleanest source you'll find: catch it in any container, clothing, or a tarp angled into a bottle.
  • Morning dew can be wiped from grass and leaves with a cloth and wrung into a container — slow, but real.

Making it safer

  1. Let it settle and strain it through cloth to remove grit and debris. This is clearing, not cleaning — it makes the next step work better.
  2. Boil it — the documented standard. A rolling boil for one minute (longer at high altitude, where water boils cooler) kills the bacteria, viruses, and parasites that make wild water dangerous. If you can do only one thing, do this.
  3. Let it cool covered, and drink from the clean side of the container.
Filtering is not purifying — the honest difference: a typical squeeze or straw filter strains out particles, bacteria, and parasites, but most do not remove viruses — that takes a purifier (a higher-rated filter, chemical treatment, or boiling). Read what a device actually claims. When in doubt, boil. Boiling does not remove chemicals or heavy metals — no field method reliably does — so avoid water downstream of mines, industry, or heavy farm runoff when you have any choice.
How it fails: trusting clear water ("it looks clean" tells you nothing about microbes); sipping untreated water because you're thirsty now — the illness arrives in a day or two and dehydrates you far worse; rationing water into uselessness. Drink when thirsty: water in your body works; water hoarded in a bottle while you weaken does not.

Containers and carrying

  • Any bottle works; a metal bottle or pot also boils, which makes it two tools in one.
  • Carry water whenever you move — finding a source and leaving it empty-handed trades a known good for an unknown.
  • In cold weather, sleep with your bottle so it doesn't freeze; in heat, keep it shaded.

⛺ Shelter Priority: first — exposure kills fastest

Choose the site before you build anything

  • Look up: dead branches above. A dead limb hanging over your camp — campers call them widowmakers — can fall in wind or under its own rot, silently. Never sleep under one.
  • Look down: where will water flow? Dry gullies, dips, and smooth channels are rivers waiting for rain. Camp on ground that sheds water, never in the path it takes.
  • Avoid hilltops (wind) and valley bottoms (cold air pools there, and so does water). A middle slope, out of the wind, near — not in — your water source is the textbook answer.
  • Check for ant nests, animal runs, and signs of regular flooding before you commit.
Insulate from the ground first — and why: the ground steals heat by conduction far faster than air steals it by convection. Lying on cold ground in a good sleeping bag still leaves you cold, because your weight crushes the insulation under you to nothing. A thick bed of dry leaves, pine boughs, or any dry material under you — aim for a layer that stays several inches thick when compressed — is worth more than the same material piled on top. Ground first, roof second.

Lean-to, step by step

  1. Lash a sturdy ridge pole between two trees at about waist-to-chest height, or rest it on two forked uprights driven into the ground.
  2. Lean poles against it on the windward side, close together, at an angle steep enough to shed rain.
  3. Cover with boughs, bark, or debris, starting at the bottom and overlapping upward — like shingles, so water runs over each layer, not under it.
  4. Build the ground bed inside: the thickest pile of dry material you can gather.
  5. If you have a tarp or poncho, it goes over the frame as the roof — minutes instead of an hour.
How it fails: building too big (a palace you can't warm and can't finish before dark — build body-sized); covering top-down so rain funnels inside; skipping the ground bed; and starting at dusk. A shelter is a daylight job.

Staying dry

  • Wet clothing loses most of its insulation. If you have any dry layer, save it for sleeping and wear the wet one while working.
  • Wring clothes out, and dry them by the fire in rotation — never leave them unattended near flame.
  • In rain, do heavy work slowly: sweat soaks you from the inside as surely as rain from the outside.

📣 Signalling and being found

The strongest survival advice on this page: before you go anywhere, tell somebody where you are going and when you'll be back — and if you get lost, stay put. Searchers start from your last known point and work outward; a person who stays put gets found, and a person who wanders turns a search into a chase. Your car, your camp, your last known spot — staying with something findable is a signal all by itself.

Three of anything — the distress pattern

Groups of three are the recognised distress signal almost everywhere: three whistle blasts, three fires (in a triangle), three flashes of a light, three shots, three of anything repeated — pause, repeat. One could be an accident; three in a pattern is a person asking for help. A whistle carries far further than a shout and costs no voice: three long blasts, pause, repeat, and rest between rounds.

Signal mirror

  1. Hold the mirror close to your eye and catch the sun on it.
  2. Stretch your other arm toward the target — aircraft, boat, distant road — and make a V with two fingers, framing the target in the V.
  3. Tilt the mirror until the bright reflected spot lands on your fingers, in line with the target. If the spot is on your fingers and the target is in the V, the flash is on the target.
  4. Sweep slowly. A mirror flash can be seen for miles in clear sun — farther than any shout, fire, or waving arms.

Ground-to-air basics

  • In open ground, make big shapes from branches, clothing, or trampled snow/vegetation: an X means "need help", an arrow points the way you went if you truly had to move. Big means big — letters several paces tall, with contrast against the ground.
  • A smoky fire signals by day (add green vegetation for thick smoke); a bright fire signals by night.
  • Signal when you hear or see rescuers — an aircraft, a vehicle, voices. Save batteries and energy between chances.
How it fails: leaving a findable spot to "walk out" without knowing the way; signalling once and giving up — rescuers may need several passes to pinpoint you; and never telling anyone your plans in the first place, so nobody knows a search is needed.

🩹 First aid basics Knowledge level only

Honest limits — read this at full size

This page is not medical training, and it is not a substitute for a first-aid course or a professional. What follows is the widely taught knowledge level: what the first moves are, and why. Hands-on skills — real pressure on a real wound, real CPR — are learned on a course, with an instructor's hands correcting yours. If you take one thing from this shelf, take a course.

Heavy bleeding — direct pressure

  1. Get help coming first if you can — call emergency services or send a specific person to call. "You — call 911" beats "somebody call".
  2. Find the source of the bleeding. Place a clean cloth, dressing, or clothing directly on the wound.
  3. Press hard and keep pressing. Steady, firm pressure, with the injured part resting on something solid. Do not lift the cloth to check — checking breaks the clot you're building.
  4. If blood soaks through, add more material on top — never remove the first layer — and keep pressing.
  5. Hold pressure until help arrives or the bleeding stops. Severe bleeding can kill in minutes, which is why pressure starts immediately, not after a search for perfect supplies. A gloved or bare hand on the wound beats a perfect dressing a minute later.
Why it works: pressure slows the flow enough for clotting to begin, and the clot is the real bandage. Everything you do buys the clot time. Lifting to peek spends it.

Keeping somebody warm

  • Cold worsens bleeding and shock. Get insulation under the person as well as over them — the ground drains heat even in mild weather.
  • Cover the head, keep them still and lying down, and talk to them: a calm voice is a real intervention for panic and shock.
  • Don't give food or drink to somebody who may need surgery or who isn't fully alert.

When the only right move is getting help

  • Uncontrolled bleeding, chest pain, difficulty breathing, a head injury, a suspected broken neck or back, confusion or fading consciousness, severe burns — these are not treat-in-place problems. Stabilise what you can (pressure, warmth, stillness), and put your energy into getting professionals there.
  • If you must leave an injured person to get help, leave them sheltered, insulated, and — if they're alert — knowing exactly what you're doing and when you'll be back. Mark the spot so you can find it again.

🪢 Knots that matter

Three knots cover most of what survival asks of rope. Learn them until your hands know them in the dark — that standard, not "I tied it once", is what the practice log is for. A note of honesty: no knot on this page is taught for climbing or life-support use; those need in-person instruction.

How knots fail: tying from memory you don't have yet (practise at home, in warmth, first); leaving tails too short — leave a hand's width and check the knot after loading it; and using a pretty knot where the job needed a different one. Every knot weakens rope somewhat; a knot is a tool, not magic.

⭐ Finding your way without a phone

The shadow stick — direction from the sun

  1. Push a straight stick upright into flat, sunny ground and mark the tip of its shadow with a stone. Wait 15–20 minutes.
  2. Mark the new shadow tip. The line from the first mark to the second runs roughly west to east — the first mark is the west end.
  3. Stand with your left foot on the first mark and right foot on the second: in the northern hemisphere you are now facing roughly north.
Why it works, and its honest limits: the sun travels east to west, so shadows travel west to east. It gives you a rough compass, not a precise one — accuracy is poor near midday and in high summer, and it needs sun, flat ground, and patience. Rough direction is still far better than walking in a circle, which is what unaided people reliably do.

The North Star (northern hemisphere)

  1. Find the Big Dipper: a saucepan shape of seven bright stars.
  2. Take the two stars at the outer edge of its bowl and extend the line between them upward, about five times their gap. The star you reach is Polaris, the North Star.
  3. Polaris sits almost directly over the north pole: face it and you face true north, wherever you are in the northern hemisphere.
Honest limits: the North Star works only in the northern hemisphere and only on a clear night. The sun method needs sun. Neither replaces the real skills: tell somebody your route, carry a map and compass you've practised with, and — the rule from the signalling shelf — when lost, staying put beats navigating badly.

🍂 Food, honestly

Why food is last: a healthy body carries weeks of energy. In the first days of a survival situation, food is mostly morale — while shelter, warmth, and water are life itself, on a clock of hours and days. Spending your first afternoon hunting instead of building shelter is the classic fatal trade. Eat what you safely have; don't gamble for more.

Wild plants — a trap for beginners, stated plainly

The documented guidance for beginners is blunt: do not eat wild plants you cannot identify with complete certainty. Many poisonous plants look like edible ones; some toxins have no field remedy; and a stomach emptied by vomiting and diarrhoea dehydrates you faster than hunger ever would. The calories in a handful of unknown greens are trivial. The risk is not. Plant knowledge is real knowledge — learned slowly, from experts and books, at home, in safety. A survival emergency is the worst classroom there is.

Fishing and trapping — knowledge level only

  • A simple hand line, hook, and patience is the lowest-effort food tool that exists — which is why a small fishing kit lives in many survival kits. It still takes practice to catch anything.
  • Trapping is a skilled craft: it depends on knowing animal habits, runs, and seasons, and it is governed by local law — seasons, licences, and permitted methods vary everywhere, and much of it is illegal outside a genuine emergency. This page teaches no trap construction; the honest lesson is that trapping is not a beginner's shortcut but a studied skill with legal boundaries.
  • Cook wild food thoroughly. Parasites and bacteria are a far more common threat than starvation in the short term.
How it fails: the "universal edibility test" passed around online is unreliable and is not taught here — it takes a day per plant, reacts too late to some toxins, and spends the one resource you can't spare. Certainty or nothing.
