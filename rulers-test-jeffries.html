The Ruler's Test — Hakeem Jeffries, Minority Leader of the United States House of Representatives

Subject: Hakeem Sekou Jeffries (D-NY, New York's 8th district) — House Minority Leader since 3 January 2023, unanimously elected Democratic Leader by his caucus in 2022 and again in 2024 (Jeffries House biography, 2026). The seat graded is the leadership office itself, and its framing is stated plainly: a minority leader holds the powers of organized opposition, not of government. He does not control the agenda, cannot schedule a vote, sign or veto anything, or pass a program — the majority and the Speaker do that. What the office actually holds is floor strategy (the leader's unlimited "magic minute," motions, and the discharge petition that can pry a bill out of committee with 218 signatures), caucus discipline (whether 200-plus members vote as one or scatter), negotiation position (whether the majority needs his votes to fund the government, raise the debt limit, or keep its own Speaker), and the public counter-case (the daily argument for the other side). He is graded on how that opposition was wielded — never on failing to enact a program he never held the gavel for. His wider career enters only where it bears directly on the office. The premise, verified: Democrats held the minority through the 118th and 119th Congresses, so Jeffries is the holder in November 2026 — but the seat changed hands underneath him this month. On 3 November 2026 Democrats won the House decisively (at least 259 seats secured by 24 November, against at least 173 Republican, per AP reporting); on 18 November 2026 the Democratic Caucus nominated Jeffries for Speaker, 251–11, for the Congress convening 3 January 2027; and House Republicans, outgoing Speaker Mike Johnson having stepped back from leadership after the defeat, elected Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio their Minority Leader for the 120th Congress, 106–73 over Rep. Steve Scalise (AP, November 2026; NACo midterm analysis, November 2026; House Republican Conference statement via Speaker Johnson's office, 10 November 2026). Jordan is leader-elect only: he has exercised none of the office's powers and has no record in the seat to grade. This dossier therefore grades Jeffries on his completed tenure as Minority Leader — 3 January 2023 to the end of the 119th Congress — under the series' completed-tenure handling, and says so on its face. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic, printed verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. The mandate rule applies: governing — or, for a minority leader, opposing — on the platform the voters elected is NOT inherently a failure; carrying out the opposition mandate his voters elected is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for HOW it is done. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The expulsion standard was applied to a Republican on evidence, through the referee process, not on party instruction alone. Jeffries's caucus waited for the bipartisan House Ethics Committee's unanimous report on Rep. George Santos (R-NY) — "overwhelming evidence" of lawbreaking, referred to the Justice Department — and on 1 December 2023 nearly all Democrats joined the 311–114 vote to expel him, only the sixth expulsion in House history; an earlier attempt, before the report, had drawn dozens of Democratic no votes precisely because the investigation was unfinished (AP, 1 December 2023; FactCheck.org on the Ethics report, November 2023). Jeffries's own pre-vote words were procedural, not gleeful: Santos was "a malignant distraction, and hopefully that issue gets resolved" (AP, December 2023).
  • The same presumption standard was stated, in the same words, for a member of his own caucus. When Rep. Henry Cuellar (D-TX) was indicted in May 2024 on bribery and foreign-influence charges — an ALLEGATION, pleaded not guilty, untried at grading — Jeffries said Cuellar was "entitled to his day in court and the presumption of innocence throughout the legal process," and Cuellar stepped down from his committees, his chief-deputy-whip post, and his subcommittee ranking role while the case proceeded (Jeffries statement, May 2024; Caucus Chair Pete Aguilar, May 2024). Committee power was surrendered on indictment; the seat was not surrendered before verdict. That is one rule, stated identically for both situations the record offers.
  • The motion-to-vacate rule he applied to two Republican Speakers was, on its stated terms, one rule: a Speaker who governs by bipartisan bills gets Democratic protection; one who does not, does not. Democrats voted to table Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene's motion against Speaker Mike Johnson on 8 May 2024, 359–43, after Johnson moved Ukraine aid and government funding — Jeffries: "Our decision to stop Marjorie Taylor Greene from plunging the country into further chaos is rooted in our commitment to solve problems" (AP, 30 April and 8 May 2024). See the against shelf for the other half of this pair.

Evidence against:

  • The other half of the pair is the seam. On 3 October 2023 Jeffries instructed his caucus not to save Speaker Kevin McCarthy, and all 208 Democrats present voted with eight Republicans to vacate the chair, 216–210 — the first removal of a Speaker in history — on Jeffries's stated ground that McCarthy was "unwilling to break from MAGA extremism in an authentic and comprehensive manner" (National Review reporting of Jeffries's caucus letter, 3 October 2023). The distinction he draws (McCarthy had reneged on the debt-limit spending framework and opened an impeachment inquiry; Johnson, later, delivered the bills) is a real distinction, and it is also a distinction whose application runs exactly along his caucus's interest each time. A rule that protects the Speaker who needs you and removes the one who doesn't is a rule with a thumb on it, and the shelf records both the stated principle and the convenience.
  • The Cuellar standard was defended by his own leadership team in terms that concede a second track. Asked why Cuellar drew presumption while Democrats had called for Sen. Bob Menendez (D-NJ) to resign and for Santos to go, Aguilar answered that the circumstances were "very different" and that Cuellar was a "serious legislator" — "the individuals are also night and day" (The Hill, May 2024). Presumption of innocence is one law; grading the accused by seriousness and service is a second one. The charges against Cuellar remain ALLEGATIONS; what is documented is the leadership's own explanation, and it is not one-law language.
  • On the map, the stated rule changed when his side held the pen. Jeffries built his national case for years on fair maps and condemned the 2025 Texas mid-decade redraw — positioned to net Republicans up to five seats — as an "extreme scheme to rig the midterm elections." He then backed California's Proposition 50, a legislature-drawn map explicitly designed to net Democrats five seats by temporarily setting aside that state's independent redistricting commission; voters approved it on 4 November 2025 by 64% (Ballotpedia analysis, 2025; Jeffries statements, October 2025 and October 2026). Proposition 50's own text professes support for independent commissions nationwide and returns California's commission in 2031 — the countermeasure was voter-ratified and time-limited, which mitigates it — but "partisan maps are rigging, except ours, as an answer" is two laws wearing one justification. This item is also recorded under Test 2, where the referee question belongs.

Why: The grade is MIXED because the documented pattern is a real standard with real exceptions, not a slogan with none. Where a neutral process had spoken — the unanimous Ethics report on Santos, an indictment answered by surrender of committee power in Cuellar's case — Jeffries followed it across party lines. Where judgment was his to exercise — which Speaker to save, which accused member to call serious, which map to call rigged — the judgment landed, every time, on his caucus's side of the line. One law at the source where the source was a process; a second law available where the source was himself.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the House as an institution and voters in both parties, when the Santos precedent said an Ethics finding, not a party, ends a member; Johnson and the governing majority in May 2024, protected under the stated half of the rule; Cuellar's constituents, who kept their elected member while his case proceeds. Hurt — McCarthy, removed under the other half of the same rule; voters in five California districts drawn to a partisan purpose their state's commission existed to prevent; and, in perception the record documents from his own team's words, any future accused member who is not judged a "serious legislator" and so does not receive Cuellar's version of the presumption.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • He accepted the referees' verdicts against his own side, in the open, at the moments it cost most. Jeffries received all 212 Democratic votes on nearly every ballot of the fifteen-ballot January 2023 Speaker election, lost, and handed McCarthy the gavel with a fifteen-minute speech rather than contesting the result (House record, 3–7 January 2023). After the 2024 election he had lost, he committed his caucus in advance — "House Democrats are committed to free and fair elections and to the peaceful transfer of power that will begin on January 6" (C-SPAN, 12 September 2024) — and the 6 January 2025 certification of President Trump's victory proceeded with no Democratic objection (AP, 6 January 2025). A minority leader who wanted a handle on the count had his chance twice; the record shows him declining it twice.
  • His fights with the executive were taken to the referees, not around them. In the 119th Congress his caucus stood up a litigation task force to oppose the Trump administration in court, which his own Speaker-candidacy letter credits with wins upholding birthright citizenship, protecting mail voting, and stopping unilateral National Guard deployments (Jeffries Dear Colleague letter, November 2026) — a self-interested source, recorded as his claim, but the method it describes is verifiable in kind: lawsuits, argued before judges, accepting judicial process as the venue. No documented instance in this record shows Jeffries urging defiance of a court order, threatening a judge, or punishing a prosecutor, election official, or news outlet.
  • His signature legislative weapon is the House's own rule, used as written. The discharge petition — 218 signatures prying a bill from a committee the majority controls — is the minority's constitutional-era tool, not a capture of one. In the 119th Congress his caucus used it to force floor votes and, on the House Democrats' count, passed seven bills by discharge petition, a modern record: the Epstein files release, restoration of federal workers' collective bargaining, the three-year ACA credit extension, Haiti TPS, Ukraine aid, union-contract negotiation rights, and the Major Richard Star Act for wounded veterans (House Democrats, 13 November 2026; Jeffries House biography, 2026). Gripping would be changing the rule or the counter; this is playing the board as dealt.
  • He spent his leverage to keep the majority's own referee standing rather than to own the chair. The May 2024 vote to table the motion to vacate Johnson (Test 1) kept a Speaker of the opposing party in office because that Speaker had moved bipartisan bills — the opposite of centralizing control: the minority's votes preserved the majority's chosen presiding officer (AP, May 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The closest approach to a grip is the map, again — and it is a state referee, not a federal one, and not his to command. Proposition 50 suspended California's independent redistricting commission — an institution that exists precisely to keep map-drawing out of politicians' hands — for the 2026–2030 cycles. Jeffries supported the measure and his aligned committees were involved in the map effort (Ballotpedia, 2025; reporting on the DCCC role, January 2026, contested by California Democrats in the pending Republican litigation, which ALLEGES racial gerrymandering in up to 16 districts — an allegation in an active case, not a finding). The mitigations are documented: the suspension was ratified directly by California's voters, 64%, and the commission returns by the measure's own terms in 2031. A grip the voters themselves fastened, with a sunset, is a lesser thing than a grip — but a leader who elsewhere defends independent commissions backed unfastening one when it stood between his caucus and five seats, and the shelf does not launder that.
  • The October 2023 McCarthy removal left the House without a Speaker for 22 days — during which the chamber could not act at all — and Jeffries's caucus held unanimously for Jeffries himself through four ballots rather than supplying votes to any Republican candidate (House record, October 2023). That is a caucus's right, and the paralysis was the majority's failure to unite behind anyone; it is recorded because "without gripping" includes not using total discipline as a blockade when the institution itself is the thing stalled. His published answer at the time was an offer, not a blockade: a Washington Post column proposing a "bipartisan governing coalition" in which widely supported bills could not be blocked by "a small handful of extreme members" (Washington Post, October 2023). The offer was not taken up; the discipline, and the stall, were real.
  • Structurally, the office itself limits this test: a minority leader commands no prosecutors, no election administrators, no central bank, and no media regulator, so much of HOLDS here is the absence of opportunity as well as the presence of restraint. The dossier grades what he did with the levers he had — the count, the courts, the map fight — and notes the limit rather than inflating the virtue.

Why: The grade is HOLDS because on every occasion where the record shows Jeffries touching a referee — the Speaker count he lost, the presidential certification he lost, the courts he sued in, the discharge rule he used — he took the institution's answer, including when the answer was Trump certified and McCarthy gavelling. The one genuine seam, Proposition 50, was a state commission suspended by that state's own voters for a fixed term, with his support recorded against him; it is enough to mark the file, not enough, on this evidence, to move a grade built on the federal record of the office being graded.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — voters of both parties whose 2024 result was certified without a fight; the courts, used as the venue rather than attacked as an obstacle; Speaker Johnson in May 2024; and every future minority, of either party, whose discharge-petition precedent this tenure enlarged. Hurt — Californians who had voted the independent commission into their constitution and saw it set aside for a decade's maps, whatever the provocation from Texas; and the House's own capacity during the 22 speakerless days of October 2023, a hurt shared with the majority that caused most of it.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? Test 3 also grades whether, when another option became available, he at least considered it openly. Considering it and declining with stated reasons is NOT against-evidence. Refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The hardest wobble of the tenure — his own president's collapse — was answered by carrying the pushback to the man, not by denying it existed. After President Biden's 27 June 2024 debate performance, Jeffries convened his senior members (four ranking members told him on a 7 July call that Biden should step aside), declined to take a public position while the process ran — "I'm going to respect the sanctity of those conversations until we conclude the process" — and on 11 July 2024 met Biden privately and, by his own 12 July letter to the caucus, "directly expressed the full breadth of insight, heartfelt perspectives and conclusions about the path forward that the Caucus has shared" (ABC News, July 2024; Jeffries letter, 12 July 2024). Biden withdrew on 21 July 2024. The adjustment was private in form, which the shelf notes; it was an adjustment, delivered by the leader whose job was to deliver it, and he neither doubled down on a failing nominee nor performed a public execution of his own president.
  • When another option became available inside his own coalition, he eventually took it, openly, with his disagreement stated rather than hidden. Zohran Mamdani won New York's Democratic mayoral primary on 24 June 2025; Jeffries, who had "areas of principled disagreement" with him, met with him, stated his test publicly (that Mamdani show himself "prepared to aggressively address the rise in antisemitism"), and on 24 October 2025 endorsed him — because Mamdani had won "a free and fair election," had "relentlessly focused on addressing the affordability crisis," and had "explicitly committed to being a mayor for all New Yorkers, including those who do not support his candidacy" (Reuters, 24 October 2025). Considering the option for four months and then adopting it with reasons is the clause working, slowly.
  • He adjusted his Speaker strategy between 2023 and 2024 in the open. Having voted McCarthy out in October 2023, he publicly offered Johnson protection before the fact (30 April 2024 statement) and delivered it (8 May 2024) once Johnson moved the aid and funding bills — a stated change of treatment in response to a stated change of conduct, explained both times (AP, April–May 2024).
  • When the street and the Senate ended his chosen fight, he changed weapons rather than denying the result. The 43-day shutdown of October–November 2025 — the longest in US history — was the opposition's stand for the expiring ACA credits; when eight Senate Democrats broke ranks and the government reopened on 12 November 2025 without the extension, Jeffries did not pretend victory or re-litigate the Senate's vote: he filed a discharge petition in November 2025 to force the extension onto the House floor, and the House passed the clean three-year extension on 8 January 2026, 230–196 (AP and Congress.gov records, November 2025–January 2026). The wobble — his own side's other chamber folding — was answered with the one tool his office actually holds.
  • The no-good-choice clause, applied by its mechanics — May 2024: the options that actually existed were (a) save Johnson, the Speaker who had just moved Ukraine aid against his own right flank, and be branded his protector by both parties' extremes; or (b) let Greene's motion proceed, remove a second Speaker in seven months, and own another speakerless paralysis with the aid barely signed. Each carried real harm; no third door existed, because the minority cannot elect a Speaker. He took the least-harm door actually open, said why in advance, and the choice is recorded as the best possible choice available in the moment — not held against him. The same mechanics cover November 2025: once the Senate supplied the reopening votes, no House vote of his could keep the government closed or the credits alive, and hindsight options do not count as options refused.

Evidence against:

  • The Mamdani adjustment took four months and arrived eleven days before the general election, the day before early voting — after a summer of "stay tuned" deflections, including on the morning of the endorsement itself ("I have not refused to endorse. I have refused to articulate my position"). A leader who requires the nominee of his own party, in his own city, to pass tests for a third of a year before acknowledgment is considering the other option at the slowest speed the calendar allows; the delay itself became the story and a Republican talking point, and members of his own coalition read it as reluctance, not deliberation (Reuters, 24 October 2025; Fox News reporting, October 2025).
  • The March 2025 wobble inside his own leadership pair was handled by public distance first and repair later. When Senate Leader Chuck Schumer voted to advance the Republican funding bill in March 2025 — a bill House Democrats had voted almost unanimously against — Jeffries, asked if he had confidence in Schumer, answered with a conspicuous non-answer ("declined to express confidence," per AP's account), and the rift ran in public for months before the two repaired it ahead of the autumn shutdown, appearing together and "united" (AP, March and November 2025). The eventual repair is for-shelf in kind; the first response to pushback from his own side was a public cold shoulder, not an adjustment.
  • On the biggest floor wobble of 2025 he could delay but not adjust the outcome, and chose theatre the record should name as such. His 3 July 2025 "magic minute" speech — 8 hours 44 minutes, breaking McCarthy's House record — was a genuine act of public counter-case, reading the megabill's Medicaid and food-assistance cuts into the record name by name; the bill then passed 218–214 the same day, as everyone in the chamber knew it would (NPR/AP, 3 July 2025). Delay and witness are legitimate minority tools, and the shelf credits them under the office's framing; they are recorded here because a wobble answered only with a record-length speech is an answer about the audience, not about the vote.
  • On impeachment — the loudest pushback from his own base through 2025–2026 — his posture for most of the tenure was refusal to look in public: he neither ruled it in nor out, and individual members' articles (against the President, and later against Secretary Pete Hegseth) moved without his hand. Only after the 2026 midterms did he engage, calling himself "a hard yes" on the Hegseth resolution on 8 November 2026 — and the 11 November vote then failed 204–211 with eleven Democrats absent (Common Dreams roll-call reporting, 11 November 2026; The Guardian via syndication, November 2026). The lateness is Test 3's; the absent eleven are Test 5's.

Why: The grade is MIXED because his adjustments are real but habitually arrive at the last responsible moment and often in private: Biden's concerns carried behind a closed door, Mamdani endorsed on the eve of early voting, Schumer repaired after a public freeze, the shutdown converted to a discharge petition only after the Senate had folded it. When he does move, he moves openly and with reasons, and twice — Johnson, November 2025 — he took the least-harm door a no-good-choice situation actually offered. What he does not do is move early, and on impeachment he declined to look at all until the election had already answered the question for him.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Biden himself, spared a public repudiation by his own House leader and given the caucus's true position in private; Johnson, adjusted toward rather than ousted on reflex; ACA enrollees, whose fight was re-armed with a discharge petition within days of the shutdown's collapse. Hurt — Mamdani and New York Democrats, left unendorsed through a summer of speculation; Schumer, publicly un-confided-in by his partner in March 2025; federal workers and service users who bore the 43 days of a shutdown whose end he could not control and whose central demand was not won in it; and his own base's impeachment advocates, answered with neither yes nor no until November 2026.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include those who did not elect him — the other party's voters, the dissenters inside his own coalition, and the people with no vote in his caucus room — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The discharge-petition record — the tenure's concrete output — is a counting record, and its beneficiaries are mostly people with no seat in his caucus room: nearly one million federal workers whose collective-bargaining rights, stripped by executive order, the House voted to restore; Haitian migrants whose Temporary Protected Status the House voted to extend; wounded veterans whose full benefits the Major Richard Star Act carries; ACA marketplace enrollees, some 24 million people, whose enhanced credits the House voted to extend for three years; Ukrainians, whose aid his votes helped move in April 2024 (95 billion dollar supplemental, Democrats supplying the margin) and again by petition in the 119th Congress; and the Epstein survivors, whose files the House forced toward release, Jeffries's letter "partnering with the courageous and patriotic survivors" (Jeffries House biography, 2026; House Democrats, 13 November 2026; Jeffries Dear Colleague letter, November 2026; AP, April 2024).
  • His counter-case on the 2025 megabill was built as an accounting of the counted-out: the 8-hour-44-minute speech and the caucus's unanimous no vote were framed, with figures, around Medicaid recipients and food-assistance households who would bear the bill's cuts while its tax benefits ran upward (NPR/AP, 3 July 2025). No Democrat voted for the bill — the opposition's count included the poor of Republican districts as well as his own, which is what counting everyone means from a minority chair.
  • He counted the other party's voters when governing required it: Democratic votes repeatedly supplied the margin for the majority's own must-pass bills — government funding, the 2024 foreign-aid package, FAA reauthorization (195 Democrats to 192 Republicans, May 2024), successive defense authorization bills — delivering functioning government to districts that had voted against every Democrat on the ballot (AP, 2024; Jeffries House biography, 2026).
  • The January 2026 anniversary record shows him counting the attack's targets by name and office: on 5–6 January 2026 he led a Democrat-only panel with victims' families and former January 6 committee members, named Officer Brian Sicknick among the fallen, and observed a moment of silence for the more than 140 injured officers (PBS/AP reporting, January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On Gaza, a documented bloc inside his own coalition says, on the record, that it was counted out — and in November 2026 it voted that way. Jeffries led an AIEF-organized delegation to Israel in August 2023 and declared the party would "stand with Israel… period, full stop"; through the war he opposed conditioning military assistance, and in July 2026 announced he would vote against an amendment to cut US funding to Israel (The Intercept reporting, 2023; Quds News Network and HBCU News reporting, November 2026 — partisan-leaning sources, used here for the dissenters' stated position, not as findings about the war). On 18 November 2026, eleven Democrats voted against his Speaker nomination; seven of them — Reps.-elect Darializa Avila Chevalier, Adam Hamawy, Frederick Haynes, Melat Kiros, Donavan McKinney, Chris Rabb, and Claire Valdez — said publicly they were still seeking commitments including ending US military aid to Israel and action on Gaza, alongside Medicare for All (AP, 18 November 2026). Whether his Israel position is right is the platform, which the mandate rule forbids grading; what Test 4 grades is that a named group of his own incoming members experienced his leadership as not counting them, said so, and were answered with a unity speech ("our job is to fight MAGA extremism, not each other") rather than a commitment (C-SPAN, 18 November 2026).
  • Immigrant families bore the cost of the one whip he declined to run. On 22 January 2026, after the killing of Renee Nicole Good by an ICE agent in Minneapolis, Jeffries and his leadership team announced they would vote no on the Homeland Security funding bill for lack of guardrails on ICE — "Kristi Noem and ICE are out of control" — but leadership mounted no formal whip operation against it, and seven Democrats (Reps. Don Davis, Tom Suozzi, Henry Cuellar, Vicente Gonzalez, Laura Gillen, Marie Gluesenkamp Perez, and Jared Golden) supplied the margin in the 220–207 passage (Fox News and AP reporting, 22 January 2026; Jeffries joint leadership statement, 22 January 2026). The seven are named because the roll call is the documented record. A leader may choose not to whip; the people the un-whipped vote funded enforcement against are still the ones who paid for the choice.
  • The ACA count, won in his chamber, was lost where it landed. The enhanced credits expired on 31 December 2025; the House-passed extension sat on the Senate calendar from 10 February 2026 with no vote, and by mid-2026 roughly 5 million people had dropped marketplace coverage against the prior year's record high, with average out-of-pocket premiums up roughly 58% in 2026 (NPR figures via LegalClarity, 2026; Congress.gov, H.R. 1834 status). Jeffries did not cause the Senate's stall — the framing of this dossier forbids charging him with a gavel he didn't hold — but Test 4's ledger records outcomes as well as efforts, and the outcome for the 24 million people the shutdown was fought over is that they were counted in the argument and not, in the event, in the law. This is the two-axes crossing named in Test 5: the word was kept in his chamber, and people were hurt anyway.

Why: The grade is MIXED because the counting is genuinely broad where his tools reach — workers, veterans, Haitian migrants, aid recipients abroad, the poor of both parties' districts, the survivors and the fallen officers — and genuinely thin where his coalition's internal dissent or his own tactical restraint decides: Palestinian-aligned and progressive members of his own incoming class voting no on his name over Gaza, and immigrant families funded against by a bill his leadership opposed in words and declined to whip in fact. He counts everyone he can reach with a petition; not everyone he could reach with a decision.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — nearly one million federal workers (bargaining restoration passed the House); Haitian TPS holders; combat-wounded veterans under the Star Act; Ukraine's defenders in April 2024 and after; Epstein survivors pressing for the files; Medicaid and SNAP households in every district, whose cuts his caucus unanimously opposed and documented. Hurt — the roughly 5 million people who left marketplace coverage in 2026 after the credits lapsed (a Senate-caused outcome, recorded here as outcome, not as his act); immigrant families subject to the ICE operations the January 2026 bill funded without the guardrails he named; and the eleven-member dissenting bloc of his own caucus, seven of whom put Gaza and military aid in writing as their reason.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 3 January 2023, with dates. For a minority leader, the platform carried is the opposition mandate their voters elected: the counter-case, the protections named, and the promise to win the gavel back. Carrying out that platform is FOR-shelf evidence; marks are lost only for HOW.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The opposition mandate — carried, at a scale the office rarely reaches. Elected in 2022 and 2024 on the promise to stand as the organized check, his caucus held unanimously against the 2025 megabill (218–214, not one Democratic yes), forced the Epstein files vote over the Speaker's objection, restored federal workers' bargaining rights on the House floor, extended Haiti TPS, and passed the ACA extension — seven discharge-petition bills in one Congress on the caucus's count, against a historical norm of near zero (House Democrats, 13 November 2026; AP, July 2025 and January 2026). Under the mandate rule, this is the platform his voters elected, delivered with the only tools the seat holds.
  • A specific, dated word — kept to the letter. On 30 April 2024 Jeffries and his leadership team stated: "We will vote to table Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene's Motion to Vacate the Chair. If she invokes the motion, it will not succeed." On 8 May 2024 the motion was tabled, 359–43 (Jeffries/Clark/Aguilar statement, 30 April 2024; AP, 8 May 2024).
  • A small word — kept, barely, but kept. Through October 2025 Jeffries promised he would articulate his Mamdani position "in advance of early voting." Early voting began 25 October 2025; the endorsement arrived 24 October 2025 (Reuters, 24 October 2025). The letter of the promise was honored on its last possible day.
  • The largest word — kept at the second asking. The standing promise of a minority leader is to win the House back. It failed in 2024 (below) and was then kept beyond the asking: on 3 November 2026 Democrats won at least 259 seats, the party's best House result in decades, and on 18 November 2026 the caucus that had twice elected him unanimously in the minority nominated him for Speaker 251–11 (AP, November 2026). Two axes, named: this is crossing 1 — word kept, and it helped the people who elected the opposition to become the government.
  • The ACA word in his own chamber — kept. He promised to force the three-year extension to a vote and pass it in the House; the discharge petition did, 230–196 on 8 January 2026, with 17 Republicans joining (ABC News, 8 January 2026; Congress.gov, H.R. 1834). What happened in the Senate is recorded in the against shelf as outcome, not as his breach — and the crossing is named there.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The 2024 word — not kept on schedule. The minority leader's 2024 promise to his voters was a Democratic House; the 2024 election returned a Republican majority (218 Republicans to 215 Democrats at the 119th Congress's convening, on the pre-election baseline of 218–214 with vacancies, per NACo's analysis). Jeffries kept the seat and the caucus's unanimity, and the promise was redeemed in 2026 — but a word kept two years late is recorded as late, and the two years between were governed by the trifecta his voters had been promised would be stopped.
  • The ACA word as outcome — crossing 2, named. Kept their word AND it still hurt people: Jeffries did everything the office allows — shutdown stand, discharge petition, House passage — and the enhanced credits still expired, the Senate never voted, roughly 5 million people left the marketplace, and premiums rose roughly 58% on average in 2026 (Congress.gov; NPR via LegalClarity, 2026). The keeping earns the credit recorded above; the hurt is recorded here in full, and neither launders the other.
  • The Hegseth word — announced as settled, delivered seven short. On 8 November 2026 Jeffries called himself "a hard yes on the Hegseth resolution" and said the Secretary "has got to go."
