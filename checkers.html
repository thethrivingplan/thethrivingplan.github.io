How to play — tap to open

The aim: capture all of the other side's pieces, or leave them with no legal move. Either way wins.

Moving: pieces move one square diagonally forward on dark squares. Tap your piece, then tap a glowing square — or drag the piece. Esc puts a selected piece back down.

Jumping: jump diagonally over a next-door enemy piece onto the empty square just beyond it, and that piece is captured. In standard rules a jump is forced — if you can jump, you must. If the same piece can jump again after landing, it must keep jumping in the same turn: double and triple jumps are one move.

Kings: a piece that reaches the far row is crowned — it gets a crown and from then on moves and jumps both forward and backward.

Draws: tap Call it a draw to offer one. Against the bot, it accepts when it is not ahead. A game also ends drawn after 80 half-moves with no capture and no ordinary piece moving — only kings shuffling cannot drag a game out forever.

Undo & Hint: Undo takes back the last move (against the bot, yours and the bot's, so it is your turn again). Hint lays out the real ways to play from here — the search's top pick and the genuine alternatives, each with what it costs and buys. It names a single move only when there is genuinely one legal move.

About the bot levels — tap to open

The four levels are built differently, not just named differently. Learning searches 1 move ahead and takes a random legal move about 4 times in 10. Steady searches 3 ahead and slips about 1 time in 10. Sharp searches 5 ahead with no random moves. Pro searches 7 ahead with move ordering and the full evaluation — material, crowning, advancement, centre control and mobility — and no random moves ever.

That gap is real by construction: a bot that can only see one move ahead cannot defend a piece that is attacked in two, and the Pro search sees the whole exchange. If a level ever played the same as another, that would be a bug, not a feature — the depths and slip rates printed here are the exact numbers the code uses.

How pros think — tap to open

Four ideas checkers players actually train on, in plain words. The little boards are real positions, set up the way the text describes. The teaching notes under the board point at these ideas when they show up in your game.

1 · Control the centre

The outlined squares are the centre. A piece standing there can move or jump toward either side of the board; a piece on the edge has half as many moves and can be trapped against the wall. Pros build toward the middle first, trade away enemy pieces that sit there, and only use the edges to slip past. The other school plays it patient — hold the back rows, guard your crowning squares, and let the other side overreach: you give up the middle, and in return your home squares can never be rushed. Neither school is the one way; pros pick between them from the position in front of them.

2 · Force trades when you're ahead

Every swap shrinks the board. Here Black is a piece up (four against three) and the jump starts a one-for-one trade — after it, Black is still a piece up, but with fewer pieces left for Red to catch up with. The side behind runs out of pieces and chances first. So: ahead, offer swaps and make the other side take them. Behind, refuse swaps, keep your pieces on, and hunt for jumps that change the count.

3 · The opposition

When two kings stare at each other down a diagonal with one empty square between them — the outlined square here — whoever has to move must step aside and give ground, and the other king walks in behind. Holding that stare so the other side has to break it is called having the opposition. It is how a lone king wins ground it could never take by force, so pros count whose move it is before stepping into the stare-down.

4 · King endgames

Once kings are out, the game changes: ordinary pieces become targets, because a king hunts in both directions. One king against two loose pieces — like this corner position — is often a win for the king: it herds them toward a corner, where they have the fewest escapes, and picks them off one at a time. Pros defending here keep their pieces guarding each other and their own king close; pros attacking keep their king between the enemy king and the pieces it wants to save.
