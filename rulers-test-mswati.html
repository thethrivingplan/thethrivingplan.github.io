The Ruler's Test — Mswati III, King of Eswatini

Subject: Mswati III (born Makhosetive Dlamini, 19 April 1968) — King of Eswatini, reigning since his coronation on 25 April 1986, when he succeeded his father Sobhuza II at eighteen. The seat graded is the kingship itself, and the reason is stated plainly: Eswatini is sub-Saharan Africa's last absolute monarchy, and the king is the executive seat in substance, not in ceremony. Under the 2005 Constitution he promulgated, executive authority vests in the King, supreme legislative authority vests in the King, and he appoints the prime minister, the cabinet, the judges, and the traditional chiefs through whom the country is governed; the prime minister — currently Russell Mmiso Dlamini, appointed by the King in November 2023 after the September 2023 elections — serves at his pleasure. Political parties have been banned since Sobhuza II's decree of 12 April 1973, which Mswati has never rescinded in forty years on the throne; elections are held every five years under the tinkhundla system, in which voters choose individuals, never parties or programs. He is confirmed in office at grading: he marked forty years on the throne at a Ruby Jubilee ceremony at Somhlolo National Stadium on 25 April 2026, attended by foreign heads of state, and was photographed on official business in October 2026. He is graded on the full reign since 1986, weighted to recent years, because the structures the tests examine — the party ban, the appointment powers, the off-budget royal finances — are his standing arrangements, not episodes. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic of the Test, printed on its card above the five tests, governs this dossier: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule, as it applies to this seat: the Test's standing rule is that governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure — the Test grades the holding, never the platform. An absolute monarch has no elected platform, and that absence is recorded here rather than filled in. There is no voters' program to grade him against, so the platform of record for this dossier is twofold: his own public undertakings, given in his own voice over forty years, and the 2005 Constitution he signed into law — including its bill of rights and its judicial-independence provisions — which is the nearest thing to a contract between this seat and the nation. Carrying out his own stated undertakings is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost, as with any ruler, only for HOW power is held: a second law for his own house (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), refusing to look when another option becomes available (Test 3), and counting out those who did not choose him — here, everyone, since no one did (Test 4).

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The state's two great universal programs run without a loyalty test. Free antiretroviral treatment was rolled out to everyone living with HIV — the government procured some 80% of antiretroviral drugs itself, treatment on diagnosis became policy in 2016, and the country reached the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets in 2020, the first in Africa to do so (WHO Regional Office for Africa; UNAIDS, 2024). Free primary education, promised in the 2005 Constitution (section 29(6)), was phased in from January 2010 and extended to Grade 7 by 2015, applying to every child in a public school (IRIN, January 2010; UNESCO GEM Report education profile). In a system without parties, neither program asks who a family supports before it treats or teaches them.
  • The state's own watchdog once published against the state's interest. The Eswatini Commission on Human Rights and Public Administration — a body whose members the King appoints — released a preliminary report on 29 October 2021 recording 46 people killed and 245 suffering gunshot injuries in the June 2021 crackdown, and noted its own findings likely underestimated the toll (Commission preliminary report, via Human Rights Watch, October 2025). The number was allowed to exist in public.
  • The law does sometimes reach the connected, at the lower levels. In November 2026 the Anti-Corruption Commission arrested seven accused — including a serving police officer — over an alleged E960,000 fraud against the Reconstruction Fund established after the 2021 unrest; the accused were granted bail by the High Court on 19 November 2026 (Independent News Eswatini and Eswatini Observer, November 2026). The charges are ALLEGATIONs, denied by at least one accused, and no verdict exists; the shelf records that process was started, not that guilt was shown.
  • The royal trust structure is, in its formal account, a trust and not a private purse: Tibiyo Taka Ngwane, the national investment fund, is held by the King in trust for the Swazi nation — the official account, printed here because it is the seat's own answer to the favoritism charge. Critics contest what the trusteeship means in practice (Daily Maverick reporting, via reference sources), and the against shelf below records why the form does not settle the question.

Evidence against:

  • One law stops at the palace gate by constitutional design. The King is constitutionally unimpeachable — no court may try him — while the Sedition and Subversive Activities Act of 1938 makes uttering criticism of the King itself a crime carrying up to 20 years (PPLAAF country assessment, 2025; ICJ, January 2025). There is a law for the man who criticizes the King, and no law that can reach the King: that is two laws at the source, written into the structure rather than committed in a moment.
  • The enforcement map tracks the political map exactly. The two MPs who called for reform, Mduduzi Bacede Mabuza and Mthandeni Dube, were prosecuted under the Suppression of Terrorism Act and the 1938 Sedition Act, convicted in a trial the IPU's observer and Amnesty International found marred by serious due-process violations, and sentenced in July 2024 — cumulative counts of 85 and 58 years as Amnesty International reports them; effective terms of 25 and 18 years as the Times of Eswatini reports them (Amnesty International Urgent Action, 26 January 2026; Times of Eswatini, 2026). The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention, in Opinion No. 55/2025 (28 August 2025), found both men's detention arbitrary and called for their immediate release; Mabuza remains in prison at grading, his 2024 appeal not yet enrolled (Amnesty International, January 2026; Times of Eswatini, 2026). No member of the security forces, by contrast, has been held to account for any of the 2021 killings in more than five years (Human Rights Watch report, 30 October 2025).
  • The country's most prominent reform lawyer was murdered, and the law has produced nothing. Thulani Maseko, chair of the Multi-Stakeholder Forum, was shot dead at home in front of his wife and children on 21 January 2023, hours after the King publicly warned those calling for democratic reforms that mercenaries would deal with them (Human Rights Watch World Report 2024). Activists allege state involvement; the government denies it; no perpetrator has been identified by any finding, and the allegation is recorded as an ALLEGATION. What is documented is the aftermath: nearly four years on, no one has been apprehended, no findings published, and Maseko's widow states she has heard nothing from the police despite repeated inquiries — while a government statement of 29 December 2023, issued after she accepted a human rights award in his name, attacked her personally (Human Rights Watch, January 2024 and World Report 2026). The government's September 2026 response to UN experts states the investigation remains active with a breakthrough pending.
  • The royal household is financed by a second, unaudited channel alongside the budget. The royal family receives about SZL 1 billion a year from the general budget, plus the revenues of Tibiyo Taka Ngwane — controlled by the King, excluded from the national budget, and not subject to taxation (IMF and BTI reporting, 2025–2026). Forbes's 2009 richest-royals list placed his personal fortune in the low hundreds of millions of US dollars (figures of US$100–200 million appear in Forbes-derived reporting). In November 2019, as poverty stood near three-fifths of the population, The Times reported £13 million spent on luxury cars for his wives; photography of the King's vehicles had earlier been banned after criticism of a reported US$500,000 Maybach purchase (The Times, 11 November 2019, via UK Parliament Research Briefing 9316; contemporaneous reporting). The contrast is graded under Test 4 as well; here it is a one-law fact: the nation's largest single beneficiary of public money is the one person no audit, tax, or court can reach.
  • His own decrees bind him last. In 2001 he invoked the umcwasho chastity rite, forbidding sexual relations for the nation's maidens for five years; within the ban's own period he took a new fiancée, and settled the breach of his own decree with the customary fine of one cow (contemporaneous reporting, BBC, 2002). A small file from early in the reign, kept because Test 1 grades the source: at the source, the rule-maker fines himself a cow where his subjects face prison.

Why the grade lands here: the FOR shelf is real — treatment and schooling reach everyone, and process occasionally starts against the connected — but each item on it is a program or a beginning, while the against shelf is the structure itself: immunity for one man, prison for his critics, impunity for his soldiers, and a parallel treasury for his house. A law that cannot reach the lawgiver, his family, or his forces is not one law, however evenly the clinics are run.

Helped: the royal household and extended family — a budget allocation plus off-budget trust revenues, beyond audit or tax; the security forces — five years of complete impunity for the 2021 shootings; people living with HIV and schoolchildren, genuinely — the universal programs are the reign's strongest one-law fact. Hurt: the families of the 2021 dead, still without a single prosecution; Mduduzi Bacede Mabuza, imprisoned under a detention the UN's own working group calls arbitrary; Thulani Maseko's widow and children, answered with a government attack rather than an investigation; and every taxpayer funding an allocation no one may audit.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The grip is not total in practice, and the gaps are documented. On 9 April 2026 the Supreme Court dismissed, with costs, an appeal by the King's own correctional services and attorney-general, and confirmed that a lawyer must be given access to the detained US deportees at Matsapha — a ruling against the executive, in the executive's own case, which the executive then obeyed (Supreme Court of Eswatini, [2026] SZSC 14, 9 April 2026). The 2021 preliminary human-rights report was published (Test 1). Parliament questions ministers in the open: in November 2025 Finance Minister Neal Rijkenberg told the chamber his ministry had been kept in the dark about the US deportee money and that the transaction was handled by the prime minister's office — a minister saying, on the record, that the palace's deal bypassed his own ministry (reporting of parliamentary proceedings, November 2025).
  • Elections happen on schedule and are locally real. Tinkhundla elections were held in September 2023 and are due again around 2028; voters choose individual MPs, and MPs so chosen have used the chamber to criticize the government's record, including, in November 2026, a member's public admission that two decades of anti-corruption pledges have failed (domestic reporting, November 2026). Eswatini also rose in the RSF World Press Freedom Index, from 131st in 2022 to 85th in 2024, after the Broadcasting Act's enactment opened space beyond the state broadcaster (Afrobarometer Dispatch 824, citing RSF 2024). These are recorded at full strength: the seat permits a chamber, a calendar, and some print.

Evidence against:

  • By constitutional design, every referee's chair traces back to his hand. The King appoints the prime minister, the cabinet, the judges, and the traditional chiefs who constitute the entire governance apparatus; executive and supreme legislative authority vest in him personally (ICJ, January 2025; IMF/World Bank-cited governance assessments, 2025–2026). He may dissolve parliament. The Judicial Service Commission that advises on judges is, in the International Commission of Jurists' February 2025 assessment, neither adequate nor independent in its appointment process, and the UN Special Rapporteur on judges and lawyers has flagged the use of temporary judicial appointments without security of tenure and the judiciary's alleged close ties to the royal family (ICJ, February 2025; ICJ UPR submission, April 2026). Freedom House scores the country 17 of 100, Not Free (Freedom House, 2025).
  • The one time the referees ruled against the palace as a body, the palace abolished the match. In November 2002 the Court of Appeal held that the King could not override parliament by decree and that the police commissioner was in contempt for blocking court-ordered returns of evicted families at Macetjeni and KaMkhweli — some 200 residents the army had expelled in 2000 for refusing the King's brother, Prince Maguga, as their chief. The King's prime minister announced on 28 November 2002 that the government "does not intend to recognise the two judgements of the Court of Appeal"; the entire six-judge appeal bench resigned in protest, the DPP resigned, and the country was left without its highest court for two years (ICJ/CIJL fact-finding report, June 2003; International Bar Association mission report, March 2003; UN Special Rapporteur statement, 4 December 2002). The precedent stands unreversed in kind: a court that rules against the seat rules at the seat's sufferance.
  • The current bench illustrates the mechanism. Chief Justice Bheki Maphalala died in office in 2026 after almost eleven years. The King has appointed as Acting Chief Justice Mumcy Dlamini — the same judge who presided over the trial and sentencing of Mabuza and Dube — in an acting capacity, the form of appointment the UN Special Rapporteur identifies as the tenure risk: an acting chief justice holds the office at the pleasure of the man who appointed her (Times of Eswatini, November 2026; ICJ UPR submission, April 2026).
  • The media's referees are owned, exiled, or charged. The state broadcaster is the government's; the Swazi Observer sits inside the Tibiyo royal trust structure; and in 2025 the Times of Eswatini group — the main independent daily — was acquired by a businessman whose interests are intertwined with state power, leaving, in the assessment of a February 2026 media-threats study, regime influence over almost all leading platforms (Intelwatch, A Press Under Siege, February 2026). The editor of the online Swaziland News, Zweli Martin Dlamini, has been in exile in South Africa since a 2020 police raid on his home; in 2022 the government declared both him and his publication "terrorist entities" under the Suppression of Terrorism Act (Afrobarometer Dispatch 824, citing MISA and CPJ). Sedition law makes criticism of the King a crime; journalists, in the same study's finding, routinely self-censor on the monarchy, the security forces, and corruption.
  • The electoral referee excludes the contest itself. Parties remain banned under the never-rescinded 1973 decree; PUDEMO, the largest opposition movement, is proscribed under the terrorism act. The 2023–2028 parliament was elected in September 2023 under rules in which no organized alternative to the system may stand (election reporting, 2023; CRCA encyclopedia assessment, 2026). A referee who bars one side from the field is not a referee the seat refrains from gripping; it is the grip, codified.
  • The deportee file shows the grip applied to the courts' doorway. When civil society challenged the secret US deportee agreement as unconstitutional for want of parliamentary ratification, the High Court dismissed the case on 3 February 2026 on standing grounds — the applicants could not even obtain a copy of the agreement to plead it — and never reached the substance ([2026] SZHC 105; Reuters, 4 February 2026). The one ruling the courts did land, lawyer access, took nine months of detention to obtain (Test 1).

Why the grade lands here: the FOR shelf shows the grip has gaps — a court ruling obeyed, a minister speaking out of turn, a chamber that sometimes bites. But Test 2 grades who holds the referees, and here the answer is constitutional and singular: he appoints them, he can unmake them, he has unmade them before (2002), the opposition may not organize against him, the press that covers him is owned by his trust or exiled by his prosecutors, and the chief justice now serves in an acting capacity after sentencing his two most prominent parliamentary critics. Independence that exists only where he does not reach for it is not independence held; it is independence lent.

Helped: the King and the royal house — no institution in the country can compel, remove, audit, or outvote him; appointees at every level, whose tenure runs through his favor; the ruling circle around the prime minister's office, which handled a US$5.1 million international agreement without the finance ministry or parliament. Hurt: the judiciary and the legal profession — the ICJ records lawyers facing adverse consequences for taking political cases, and a profession chilled since Maseko's killing; journalists and their readers; voters, who may choose persons but never a direction; and the deportees at Matsapha, whose constitutional challenge died at the standing doorway.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • He changed the government at the height of the crisis. On 16 July 2021, with the country still burning from the June unrest, the King convened the Sibaya at Ludzidzini and replaced the acting prime minister, installing Cleopas Dlamini — a personnel adjustment made in public, in the traditional forum, within three weeks of the worst violence of his reign (Amnesty International campaign record, November 2021).
  • He accepted, at least in form, the region's proposed remedy. In November 2021, after SADC fact-finding missions and a visit by South African President Cyril Ramaphosa as chair of the SADC Organ, the King agreed that the kingdom "will embark on a process that will work towards the establishment of a National Dialogue forum" (SADC reporting via ACCORD; US Senate Resolution 174, 2023). The agreement is printed here at full strength: the wobble was heard, and a door was named.
  • He has since complied with the one court ruling that went against his government in the deportee file (the April 2026 lawyer-access order, Test 2), and his government has processed the deportee arrangement toward its stated end — voluntary repatriations proceeding in the open, 13 third-country nationals returned by 3 November 2026 on the government's own count (government statements via Eswatini Positive News, November 2026). When the policy is his, events are answered with administration rather than denial.
  • Mercy exists in the system, and it moved once. On 5 November 2025 Mthandeni Dube was released under a Special Royal Pardon (Amnesty International, January 2026). The conditions and the route are graded on the against shelf; the release itself is an adjustment, and it is recorded as one.

Evidence against:

  • The first answer to the wobble was a ban and live ammunition. The protests began in May 2021 over the death in police custody of law student Thabani Nkomonye. When petitions calling for democratic reform were being delivered to MPs across the country, the government — by the King's decree, as Human Rights Watch records it — banned the delivery of petitions on 25 June 2021, and the security forces' crackdown followed (HRW World Report 2022). The tolls are kept in their separate counts, as this Test requires: the national Human Rights Commission's preliminary report recorded 46 killed and 245 gunshot injuries, and warned the figures likely understated the toll (Commission preliminary report, 29 October 2021); Amnesty International's timeline records over 70 people allegedly killed in the days after the ban and a toll rising above 80 by 18 October 2021 (Amnesty International, November 2021); Human Rights Watch's count is at least 46 killed, with hundreds injured, including children (HRW, October 2025). The internet was shut down at the height of the killing (HRW World Report 2022). Whether the King left the country at the peak is contested — reports said he had fled to South Africa; the government denied he had fled — and is recorded as contested. What is not contested is the order of answers: prohibition first, force second, conversation last.
  • The promised conversation was redefined until it was a monologue, then refused. The November 2021 agreement named a national dialogue. The King ruled it would take place through the Sibaya — the traditional gathering at the royal kraal at which, in the form he convened it, he alone speaks. After much stalling he called the nation to Sibaya in July 2023 and announced elections; a further Sibaya in October 2023 announced there would be no political dialogue. Both gatherings were, in the Institute for Security Studies' assessment, "de facto monologues by the monarch; no participants were allowed to speak, make submissions or respond" (ISS Today, 2026). In January 2023 his prime minister had already told a SADC summit that an inclusive multi-stakeholder dialogue was "impossible" in the current climate (HRW World Report 2024). SADC removed Eswatini from its agenda in August 2024. Five years after the unrest, the ISS assessment is that he "has done nothing to address the causes," and a Swazi human rights lawyer quoted there calls the present calm "enforced silence" (ISS Today, 2026).
  • The other-option rule, applied by its mechanics: in November 2021 another option became available — an inclusive, SADC-supported national dialogue, on the table with regional backing and the opposition organized and asking for exactly that forum. The rule does not require accepting an option; it requires at least considering it openly, and it expressly provides that considering and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence. What the record shows is not open consideration followed by a stated refusal. It shows acceptance in November 2021, two years of redefinition into a format in which the other side cannot speak, and a refusal delivered in October 2023 inside that format — the option declined without ever having been looked at in the form in which it was offered. The stated reasons on the record (the climate of violence, per his prime minister in January 2023; the claim that tinkhundla is the nation's own chosen system) are printed here as his side; the MSF's counter — that the delay and redefinition were the refusal, conducted slowly — is printed as the disputants' side. The grade rests on the sequence, which neither side disputes.
  • The no-good-choice clause, applied by its mechanics: the nights of late June 2021 presented a genuine trap, and this dossier names it rather than pretending otherwise. The options actually open were: deploy the security forces to retake streets on which arson and looting were underway and roadblocks had turned lethal — two men, Siphosethu Mntshali and Thando Shongwe, were killed at a roadblock on 29 June 2021, the deaths for which Mabuza and Dube were later convicted — or hold the forces back and leave the burning and the roadblocks to run. Each carried real harm, and the choice to restore order is not held against him here. What remains gradable, under the clause's own terms, is the rest: the trap's origin — a political system with every lawful channel of dissent closed, capped by a decree banning the petitions themselves, so that the street was the only forum left; the honesty inside the trap — the toll was never fully counted by the state, the Commission's final report never released; and whether the least-harm door was taken once the streets were retaken — the five years of impunity and the refused dialogue graded above. The clause covers the weekend. It does not cover the decade around it.
  • The mercy on the FOR shelf shows its price on this one. Dube's pardon was not routed through the courts whose verdict it displaced. He withdrew from the appeal process and instead engaged the Minister for Justice, Prince Simelane, as his emissary to carry a personal apology to the King; the pardon followed, conditioned on silence — no press, no social media, no political activity, no public speaking, until his sentence would have expired (Times of Eswatini, 2026; Amnesty International, January 2026). In September 2026 the Times of Eswatini reported there was "still no trace" of Dube: he had disappeared amid a correctional-services investigation into an alleged audio recording said to breach those conditions, and his family said they did not know where he was. Mabuza, who did not apologize, remains in prison. The wobble's answer, in its final form: submission is the key that turns.

Why the grade lands here: every pushback of the reign meets the same sequence — the courts in 2002 (defiance), the street in 2021 (ban, then bullets, then shutdown), the region in 2021–2023 (agreement, redefinition, refusal), the UN's detention experts in 2025 (silence; Mabuza still inside). The adjustments on the FOR shelf are real but they are all of one kind: changes of personnel, procedure, and individual mercy — never a change in the distribution of voice. When another option became available, he did not consider it in the open; he renamed it until it was his own monologue, and then closed it.

Helped: the seat itself — each wobble has ended with the King's authority intact and, after 2023, formally unchallengeable in any forum he recognizes; the security establishment, never called to account; Mthandeni Dube, freed — at the price recorded. Hurt: the 2021 dead and injured, in their separate counts above, and their families, who received neither a final count nor a prosecution; the pro-democracy movement — its MPs imprisoned or exiled (a third reform MP, Mduduzi Simelane, fled in 2021 and remains abroad), its forum's chair murdered and unavenged, its requested dialogue refused; and the nation's own stated grievances of 2021 — service delivery, poverty, unemployment, exclusion — which the ISS records as entirely unaddressed five years on.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition, the protesters, the poor, the detained, and those with no claim on the seat's favor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • In health, the count is genuinely universal, and it is the reign's finest file. The country carries one of the world's highest HIV prevalence rates — 25.9% of adults 15–49 in 2022, some 223,000 people living with HIV (UNAIDS country data, 2024) — and the state he heads declared HIV a national emergency in the late 1990s, built a coordinating council in the prime minister's office (NERCHA, 2001), provided free antiretroviral treatment, procured roughly 80% of the drugs itself, and in 2020 became the first country in Africa to achieve the 95-95-95 targets, ten years ahead of the 2030 goal; incidence among adults fell from 1.44 in 2016 to 0.62 in 2021 (WHO Regional Office for Africa; UNAIDS, 2024). The health authorities themselves credit community organizations alongside the state — the shelf records the achievement as the system's, under his seat. Nobody was asked their politics at the clinic door. This is what counting everyone looks like, and it is printed at full strength.
  • Schooling counts everyone too: free primary education reached every public-school child by 2015 (Test 1). And the state's planning still speaks in universal terms: on 20 November 2026 the government unveiled a E1.7 billion Child Poverty Analysis Action Plan targeting a cut in child poverty from 56.5% to 40% by 2028 (government announcement, November 2026). The target is a promise, graded in Test 5's terms later; here it records that the poor are, at minimum, counted in the plan.

Evidence against:

  • The political count excludes by design everyone who would organize. A fifth of the adult population may vote — for individuals, inside a system in which parties are banned, the largest opposition movement is a proscribed terrorist entity, its MPs are in prison or exile, and the head of its dialogue forum was murdered without consequence (Tests 1–3). Emaswati are counted as subjects at the clinic and the school gate; as citizens with a collective political will, they are not counted at all. Afrobarometer's late-2022 survey found citizens broadly insisting the media should act as a watchdog and rejecting state control of information (Afrobarometer Dispatch 824) — the counted, asking to count for more, in the only instrument that asks them.
  • The 2021 dead were counted halfway and then uncounted. His own Commission's preliminary count — 46 dead, 245 gunshot injuries, itself flagged as an underestimate — was published in October 2021; the final report has never been released in the five years since, no comprehensive investigation has followed, and no family has received a prosecution, a finding, or reparations (HRW, October 2025). A count begun by the state and abandoned by the state is a decision about who counts, taken twice.
  • The poor are the majority, and the arithmetic of the house runs the other way. Poverty figures are kept in their separate counts: the World Bank's 2022 measurement puts 32% of the population below US$2.15 a day and 55% below US$3.65 a day (World Bank, 2022); the IMF-cited national measure puts poverty at 59% (IMF, 2023); the government's own November 2026 plan works from child poverty of 56.5%. Unemployment stands at about 34% overall and 58% among the youth (IMF/BTI reporting, 2025–2026). Against those counts stands the other count, from Test 1: roughly SZL 1 billion a year from the budget to the royal family, plus an off-budget, untaxed royal trust, in a country of some 1.2–1.3 million people. The poor are counted in the plans. In the budget's first charge, the house is counted first.
  • The newest counted-out are not his subjects at all. Under the agreement of 14 May 2025, Eswatini accepted up to 160 third-country nationals deported from the United States in exchange for US$5.1 million, confirmed by the Finance Minister in November 2025 (ACE joint statement, 2026; parliamentary reporting, November 2025). The counts are kept separate: the Africa Coalition Against Externalization records five flights transferring 32 people by 27 August 2026; the government states 31 received and 13 repatriated by 3 November 2026 (government statement, November 2026). The men are held at Matsapha Correctional Complex, a maximum-security prison, without charge; a US lawyer cited by Human Rights Watch counted at least 19 of them holding US findings of protection against return to their home countries — men who cannot lawfully be sent home and cannot be freed where they are, held, in the government's own word, "in transit," for over a year (International Relations Review, October 2026; HRW, 2025). They appear in the kingdom's accounts as revenue. In no other ledger do they appear at all.

Why the grade lands here: the FOR shelf proves the seat can count everyone when it chooses — the HIV file is a world-class act of counting, done for the same population, under the same king. The against shelf shows the choice's boundary: the count stops exactly where counting would cost the seat power or money — the opposition uncounted as a political force, the protest dead uncounted to the end, the poor counted in plans and charged after the palace, and thirty-odd foreign detainees counted only as income. A ruler who counts everyone at the clinic and no one at the ballot box has shown the Test the difference between his capacity and his practice.

Helped: people living with HIV — 223,000 of them, in a program that reached global targets a decade early; every child in a public primary school; the royal household, counted first in the fiscus. Hurt: the 2021 dead, whose count the state began and buried; the political nation — parties, MPs, editors, lawyers — counted as criminals or terrorists for asking to be counted; the 59% poor and the 58% of unemployed youth, counted in plans while the first charge on their taxes is a palace; and the men in Matsapha, counted as US$5.1 million.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Public undertakings and the constitution he swore to vs. the record since 1986, weighted to recent years. The platform of record, per the Method: his own stated undertakings, and the 2005 Constitution.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The HIV commitment — kept, at national scale. The state declared HIV a national emergency in the late 1990s and committed to ending AIDS as a public-health threat; the machinery was built (NERCHA, 2001), the drugs were made free and then immediate on diagnosis (2016), and the 95-95-95 targets were achieved in 2020, a decade early, with incidence more than halved between 2016 and 2021 (WHO Regional Office for Africa; UNAIDS, 2024). This was a word given to a dying nation and kept in front of the world. It is the heaviest single item on either shelf of this test.
  • Free primary education — kept, late. The 2005 Constitution (section 29(6)) required free primary education beginning within three years; it began in 2010, a year beyond the constitution's own timetable even on the government's account, and was completed to Grade 7 by 2015 (Ministry of Education and Training; UNESCO profile). A kept word with a recorded delay.
  • The system's permanence — kept exactly. For forty years the King has said the tinkhundla system is the nation's own and would not be replaced, and that political parties would not return. Whatever else is graded elsewhere in this dossier, as a matter of word-keeping the undertaking has been performed to the letter, in every year, without exception. The Test records the keeping here; the harm the kept word does is recorded in Tests 1, 2, and 4, where it belongs.
  • Elections on the calendar — kept. Tinkhundla elections have been held at five-year intervals throughout the reign, most recently in September 2023, with the next due around 2028 — the procedural word of the system, kept on schedule, inside the limits graded in Test 2.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The national dialogue — the reign's central broken word. November 2021: agreement with SADC, announced by President Ramaphosa, that the kingdom would embark on a process toward a national dialogue. October 2023: the King's own announcement, at his own Sibaya, that there would be no political dialogue. Between the two dates stands the redefinition graded in Test 3. The word was given to end a crisis in which his forces had killed scores of his subjects; it was withdrawn once the region's attention moved on, and SADC formally closed the file in August 2024 (SADC reporting; ISS Today, 2026; US Senate Resolution 174, 2023).
  • Vision 2022 — the signature promise, missed in full. The National Development Strategy he championed promised first-world status by 2022, in his own repeated words: "In 2022, the country will be a first world country said King Mswati III" (BTI 2020 country report). The year 2022 arrived with poverty near three-fifths of the population, the world's highest HIV prevalence, and Freedom House scoring the country among the least free in the region. The promise was never owned as missed; by 2025–2026 court voices were re-narrating it as a miracle "already unfolding" (Eswatini Positive News, 2026). A word redefined after its date is a word broken with the record falsified afterward — the dossier records the break and the re-narration separately.
  • The constitution he signed — its rights provisions unperformed. The 2005 Constitution guarantees freedom of expression and of the media (section 24) and declares the judiciary "independent and subject only to this Constitution," not "subject to the control or direction of any person or authority" (ICJ UPR submission, April 2026, quoting the Constitution). The practice is graded in Tests 1 and 2: sedition prosecutions for criticizing the King, a judiciary appointed through a commission the ICJ finds neither adequate nor independent, an editor declared a terrorist entity for publishing. The constitution is the platform of record for this seat; its fine print is the part that has not been carried out.
  • The word given to the United Nations — given twice, unperformed twice. Eswatini accepted recommendations in its 2016 and 2021 Universal Periodic Reviews to strengthen judicial independence, investigate abuses against human rights defenders, and reform the laws restricting expression and association — including, in Amnesty International's account of the 2021 cycle, a supported recommendation to reform the Suppression of Terrorism Act and the Sedition Act (ICJ UPR submission, April 2026; Amnesty International UPR submission, April 2026). By the November 2026 review cycle, neither act had been reformed, the Maseko investigation had produced no apprehension, and the 2021 crackdown remained un-investigated (HRW World Report 2026). Accepting a recommendation at Geneva is a word; the record is the same file, twice.
  • Anti-corruption — two decades of pledges, admitted failed by his own chamber. The King has pledged action against corruption across the reign; in November 2026 a member of his own parliament stated publicly that two decades of anti-corruption pledges have failed, an Anti-Corruption Commission advisor described "untouchables" and legal bottlenecks crippling the fight at a forum attended by the Justice Minister, and reporting in the same week alleged — as an ALLEGATION, unproven — that an ACC assistant investigator had used threats of arrest to extort money and goods from schools (Times of Eswatini, 20 November 2026; domestic reporting digest, November 2026). The prosecutions that do proceed reach police officers and small companies (Test 1). The word's twenty-year trajectory is downward, in his own parliament's words.

The four crossings, named: 1. Kept his word AND it helped people: the HIV commitment — declared, funded, and delivered a decade early; and free primary education, delivered late but delivered. 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt people: the tinkhundla permanence pledge — forty years of exact fidelity to a promise whose content is the exclusion graded in Tests 1, 2, and 4. The keeping earns its mark on this test's FOR shelf; the hurt stands in those tests' ledgers in full, and neither cancels the other. 3. Went against his word AND it helped people: not documented in the record assembled here. The one candidate examined — the 2025 pardon of Mthandeni Dube, which set aside the outcome of his own courts' process — was an exercise of a lawful royal prerogative rather than a broken undertaking, and its help came conditioned on personal submission; it is recorded in Tests 1 and 3 and is not counted as this crossing. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt people: the national dialogue (2021–2023), the unperformed rights provisions of his own constitution, the twice-accepted and twice-unimplemented UN recommendations, and the anti-corruption pledges — each break landing on the same people: the reform movement that negotiated in good faith, the families awaiting investigations, and the public whose money the pledges were meant to protect.

Why the grade lands here: forty years is long enough for every open file to have closed, and the closed files divide cleanly. The words he kept are the words that cost the seat nothing or served it — the system's permanence, the election calendar — plus one great exception, the HIV commitment, which this dossier prints at full weight. The words he broke are the words that would have cost the seat something: a dialogue, a constitution's rights, an investigation, an audit with teeth, a development promise with a date on it. Vision 2022's date is four years past; the dialogue was refused in terms; the UN file has run two full cycles. A word kept only when it is free is not a kept word over time; it is a kept convenience. The HIV file is why this grade was weighed rather than assumed — and one kept word, however large, does not carry a forty-year ledger whose every expensive promise is in default.

Helped: the nation, on the HIV file — the kept word there is measured in hundreds of thousands of lives on treatment; schoolchildren, on the education file; the seat itself, whose permanence pledge has been the most reliably performed sentence of the reign. Hurt: the Multi-Stakeholder Forum and the reform movement, who took the dialogue word at face value and organized around it for two years; the families of Maseko and of the 2021 dead, promised investigations that remain "active" and "pending" as a form of words; and the public, promised first-world status by 2022 and an end to corruption across two decades, holding both receipts.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Mswati III is the second straight-fail card in this series, and the first earned by a hereditary seat. The five failures are one failure seen from five sides, and the dossier says so plainly: a man who cannot be prosecuted (Test 1), who appoints every referee and once watched the entire appeal bench resign rather than bend (Test 2), who answered the largest pushback of his reign with a petition ban and live fire and then refused the dialogue he had agreed to (Test 3), who counts his people at the clinic and not at the ballot box (Test 4), and whose kept words are the ones that cost him nothing (Test 5). The receipts are not thin: the tolls of June 2021 kept in their separate counts — his own Commission's 46 dead and 245 gunshot-wounded, Amnesty's higher running toll — with not one prosecution of a security-force member in five years; two MPs sentenced to effective 25 and 18 years for speeches, their detention declared arbitrary by the UN's working group, one freed only after a personal apology carried to the King, and reported missing within the year; a murdered lawyer, unavenged, whose widow is attacked by government statement; a billion emalangeni a year and an untaxed royal trust beside 59% poverty; and thirty-odd foreign men in a maximum-security prison, held without charge as a US$5.1 million line item. Against all of it stands the FOR shelf, which this dossier has printed at full strength because the method requires it and because it is genuinely there: the first 95-95-95 achievement in Africa, free schooling for every child, elections on their calendar, a court ruling against his own government obeyed in April 2026. The HIV file alone proves the seat capable of the thing the tests ask. That is what settles the grades. The same king who organized a nation to count every person living with a virus chose, for forty years, not to count them as citizens, not to hear them in a forum where they could answer back, and not to let any law reach the house that rules them. We don't pick who lands where they land. We just apply the grading system to the record — and on this record, the only complete holds remain the Sun and the six single-test holds, and Eswatini's king is not among them. The open files that could move these grades are named so the record can move when they do: Mabuza's release or a heard appeal; a final report and prosecutions for 2021; an apprehension in the Maseko case; a substantive, rather than acting, chief justice; a dialogue in which someone other than the King may speak.
