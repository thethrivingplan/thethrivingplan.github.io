The Ruler's Test — Alexandru Munteanu, Prime Minister of Moldova

Subject: Alexandru Munteanu — Prime Minister of Moldova from 1 November 2025 to 3 July 2026 (acting until 8 July 2026), graded here on that completed tenure. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Moldova is a parliamentary republic — the prime minister heads the government and holds executive power, while the president, Maia Sandu, is head of state with defined but lesser formal powers (she nominates the prime minister, who must win parliament's confidence). Sandu's role is nonetheless material to this record and is stated where it bears on a test: she is the de facto leader of the ruling Party of Action and Solidarity (PAS), she chose both prime ministers of this parliament, and in June 2026 she publicly intervened to pause her own prime minister's signature reform. The premise check matters and is printed in full: PAS won the parliamentary election of 28 September 2025 with 50.20% of the vote and 55 of 101 seats (turnout 52.21%), defeating the pro-Russian Patriotic Electoral Bloc (24.20%, 26 seats) (Central Electoral Commission results via OSW and International IDEA, September 2025). The incumbent prime minister, Dorin Recean (in office since 16 February 2023), announced on 13 October 2025 that he would step down and leave politics; Sandu nominated Munteanu — an economist and investment-firm founder, born 20 January 1964, who had worked abroad for some twenty years, including for the World Bank, and had never held political office — on 24 October 2025; parliament approved his government and its programme, "EU, Peace, Development," on 31 October 2025 with 55 votes, and he was sworn in on 1 November 2025 (Reuters, October 2025; gov.md, October–November 2025). On 3 July 2026, eight months in, Munteanu resigned without giving a specific reason, saying only that he could no longer carry out his mandate "in accordance with my principles and convictions" (Reuters, 3 July 2026). Because the premiership changed hands mid-term, this dossier follows the series' completed-tenure handling: it grades Munteanu's substantial, finished record, and states the succession plainly — Eugen Osmochescu acted as prime minister from 8 to 22 July 2026, and Vasile Tofan, a 44-year-old investor nominated by Sandu on 11 July, was approved by parliament on 21 July 2026 (53 PAS votes; 53–21 with 21 abstentions in the full count reported by AP) and sworn in on 22 July 2026 (Reuters and AP, July 2026; OSW, 23 July 2026). Tofan is the incumbent at grading, with roughly four months in office; his acts are recorded here only where they complete, confirm, or reverse an item of Munteanu's record, and he is not separately graded. Recean appears as context where the record Munteanu inherited bears directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure: PAS ran on EU accession, judicial reform, and anti-corruption, and carrying that platform out is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how it was done. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting figures are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • When the MoldATSA scandal broke inside his own camp, the law and the sackings ran inward, not only outward. On 18 June 2026 the investigative outlet Ziarul de Gardă reported that Dumitru Vangheli, director of MoldATSA (the state air-navigation enterprise), had included false information in his CV — a claimed Canadian pilot's licence and two years as an Air Canada pilot, contradicted by the airline's own records — while earning more than seven times the national average salary (Ziarul de Gardă investigation, 18 June 2026, via OSW, 7 July 2026). Vangheli was suspended and then dismissed effective 22 June; the National Anticorruption Centre (CNA) opened a criminal investigation, later handled by the Anti-Corruption Prosecutor's Office as a case of suspected aggravated abuse of office — an ALLEGATION, with no verdict recorded (OSW, July 2026; MoldovaLive, 2026). The head of the Public Property Agency, which had run the recruitment, resigned (OSW, July 2026).
  • The consequences reached the president's own family and the ruling party's own ranks. RISE Moldova reported that Anastasia Taburceanu, President Sandu's cousin, had been hired by MoldATSA without an open competition and paid more than one million lei in under a year, her monthly remuneration reaching roughly 120,000 lei in 2026 (RISE Moldova, June 2026). She resigned on 23 June and said she would return the money; Sandu called the payment "immoral" and "our failure," and audits of all 74 state-owned enterprises were ordered at the government's request (Logos Press, July 2026). PAS lawmaker Radu Marian resigned as chair of parliament's Economy, Budget and Finance Committee on 29 June, accepting responsibility for having recommended Vangheli — "I admit that I misjudged the person I recommended" — while denying any personal benefit or involvement in the company's operations (MoldovaLive and Logos Press, 29 June 2026). Tatiana Batin, chief of staff to the Speaker and Taburceanu's sister, also resigned on 26 June (Moldova Matters, June 2026).
  • The anti-corruption and anti-interference prosecutions of the opposing camp proceeded through courts, with verdicts that survived appeal on his watch. The Chișinău Court of Appeal on 28 May 2026 upheld the seven-year sentence of Evghenia Guțul, the former governor (bashkan) of Gagauzia, convicted in August 2025 of complicity in the illegal financing of the banned Shor Party with funds brought from Russia in 2019–2022; Guțul denies the charges and calls the case politically motivated — her position is on the record and printed here (Bytes Europe and regional reporting, May–November 2026). In December 2025 the former speaker of Gagauzia's People's Assembly, Dmitry Konstantinov, was sentenced to 12 years on corruption charges (regional reporting, December 2025); his supporters dispute the fairness of the process, and that dispute is recorded, not resolved, here.
  • The external referee scored the direction as real: the European Commission's enlargement report of 4 November 2026 — covering the period 1 September 2025 to 1 September 2026, most of it Munteanu's tenure — recorded "good progress" in the fight against corruption and in the functioning of the judiciary, and in November 2026 the Commission sent the Council a positive interim benchmark assessment on Moldova's rule-of-law chapters (European Commission Enlargement Package, 4 November 2026; OSW, 13 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The scandal the system answered was a scandal the system had made. Vangheli was appointed despite a negative opinion from the Information and Security Service, and he had made a sizeable donation to PAS's campaign shortly after taking office (Infotag, 29 June 2026; OSW, 7 July 2026). The president's cousin was hired at a strategic state enterprise with no competition at all. Investigative journalists — not the government's own controls — uncovered what the Carnegie Endowment's assessment calls "a pattern of politically connected appointments, inflated remuneration, questionable qualifications, and weak oversight across state institutions and state-owned enterprises" (Carnegie Endowment, 8 July 2026). A one-law state does not need its newspapers to find its favorites; on this record, it did.
  • The heaviest enforcement of the era fell, in volume, on one side of the map. The prosecutions of the Shor network — Guțul, Konstantinov, the MPs convicted in absentia in 2025 — rest on documented illicit-financing evidence and court verdicts, and are recorded on the FOR shelf for that reason. But no comparably senior figure of the governing camp faced a court on Munteanu's watch: the MoldATSA file produced dismissals, resignations, and an open investigation, not charges against any office-holder, and the question of who overrode the security service's negative opinion on Vangheli has no documented answer in the record assembled here. One law at the level of verdicts; a visibly lighter law at the level of appointments, which is where Test 1 begins.
  • The mandate itself was seated under a cloud the ODIHR final report names: the September 2025 elections were "competitive and offered voters a clear choice," but decisions issued days before the vote barred two parties over alleged illegal funding and "limited the right to an effective remedy" for them, and several Central Electoral Commission decisions raised doubts about its impartiality (OSCE/ODIHR Final Report, 18 February 2026; PACE observation report, 20 November 2025). Those decisions predate Munteanu's premiership — they belong to the Recean government and the electoral authorities — but they are the source of the seat he held, and Test 1 grades the source. The interference they answered was itself documented and massive: Moldovan authorities reported a Russian-funded vote-buying operation reaching some 150,000 voters in 2024, run through the network of fugitive oligarch Ilan Shor (New Strategy Center analysis, 2025; IFES case study, 2026). Both facts stand; neither cancels the other.

Why this grade: The same government that let a security-service warning be overridden to install a party donor, and that employed the president's cousin without competition, also dismissed him, investigated the enterprise, accepted resignations from its own committee chair and the Speaker's chief of staff, and ordered audits of every state enterprise. That is one law applied after exposure, and a second, quieter law operating before it. MIXED is the grade the two shelves together earn.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: the Moldovan public and honest state-enterprise employees — the MoldATSA dismissals, the CNA investigation, and the all-enterprise audit order established that exposure has consequences even at the president's family's address. EU partners assessing Moldova's credibility were helped by the visible response (Commission's positive November 2026 assessment).
  • Hurt: Dumitru Vangheli and Anastasia Taburceanu, dismissed and resigned respectively, on documented findings (the falsified CV; the competition-free hire) — their criminal liability, if any, remains ALLEGATION, unadjudicated. Moldovan taxpayers and air passengers were hurt first, by an unvetted appointee running the airspace authority at seven times the average wage. PAS's own voters were hurt in trust: OSW records the affair as the ruling camp's most serious reputational crisis since 2021 (OSW, 7 July 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The media that destroyed his government was free, and was thanked rather than punished. The MoldATSA and fiscal scandals were broken by independent investigative outlets (Ziarul de Gardă, RISE Moldova, TV8); a government spokesperson responded: "We appreciate the role of the independent press and control institutions in reporting such situations. A functional democracy needs both institutions that do their duty and a free press" (government statement via Rapporteur/Euractiv, June 2026). The European Commission's November 2026 report recorded "good progress" on freedom of expression, with Moldova moderately prepared in the area (European Commission, 4 November 2026).
  • The prosecution moved against the governing camp's own appointees: the CNA opened the MoldATSA criminal investigation and the Anti-Corruption Prosecutor's Office took the abuse-of-office case while Munteanu was still in office (OSW, July 2026; MoldovaLive, 2026). No documented act of his government stopped, steered, or publicly attacked that investigation.
  • Parliament checked the executive in the open, including from inside the majority: the June 2026 fiscal package was publicly criticised by PAS's own Education Minister Dan Perciun as inadequately discussed, and the Health Minister publicly rejected parts of it — ministers dissenting in public without documented reprisal (OSW, 7 July 2026; Carnegie Endowment, 8 July 2026). After Munteanu's resignation, parliament set up a committee of inquiry into state-owned enterprises in July 2026 (Logos Press, July 2026).
  • The judiciary's reform machinery — the vetting (external evaluation) of judges and prosecutors inherited from the previous government — continued under independent commissions rather than executive direction: by the Commission's November 2026 account, vetting had covered all courts-of-appeal judges and first-instance court presidents, and five new appointments let the Supreme Court reach a quorum (European Commission, 4 November 2026, via OSW, 13 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Strategic authority sat above the prime minister, in the presidency, and the record shows it being used. Carnegie's assessment is blunt: "Strategic authority has long remained concentrated in the narrow circle around President Sandu," Munteanu "never fully controlled either his office or the wider executive," and much of the senior administrative structure inherited from Recean remained intact amid persistent perceptions that Recean continued to exercise influence behind the scenes (Carnegie Endowment, 8 July 2026). On 26 June 2026 it was Sandu, not the prime minister, who publicly intervened to pause the government's fiscal reform — a head of state with defined, lesser powers publicly countermanding her own government's signature policy (Moldova Matters, June 2026; Carnegie, July 2026). Sandu denied that anyone had prevented Munteanu from tackling abuses, insisting he had full freedom; that denial is on the record. The structure, however, is the structure: the seat this dossier grades did not hold the whole of its own government's power, and the part it did not hold sat with a president the constitution does not make head of government.
  • The referees' benches were still thin enough to grip. The Supreme Court sat at 12 of 20 positions filled; none of the judges on anti-corruption panels had yet undergone vetting, and vetting of organised-crime prosecutors was at a very early stage; the Commission stressed the need to safeguard the institutions' long-term independence and capacity (OSW summary of the Commission report, 13 November 2026). Almost a third of judge posts nationwide were vacant, with low salaries named as a cause ( Moldovan press reporting, October 2026).
  • The parliamentary inquiry into state enterprises was constituted as a one-party instrument: the commission formed in July 2026 consisted of 11 PAS deputies, and opposition parties refused to join, citing concerns over impartiality (Logos Press, July 2026). Its November 2026 report was substantive — it found high management pay (the Chișinău airport head averaging about 123,100 lei a month), debts of over 25 billion lei at state companies, and 37 of 87 monitored companies loss-making in 2024, and recommended further audits (parliamentary inquiry report via Moldova 1 and Radio Moldova, 14 November 2026) — but a referee panel drawn from one team is a grip-shaped instrument even when its findings cut against that team's record. This was parliament's act in the transition weeks, not Munteanu's; it is recorded as the environment his succession produced.
  • The electoral referee entered his tenure already doubted: ODIHR's final report found that several CEC decisions on contentious issues "called into question its impartiality and independence," including the last-minute ineligibility of two parties (ODIHR, 18 February 2026). The report landed on Munteanu's desk; no documented reform of the CEC by his government appears in the record assembled here.

Why this grade: No documented act shows Munteanu personally gripping a court, a newsroom, a prosecutor, or the central bank — the press that felled him worked unhindered and the prosecutors investigating his own camp worked in daylight. But the seat he held was itself held: real strategic control sat in the president's circle, the president publicly overruled his government's central policy, and the judicial benches meant to referee the system ran a third empty. A ruler can fail this test by being gripped as well as by gripping. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: investigative journalists and their audiences — Ziarul de Gardă, RISE Moldova, and TV8 published career-ending findings about the governing camp and were publicly thanked by the government. Citizens were helped by prosecutors and auditors who could act against the ruling camp's appointees.
  • Hurt: Munteanu himself, and the premiership as an institution — a prime minister publicly paused by his own president is a precedent that weakens the seat for whoever holds it next. Voters and candidates in future elections were hurt by the unreformed, doubted electoral commission. Litigants in a court system with a third of judge posts vacant were hurt in delay, whoever they were.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The fiscal wobble was answered with a retreat, stated as listening. The tax package published for consultation on 11 June 2026 (Test 5) drew public protests outside parliament, business criticism, and open dissent from his own ministers. On 26 June President Sandu called on the government to "take a break"; the government spokesperson announced: "We have heard the various opinions and listened to the people. We have already announced that we are taking a break to rethink fiscal policy… we will review the proposals and continue extensive consultations, to identify a fair and sustainable formula" — and the controversial measures were then withdrawn or postponed one after another (Moldova Matters, June 2026; Carnegie Endowment, 8 July 2026). When another option became available — a slower, consulted, partial reform — the government at least considered it openly, and then took it: under Tofan, the revised package presented in autumn 2026 kept reduced VAT rates (8% on essential goods including bread, dairy, and medicines; 12% on most other agri-food and hospitality; 20% standard) instead of abolishing them (VATCalc summary of the revised Fiscal Policy 2027, 2026; Moldovan press, October 2026).
  • The MoldATSA wobble was answered with action, not denial: dismissals, a criminal investigation, ordered audits of all state enterprises, and Munteanu's own call for a "reorganisation from the bottom up to consolidate transparency" at MoldATSA and other branches of government (Rapporteur/Euractiv, June 2026; Logos Press, July 2026).
  • The local-government wobble was met with a consultative design rather than an imposed map: the administrative-territorial reform concept presented on 8 April 2026 proposed cutting districts from 32 to 10 and a 3,000-inhabitant threshold for mayoralties, but paired it with simplified voluntary amalgamation and increased financial incentives — over 320 local decisions to initiate voluntary amalgamation were recorded — with the new structure to enter into force only after the 2027 local elections (Anticoruptie.md editorial analysis, 2026; Logos Press territorial-reform coverage, 2026). Pushback from mayors and communities continued into the autumn under his successor, and the process remained consultative rather than forced (Logos Press, October–November 2026).
  • The no-good-choice clause applies to the fiscal file, and is applied here by its mechanics. The options that actually existed in June 2026 were: (a) broaden the VAT base onto food, medicines, and gas — meeting the deficit path agreed with the IMF but raising prices on essentials in one of Europe's poorest countries; or (b) keep the reduced rates and the deficit — OSW records the deficit at 5.5% of GDP under Munteanu, and the successor government later put the adjusted 2026 deficit at 6% of GDP, 23.07 billion lei (OSW, 23 July 2026; Interfax, 6 November 2026) — risking the IMF relationship and future financing. Each carried real harm; there was no third door in the moment, and hindsight options are not counted as options refused. Munteanu did not build this trap: the previous IMF programme had already ended early in October 2025 with a final tranche of about USD 170 million withheld over delayed financial and governance commitments — a failure of the government he succeeded, weeks before he took office (Interfax, February 2026). The choice he eventually took — retreat and rethink — is therefore not held against him. What remains gradable is the honesty and workmanship inside the trap, which is the shelf below.

Evidence against:

  • The wobble was self-made, and the making was the opposite of open adjustment. The package reached the public with a comment window of six working days (11–19 June); the American Chamber of Commerce's Veronica Sirețeanu stated that a reform of this scope required a minimum of twenty working days of consultation; Finance Minister Andrian Gavriliță acknowledged the package had gone out without meaningful consultation across government — the Health Minister learned of provisions he said would "destroy the healthcare system" after publication — citing time constraints and the sequencing of IMF negotiations (Moldova Matters, June 2026; Carnegie Endowment, 8 July 2026). The authorities then explained the reform's timing through IMF and EU requirements, in Carnegie's words "shift[ing] political responsibility for unpopular measures onto external actors" (Carnegie, 8 July 2026). Blaming the referee is a form of not answering.
  • The prime minister himself was largely absent from the answering. Carnegie records that responsibility for inter-ministerial coordination rested with the prime minister's office, yet Munteanu "remained largely absent from the debate, reinforcing perceptions that the government lacked an effective political center" (Carnegie Endowment, 8 July 2026). The public pause was announced by the president; the retreat was voiced by a spokesperson; the defence was left to the finance minister.
  • The final answer to the wobble was departure without explanation. On 3 July 2026 Munteanu resigned citing only his "principles and convictions," giving no specific reason then or in the record assembled here since (Reuters, 3 July 2026; OSW, 7 July 2026). Resigning on a point of principle can be an honourable act — speculation recorded by OSW holds that he was unwilling to accept allegedly corrupt arrangements benefiting officials linked to the ruling camp, which if true would speak for him; it is speculation, labeled as such, and Sandu publicly denied that anyone had blocked him (OSW, 7 July 2026). But a ruler who promised to "tell the truth, even when solutions require collective effort" (his investiture speech, 31 October 2025) and then leaves the country to guess why he quit has not answered the wobble openly — he has exited it. The opposition's answers went unanswered too: Igor Dodon called for early elections and Ion Ceban for the resignation of the entire state leadership; neither demand was owed agreement, but neither received a documented substantive reply from the departing prime minister (OSW, 7 July 2026).

Why this grade: On the substance of both big wobbles — the tax package and MoldATSA — the government adjusted: measures withdrawn, officials dismissed, audits ordered, the gentler option considered openly and adopted by his successor. On the manner, it failed the test's core: the crisis was created by refusing consultation, responsibility was shifted onto the IMF, the adjustment was announced by the president rather than the prime minister, and the prime minister's own final act was an unexplained exit. Adjustment happened; openness, from the man graded, largely did not. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: households buying food, medicines, and gas — the withdrawal of the single 20% VAT rate spared them a price rise the original package would have imposed from October 2026. Small businesses and the hospitality sector were helped by the same retreat and by the revised rates his successor adopted.
  • Hurt: the public finances and everyone depending on them — the deficit path the package was built to fix remained, at 5.5% of GDP on OSW's figure and 6% on the successor government's adjusted figure, with planned public-sector pay rises publicly linked by the finance minister to the abandoned reform (Moldova Matters, June 2026). Citizens were hurt in comprehension: a government that falls without its head explaining why leaves every voter to fill the silence with rumour.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Gagauz, the Transnistrian residents, the Russian-speakers, the farmers, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Transnistrian file was handled as counting-in by instalments, with the counted-in side funded. Parliament adopted, in first reading on 26 March 2026 and by Law No. 67 of April 2026, the gradual abolition of tax and customs exemptions for Transnistrian businesses — "same country – same taxes" — phased over years to full unification on 1 January 2030, with the revenue channelled to a Convergence Fund for the region's reintegration: infrastructure, public services, and social projects in education, healthcare, employment, and social protection for the left bank's residents, financed without transferring resources to the separatist administration (Interfax, 26 March 2026; Moldovan legal reporting via Cotidianul, 2026). From 1 September 2026 VAT and excise applied to selected imports (alcohol, tobacco, perfume, jewellery), with petroleum, gas, and electricity phased later (OSW, 4 September 2026). The government stated in October 2026 that the fund would become operational in early 2027 (gov.md via Comunica, 27 October 2026). Chișinău also continued compensating Dubăsari-district farmers for the duties Tiraspol imposes on them — 3.34 million lei allocated in 2025 (Cotidianul, 2026).
  • The winter safety net ran at scale through his first cold season: the 2026 budget continued the energy-compensation programme for vulnerable households (gov.md, December 2025), and the programme he inherited paid compensation of up to 1,000 lei a month toward sky-high bills through the 2025–26 season. For the 2026–27 season under his successor, some 670,000 households were expected to qualify — about 50,000 more than the year before (TV8 and Moldovan press, 19 October 2026).
  • The floor was raised, on the record: the minimum wage rose from 5,500 to 6,300 lei a month on 1 January 2026, with the salary reference value raised from 2,200 to 2,400 lei "primarily helping those with low incomes," and pensions were indexed in April 2026 (gov.md, December 2025; legal monitoring, December 2025).
  • Gagauzia's voters were ultimately counted back in: after the region's institutions stood vacant (below), elections for bashkan and the People's Assembly were set for 15 November 2026 under national electoral oversight, with some 129,000–130,000 eligible voters, 67 polling stations, and ballots printed in Russian, Romanian, and Gagauz (Jamestown Foundation, November 2026; Bytes Europe, November 2026). The first round was held on 15–16 November 2026 — the two counts differ by a day across sources and are printed as reported — and a runoff between Alexandr Stoianoglo (38.44%) and Fiodor Gagauz (24.22%) was set for 29 November 2026 (Reuters via Europe Says, November 2026). The ruling party fielded no bashkan candidate of its own (Jamestown, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Gagauzia spent his entire tenure counted out of its own government. The People's Assembly's mandates expired on 12 November 2025; elections scheduled for 16 November 2025, then 22 March 2026, then 21 June 2026 never took place, blocked in disputes over the regional electoral body; the bashkan's post fell vacant when Guțul's conviction became final, and the Assembly declared it vacant on 17 August 2026 (Jamestown Foundation, November 2026; regional reporting). On 9 July 2026 — six days after Munteanu's resignation, in the transition his departure created — the Constitutional Court ruled that the People's Assembly cannot form the region's electoral body or approve the regional heads of police, security, and justice; parliament then amended the Electoral Code to place regional elections under national oversight, require bashkan candidates to prove Romanian-language proficiency, and replace the Assembly's single-member districts with proportional representation after this election (Evropeiska Pravda and Logos Press, July 2026). The Assembly's leaders formally rejected the overhaul, and the Party of Communists' leader Vladimir Voronin called the ruling unconstitutional — positions recorded here as the disputants' own (Logos Press via regional reporting, 2026). Carnegie's verdict on the governing method, delivered during Munteanu's tenure: in Gagauzia the government "largely deferred political engagement while increasingly relying on judicial instruments to manage tensions," measures "perceived as a gradual erosion of the autonomy guaranteed to the region by the 1994 settlement" (Carnegie Endowment, 8 July 2026). The November 2026 election repaired the vacancy; it did not restore the prerogatives, and it came a year late.
  • The poor were nearly taxed at the essentials shelf. The June 2026 fiscal package proposed abolishing the reduced 8% VAT rate on food, medicines, and natural gas in favour of a single 20% rate (OSW, 7 July 2026). In a country where the national poverty threshold is 6,294 lei a month (National Trade Union Confederation figure, November 2025), that is a levy aimed, in incidence, at those least able to pay. It was withdrawn after backlash (Test 3) — but it was his government that proposed it, and the withdrawal does not erase the proposing.
  • The floor he raised barely reaches the poverty line he governed over. The unions had asked for a minimum wage of 8,050 lei from 1 January 2026, calculating that 6,300 lei sits essentially at the poverty threshold (6,294 lei) and that the previous 5,500 lei covered only 70% of the subsistence minimum for an adult with one child; the government's programme promise is 10,000 lei (CNSM request to the prime minister, 10 November 2025). Farmers, meanwhile, were counted as an afterthought: the Farmers' Force association warned in spring 2026 that micro, small, and medium farmers faced an "almost catastrophic" situation and threatened protests (MoldovaLive news digest, April 2026), and by November 2026 the state still owed farmers about 1.7 billion lei in unpaid subsidies (Bani.md, 10 November 2026 — a figure from his successor's tenure, recorded as the state of the file his government left).
  • Transnistria's residents were taxed before they were funded. The Convergence Fund's governing regulation was still in public consultation in October 2026 and the fund operates only from early 2027, while the duties began on 1 September 2026 (Cotidianul, 2026; TOL, November 2026). Tiraspol's authorities warned of a "large-scale humanitarian crisis" and a rare People's Assembly in Tiraspol on 23 October 2026 unanimously condemned the measures as economic pressure — the de facto authorities' characterisation, printed as theirs (MoldovaLive digest, 2026; TOL, November 2026). The phasing to 2030 and the fund are real mitigations; the sequence — revenue first, visible benefit later — is also real.

Why this grade: The design of his reintegration and welfare policy counted everyone in on paper — left-bank residents funded rather than merely taxed, 670,000 households inside the compensation net, the minimum wage and pensions raised. The lived sequence counted some people out for long stretches: Gagauzia without any elected institution for a year and its autonomy narrowed by court and code, the poor targeted by a VAT rise on bread and medicine until the street and his own ministers stopped it, farmers owed their subsidies. Design FOR, sequence AGAINST. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: vulnerable households inside the energy-compensation system through the 2025–26 winter; minimum-wage earners and pensioners, by the January and April 2026 rises; Dubăsari farmers compensated for Tiraspol's duties; Transnistrian residents, prospectively, by a Convergence Fund designed to spend reintegration revenue on their services.
  • Hurt: Gagauzia's roughly 130,000 voters, left without an elected assembly or governor for a year and voting in November 2026 under rules rewritten from Chișinău; poor consumers, who carried the fear — and nearly the fact — of 20% VAT on food, medicines, and gas; farmers waiting on unpaid subsidies; left-bank households paying new duties from September 2026 while the fund meant to offset them does not open until 2027.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Programme pledges and public statements vs. the record from 1 November 2025 to 3 July 2026, with dates; the two axes — word-keeping and helped/hurt — graded independently, and all four crossings named.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The European promise — kept at speed, and it helped. The programme "EU, Peace, Development," approved 31 October 2025, pledged to complete accession negotiations by 2028 and make Moldova ready for membership by 2030 (gov.md, October 2025). On 15 June 2026 Moldova opened the first negotiating cluster (Fundamentals) in Luxembourg, after Hungary's new government lifted the two-year veto its predecessor had held (OSW, 16 June 2026). Four clusters were open by October 2026 — Fundamentals (15 June), External Relations (14 July), Internal Market and a further cluster in October (European Commission report summary via Comunica, November 2026). The Commission's 4 November 2026 report was its most favourable on Moldova since assessments began: "good" progress in 25 of 33 chapters, "very good" in foreign and security policy, "very significant progress" toward the interim rule-of-law benchmarks, a positive interim benchmark assessment sent to the Council, and a roadmap under which Moldova could close 25 chapters in 2027 and conclude talks in early 2028 (European Commission, 4 November 2026; OSW, 13 November 2026). Crossing 1 — kept his word AND it helped people: the accession track his voters elected is measurably closer, carrying EU growth-plan financing and market access with it.
  • The IMF promise — kept. He sought a new IMF arrangement after the old programme's early end; the IMF Board on 27 February 2026 backed launching discussions, and in May 2026 his government reached a staff-level agreement on a three-year Policy Coordination Instrument programme, focused on public-finance management, state-enterprise risk, and protecting public funds (gov.md, February and May 2026). Munteanu called it "not a mere formality, but the result of a consolidated effort" (gov.md, May 2026).
  • The governing-style promise — partly kept. He promised parliament "a prime minister who works — not a populist one," who would "tell the truth, even when solutions require collective effort" (investiture speech, 31 October 2025, via MoldovaLive). The unglamorous record matches the first half: a 2026 budget passed on time raising capital investment by 55% to 3 billion lei, the minimum wage and pension rises of Test 4, the administrative-reform concept of April 2026, and the Transnistria convergence law — work, delivered as administration rather than spectacle (gov.md, December 2025).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The fiscal-predictability promise — broken in the process, and the break hurt. PAS's 2025 election platform promised fiscal stability: tax policy changed at most once every two years, on a "predictable fiscal calendar for the next 4 years, consulted with the business environment, civil society and social partners" (PAS platform, quoted in Moldova Matters, June 2026). Eight months in, his finance ministry published the largest tax overhaul in years with six working days of consultation and without consulting the health ministry (Test 3). Crossing 4 — broke his word AND it hurt people: businesses and households were given days to model a reform touching food, medicine, energy, and income tax; trust in the government's competence, PAS's core asset, was the casualty, and the episode ended his government.
  • The deficit commitment — receding. The fiscal consolidation his IMF track required — deficit down from 5.5% of GDP toward 3.5% by 2029 under the PCI's objectives (Interfax summary of the programme, 6 November 2026) — was staked on the package that collapsed; the adjusted 2026 deficit his successor reported was 6% of GDP (Interfax, 6 November 2026). Not yet a broken dated promise; a promise whose vehicle he withdrew and whose arithmetic worsened.
  • Crossing 2 — kept his word AND it still hurt people: the Transnistria plank of his programme ("reintegration and cohesion — peace and shared EU benefits on both sides of the Dniester," gov.md, October 2025) was carried out as written: the tax-unification law passed, the duties began 1 September 2026, the Convergence Fund was legislated. The keeping earns credit here; the hurt is recorded in Test 4 in full — left-bank residents paying new duties months before the fund that is supposed to return the money opens, under a design they had no seat in shaping.
  • Crossing 3 — went against his word AND it helped people: abandoning the VAT-on-essentials package broke the consolidation commitment he had just made to the IMF timetable and to his own published reform; the break is recorded here, and the help is recorded in full in Test 3's ledger — poor households were spared a 20%-rate rise on food, medicines, and gas. The axes are independent: the sparing does not launder the breaking, and the breaking does not erase the sparing.
  • The truth-telling promise — broken at the exit. A prime minister who pledges to tell the truth and then resigns with one sentence about his principles, offering no specific reason in July 2026 or after, leaves his central personal pledge unkept at the moment it mattered most (Reuters, 3 July 2026). His stated reason may be entirely genuine; the record cannot grade what it cannot read, and "not documented" is the entry for his actual grounds.
  • The long promises are recorded, not graded: €4 billion of investment and support for 25,000 enterprises, 3,000 km of modernised roads, a 10,000-lei minimum wage, 60,000 hectares of new forest (programme, October 2025) — mandate-length pledges by a man who served eight months of the mandate. Economic delivery in his window was thin: GDP grew 2.7% in 2025 on the IMF's estimate but only 0.4% year-on-year in the first quarter of 2026 (IMF via gov.md, February 2026; OSW, 23 July 2026). His successor Tofan adopted the same programme — pledging an EU accession agreement by end-2028, deficit reduction of two points by 2029, and audits of state enterprises — so the promises live on under another name; whether they are kept is Tofan's file, not this one (Reuters, 21 July 2026).

Why this grade: On the promise his voters most elected — Europe, on schedule — his word held and is documented by the referee itself: four clusters open, the best Commission report Moldova has had, a 2028 finish the Commission calls ambitious but achievable. On the promise about how he would govern — consultation, predictability, truth-telling — his word broke in June and July 2026, in ways that hurt. A word kept on the destination and broken on the manner is a MIXED file, and the four crossings above show why no single letter could carry it.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: Moldovan exporters, students, and citizens who gain from each opened EU cluster and the growth-plan money behind it; future governments, which inherit an IMF programme and a Commission roadmap instead of a stalled file; poor households, by the broken promise of Crossing 3.
  • Hurt: businesses and families who planned around the promised fiscal predictability and got a six-day consultation instead; the public finances, by the consolidation year lost; every citizen's stock of trust, by an unexplained resignation from a man whose pledge was to tell the truth.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Alexandru Munteanu held the premiership for eight months — 1 November 2025 to 3 July 2026 — as a technocrat chosen by President Maia Sandu and her PAS majority (55 of 101 seats, won with 50.20% on 28 September 2025) precisely because he stood outside politics, and he left it the same way he had been damaged inside it: quietly, on a point of principle he never specified. The one-law test split down the middle of a single scandal: his governing camp appointed a party donor over a security service's warning and employed the president's cousin without competition at MoldATSA, and then — once journalists exposed it — dismissed the director, opened a criminal investigation, accepted resignations reaching the Speaker's office, and ordered audits of all 74 state enterprises; the hurt fell on taxpayers and on PAS's own voters' trust, the help on a public that learned exposure still has consequences. On gripping, no act of his hand on a court, newsroom, or prosecutor is documented — the press that felled him was thanked — but the seat itself was gripped from above: strategic authority sat in Sandu's circle, and it was the president who publicly paused his signature reform on 26 June 2026. The wobble test records a government that did adjust — the 20% VAT on food, medicines, and gas was withdrawn, the gentler option considered openly and adopted by his successor — but that created the wobble by refusing consultation, blamed the IMF for its timing, and ended with the prime minister absent from his own government's defence and then gone without explanation; the no-good-choice clause covers his fiscal dilemma, which he inherited rather than built, but not his silence inside it. On counting everyone, the designs were inclusive — a Convergence Fund legislated to return Transnistrian tax revenue as services, 670,000 households in the compensation net, the minimum wage raised to 6,300 lei — while the sequences were not: Gagauzia spent a year with no elected institutions and saw its autonomy narrowed by the Constitutional Court's 9 July 2026 ruling before voting again on 15 November 2026, and the poor were nearly taxed at the essentials shelf. His word held where his voters most cared — four EU clusters open by October 2026, the Commission's most favourable report yet, an IMF programme agreed — and broke on manner: the promised consulted, predictable fiscal calendar became a six-day consultation, and the promised truth-telling ended in an unexplained resignation. He was succeeded, after Eugen Osmochescu's two acting weeks, by Vasile Tofan (sworn in 22 July 2026), who inherited both the programme and the open files that will move these grades when his own is written: whether the MoldATSA investigation reaches verdicts against anyone senior, whether the Convergence Fund opens in early 2027 as stated, whether Gagauzia's rewritten rules produce a governable autonomy, and whether the fiscal reform returns in the consulted form its first version never had.
