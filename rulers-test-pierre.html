The Ruler's Test — Philip J. Pierre, Prime Minister of Saint Lucia

Subject: Philip Joseph Pierre — Prime Minister of Saint Lucia since 28 July 2021. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Saint Lucia is a parliamentary monarchy in which the governor-general, for the King, is head of state, while executive power — the government, the budget (Pierre is also Minister for Finance), and, in his own holding of the national security portfolio for much of the tenure, the police file — sits with the prime minister. Premise check, stated explicitly because the brief required it: the expected holder still holds the seat, but the mandate underneath him changed. Pierre, born 18 January 1954 in Castries, an accountant and MP for Castries East since 1997, first won office on 26 July 2021, when the Saint Lucia Labour Party (SLP) took 13 of 17 House of Assembly seats on the "Putting People First" manifesto (St. Lucia Times manifesto review, November 2025; Government of Saint Lucia, swearing-in address, 2021). He then called a snap general election on 9 November 2025, a year early, and on 1 December 2025 the SLP won 14 of 17 seats with 55.77% of the vote on 48.5% turnout, the United Workers Party (UWP) falling to one seat and two independents taking the rest — the first time since independence an incumbent government had increased its majority at a second consecutive election (International IDEA, election record, December 2025; Reuters, 2 December 2025; CARICOM Election Observation Mission preliminary statement, 5 December 2025). He is therefore graded on a completed first term (July 2021 – December 2025) plus the first year of a second term, through November 2026 — a full tenure file, not a six-month one. In November 2026 he was still in post in the documented record: chairing CARICOM from 1 July to 31 December 2026, leading the delegation to CHOGM in Antigua on 1–4 November 2026, addressing COP31 on 12 November 2026, and holding his regular Monday press briefing on 16 November 2026 (CARICOM Secretariat, July 2026; Government of Saint Lucia, November 2026; UN Barbados and Eastern Caribbean, 12 November 2026). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one: carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption instrument he built is written to reach his own side as well as the last one. The Special Prosecutor Act — appointment announced March 2022, enacted by the House on 10 August 2022 (Act No. 19 of 2022) — empowers a prosecutor appointed by the Judicial and Legal Services Commission, not by the prime minister, to receive complaints from any person, investigate on his own motion, and institute civil and criminal proceedings against "any public official" for malfeasance or misappropriation, expressly including incumbent officials and officials who have demitted office (Government of Saint Lucia, Special Prosecutor Act release, 2022; Special Prosecutor Act, Cap. 2.20, sections 6, 28–29; US State Department, Saint Lucia 2022 Human Rights Report).
  • At his swearing-in he stated the rule in the open and in both directions: "justice demands that those who have been engaged in acts of corruption must be held to account," paired with "this is not the time for retribution" and a promise to be "prime minister for not some of the people but all the people" (Government of Saint Lucia, swearing-in address, 2021).
  • The social floor he legislated applies by category, not by connection: Saint Lucia's first-ever national minimum wage — EC$6.52 an hour, EC$1,131 a month, under the Labour (Minimum Wage) Order 2024, in force 1 October 2024 for all categories of worker — and the income-tax personal allowance raised from EC$18,000 to EC$25,000 from 1 January 2023, with the 10% band abolished (Labour (Minimum Wage) Order 2024; Income Tax (Amendment) Act No. 19 of 2023, verified against the Inland Revenue Department, September 2026).
  • The final court was moved closer to everyone equally: parliament voted on 28 February 2023 to replace the London Privy Council with the Caribbean Court of Justice as Saint Lucia's final court of appeal, and accession was announced by the CCJ on 9 May 2023, making Saint Lucia the fifth state in the appellate jurisdiction (Parliament of Saint Lucia, 28 February 2023; CCJ media release, 9 May 2023; CCJ Annual Report 2024–25, February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Citizenship by Investment Programme (CIP) is where one law is most seriously contested, and the contest is in court, not only in speeches. In February 2025 the then opposition leader Allen Chastanet filed a fixed-date claim (SLUHCV2025/0072) alleging illegality and unreasonableness in the CIP's administration, including the Galaxy real-estate approvals; the High Court refused his interlocutory injunction on 30 October 2025, and the substantive claim remained the vehicle for the dispute — the defendants deny any wrongdoing (Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court judgment, Chastanet v. Hilaire and the CIP Board, 30 October 2025). Separately, MSR Media's US RICO litigation named the CIP minister, Ernest Hilaire, first as co-conspirator and, per 2025 reporting, moved to upgrade him to defendant; Hilaire and the CIP Unit's CEO have publicly denied the allegations. These are ALLEGATIONS, unproven in any court finding recorded here — but Pierre's response to them is a documented act of the seat: he kept Hilaire in cabinet as Deputy Prime Minister and CIP minister throughout, declining Chastanet's public demand that he remove Hilaire, terminate the CEO, and replace board members pending investigation (IMI Daily, 2025). A rule that investigates former officials through a special prosecutor while the sitting minister in the contested file stays in place by the prime minister's choice is, on its face, two tempos of one law.
  • The CIP's own pricing record is disputed between the two administrations: Chastanet alleges the Galaxy hotel file price was cut from US$300,000 to US$200,000 per file under Pierre and that up to 7,000 files were allocated to one developer, and alleges an infrastructure option was launched without enabling legislation and given to Galaxy alone; the government disputes the characterisation and says all moneys were accounted for. Both versions are printed here as the disputants' claims; no court has settled them in the record assembled (IMI Daily, 2025).
  • The IMPACS file is the oldest one-law failure on the island, and it did not close on his watch. Between 2010 and 2011, twelve citizens were killed by police in Operation Restore Confidence under a UWP government; the CARICOM IMPACS investigation (commissioned August 2014, reported February 2015) found, as then prime minister Kenny Anthony summarised it publicly in 2015, that "death lists" existed, that the shootings reviewed were "'fake encounters' staged by the police," and that weapons were planted — findings the report's subjects dispute and which were never tested in a prosecution (US State Department, Saint Lucia 2017 Human Rights Report, summarising the IMPACS report and Anthony's statement). The United States imposed Leahy Law sanctions in August 2013, suspending assistance to the whole police force; partial support to particular departments was restored in June 2021. The Director of Public Prosecutions stated in 2021 that the investigations were incomplete, had no limitation period, and would continue (DPP statement, Government of Saint Lucia, 2021). Under Pierre — who holds or held the national security portfolio himself — the record assembled here shows no IMPACS prosecution, no public release of the report, and, in July 2025, the prime minister answering a journalist's IMPACS question at a press briefing with "That's not your business" before insisting the questioner let him finish (press-briefing footage and reaction reporting, July 2025). The killings predate him by a decade; the continued absence of a verdict, for or against the officers, happened on his tenure too.
  • The Special Prosecutor's output, as distinct from its statute, is thin in the public record: a November 2026 public audit of the justice system — a commentator's assessment, recorded as such, not an official finding — describes the office created in 2022–23 as inactive with no charges by 2026 (public commentary, 6 November 2026). No documented senior conviction under the Act appears in the record assembled here.

Why this grade, in plain words: The laws he wrote are genuinely one-law laws — the special prosecutor, the minimum wage, the tax allowance, and the CCJ all apply to everyone on their face, and the prosecutor is appointed by a commission he does not control. But the two files where one law would bite hardest — the CIP, where his own deputy is the contested minister, and IMPACS, where the state's own officers are the accused — are exactly the two files where, five years in, there is still no verdict either way. A law that has not yet reached the powerful on either shelf is a mixed record, not a held one. Helped: low-paid workers in every sector (the first minimum wage); every taxpayer under the EC$25,000 allowance; complainants of any party, who gained a statutory door for corruption complaints; litigants, who gained a regional final court. Hurt: the families of the twelve people killed in 2010–11, who still have no court's answer; ordinary CIP applicants and the programme's due-diligence staff, whose programme's reputation is litigated rather than settled; Saint Lucian citizens generally, whose visa-free access is the asset put at risk while the CIP dispute stays unresolved (see Test 3).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • The referees ruled against, and charged, his own government in public — the strongest documented sign in this dossier. On 20 October 2025 the Royal Saint Lucia Police Force charged his Deputy Prime Minister, Ernest Hilaire, with unlawful assault over an alleged incident involving journalist Eldrise Charles at the parliamentary compound on 19 August 2025; Hilaire was summoned to the First District Court for 4 December 2025, denies the allegation, and says video footage does not support it — an ALLEGATION before the court, recorded as such (CIVICUS Monitor, October 2025–August 2026 update; First District Court summons, case LUCRO2025/1479, October 2025; IACHR Special Rapporteur, 2025 Annual Report). A police force that charges the deputy prime minister, and a court that summons him, are not gripped by the prime minister.
  • The courts decided the CIP fight's first round on the papers, against the opposition's application and without executive interference documented on either side: the High Court's reasoned 30-page judgment refusing Chastanet's injunction (30 October 2025) is a court working in the open against the government's critic — and the substantive judicial review he filed remains available to him (ECSC judgment, SLUHCV2025/0072).
  • The electoral referee ran a snap election the incumbent could have lost, and outside observers certified the running of it. The CARICOM Election Observation Mission — eleven members from nine states, invited by the government on 11 November 2025, present 24 November–3 December — observed 317 of 480 polling stations across 14 of 17 constituencies and concluded that "electors were able to exercise their franchise in a peaceful, orderly and transparent manner," noting long advance-poll queues on 28 November but no disenfranchisement and no election-related violence (CARICOM CEOM preliminary statement, 5 December 2025).
  • The external scorecards agree, on reporting periods inside his tenure: Freedom House's Freedom in the World 2026 scores Saint Lucia 91/100, "Free" — 38/40 political rights, 53/60 civil liberties — unchanged from the prior year (Freedom House, 2026).
  • Critical media operate openly and litigate openly: opposition-aligned and independent outlets publish detailed attacks on the CIP, the airport file, and the prime minister personally; a 2026 documentary publicly aired the IMPACS report's contents; and when Pierre himself sued over Facebook posts he alleged defamed him over the CIP, he used the civil courts and obtained a default judgment on 30 January 2026 only after the defendants failed to file any defence — the court's process, not an executive order (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026; High Court Claim No. SLUHCV2025/0332).

Evidence against:

  • The seat itself concentrates portfolios: Pierre has simultaneously held the premiership with the finance ministry throughout, and the national security (and at points justice) portfolio for much of the tenure — the 2026 acting arrangements name the finance, justice, and national security files together as the bundle another minister acts in when he travels (Government of Saint Lucia, November 2026). Concentration by lawful appointment is not gripping, but it narrows the distance between the executive and two referees — the treasury and the police — and is recorded as the structural seam in an otherwise held test.
  • Criminal defamation remains on the books unrepealed on his watch: Saint Lucia's January 2026 Universal Periodic Review produced a recommendation from Spain to decriminalise defamation to protect journalists, and a recommendation from Australia to ratify the ICCPR, signed in 2011 and still unratified; the UPR also recorded the absence of a Paris-Principles national human rights institution (UN Human Rights Council, UPR fourth cycle, adopted July 2026, via CIVICUS Monitor). The prime minister's own civil defamation suit against a Facebook page — however lawful, and decided by default rather than on contested merits — is recorded by CIVICUS as capable of chilling expression precisely because the claimant is the serving prime minister.
  • In the Hilaire assault file, the Speaker of the House publicly stated before the police investigation concluded that video footage did not support the journalist's claim and promised to release the footage after the investigation — a parliamentary officer's intervention in a live police file, criticised by opposition supporters as prejudgment; the Speaker is not the prime minister, and no direction by Pierre is documented, but the episode is the nearest documented approach to a referee in his government's favour (DBS Television reporting, 21 August 2025).

Why this grade, in plain words: This is the test where the receipts point one way. In a single year the police charged his deputy, a court heard his opponent's case against his government and published its reasons, an electoral commission ran his snap election under foreign observation, and his country kept a 91/100 "Free" score. The seams are real — he holds too many portfolios himself, criminal defamation survives, and the ICCPR still waits — but they are seams in a held test, not a grip. Helped: voters, whose 2025 election was independently observed and certified as orderly; journalists and critics, who publish and broadcast against the government daily; the opposition, which kept its court door and used it. Hurt: no group is documented as punished for independence on this record; the nearest documented cost falls on journalist Eldrise Charles, whose alleged-assault complaint had to proceed against the second most powerful man in the government — and whose case the police, to their credit on this shelf, did bring.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Hewanorra airport file is his largest documented adjustment. On taking office he halted the inherited Hewanorra International Airport (HIA) redevelopment, appointed a seven-member review committee in August 2021, and told the Hotel and Tourism Association the inherited scheme — a direct-awarded contract, a terminal relocated to ground requiring 3,006 piles against 310 planned, foundation costs risen from EC$4.86 million to EC$51.3 million — projected to roughly US$400 million, and that "in all good conscience we could not continue on that path"; the World Bank's IFC, originator of the earlier PPP design, was re-engaged to advise (Caribbean Times reporting of the SLHTA address, 2022). The restart was slow but it was a restart by stated method: a competitive process commenced in 2025, NH International (Caribbean) Ltd was publicly announced as contractor in October 2026, the control tower was reported more than half complete in April 2026, and a World Bank-supported runway rehabilitation contract was signed on 3 November 2026 (Government of Saint Lucia / SLASPA announcements, April and November 2026; The Voice St. Lucia, October 2026).
  • On the CIP, foreign pushback produced documented policy movement rather than denial: Saint Lucia joined the OECS memorandum aligning regional programmes in July 2024, engaged in the US Treasury roundtable process and the Six CBI Principles on due diligence and information-sharing, and in March 2025 Hilaire announced an intention to restore elements of the programme's original 2015 structure — annual quotas, a US$3 million net-worth floor, mandatory escrow (regional MOA, July 2024; programme reporting, 31 March 2025). When the EU moved in late 2025–2026 toward requiring phase-out of CBI programmes by 1 June 2028 under Regulation 2025/2441, Pierre confirmed publicly on 9 and 16 November 2026 that his government had written to the EU and was in dialogue — while declining to publish a confidential letter before Brussels confirmed receipt, and stating Saint Lucia would not simply abandon the programme (Government of Saint Lucia, 16 November 2026; EU Regulation 2025/2441, in force 30 December 2025).
  • The hardest wobble of the second term — Washington's request that Caribbean states accept third-country nationals removed from the US — was answered with a bounded, stated adjustment rather than either refusal or open-ended acceptance. The January 2026 announcement was of a non-binding MOU that "does not create any legal obligations"; by the government's own September 2026 account, Saint Lucia kept discretion to accept or reject every individual, capped transfers at ten people per quarter, excluded unaccompanied minors, Saint Lucian nationals, and anyone with a criminal record known to US authorities beyond immigration violations, required five days' manifest notice with a two-day accept/reject window, and required the US to fund an international organisation (the IOM was engaged) to provide services. The first transfer, on 31 August 2026, was six people, of whom Saint Lucia had accepted six of ten proposed after its own assessment; by 21 September 2026 one had departed voluntarily and a second return was being arranged, and those remaining were not detained (Third Country Deportation Watch, updated September 2026, compiling government statements; Government Information Service, 3 and 14 September 2026). This is also where the no-good-choice clause genuinely fits, and it is applied by its mechanics: a state of 180,000 people, dependent on US tourism, remittances, and correspondent banking, was asked by its largest partner in a season when Washington had already imposed visa bonds on two neighbouring CBI states (16 December 2025) and paused immigrant visas across twelve Caribbean states (21 January 2026). The options that actually existed were refusal at documented risk to Saint Lucians' own US access, acceptance without conditions, or the bounded acceptance he chose; each carried real harm, hindsight options do not count, and the choice is recorded as the least-harm door actually open — not a demerit. What remains gradable, and is graded on the against shelf, is the honesty and timing of how it was presented at home.
  • The 2025 election itself was a wobble answered at the ballot box by his own choice: facing cost-of-living and CIP criticism, he called the vote a year early and submitted the record to the voters, who returned him with a larger majority (International IDEA, December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The deportee MOU's disclosure timing is contested, and the contest goes to openness. Critics alleged in 2025–26 that public disclosure of the arrangement was delayed until after the 1 December 2025 general election — an ALLEGATION about motive, recorded as such; the documented facts are narrower: discussions with Washington began in 2025, and the public announcement came in January 2026, after the election (Third Country Deportation Watch, 2026; CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). Pierre also told the public in mid-January 2026 that he had received no information about a change in US visa policy affecting Saint Lucians; on 21 January 2026 the US State Department's immigrant-visa pause covering Caribbean states followed (Third Country Deportation Watch, 2026). Whether the two files were linked is not established in the record; the sequence is printed because Test 3 grades openness under pushback.
  • On the EU/CIP file, the November 2026 answer to journalists was confidentiality as a principle — "Saint Lucia style is not to discuss letters written to people in public" — and public reaction recorded the same week was sharply critical, with commentators invoking democratic ownership of the passport itself (Government of Saint Lucia, 16 November 2026; public reaction reporting, 24 November 2026). Dialogue with Brussels is adjustment; declining to tell his own public what position was taken in their name, on the programme that carries their visa-free access, is the denial-shaped half of the same act.
  • On IMPACS, the wobble was answered with a closed door: "That's not your business" to a journalist's question at a July 2025 press briefing is the documented response of the seat to the island's longest-running accountability pushback (press-briefing footage, July 2025).
  • The St. Jude handover (Test 5) also belongs here in part: when the November 2025 "delivered" hospital did not become a fully operating hospital, the government's November 2026 answer was explanation without a date — the health minister stating "We do not have an exact timeline" and projecting complete transition "within a reasonable time in 2027" (St. Lucia Daily Post, 16 November 2026). Explanation is more than denial; it is less than the firm revised commitment the wobble called for.

Why this grade, in plain words: He adjusts — the airport was genuinely stopped, reviewed, and re-tendered; the CIP was genuinely re-aligned under foreign pressure; the deportee demand was genuinely bounded with caps, exclusions, and a veto over every name. But on the two files where the pushback is domestic and moral rather than foreign and financial — IMPACS and the public's right to see the EU letter — the answer was a closed door. Adjustment abroad, deflection at home: that is a mixed wobble. Helped: taxpayers, spared the inherited airport scheme's projected cost escalation; the six transferred third-country nationals, who arrived under stated criteria, were not detained, and two of whom obtained arranged returns; Saint Lucian travellers, whose US and EU access the bounded diplomacy aims to protect. Hurt: voters who cast ballots on 1 December 2025 without the deportee arrangement yet announced; IMPACS families and the journalists questioning them, met with refusal; southern patients, who got an explanation for St. Jude instead of a date.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's voters, the south, the poor, the young, the disabled, and the people the state itself once shot — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The distributional core of "Putting People First" was delivered as universal, category-based provision, not constituency patronage, on the independent record: the IMF's 2025 Article IV staff statement (mission concluded 14 November 2025; Board conclusion 12 January 2026) lists the government's own measures as increasing pensions and minimum pensions, implementing the minimum wage, expanding the Universal Health Care programme, establishing a Ministry for Persons with Disabilities, and announcing an unemployment-insurance plan — and records three consecutive years of primary budget surpluses alongside them (IMF, January 2026).
  • The cost-of-living shield was broad and sustained: fuel and cooking-gas prices held stable by subsidy and forgone revenue across the term — the party's own five-year accounting claims sixty months of it, a partisan source recorded as such — VAT removed from selected food items from July 2025, no new taxes in the 2025/26 or the record 2026/27 budgets, and a tax amnesty extended to 1 May 2026 (SLP five-year accounting, July 2026; Budget Highlights 2025/26; ECCB Budget Summary FY2026/27, April 2026).
  • Inclusion was practised across the aisle at the start: his first cabinet incorporated two former UWP cabinet members who had won as independents, and the SLP's 2026 accounting pledges service to all 17 constituencies (SLP five-year accounting, July 2026). The 2021 manifesto's stated values were "equity, respect for the rule of law, and inclusiveness," and its specific inclusions were partly delivered: facility fees paid for students, a Youth Economy disbursing over 1,000 youth grants and an MSME loan-grant facility of EC$6.9 million to 450-plus businesses by 2025, Emancipation Month observances and Kwéyòl and African studies entering classrooms (2021 manifesto review, St. Lucia Times; Budget Highlights 2025/26).
  • The south, historically the counted-out end of the island, received the term's flagship: St. Jude Hospital, destroyed by fire in 2009 and housed in a stadium for sixteen years, rebuilt at Augier, Vieux-Fort with a US$75 million Saudi Fund for Development concessional loan (contracts signed 13 September 2024) and handed over to the Ministry of Health on 16 November 2025 as a 100-bed facility doubling the old capacity (Saudi Fund / Government of Saint Lucia, September 2024; Prime Minister's handover address, 16 November 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The homicide file counts the failure in bodies, and the bodies are not evenly spread. Conflicting counts are printed separately, never blended: police-reported figures compiled publicly give 72 homicides in 2022, 75 in 2023, 76 in 2024, 70 in 2025, and 50 year-to-date to 18 October 2026; InSight Crime gives 77 victims in 2024 and 70 in 2025, a rate of 38.9 per 100,000 in 2025, down 9.2%; UNODC's rate for 2024 is 40.6 per 100,000 (police figures via public compilation, October 2026; InSight Crime 2025 Homicide Round-Up; UNODC via homicide-rate tables, February 2026). On any shelf, Saint Lucia under Pierre remained one of the most violent countries in the region — a national record was set in 2024, on his watch — with the violence concentrated in poor communities whose names recur in the reporting, Marchand and Vieux-Fort among them; police reported overall crime down 474 cases to 23 September 2026 and homicides down from 59 to 48 over the same span, a genuine recent improvement that still leaves roughly one killing a week (Royal Saint Lucia Police Force statistics via Choice TV, 24 September 2026). The 2021 manifesto promised proactive policing and improved detection; police reported a detection rate of 44% of homicides to August 2026 (RSLPF via DBS Television, 20 August 2026).
  • The IMPACS twelve and their families are the clearest counted-out group in the file: a decade and a half after the killings, and five years into the tenure of a prime minister who also held national security, they have neither a prosecution nor an official exoneration to point to — only the DPP's 2021 statement that there is no time limit (see Test 1). The joint civil-society submission to Saint Lucia's 2025 UPR records the families' continuing financial, social, and emotional hardship amid the homicide surge (Joint Submission to the UPR of Saint Lucia, 2025).
  • The economic count improved and then slipped for those at the margin: unemployment reached a historic low of 10.8% in 2024 and then rose to 13.4% in the second quarter of 2025 as stayover arrivals fell 3.2%; public debt stabilises around 77% of GDP, well above the 60% regional target, limiting what the state can count on spending for the poor; and the IMF records income per capita diverging from the United States over decades (IMF Article IV, January 2026). The UWP's cost-of-living criticism — that households still feel squeezed despite the subsidies — is an interested party's claim, recorded as such; the IMF's inflation figures (headline CPI −0.5% in 2024, ~0.8% projected 2025) support the government on prices in aggregate while saying nothing about any individual household's basket (IMF, January 2026; TVC reporting of UWP criticism, 2025).
  • The third-country nationals accepted in 2026 are counted in the arrangement's arithmetic — capped, screened, serviced by an international organisation — but Saint Lucia is not a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention or its 1967 Protocol, a gap monitors flag for chain-refoulement risk to people US judges had found likely to face persecution at home; no such return is documented, and the risk is recorded as a risk, not an event (Third Country Deportation Watch, 2026).

Why this grade, in plain words: The money genuinely went broad — a first minimum wage, higher pensions, a disability ministry, subsidised fuel and cooking gas, food VAT relief — and the south genuinely got its hospital building. But the two groups with the strongest claim to be counted first, the families of the men the police shot in 2010–11 and the young men dying in the poor districts at a rate near 40 per 100,000, are the two groups the record shows still waiting. Universal provision plus unclosed wounds is a mixed count. Helped: minimum-wage earners and pensioners (first-time floors and increases); persons with disabilities (a dedicated ministry); young entrepreneurs (Youth Economy grants); public servants (EC$46 million backpay and $500 bonuses, Budget Highlights 2025/26); southern residents (St. Jude rebuilt). Hurt: homicide victims' families across the poor districts — 76 dead in 2024 on the police count, 70 in 2025; the IMPACS families, still uncounted by any court; jobseekers in 2025, when unemployment rose back to 13.4%.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 28 July 2021, with dates; the two axes — word-keeping and helped/hurt — graded independently, with all four crossings named.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The Caribbean Court of Justice — kept, on the manifesto's own terms. The 2021 manifesto pledged ascension to the CCJ; parliament voted on 28 February 2023 and accession was announced on 9 May 2023 (Parliament, February 2023; CCJ, May 2023). Crossing 1 — kept his word AND it helped people: every Saint Lucian litigant's final appeal moved from London to the region.
  • The minimum wage and tax relief — kept. A first national minimum wage took effect 1 October 2024 at EC$6.52 an hour; the EC$25,000 personal allowance took effect 1 January 2023 (Minimum Wage Order 2024; Income Tax Amendment, 2023). Crossing 1 — kept AND helped: the lowest-paid gained a floor and every worker gained allowance, though the IMF's standing advice to monitor the wage's employment effects is recorded with it (IMF, January 2026).
  • No new taxes — kept across the graded budgets, including the record FY2026/27 budget presented 21–22 April 2026 (ECCB Budget Summary, April 2026; Budget Highlights 2025/26). Crossing 1 in form; the helped/hurt ledger notes the same budgets were financed while debt stabilised near 77% of GDP, a cost carried forward rather than avoided (IMF, January 2026).
  • St. Jude Hospital — the building kept, the opening receding (see against shelf). The promise to "return St Jude Hospital to its rightful home" was made in 2021; financing was secured 13 September 2024 and the completed facility was handed over on 16 November 2025 with the prime minister declaring "Promise Made! Promise Kept! Promise Delivered!" (handover address, 16 November 2025).
  • Anti-corruption machinery — built as promised. The 2021 manifesto pledged anti-corruption measures; the Special Prosecutor Act was enacted 10 August 2022 with an independently appointed prosecutor (Government of Saint Lucia, 2022). The machinery exists; its output is graded in Test 1, not double-counted here.
  • The 2025 word, for the record: the second manifesto, "Moving Forward Together" (November 2025), promises a national unemployment-insurance programme with the NIC, expanded universal health coverage, Hewanorra redevelopment advanced, half of classrooms digitally powered by 2035, and Saint Lucia "the most technologically driven Small Island Developing State by 2035" (St. Lucia Times, November 2025). These are recorded so the record can grade them later; a year into the term they are direction, not yet record.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • St. Jude's full opening — the declared-kept promise that wasn't finished. A year after the handover at which the prime minister declared the promise delivered, the health minister told the pre-Cabinet briefing on 16 November 2026 that full transition from the George Odlum Stadium was now expected "within a reasonable time in 2027," with no exact date, after an April 2026 expectation of full commissioning within three to four months had passed; some services operate at the new site, the full hospital does not (St. Lucia Daily Post, 16 November 2026). Crossing 4 in part — the word as declared ("delivered," November 2025) broke against the fact, AND it hurt people: southern patients continue to be served from a stadium facility sixteen years after the fire. The building itself is Crossing 1; the declaration is graded as spoken.
  • Hewanorra — the terminal is five years behind the rhetoric. The 2021-era commitment to fix the airport produced, by November 2026, a half-built control tower, a signed runway contract, and a terminal contractor announced in October 2026 — no terminal. Opposition critics allege the halt cost some EC$800 million and 3,000 jobs; those figures are an interested party's ALLEGATION, unverified in the record assembled. The documented fact is delay, and the delay's origin is partly his own earlier choice to stop and review — graded in Test 3 as a defensible adjustment, and recorded here as a promise whose delivery date has never been met.
  • Universal health care and unemployment insurance — still becoming.
