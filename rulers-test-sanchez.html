The Ruler's Test — Pedro Sánchez, Prime Minister of Spain

Subject: Pedro Sánchez Pérez-Castejón — Prime Minister of Spain since 2 June 2018, when he won a no-confidence vote against Mariano Rajoy in the wake of the Gürtel corruption judgment; Secretary General of the Spanish Socialist Workers' Party (PSOE). He lost the July 2023 general election's plurality to the People's Party (PP, 137 seats to the PSOE's 121) but won the investiture on 16 November 2023 by 179 votes to 171, on the votes of Sumar, ERC, Junts, EH Bildu, the PNV and others. On 5 October 2026, after parliament defeated his government's housing decrees, he dissolved the Cortes and called a snap general election for 29 November 2026; he is graded as the sitting (caretaker) prime minister and candidate in that election. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The machinery of justice reached his innermost circle and was not stopped. On 22 June 2026 the Supreme Court (judgment 418/2026, unanimous, after a 14-session trial) convicted José Luis Ábalos — Sánchez's former transport minister, former PSOE organisation secretary and closest early ally — of criminal organisation, bribery, embezzlement and influence peddling in the pandemic mask-contracts scheme, sentencing him to 24 years and three months; his aide Koldo García received 19 years and eight months, and the businessman Víctor de Aldama four and a half years, suspended for cooperation (Supreme Court judgment, 22 June 2026; Reuters and OCCRP reporting). Ábalos and García had been in custody since November 2025; García's constitutional appeal was pending at grading.
  • His own Attorney General was convicted in office's aftermath: Álvaro García Ortiz, appointed by Sánchez's government in August 2022, was found guilty by the Supreme Court on 20 November 2025 of revealing secrets in the tax case of the Madrid regional president's partner — the first conviction of a Spanish attorney general — receiving two years' disqualification, a €7,200 fine and €10,000 in compensation (Supreme Court judgment, 20 November 2025; full reasoning published 9 December 2025, with two dissenting judges). He resigned on 24 November 2025.
  • His family was not shielded. His brother David Sánchez was convicted by the Badajoz provincial court on 14 July 2026 of administrative abuse of office (prevaricación) as a necessary co-author in the creation of a provincial-council post tailored to him, receiving nine years' disqualification; he was acquitted of influence peddling, the prosecution had itself sought acquittal, and the judgment is under appeal and not final (Reuters, 14 July 2026; Audiencia de Badajoz). On 18 November 2026 the Court of Auditors opened an accounting investigation into the more than €427,000 in salary he received in the post between 2017 and 2025 (Tribunal de Cuentas ruling, November 2026).
  • His wife Begoña Gómez was ordered to stand trial before a popular jury for alleged influence peddling and embezzlement, with her passport surrendered and a ban on leaving Spain imposed on 20 June 2026 (investigating court No. 41, Madrid; Reuters and AP, 20 June 2026). The charges are ALLEGATIONs; Gómez denies wrongdoing, the public prosecutor seeks her acquittal, and the higher provincial court had earlier struck the business-corruption and misappropriation counts from the case. No trial date had been set at grading.
  • When the Civil Guard's Central Operative Unit (UCO) report of 12 June 2025 implicated his party's organisation secretary Santos Cerdán in an alleged public-works kickback scheme, Sánchez accepted Cerdán's resignation from the party post and his Congress seat the same day, apologised publicly, and ordered an external audit of the party's accounts (IDEA Global State of Democracy report, June 2025; Reuters, June–July 2025). Ábalos had earlier been expelled from the PSOE in February 2024, days after Koldo García's arrest opened the case.
  • A former prime minister of his own party and his close political ally, José Luis Rodríguez Zapatero, was placed under formal investigation on 19 May 2026 — the first such case against a former premier in democratic Spain — and the investigation proceeded with searches of his office (Audiencia Nacional, Judge Calama; The Times and Euractiv reporting, May 2026). The allegations are set out under Evidence against, because the government's political response belongs there; the procedural fact that the case moved forward belongs here.

Evidence against:

  • The pattern of protection-until-undeniable runs through the file. Sánchez publicly stood by Cerdán — he said on 12 June 2025 that until that morning he had been fully convinced of Cerdán's innocence — and the UCO report that forced the resignation also contained material reported as suggesting Sánchez may have known of Ábalos's and García's activities earlier than he acknowledged (UCO report as reported, June 2025). That suggestion is an ALLEGATION, unadjudicated; Sánchez's position is that he knew nothing of any wrongdoing.
  • Cerdán was then held in pre-trial detention from 30 June 2025 until his release on 19 November 2025, and the investigation widened: an October 2025 UCO report described suspicious cash payments collected in party-branded envelopes at PSOE headquarters — the core of an alleged illegal party-financing scheme that remains an ALLEGATION under judicial investigation (UCO reporting as published, October 2025; case before the Audiencia Nacional).
  • The gravest one-law item is an ALLEGATION still being investigated: the "Leire Díez case," in which National Court Judge Santiago Pedraz is investigating an alleged plot — attributed by the UCO to Cerdán's direction and coordinated by the former party member Leire Díez — to obstruct judicial proceedings affecting the PSOE, the government and the prime minister's relatives, and to gather compromising material on judges, prosecutors and Civil Guard officers. The UCO searched the PSOE's Ferraz headquarters and homes in May 2026 and attributes to Cerdán the instruction to pay Díez's travel from party funds; the Anti-Corruption Prosecutor has sought the party president's testimony as a witness, the party's manager is under investigation, and Pedraz extended the instruction to June 2027 (Audiencia Nacional proceedings and UCO reports as reported by El País, Europa Press and Demócrata, 2025–2026). Sánchez denies any knowledge of Díez's activities. If established, the scheme would be the use of party machinery against the courts themselves; at grading it is an allegation, and it is weighed as one.
  • The Zapatero case: Judge Calama's order of 19 May 2026 charges the former prime minister with money laundering, influence peddling, forgery and membership of a criminal organisation, describing him as the alleged leader of "a stable, hierarchical influence-peddling network" built around the €53 million state bailout of the Venezuelan-linked airline Plus Ultra, approved by Sánchez's government in March 2021, with some €1.95 million in alleged improper commissions; a search of Zapatero's office found jewellery valued at €1.3 million whose origin he has yet to justify, now the subject of a separate tax and smuggling probe (Audiencia Nacional order, May 2026; El País and The Times reporting). All of it is ALLEGATION: Zapatero denies ever acting on the bailout, is cooperating, seeks the case's annulment, and case reporting notes no direct evidence against him has yet been produced. Sánchez and the PSOE publicly closed ranks in his defence — a political choice recorded here because Zapatero was also the PSOE's emissary in the 2023 investiture negotiations with Junts (see Test 5).
  • The amnesty law of 2024 is a one-law problem of a different kind (its promise dimension is graded in Test 5): a general law whose decisive beneficiaries were the leaders of the parties whose seven votes Sánchez needed, negotiated as an investiture price and initially voted down on 30 January 2024 by Junts itself for not going far enough. The Constitutional Court upheld its core on 26 June 2025 as admissible in an exceptional situation of legitimate public interest (Constitutional Court ruling, June 2025; Reuters). Its legality is settled by that ruling; its character as a law shaped to a transaction is part of the record.
  • His government opened a pardon file for its own convicted Attorney General. The Justice Ministry began processing a pardon for García Ortiz in January 2026; the Supreme Court reported against it, finding no grounds of justice, equity or public utility; the Constitutional Court postponed his amparo appeal until after the 29 November 2026 election; and the Justice Minister said in November 2026 that "neither decision has been ruled out" (Demócrata and Europa Press reporting, November 2026). The government publicly maintained its belief in García Ortiz's innocence after his conviction. A pardon granted to the government's own top prosecutor, against the sentencing court's reported advice, would be one law for the insider; at grading the file is open and undecided.
  • His standing public frame for all of the above is that the cases are a "mud machine" (máquina del fango) — a campaign of harassment by the right, parts of the media and rogue judges. That framing is his stated position and is recorded as such throughout this dossier; as a one-law matter it treats scrutiny of his own camp as illegitimate by definition, whatever any individual case's merits.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the judiciary council (CGPJ), prosecutors, media, and the regions keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The judiciary's governing body was unblocked by negotiation, not capture. The CGPJ's mandate expired in December 2018 and the council sat in caretaker form for over five years — a blockade sustained principally by the PP's refusal to renew under the existing rules. On 25 June 2024 PSOE and PP signed a renewal deal in Brussels under European Commission mediation; the new council took office on 25 July 2024 and its members chose Isabel Perelló, the body's first woman president (agreement of 25 June 2024; Organic Law 3/2024). The accompanying law barred anyone who had held political office in the previous five years from council membership or the attorney generalship, and required the council to propose a new election system with direct judicial participation.
  • The courts ruled against his government's central project for over a year and were obeyed. The Supreme Court refused from July 2024 to apply the amnesty to the embezzlement convictions of the Catalan leaders, holding that the offence fell outside the law; the government criticised the reading but complied with it, and the question was resolved against the Supreme Court only on 6 October 2026, when the Constitutional Court (7–5, on Dolors Bassa's amparo appeal) held that embezzlement without personal enrichment is amnestied and Judge Llarena lifted the arrest warrants on Carles Puigdemont and two former ministers the same day (Constitutional Court, October 2026; Reuters, 6 October 2026). Whatever the merits, the channel was judicial throughout.
  • No judge has been removed, transferred or sanctioned at his government's hand for ruling against it; the opposition-controlled Senate functions as an institutional check (it ran its own Koldo investigation committee, before which Cerdán testified on 30 April 2024, and in December 2025 filed a conflict of powers against the government at the Constitutional Court over the missing budgets); private and opposition media operate freely and, in several cases, broke the stories that damaged him.
  • The regions keep their governments: Catalonia has been governed since 8 August 2024 by Salvador Illa of Sánchez's own party, who won the Catalan election of 12 May 2024 at the ballot box — the first non-independentist Catalan government in fourteen years — not by any Madrid imposition; PP-governed regions have repeatedly defied his government (including over migrant-minor distribution) without institutional retaliation documented.

Evidence against:

  • The prosecution's apex was treated as part of the government's house. His first attorney general, Dolores Delgado, was appointed in 2020 directly from the justice minister's chair — a revolving-door appointment criticised across the legal spectrum. Her successor García Ortiz was kept in office by the government for thirteen months while under Supreme Court investigation and then on trial for leaking material damaging to the opposition's Madrid president, defended publicly throughout, and — after conviction — maintained as innocent by the government with a pardon file opened (see Test 1). The 2024 CGPJ law's own five-year cooling-off rule for the attorney generalship was written after, and because of, this pattern.
  • Pressure on the judiciary was written into his governing programme. The PSOE–Junts investiture pact of 9 November 2023 adopted the term "lawfare" and committed to parliamentary inquiry commissions into alleged politically motivated judicial operations — an instrument aimed at named ongoing proceedings, criticised by all four Spanish judges' associations at the time. Sánchez himself filed a complaint for alleged prevarication against Judge Peinado, the judge investigating his wife, after Peinado questioned him at the Moncloa palace (complaint reported 2024–2025); Peinado completed the investigation and retired at the mandatory age of 72 on 27 September 2026, days after ordering Gómez's trial.
  • Parliament's core functions were hollowed by practice. His government presented no budget at all in the entire 2023–2026 legislature (graded for word-keeping in Test 5); it legislated extensively by royal decree-law, including the largest migrant regularisation in Spanish history (Test 4), enacted by decree in April 2026 after the parliamentary route stalled; and it repeatedly used its Congress majority to block opposition investigation commissions into the Koldo case in the lower house while the Senate's proceeded.
  • The depoliticisation promised in the 2024 CGPJ deal remains unfinished on his watch: the council sent two election-system options in February 2025; the Venice Commission held in October 2025 that only direct election of judicial members by their peers meets European standards; and the European Commission's July 2026 Rule of Law report records that no consensus to complete the reform had been found (Venice Commission opinion, October 2025; European Commission Rule of Law Report, July 2026). The government did not force the issue against its own 2024 partners — recorded here as an unkept institutional commitment rather than a grip.
  • His September 2024 "Democratic Regeneration" action plan — announced as the fruit of his April 2024 reflection — proposed state registers and advertising-transparency rules for media that press associations and the opposition characterised as instruments of pressure on critical outlets; the government characterised them as anti-disinformation measures. CONTESTED as to purpose; the measures' progress through parliament was limited, and the characterisation dispute is recorded rather than resolved.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His signature adjustment is electoral. After the PSOE's heavy defeats in the May 2023 regional and local elections, he dissolved parliament the next day and called the July 2023 general election — submitting his government to the voters rather than serving out the term. He lost the plurality and rebuilt his majority by pact (Test 5 grades the price).
  • In the Cerdán crisis he moved within hours and in the open: same-day acceptance of the resignation, a public apology ("I was wrong" to trust, in his July 2025 formulation accepting responsibility as the one who appointed them), an external audit of party accounts ordered, a purge of Cerdán allies from the party leadership at the July 2025 federal committee, and on 9 July 2025 a State Plan Against Corruption of fifteen measures drawn up with the OECD — an Independent Public Integrity Agency, mandatory external audits of parties, donation-publication thresholds lowered to €2,500, procurement blacklists for convicted companies (La Moncloa, 9 July 2025; Reuters, July 2025).
  • After the Urda rail disaster of 28 October 2026 — a collision of two high-speed trains near Urda (Toledo) that killed 69 people — the transport minister, Óscar Puente, resigned in the days that followed (press reporting, October–November 2026). Ministerial resignation after catastrophe is the system's accountability reflex working.
  • When parliament finally defeated a central government measure — the housing decrees, voted down on 2 October 2026 by PP, Vox and Junts — he did not govern around the defeat: he dissolved the Cortes on 5 October 2026 and called the 29 November election, framing the choice for voters directly (Royal Decree 806/2026; Le Monde, October 2026). In the 19 November 2026 televised debate with Feijóo he publicly admitted to "mistakes" (Le Monde, 20 November 2026).
  • On Catalonia, his adjustments were substantive and sustained: the 2021 pardons and the 2024 amnesty were followed by measurable de-escalation — a Socialist Catalan government elected in May 2024, independence support at historic lows in Catalan polling, and Junts itself negotiating over financing and rail services rather than secession by late in the legislature (his own account in the Salvados interview, 22 November 2026, is consistent with the electoral record).

Evidence against:

  • The April 2024 reflection is the type case of the other pattern. On 24 April 2024, hours after a court opened the investigation of his wife, he published a letter suspending his public duties for five days to consider resigning, denouncing the "mud machine"; on 29 April he announced he would stay "with more force if possible," attributing the decision partly to supporters' rallies (letter of 24 April 2024; address of 29 April 2024; Reuters/AP reporting). The promised "before and after" produced the contested media plan of Test 2 and no structural change; the investigation he called baseless ended, thirty months later, with his wife ordered to stand trial (Test 1).
  • For sixteen months after Cerdán's fall — through the Ábalos detention, the García Ortiz conviction, the Zapatero indictment and repeated Senate motions — he refused every call for resignation or early elections, including from the institutional opposition, presenting the pressure itself as the mud machine's work. The adjustment, when it came in October 2026, was triggered by a housing defeat, not by the corruption record (congressional and Senate record, 2025–2026).
  • The anti-corruption plan's delivery lagged its announcement by the government's own timetable: a year after July 2025, none of the fifteen measures had entered into force; the draft Public Integrity Law concentrating nine of them received first cabinet approval only on 17 February 2026 and was still awaiting its second as the legislature ended (El País-derived reporting via Europa Press, June 2026). Announcement as adjustment, delivery deferred — the pattern is documented in his own government's schedule.
  • On NATO spending he claimed more than his counterpart would confirm: on 22 June 2025 he announced Spain had secured exclusion from the alliance's new 5%-of-GDP target and would meet its capability commitments at 2.1%; Secretary General Rutte stated publicly that "NATO has no opt-outs," that there were no side deals, and that NATO was "absolutely convinced Spain will have to spend 3.5%" on core defence (AP, 23–26 June 2025). CONTESTED between the two principals; the summit text's removal of "all allies" is documented, and so is Rutte's contradiction.
  • On Valencia (see Test 4), his first institutional response was jurisdictional — alerts and emergency management were the region's competence, and Madrid's help followed the region's requests — delivered while the regional president's failures were becoming undeniable; he was evacuated from Paiporta on 3 November 2024 as residents threw mud, while the King remained to face the crowd (Reuters, 3 November 2024). The sequencing is documented; the two governments' competing accounts of who failed to ask and who failed to send remain CONTESTED between them.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Catalans, migrants, the flood-hit, the priced-out, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Catalans who broke the law in 2017 were counted back in: the 2021 pardons of the nine imprisoned leaders, then the 2024 amnesty, applied to more than 300 people by mid-2025 (Constitutional Court reporting via Reuters, June 2025) and, after the October 2026 ruling, extended to the embezzlement cases — closing the exile of Puigdemont after nine years. The political result is measurable: the independence movement's parties now negotiate budgets and rail transfers, and a non-independentist government sits in Barcelona.
  • Migrants were counted in at the largest scale in Spanish history. Announced on 27 January 2026 after an agreement with Podemos and rooted in a citizens' legislative initiative backed by over 700,000 signatures, Royal Decree 316/2026 opened regularisation to foreigners present in Spain before 31 December 2025 with five months' residence and no criminal record; applications ran 14 April–30 June 2026 and reached some 1.17 million by the deadline — more than double the ~500,000 expected (AP, 27 January 2026; Council of Ministers, 14 April 2026; registration figures as reported). Successful applicants receive a one-year renewable residence and work permit; their minor children, five years.
  • The material floor rose under him and the labour market delivered: the minimum wage rose from €736 a month in 2018 to €1,184 in 2025 through annual government increases; pensions were re-indexed to inflation by law in 2021 and the revaluation applied every year since; GDP grew 2.8% in 2025, the fastest among large euro economies, and unemployment fell to 9.9% in the fourth quarter of 2025 — below 10% for the first time since 2008 — with a record 22.46 million in employment (INE data via CaixaBank Research, February 2026). The European Commission's autumn 2026 forecast projects 2.6% growth in 2026 and public debt falling below 100% of GDP for the first time since 2019.

Evidence against:

  • The victims of the 29 October 2024 Valencia floods — 229 dead in the province of Valencia, Spain's deadliest modern natural disaster — experienced the state as absent and then as jurisdictional. The regional government's failures are documented and primary (its president, the PP's Carlos Mazón, spent the critical afternoon at a long lunch, reached the emergency coordination centre at 20:28, and did not resign until 3 November 2025, keeping his assembly seat and its immunity thereafter). Sánchez's government held that alerts were a regional competence, framed its deployments around regional requests, and its head was evacuated from Paiporta on 3 November 2024 under a hail of mud while residents shouted that the dead were still in the garages (Reuters, November 2024). Victims' associations marched monthly for over a year demanding accountability from both governments; reconstruction aid was approved in successive packages, and the sense of abandonment is itself part of the documented record (victims' association protests, 2024–2026). Blame between the two administrations is CONTESTED; that flood victims were the last to be counted is the associations' documented charge against both.
  • A generation is counted out of housing. Rents and purchase prices rose relentlessly through his tenure despite a 2023 housing law and repeated promises; by autumn 2026 housing was the country's first concern in polling, mass protests filled Spanish cities, and the eviction of an 87-year-old woman by a real-estate company in September 2026 became the symbol that detonated the legislature — his emergency housing decrees were voted down on 2 October 2026 and he dissolved parliament three days later (Wikipedia's 2026 election record; Le Monde, October–November 2026). The crisis that ended his third term was the one his governments had promised to solve.
  • Growth coexisted with a stubborn exclusion rate: 26.4% of Spain's population was at risk of poverty or social exclusion in the latest Eurostat data — the EU's fourth-highest — and the European Commission projects the 2026 deficit rising to 2.6% of GDP partly on measures cushioning energy prices after the Middle East conflict (Eurostat; European Commission forecast, November 2026). The aggregate count is strong; the tail is long and documented.
  • His opponents' voters are addressed as a bloc to be contained: his stated strategy since the 2023 investiture has been a "wall" (muro) against the "reactionary coalition" of PP and Vox — the voters of parties that together won more votes than his own in 2023 are counted as a threat to be walled off rather than a constituency to be persuaded, a framing he maintained through the 2026 campaign. It is rhetoric, recorded as rhetoric; it is also the opposite of counting everyone.
  • The regularisation's method qualifies its substance: enacted by decree to bypass a stalled parliament and announced as part of a support agreement with Podemos (AP, 27 January 2026) — the undocumented were counted in, and counted as parliamentary currency, in the same act. Both facts stand.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Signature promises and stated positions vs. the record across eight years.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The social core of his 2018–2023 offers was substantially delivered: annual minimum-wage increases (Test 4), pension revaluation with inflation enacted in 2021 and applied since, the 2021 labour reform negotiated with unions and employers, the euthanasia law (2021), and the minimum living income created in 2020. A voter who backed the social programme received much of it.
  • He came to power on an anti-corruption motion in June 2018 — the Gürtel judgment against the PP — and his governments did legislate on integrity (whistleblower protection law 2/2023; the 2025 plan of Test 3, undelivered as graded there).
  • His word to his coalition partners was kept at cost: the investiture agreements with ERC and Junts — amnesty, and negotiations on Catalan financing and the transfer of commuter-rail services — were implemented even as they damaged him nationally, and the 2023–2026 legislature's survival on 179 votes is itself evidence that his signatures bind him to partners.

Evidence against:

  • The amnesty reversal is the centre of the file, and it is admitted. Before the July 2023 election, amnesty for the procés leaders was off his table: it formed no part of the PSOE programme, his government had treated the measure as unconstitutional, and he had said Puigdemont would be brought to face Spanish justice (campaign record, 2023; The Corner, May 2024). On 28 October 2023, needing the fourteen ERC and Junts votes, he told his party's Federal Committee that "in the name of Spain, in the interest of Spain, in defence of coexistence among Spaniards, today I defend the amnesty in Catalonia," described the task as making "necessity a virtue," and conceded the measure was a condition for a progressive government (Federal Committee address, 28 October 2023). Puigdemont's account, published in El Mundo on 18 November 2026, is that Junts set out to test how far PSOE ambition would stretch, that the amnesty was accepted more easily than he expected, and that the talks ran through Santos Cerdán as Sánchez's link — Puigdemont's telling is self-interested and CONTESTED; the sequence it describes is not disputed. Asked on Salvados on 22 November 2026 why citizens should trust his word after the amnesty denial, Sánchez answered that "in addition to the word, there are also the facts" (Salvados, laSexta, 22 November 2026, via Europa Press).
  • The pattern predates 2023. In the 4 November 2019 election debate he pledged, of the Basque party EH Bildu: "I'll repeat it twenty times if you like — we will not pact with Bildu" (2019 debate record). EH Bildu became a habitual supporter of his budgets and legislation. In September 2019 he said he "would not sleep peacefully" with Podemos ministers in government; in January 2020 he formed a coalition government with Podemos. In 2019 he demanded the procés convicts serve their sentences in full; in June 2021 he pardoned all nine.
  • Budgets: the promise was structural and the breach total. As opposition leader he had defined a government without budgets as "a car without gasoline" and demanded elections of those who could not pass them (opposition record, 2018, as widely cited). In the 2023–2026 legislature his government never presented a single budget: the 2023 budget was extended through 2024, 2025 and 2026 — over 1,000 days by September 2026 (Euronews count). On 28 July 2025 he promised the 2026 budget would be presented; it was not, the spending ceiling was defeated in November 2025, Congress rejected the 2027 spending framework on 23 July 2026 (176–166), and the Senate filed a conflict of powers at the Constitutional Court on 22 December 2025 over the failure (congressional and Senate records; Reuters, July 2026). His defence — that Article 134.4 provides automatic extension — is legally accurate and is recorded; it is a defence of the mechanism, not of the promise.
  • Housing was promised as the legislature's priority and ended it instead. The 2023 programme's commitments — including 180,000 affordable homes across the term — ran far ahead of delivery; by 2026 housing was the country's leading concern, his emergency decrees failed, and the failure itself became the reason for the snap election (Test 4). A promise whose shortfall dissolves the parliament that heard it is graded on both halves.
  • The anti-corruption word. He took office in 2018 as the candidate of clean government against a corruption judgment; he ends the legislature with his former right-hand man convicted to 24 years, his party's next organisation secretary investigated for kickbacks and an alleged obstruction plot, his attorney general convicted, his predecessor of his own party indicted, and his own fifteen-measure remedy unenacted (Tests 1 and 3). His consistent explanation — that he did not know — is on the record everywhere; the appointments were his, as he himself acknowledged in July 2025.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: FAILS.

Pedro Sánchez's file is defined by a single tension that no other dossier in this series presents in quite this form: the institutions he governs demonstrably work against him — his ministers, his party's fixers, his attorney general, his brother and his wife have all been prosecuted, and two of them convicted to the longest corruption sentences of Spain's democratic era, without any documented act of his stopping a single case — and yet the same record shows a ruler whose public word has been spent so often that his own defence, offered on television a week before the election he called, is that voters should judge "the facts" instead. The grip test is mixed because the grip was attempted rhetorically and programmatically — lawfare clauses, a defended attorney general, decree governance, a budget-free legislature — and never consolidated: courts ruled against him for years and were obeyed, the judiciary council was renewed by pact, and Catalonia was won back by ballots. The wobble is mixed because his adjustments are real (two dissolutions submitted to voters, a same-day sacrifice of his number three, an OECD-drafted remedy) and so is his sixteen-month refusal to adjust at all. The count is mixed because Catalans and a million undocumented migrants were counted in at historic scale while flood victims waited, a generation was priced out of housing, and half the country was addressed as a wall to be built against. The word fails on his own admissions: necessity made virtue, on the record, in the name of Spain. He is graded five days before the voters grade him; the record above is what they have to grade from.
