The Ruler's Test — Lee Jae-myung, President of South Korea

Subject: Lee Jae-myung — President of the Republic of Korea since 4 June 2025. He won the snap presidential election of 3 June 2025, called after President Yoon Suk Yeol was impeached and removed over his 3 December 2024 martial-law declaration. Lee had led the opposition Democratic Party (DP) through the martial-law crisis; before that he was Governor of Gyeonggi Province (2018–2021) and Mayor of Seongnam (2010–2018). He took office with five criminal trials of his own suspended, not resolved — a fact that runs through every test below. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Acts of the president are distinguished from acts of his party, his ministers, prosecutors, and the courts; where the actor is not Lee himself, the dossier says so. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. His presidential record is roughly seventeen months; where the evidence is thin, the dossier says so rather than filling the gap.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

The predicate fact, stated exactly as documented. Lee was indicted in five cases arising from his Seongnam and Gyeonggi years, all of which he and his party describe as fabricated, politically motivated prosecutions by the Yoon-era prosecution service — a characterization the prosecution and the opposition People Power Party (PPP) dispute, and which no court has adopted as a finding. The cases: (1) alleged breach of trust and related charges over the Daejang-dong, Wirye and Baekhyeon-dong development projects and Seongnam FC sponsorship payments (Seoul Central District Court); (2) alleged violation of the Public Official Election Act over statements made in the 2021–22 presidential campaign; (3) alleged subornation of perjury (Seoul High Court, on appeal from a first-instance acquittal on 25 November 2024); (4) alleged illegal remittances to North Korea by the Ssangbangwool group (Suwon District Court); (5) alleged misuse of Gyeonggi provincial corporate cards (Suwon District Court). In the election-law case he was convicted at first instance (15 November 2024; one year in prison, suspended for two years), acquitted on appeal (26 March 2025), and on 1 May 2025 the Supreme Court, sitting en banc, overturned the acquittal 10–2 and remanded the case for retrial with a guilty inclination. After his inauguration, the courts — beginning 10 June 2025 and completing the set by 22 July 2025 — suspended all five trials, citing Article 84 of the Constitution, which bars criminal prosecution of a sitting president except for insurrection or treason. The suspensions were the courts' acts, applying a constitutional text; they are not graded against him. What is graded is what he and his government did around them.

Evidence for:

  • The law was allowed to fall on the previous ruling house with full weight, through bodies Lee appointed but did not visibly direct. In June 2025 Lee approved three special-counsel statutes passed by the Assembly and appointed the counsels: Cho Eun-seok (the martial-law insurrection), Min Joong-ki (former First Lady Kim Keon Hee), and a third for the death of a Marine corporal. The insurrection counsel closed its probe in December 2025 concluding that Yoon had plotted martial law for over a year and indicting 24 people; prosecutors sought the death penalty for Yoon on 13 January 2026, and on 19 February 2026 the Seoul Central District Court sentenced him to life imprisonment. The Kim Keon Hee counsel closed its 180-day probe in December 2025 with 76 people indicted — the largest total in Korean special-counsel history — Kim herself facing nine charges after her arrest in August 2025. Lee publicly welcomed the Yoon verdict, crediting the people's defense of the constitutional order (court records and contemporaneous reporting, June 2025–February 2026).
  • As of this grading, Lee has granted no pardon to Yoon or to any principal of the martial-law attempt, although the pardon power is his alone and Korean presidents have historically pardoned disgraced predecessors.
  • His first Liberation Day pardon (announced 11 August 2025, effective 15 August) was formally broad: 2,188 pardons and reinstatements inside a measure touching 836,687 people including administrative-sanction relief, with business figures and politicians of more than one camp on the Justice Ministry's breakdown (Korea JoongAng Daily, August 2025).
  • Personnel accountability inside his own office was applied at least once, quickly: his first senior secretary for civil affairs, Oh Kwang-soo, was let go in June 2025, days after appointment, over a borrowed-name loan allegation (Korea JoongAng Daily editorial survey, 27 July 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The pardon list's headline names were his own camp's. Its most prominent beneficiary was Cho Kuk — former justice minister and founder of the Rebuilding Korea Party, a DP satellite ally — released on 15 August 2025 after serving eight months of a two-year sentence for academic fraud involving his children and obstruction of an official inspection, with his eligibility for office restored; his wife was pardoned with him. Also pardoned were a former lawmaker convicted of embezzling donations meant for survivors of Japanese wartime sexual slavery, a former Seongnam mayor convicted of bribery from Lee's own mayoral era, and a former vice justice minister convicted of assaulting a taxi driver. The PPP called it "a reward for regime change"; the Citizens' Coalition for Economic Justice said it undermined the rule of law; Lee's approval rating fell from 63.3% to 56.5% in a week, with pollsters naming the pardons as a cause (Reuters, 11 August 2025; Korea JoongAng Daily, August 2025). The offenses are established by conviction; the favoritism is the documented pattern of who was chosen.
  • In May 2025, while Lee was the DP's presidential candidate, his party advanced through Assembly committees two measures whose only practical beneficiary was Lee: a Criminal Procedure Act amendment to suspend the criminal trials of a president-elect, and an amendment deleting the element of "act" from the false-statement offense of which the Supreme Court had just found his campaign statements capable of constituting — processed six days after the 1 May ruling. The DP also publicly warned it could seek the impeachment of Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae if trial dates were not moved past the election (Aju Business Daily desk column, 17 November 2026; Chosun Ilbo editorial, May 2025). These were the party's acts, not the president's; Lee, then its candidate and until April 2025 its leader, did not disavow either. Neither measure was enacted — the courts suspended the trials on constitutional grounds instead.
  • The Daejang-dong appeal. On 31 October 2025 the Seoul Central District Court delivered the first-instance verdict in the trial of the Daejang-dong private developers — the case whose factual core is the same project for which Lee himself stands indicted in a separate, suspended trial. The prosecution then let the appeal deadline lapse at midnight on 7–8 November 2025, ending the case. Acting Prosecutor General Noh Man-seok stated that the Vice Minister of Justice had presented him with options all of which implied dropping the appeal, and that he "had to comply" given the prosecution's position vis-à-vis the presidential office and the ministry; Justice Minister Jung Sung-ho stated he had merely advised caution, had not opposed an appeal, and had never discussed the case with the presidential office. Whether the presidential office was involved is an ALLEGATION, made by the PPP and denied by the minister; what is documented is that the acting head of the prosecution described ministerial pressure, resigned on 12 November 2025, and that the practical effect of the non-appeal was to close the companion case to the president's own (Korea JoongAng Daily, November 2025).
  • On 19 November 2026, Lee promulgated — while on an overseas trip, two days after Cabinet approval and without exercising the veto the PPP had demanded — the "fabricated indictment" special-counsel act passed by the DP-led Assembly. Of its 12 investigation categories, six concern the cases against Lee himself: the development projects, the election-law statements, the perjury allegation, the Ssangbangwool remittances, Seongnam FC, and the corporate cards. The counsel — up to 309 staff, a budget above 38 billion won, the largest ever — is appointed by the president from party nominees, to investigate whether the president's own indictments were fabricated. The DP removed, on 4 November 2026, an earlier clause that would have let the counsel press for withdrawal of existing indictments; the Seoul Economic Daily and the PPP argue the withdrawal route survives indirectly through ministerial direction if fabrication is "found," which the presidential office and the DP dismiss as groundless fear. CONTESTED as to purpose and effect; the structure — a president appointing the investigator of his own cases — is documented fact (Cabinet and promulgation records, 17–19 November 2026; YTN and Seoul Economic Daily reporting).
  • His government has pursued abolition of the Criminal Act's breach-of-trust offense as part of an "economic penalty rationalization" program — the same offense under which Lee stands indicted in the Daejang-dong case. The abolition had not been enacted as of this grading; the intersection is documented and is cited by critics across the legal press (Aju Business Daily, 17 November 2026).
  • In November 2026 Lee nominated Lee Yong-cheol as justice minister. The nominee is a founding member of the Saegil law firm where Lee Jae-myung was managing attorney, served on Lee's Seongnam mayoral transition team, and headed a Seongnam city public foundation under Mayor Lee. At his confirmation hearing on 24 November 2026, asked whether he would direct prosecutors to withdraw the charges against the president — a direction a justice minister has legal authority to give — he repeatedly declined to rule it out, saying facts must be established first and declining to say whether the presidential office had raised withdrawal with him during his nomination. The opposition calls him a "minister picked to withdraw the charges"; the nominee denies belonging to any "charge withdrawal group" (Korea JoongAng Daily and Seoul Economic Daily hearing coverage, 22–24 November 2026). Days before the hearing, Lee himself posted on social media questioning how recovering 550 billion won for the public in the Daejang-dong project could constitute breach of trust — remarks the Korea JoongAng Daily's editorial page said inevitably read as signaling both the special counsel and the nominee.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the prosecution, the media, the National Assembly, and the military keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts still rule against the government's side, in the open, on the largest matters. On 19 November 2026 the Supreme Court's full bench remanded in part the case against former Prime Minister Han Duck-soo — convicted at first instance in January 2026 of abetting the insurrection and sentenced to 23 years — partially acquitting him on a key charge, against the position of the special counsel Lee had appointed. A former PPP floor leader indicted by the insurrection special counsel for obstructing the martial-law repeal vote was acquitted at first trial. The courts, not the president, suspended Lee's own trials, on their reading of Article 84. No judge is documented to have been removed for a ruling (Supreme Court and trial records, 2025–2026).
  • Elections remain a real check and the opposition holds real ground: in the 3 June 2026 local elections the DP won 12 of 16 metropolitan and provincial races but lost the Seoul mayoralty to the PPP incumbent, and lost four of the fourteen parliamentary by-election seats it had mostly held. The PPP organizes, filibusters, boycotts, and criticizes the president daily; its leader calls the president's signature law a "prison escape" on the record (election results, June 2026; National Assembly record).
  • The press operates freely in fact: the president is criticized by name in every major outlet, his approval slides are reported weekly, and the journalists' campaign against his party's media law was conducted in the open (see against-shelf for the law itself).
  • Lee's own exposure to questioning is comparatively high: he held a televised press conference at 100 days (11 September 2025), a special press conference marking one year since martial law (3 December 2025), moved the presidential office back to the Blue House in December 2025, and personally shared an unfavorable approval poll on his own social-media account in June 2026 rather than suppressing it.

Evidence against:

  • The prosecution — the institution holding his five indictments — was dismantled on his watch, by his party's legislation, while those indictments sat suspended. A government reorganization finalized on 7 September 2025 split indictment from investigation; in March 2026 the Assembly passed the acts creating an Indictment Agency under the Justice Ministry and a Serious Crimes Investigation Agency under the Interior Ministry (Reuters, 20 March 2026); on 1 August 2026 the DP ended a PPP filibuster by a three-fifths vote and passed, 175–2, a Criminal Procedure Act revision stripping prosecutors of supplementary-investigation authority, after which the acting prosecutor general resigned in protest; and on 2 October 2026 the Prosecutors' Office — the institution as constituted since 1948 — was abolished and the new agencies launched. Prosecution reform of some kind was a decades-old DP platform and a stated Lee campaign pledge, and the Yoon presidency itself was the reformers' central exhibit of prosecutorial abuse; both are on the record. So is the sequence: each step was taken by a legislature his party controls, over the opposition's boycott or filibuster, while the beneficiary-in-fact of the institution's weakening was the sitting president.
  • Independence inside the prosecution was punished in personnel. In a reshuffle effective 15 December 2025, three district chief prosecutors who had publicly objected to the Daejang-dong appeal withdrawal were reassigned to research posts at the Judicial Research and Training Institute, and a fourth was effectively demoted to a line position; one filed suit on 12 December 2025 to nullify the order, calling it disguised discipline. The Justice Ministry said the moves protected the prosecution's political neutrality (Korea JoongAng Daily, 14 December 2025).
  • Ministerial control over prosecutors was expanded by statute in parallel: a June 2025 revision let the justice minister request discipline of the prosecutor general, and on 17 September 2026 an amendment adding outright dismissal to the penalties available against prosecutors passed the Assembly (National Assembly records).
  • The judiciary's structure was rewritten in ways whose benefits accrue to the presidency. The "three judicial reform laws," passed by the DP-led Assembly during 2026 and allowed by Lee to take effect, expand the Supreme Court from 14 to 26 justices (four additional appointments a year from 2028 — meaning Lee will name the large majority of the enlarged court across his term, between new seats and ordinary succession), create a crime of "distorting the law" applicable to judges and prosecutors, and allow constitutional complaints against final court judgments, adding a fourth tier above the Supreme Court. The government's stated rationale — breaking factional "clans" in the judiciary and correcting aberrant rulings — is on the record. So is the judiciary's response: in late October 2026 judges posted dozens of internal protests against the legal-distortion crime, and on 23 November 2026 the chair of the National Conference of Judges publicly protested the DP's move — adopted as party line on 20 November 2026, the day after the Han Duck-soo remand — to impeach Chief Justice Cho Hee-dae, calling the use of a ruling's content as impeachment grounds a violation of judicial independence. The PPP calls the impeachment a "shield" to keep Lee's trials suspended by suspending the chief justice; the DP says it targets procedural illegality. The impeachment drive is the party's; the president has not publicly opposed it (Seoul National University professor Kang Won-taek's critique, 2026; Newsworks, 23 November 2026; Aju Business Daily, 17 November 2026).
  • The media's legal environment was tightened with his signature. The DP's amendment to the Information and Communications Network Act — punitive damages of up to five times proven losses against outlets and large online channels that circulate "false or manipulated information," plus fines of up to one billion won for repeat distribution after a court has ruled content false — passed in December 2025 over an opposition walkout. The Journalists Association of Korea and the Seoul Foreign Correspondents' Club warned of a chilling effect and urged a veto; Lee signed it; it took effect on 7 July 2026, and the PPP has filed a constitutional complaint. The law's target — the election-fraud disinformation ecosystem that mobilized around Yoon — is documented; so is the vagueness objection, which the government answers by pointing to court adjudication as the trigger (Associated Press, December 2025 and July 2026).
  • The military is the exception that proves the pattern's limit: there is no documented move by Lee to purge or politicize the armed forces' command; the martial-law commanders were processed through courts and the special counsel, and his government has pursued reorganization of the military intelligence agencies implicated in December 2024 (government policy records, 2025–2026). This item sits on the for-shelf in substance and is recorded here so its absence from the against-shelf is a finding, not an oversight.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, markets, the United States, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The tariff negotiation is the clearest documented case of adjustment without capitulation. Facing a threatened 25% U.S. tariff and an initial American demand for the full $350 billion investment fund upfront — a sum equal to roughly 85% of Korea's foreign reserves — Lee's government agreed the framework in July 2025 but refused the upfront structure, negotiated for three months, and closed on 29 October 2025 at the Gyeongju APEC summit on terms that capped cash installments at $20 billion a year, split the package into $200 billion cash and $150 billion in shipbuilding cooperation, and added a clause allowing adjustment if foreign-exchange instability threatened. The 15% rate matched Japan's (Korea Economic Institute and Korea JoongAng Daily, October 2025).
  • The Georgia raid: after U.S. immigration agents detained 475 workers, over 300 of them Korean, at a Hyundai–LG battery plant on 4 September 2025, Lee ordered all-out efforts, negotiated "voluntary departures" rather than deportations (preserving the workers' ability to return), sent a charter plane that brought 316 Koreans home on 11 September, and then — at his 100-day press conference the same day — publicly pressed Washington on visa reform, saying Korean firms would hesitate to invest until it came. Pushback absorbed, nationals protected, grievance stated to the ally's face (Associated Press, September 2025).
  • The data-center fire: when a battery fire at the National Information Resources Service in Daejeon on 26 September 2025 forced the shutdown of 647 government systems, Lee apologized to the public within two days, said he was "deeply sorry," expressed surprise that backups had not improved since the 2023 outages, and ordered systemic fixes and emergency budgets (Reuters and Yonhap reporting, 28–29 September 2025).
  • Failed nominations were corrected, if not always promptly: Lee himself withdrew education-minister nominee Lee Jin-sook on 20 July 2025 over plagiarism and unlawful-overseas-study allegations; gender-minister nominee Kang Sun-woo, initially defended by the presidential office against aide-abuse allegations, withdrew three days later after criticism mounted (Korea JoongAng Daily, July 2025).
  • The doctors' standoff he inherited was ended by concession and negotiation rather than force: trainee doctors returned from 1 September 2025 without penalties after an eighteen-month walkout, the crisis alert was lowered in October 2025, and his own replacement plan (10 February 2026) is phased and smaller — 3,342 additional medical-school seats over five years, tied to regional service — where Yoon's had been a single imposed doubling (Korea JoongAng Daily, September 2025–February 2026).
  • Electoral pushback was acknowledged in the open: after the June 2026 local elections cost his party Seoul and four by-election seats and his approval fell some nine points (57% in the National Barometer Survey; 50.4% in a Korea Society Opinion Institute poll), Lee personally shared the unfavorable KSOI poll on his own X account (Korea JoongAng Daily, 12 June 2026). When his policy chief floated a semiconductor "national dividend" in May 2026, Lee publicly narrowed it within days — only excess tax revenue was under consideration, no new tax (The Economist, May 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the Daejang-dong appeal withdrawal (Test 1), the government's answer to documented internal protest was denial plus personnel action: the minister denied directing anything, no independent inquiry was ordered, the acting prosecutor general resigned, and the prosecutors who had objected were reassigned within weeks. Whatever the truth of the pressure ALLEGATION, the wobble — a prosecution chief's public account of ministerial options — was answered by closing ranks, not by opening the file.
  • On the fabricated-indictment special counsel, the opposition's demand was specific and institutional — veto a law that makes you the appointer of your own cases' investigator — and Lee promulgated the law from an overseas trip without a public explanation of why the conflict-of-interest objection failed. The Seoul Economic Daily's editorial response was titled "Lee Must Answer for Special Counsel Law, Not Just the Premier" (18 November 2026).
  • Housing shows a doubling-down pattern before it shows adjustment: three packages in under five months — loan caps on 27 June 2025, a supply plan on 7 September, then on 15 October 2025 the designation of all 25 Seoul districts and 12 Gyeonggi areas as regulation and land-transaction-permit zones, the strictest tools in Korean housing law, imposed over the stated objection of Seoul's mayor, who said the city was not meaningfully consulted. Prices in the capital region had kept rising through each prior package; real estate became his lowest-rated policy area, its positive assessment falling 17 points to 40% by June 2026, and the party lost Seoul partly on it (Korea JoongAng Daily, October 2025 and June 2026). Each package responded to data; each also escalated the same demand-suppression instrument rather than revisiting the approach.
  • The Kang Sun-woo sequence (above) cuts both ways and is recorded on both shelves deliberately: the correction came, but only after the presidential office first signaled it would appoint her anyway.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — workers, the poor, young people, farmers, his opponents' voters, and the people his North Korea policy touches — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The first major economic act was a universal payment with progressive weighting. Under the supplementary budget passed 4 July 2025, every citizen resident in Korea received a 150,000-won "consumption coupon" from 21 July 2025; basic-livelihood recipients received 400,000 won, near-poor and single-parent households 300,000 won, with extra amounts for non-capital and depopulating rural areas; a second round paid 100,000 won to the bottom 90% of earners in September–October 2025. Permanent residents, marriage immigrants, and recognized refugees were included; other foreign residents were not (Ministry of the Interior and Safety announcements, July 2025).
  • Workers previously outside the count were brought in by statute: the Yellow Envelope Act — broadening the definition of "employer" to firms that substantially control working conditions, extending bargaining rights to subcontract and platform workers, and limiting damages claims against strikers — passed on 24 August 2025 after two vetoes under Yoon, was signed by Lee, and took effect on 10 March 2026, over documented business-community opposition (Baker McKenzie and Korea Economic Daily reporting, August 2025).
  • Children and depopulating regions: the Child Allowance Act amendment passed 1 March 2026 raises eligibility by one year annually to under-13 by 2030, with monthly top-ups for non-capital and population-decline areas; the first expansion (to eight-year-olds) paid out in April 2026 to some 2.55 million children (Korea JoongAng Daily, April 2026). His signature rural basic income — 150,000 won a month in local gift certificates to every resident of population-decline counties, no means test — launched as a pilot in ten counties in early 2026, expanded to seventeen by mid-2026, and Lee inspected it unannounced on 31 October 2026 and pledged wider rollout (Basic Income Earth Network and Seoul Economic Daily, 2026).
  • Young people were given a named, enlarged claim on the budget: at a "Youth Budget Unboxing" event on 28 February 2026 the government presented a 2027 youth package raising youth-related spending by 53.5% to 43.3 trillion won, widening rent-support eligibility to median income and extending it for near-poor youth (Seoul Economic Daily, August 2026 budget reporting).
  • On North Korea, one inclusion held: his government co-sponsored the UN General Assembly's North Korea human rights resolution in December 2025, as its predecessor had (Human Rights Foundation letter, 25 March 2026).

Evidence against:

  • North Koreans, defectors, and abductee families are the named population counted out, as a matter of documented policy. During 2025 the Unification Ministry suspended publication of its annual North Korea human rights report, disbanded its North Korean human rights and humanitarian office and its abductees response team, and dismantled a division supporting escapees' employment and entrepreneurship; activists cite ministry funding for the field cut from 12.4 billion won to 378 million won. The government ended all state radio and television broadcasts into the North in July 2025 (the loudspeaker halt is at Test 5), and leaflet senders have been investigated under an enforcement posture defectors' organizations describe as a crackdown. Lee campaigned on improving North Korean human rights; defectors and human rights activists state on the record that the promise has been subordinated to engagement (NK News, 31 December 2025; Human Rights Foundation joint letter, 25 March 2026; testimony before the U.S. Tom Lantos Human Rights Commission, 28 April 2026 — the last two are advocacy sources and are weighted as such; the ministry's institutional changes are not disputed).
  • His opponents' voters are counted at the ballot box but governed past between elections: the defining statutes of his term — prosecution abolition, the media law, the judicial reforms, the special counsel — were each passed by his party's majority over PPP boycotts, walkouts, or terminated filibusters, with no negotiated text. The June 2026 electorate's partial correction (Seoul, the by-elections) is the documented public response.
  • In October 2026 Lee himself opened a question over a small group's place in the count: on 28 October he publicly shared material criticizing preferential treatment for multicultural families at public postpartum care centers and ordered the program reviewed, then defended the move for three consecutive days against progressive critics who said it stoked hostility toward immigrants, arguing that sensitive issues require open debate. CONTESTED: the review's merits are disputed; that the president personally singled out a minority benefit for re-examination is documented (Seoul Economic Daily, 1 November 2026).
  • The rural basic income's expansion carries a documented substitution cost: the National Assembly Budget Office warned in October 2026 that counties funding their share have in places cut existing elderly and youth welfare lines to pay for it — in one documented county, elderly care, bathing support for vulnerable residents, youth vouchers, and hygiene support for young women were reduced to fund the universal payment (Aju Business Daily, 16 November 2026). The universal count, in those places, was partly paid for out of the targeted one.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since June 2025, with dates. Lee's word has a longer paper trail than most: he has been a basic-income politician for a decade — a Youth Dividend as Seongnam's mayor, a youth basic income and a universal COVID-era disaster payment as Gyeonggi's governor (Basic Income Earth Network, July 2025) — so his 2025 manifesto, built on a "Basic Society," came from a man whose signature policy was already tested at provincial scale.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or delivered in law):

  • KOSPI 5,000. His most specific campaign pledge — the stock index at 5,000, from roughly 2,500 when he made it — was met on 22 January 2026, when the KOSPI crossed 5,000 intraday (5,019.54) less than eight months into his term, aided by Commercial Act revisions and dividend-tax changes his government passed. The index later rose to record levels above 9,000 in May 2026 — and fell back below 5,000 in November 2026 amid a global AI-stock sell-off and energy shock (CNBC-TV18, 6 November 2026). The pledge was a level, and the level was reached and held for most of a year; that it did not hold permanently is also on the record, and the opposition's charge that his government fed the bubble is CONTESTED.
  • Prosecution reform. Promised for years, delivered in full statutory form: separation of investigation and indictment, abolition of the Prosecutors' Office, new agencies launched 2 October 2026. Whatever its grade under Tests 1 and 2, as a kept promise it is not in doubt.
  • The Yellow Envelope Act. A core labor pledge, twice vetoed under his predecessor; passed August 2025, in force March 2026.
  • North Korea tension-reduction steps. He promised in the campaign to halt the border loudspeaker broadcasts; the military halted them on 11 June 2025, a week after inauguration, and dismantled the fixed loudspeakers in August 2025. His Liberation Day address (15 August 2025) pledged respect for the North's system, no unification by absorption, and gradual restoration of the 2018 inter-Korean military agreement; at the UN General Assembly in September 2025 he proposed the "E.N.D." initiative — Exchange, Normalization, Denuclearization — with a phased freeze-reduce-dismantle path. Pyongyang reciprocated the broadcast halt at the time. The promised dialogue has not occurred: Kim Yo-jong has repeatedly rejected talks, the North has entrenched its "two hostile states" line, and in October 2026 Pyongyang announced it would resume its own loudspeaker broadcasts, with relations at their lowest point of his tenure. The steps he promised were taken; the counterpart he promised to seek has refused — both halves are the record (Japan Diplomatic Bluebook 2026 summary; Al Jazeera, 22 October 2026).
  • Process promises kept in form: the return of the presidential office to the Blue House (completed December 2025); the medical-standoff settlement by negotiation (September 2025); child-allowance expansion begun on the legislated schedule (2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, narrowed, or contradicted):

  • Basic income. The universal basic income of his earlier campaigns was already softened in the 2025 manifesto to a "Basic Society" of targeted guarantees, and in office it has materialized as a county-level pilot — seventeen of 69 depopulating counties as of late 2026, paid in restricted local vouchers rather than cash, with expansion to roughly 35 counties planned before any national decision. A supporter can call this the pledge proceeding by stages; the pledge as originally made to the whole country has not been delivered, and no timetable for it exists (Basic Income Earth Network pilot tracking, 2026).
  • Housing stability. He promised supply expansion and an end to housing anxiety; through his first seventeen months capital-region prices rose through three regulatory packages, real estate became his weakest policy rating, and his party lost Seoul in June 2026 substantially on it. The promise is unkept as of this grading, on his own government's price data and his party's electoral result.
  • North Korean human rights. As a candidate he vowed to seek improvements in North Korean human rights; the institutional record of his first year runs the other way (Test 4). His government did co-sponsor the December 2025 UN resolution; the domestic apparatus for the issue was dismantled in the same year. Activists across the defector community state the promise was abandoned; the government states engagement is the route to improvement. CONTESTED in interpretation, documented in fact.
  • "No political revenge" and national integration. Lee pledged repeatedly — in the campaign and in office, including a New Year 2026 address naming national unity the most urgent task — that there would be no political retaliation and that he would be a president for all. The record since includes three special counsels directed at the former ruling house (each grounded in documented events — a martial-law declaration is not a pretext), a fourth aimed at the prosecutors who indicted him, pardons whose headline beneficiaries were his allies, and statutes passed over total opposition boycott. His supporters answer that accountability for an insurrection is not revenge and that the courts, not the president, convicted Yoon; his opponents answer that the pattern is indistinguishable from revenge with better paperwork. The dossier records the pledge and the pattern and labels the characterization CONTESTED; it notes only that a president who promised integration has, seventeen months in, no major statute enacted with opposition support.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Lee Jae-myung's file is the mirror image of most in this series: a ruler who demonstrably answers pressure — apologizing for a data-center fire in two days, withdrawing a nominee, negotiating a tariff structure down from an economy-breaking demand, ending a doctors' war by concession, posting his own bad poll — and who keeps a striking number of his written promises, including the most checkable one (KOSPI 5,000, January 2026) and the most structural one (prosecution reform, October 2026). The same record shows why those two facts do not settle the Test. The prosecution he dismantled was the prosecution that had indicted him five times; the appeal his ministry was accused of quietly killing belonged to the companion case of his own; the special counsel he promulgated in November 2026 investigates his own indictments at his own appointment; and the justice minister he has just nominated will not say he would refuse an order to withdraw the president's charges. No document shows Lee ordering any of it — his ministers deny direction, the courts suspended his trials on their own constitutional reading, and the same courts acquitted his opponents' figures and cut down his special counsel's biggest conviction on appeal. The grip, where it exists, is structural and prospective: an abolished prosecution, an enlarged Supreme Court he will mostly appoint, a crime of "distorting the law" hanging over judges, and a chief justice facing impeachment by his party. Domestically he counts broadly — universal coupons, subcontract workers, children, depopulating counties — and the exception is equally deliberate: North Koreans and the defectors among his own citizens have been moved out of the count to buy an engagement Pyongyang has so far refused. The Sun holds because it gives by one law to everything in its reach. On the documented record of seventeen months, this seat gives by one law to the previous ruler's house — Yoon is in prison for life on a court's verdict — and by another, quieter set of arrangements to its own.
