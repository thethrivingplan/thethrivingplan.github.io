The Ruler's Test — Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa, King of Bahrain

Subject: Hamad bin Isa Al Khalifa — ruler of Bahrain since 6 March 1999, as Emir until 14 February 2002 and as King since. The seat graded is the kingship, and the reason is stated plainly: executive power in Bahrain is vested in the King. He appoints and dismisses the prime minister and the cabinet, commands the armed forces, chairs the Higher Judicial Council, appoints all judges, appoints all 40 members of the Shura Council (the upper house), may dissolve the elected 40-member Council of Representatives, may extend its term, and must ratify legislation for it to become law (Constitution of 2002, as amended 2012 and 2017; Congressional Research Service, Bahrain: Issues for U.S. Policy, updated 15 December 2025 and 23 September 2026). The Prime Minister's role is stated plainly: Crown Prince Salman bin Hamad Al Khalifa, the King's eldest son and heir, has been Prime Minister since 11 November 2020, succeeding the King's uncle Khalifa bin Salman Al Khalifa, the world's longest-serving prime minister, who died in office that day. Salman chairs the weekly Cabinet, issues edicts appointing officials, and runs the day-to-day government and the economic programme — as the King's appointee, serving under him, not as an independently elected executive (Crown Prince's official site, Cabinet reports 2025–2026; CRS, 2025–2026). During the first two decades of the reign the late Khalifa bin Salman exercised wide executive authority, particularly over internal security, and CRS records that he frustrated Hamad's early liberalisation; since 2020 executive authority has been consolidated in the King and his direct descendants, with another son, Nasser bin Hamad Al Khalifa, as national security adviser and Royal Guard commander (CRS, 2025). Hamad, born 28 January 1950, educated at Sandhurst, formerly commander of the Bahrain Defence Force, is graded on the full tenure of more than 27 years — a completed arc long enough to grade in full, from the reform opening of 1999–2001, through the 2011 uprising and its aftermath, to the pardons of 2024–2025 and the crackdown and election postponement of 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Each test closes with the grade's reason in plain words and a ledger naming who his actions on that test helped and who they hurt. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is never itself a failure — and this seat was never elected at all, which is recorded as a fact about its mandate, not as a grade in itself: the Test grades the holding. Marks are lost only for how power is held — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), refusing to consider another option when one becomes available (Test 3), counting out part of the population (Test 4). Word-keeping and outcomes are graded as separate axes and printed independently; neither launders the other. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier says so and grades the best possible choice available in the moment; that is not held against him. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The opening of the reign applied release and restoration broadly, not only to loyalists. On succeeding in 1999 Hamad released political prisoners, abolished the 1974 State Security Measures decree and dissolved the State Security Court, allowed exiles to return, and scrapped the security law under which the 1990s opposition had been prosecuted (National Action Charter record; openDemocracy and Ulrichsen, Stability, 2013). The beneficiaries were, by definition, his father's opponents.
  • The accountability machinery created after 2011 exists and has processed cases against security personnel: an Interior Ministry Ombudsman and a Special Investigations Unit were established in implementation of the Bahrain Independent Commission of Inquiry (BICI) recommendations, and the U.S. State Department's human-rights reporting records that the government prosecuted low-level security-force members for abuses following investigations by those bodies (U.S. Department of State, Country Reports on Human Rights Practices: Bahrain). No senior commander is recorded among them — the limit is on the against-shelf.
  • The royal pardons of 2024–2025 were large and reached the opposition's rank and file, not only common criminals: the decree of 8 April 2024 pardoned 1,584 prisoners convicted in criminal and riot cases, the largest pardon since 2011, with the government stating 65% of those convicted in riot cases were covered (Bahrain statement to OHCHR, 2024; International IDEA, April 2024); Human Rights Watch records the 2024 pardons together freed over 2,500 prisoners, including more than 800 held for political participation and peaceful expression, and a further amnesty of 630 inmates followed on 27 March 2025 (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026: Bahrain).

Evidence against:

  • The political law itself is two laws. The "political isolation" laws introduced in 2018 bar former members of the dissolved opposition societies from standing for parliament and from sitting on the boards of civil-society organisations — a disability that attaches to one side of the political map only, the side that was dissolved (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026). No equivalent disability attaches to members of pro-government societies.
  • Citizenship is granted and taken away on different rules for different Bahrainis. The documented record shows mass revocations aimed at critics and, in 2026, at one community: Amnesty International recorded at least 316 revocations since 2012 by 2016, usually rendering people stateless; the Bahrain Institute for Rights and Democracy (BIRD) recorded at least 738 revocations since 2012 by 2018 (Amnesty International submission to the UN Human Rights Council, September 2016; BIRD via UN Watch compilation, 2018). In April 2026 the authorities revoked the citizenship of a further group — printed as separate sourced figures, never blended: 69 Bahrainis including 33 children, the youngest 19 days old, per the joint statement of 32 human-rights organisations (October 2026) and a UK Parliament Early Day Motion (2026); 67 people, the majority of them mostly children rendered stateless, per Human Rights Watch's country page (2026). The government maintains revocations rest on criminal and security grounds; the rights organisations dispute both the grounds and the absence of an ordinary judicial appeal against the revocation itself (Bahrain Forum for Human Rights submission to the Human Rights Committee, August 2026).
  • The largest political society in the country was dissolved while its counterparts were not. Al-Wefaq, the Shia opposition society that had won the largest bloc in the elected chamber, was suspended by a court on the Justice Ministry's motion in June 2016, its headquarters seized and assets frozen, and formally dissolved in July 2016 for allegedly encouraging terrorism, extremism and foreign interference; Amnesty International stated the authorities presented no credible evidence that Al-Wefaq was anything but a peaceful opposition movement — the government's allegation and the monitor's rebuttal are both recorded, and the dissolution itself is not in dispute (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2017; Amnesty International, September 2016). The secular Wa'ad society was dissolved in May 2017. Al-Wefaq's secretary general Ali Salman had his sentence increased on appeal from four to nine years in May 2016 and remains imprisoned (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026).
  • The ruling family stands inside the law's protection in a way no opponent does. Twelve of 24 cabinet ministers were members of the Al Khalifa family in the State Department's count, and the family holds the interior, justice, finance and foreign ministries and the senior Defence Force posts (U.S. Department of State; CRS, 2025). No Al Khalifa office-holder is documented as prosecuted for the abuses the BICI found in 2011 (see Test 3), while the BICI itself documented 559 torture complaints and at least 35 deaths in the unrest period (BICI report, 23 November 2011, via CJPME factsheet 161, December 2012).

Why: The early releases and the 2024–2025 pardons were real and reached opponents — but they are acts of grace from the same hand that holds the second law in reserve: isolation laws for one side only, citizenship stripped in the hundreds and, in 2026, from infants, the largest opposition society dissolved on disputed evidence, and no senior insider ever on the receiving end. A pardon presupposes the unequal law it relieves; it does not repeal it. Helped: The more than 2,500 prisoners freed in 2024 and the 630 freed in March 2025, and their families; the exiles and prisoners of the 1999–2001 opening; low-level accountability's complainants, whose cases the Ombudsman and Special Investigations Unit did process. Hurt: Former opposition members barred from public life by the 2018 isolation laws; the hundreds stripped of citizenship since 2012 on the monitors' separate counts, and the April 2026 group — including 33 children on the joint-statement count — left mostly stateless; Al-Wefaq's and Wa'ad's members and voters, whose societies were dissolved; Ali Salman, still imprisoned a decade after his sentence was increased on appeal.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The elected chamber is not a pure decoration, and the King has on one documented occasion enlarged its formal powers: the 2012 constitutional amendments, made after the 2011 dialogue process, designated the Council of Representatives the presiding chamber and gave it power to remove individual ministers by a two-thirds vote, and a royal veto can in law be overridden by two-thirds of both chambers (CRS, 2025).
  • Elections for the lower house have been held on a regular four-year cycle from 2002 through 2022, run under a supreme committee chaired by the Minister of Justice with supervisory committees chaired by judges in each governorate, and official turnout in 2018 was given as 67% (U.S. Department of State; electoral administration record).
  • The BICI episode belongs partly on this shelf too: in June 2011 the King appointed five international jurists under Professor Mahmoud Cherif Bassiouni with a public mandate to investigate his own government's conduct, and published their damning report unedited in November 2011 (Amnesty International briefing, April 2012; BICI report, 2011). A ruler who grips every referee does not usually commission that report. What was done with it is graded in Tests 3 and 5.

Evidence against:

  • The referees are, by constitutional design, the King's appointees. He appoints all 40 Shura Council members — a chamber with legislative power equal to the elected chamber's — all judges, the prime minister and every minister, and the four governorates' governors; he chairs the Higher Judicial Council; he may dissolve the elected chamber and may amend the constitution (CRS, 2025; Constitution of 2002). The U.S. State Department records that, although the constitution provides for an independent judiciary, opposition figures reported the judiciary remained vulnerable to political pressure, especially in high-profile cases (U.S. Department of State, Country Reports: Bahrain).
  • In April 2026 the grip was used on the electoral referee itself: by Royal Order 13 of 29 April 2026, citing "current circumstances," the King extended the National Assembly's term by one year, to December 2027, postponing the parliamentary election due in November 2026 beyond the standard four-year cycle — a power the constitution grants him, exercised to defer the one vote Bahrainis cast (International IDEA, April 2026; IFES ElectionGuide via GeckoKnows, checked 22 November 2026). In May 2026 the Crown Prince extended the municipal councils' terms in parallel.
  • The media referee was closed, then re-legislated. Bahrain's only non-state-owned newspaper, Al-Wasat, was closed by the authorities in 2017; the government owns all national broadcast media and the main nominally private newspapers are run by individuals close to the government, so no independent media outlet can operate in Bahrain (Human Rights Watch joint statement, World Press Freedom Day, 3 May 2026). On 30 October 2025 the King issued the Press and Electronic Media Law No. 41 of 2025, which the same four-organisation statement assesses as expanding prior censorship and punitive administrative measures to digital media while preserving criminal prosecution of journalists through the Penal Code and Counter-Terrorism Law via a "stricter penalty in other laws" clause. Reporters Without Borders ranked Bahrain 157th of 180 in its 2025 World Press Freedom Index; Freedom House scores Bahrain 12/100, "Not Free," in Freedom in the World 2025, and 28/100, "Not Free," for internet freedom (RSF, 2025; Bahrain Center for Human Rights, August 2025; Freedom House, Freedom on the Net 2025).
  • The courts' handling of the largest case of 2026 is documented as raising independence concerns on the record: in the mass trial of 69 defendants — 60 Shia clerics and nine affiliates — the verdict hearing was brought forward from 22 November to 16 November 2026 on the Public Prosecution's request, with defence lawyers notified at around 5 p.m. the previous day, after earlier rescheduling from 8 November; all 69 were convicted, 64 sentenced to life imprisonment and five to 15 years, none acquitted (BIRD, 16 November 2026). The defendants faced terrorism charges including establishing a group linked to Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps to overthrow the constitutional order — the prosecution's case, recorded as ALLEGATION against each defendant except where a court has now ruled; BIRD and a 10 November 2026 joint statement of 20 organisations record credible ALLEGATIONS of torture, coerced confessions and severe restrictions on the defence, which the government disputes, maintaining the defendants were afforded their legal rights.

Why: One commissioned inquiry and a chamber that can, on paper, remove a minister do not offset a structure in which the King appoints the upper house, the judges, the government and the governors, closed the last independent newspaper, signed a 2025 press law that four rights organisations document as widening state control, and in 2026 postponed the election itself. The referees do not merely lean; they are staffed, and when the calendar itself became the referee, it was extended. Helped: The ruling family and government, whose legislative programme faces an appointed co-chamber and a deferrable election; state and state-aligned media, operating without an independent competitor since 2017. Hurt: Voters, whose November 2026 election was postponed a year by royal order; judges and prosecutors, whose appointments and council run through the palace; journalists and online users — the Bahrain Press Association documented 37 violations against writers and online users in the first half of 2025 alone; the 69 defendants of November 2026, whose verdict was delivered early on the prosecution's request, whatever the merits of the charges against each of them.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? And when another option became available, did he at least consider it openly?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The reign began as an answer to a wobble. The 1990s uprising was met not with a renewed security law but with the National Action Charter, a referendum on 14 February 2001 that approved it by 98.4%, the release of prisoners and the return of an elected assembly in 2002 after a 27-year suspension (referendum record; Constitution of Bahrain record). The adjustment was open, named, and voted on.
  • In 2011, after the crackdown (against-shelf), the King took the most open step available to a ruler in his position: he created the BICI, gave it international membership and public terms, received its report on camera on 23 November 2011, and accepted findings that his own security forces had used excessive force, practised widespread torture and carried out unlawful killings, and that there was no evidence Iran had instigated the uprising — contradicting his government's central claim (BICI report, 2011; Amnesty International briefing, April 2012; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2012). Considering the other option — an independent inquiry over a denial — is exactly what this test asks, and he did it in public.
  • The pardon waves of 2024–2025 and December 2025 were adjustments to documented pressure: prison unrest inside Jau Prison, sustained international criticism, and — on CRS's assessment — an effort to deflect global criticism of Bahrain's human-rights record (CRS, September 2026; Human Rights Watch country page, 2026, recording a December 2025 amnesty for 963 prisoners). The releases were real, repeated, and publicly decreed.
  • Parliamentary pushback on foreign policy was answered with explanation rather than punishment of the chamber: when parliament passed a resolution in November 2023 declaring a suspension of ties with Israel during the Gaza war, the executive did not dissolve the chamber or reverse the Abraham Accords; it stated that foreign policy is an executive matter and the accords stood (Bahrain–Israel relations record, 2023). Explanation is less than adjustment; it is recorded here as more than denial.

Evidence against:

  • The defining wobble of the reign was answered first with force, and the force is the documented core of the file. After tens of thousands gathered at Manama's Pearl Roundabout from 14 February 2011 demanding democratic reform and an end to discrimination, security forces killed seven protesters in the first week; on 15 March 2011 the King declared a three-month State of National Safety (martial law), Saudi and Emirati forces entered at the government's request — at least 1,200 Saudi troops on Amnesty's count, about 2,000 Saudi and Emirati troops combined on another contemporaneous count, printed separately and never blended — and on 16 March the Roundabout was cleared by force and the Pearl Monument demolished (Amnesty International briefing, April 2012; CJPME factsheet 161, 2012). Freedom House records more than 50 people killed over the course of 2011 and thousands wounded; several thousand workers were dismissed for supporting the protests (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2012). The protesters' central demands — an elected government and a constitutional monarchy in substance — were not adjusted toward then or since (CRS, 2018–2026).
  • The BICI's recommendations were accepted in words and, on the monitors' count, largely not carried out: the U.S. State Department records continuing concern that the government had not fully implemented them, including dropping charges for nonviolent expression, criminally charging officers accused of torture, integrating Shia citizens into the security forces, and creating conditions for national reconciliation (U.S. Department of State, Country Reports: Bahrain). Accepting a report and declining its remedies is consideration without adjustment, and the shelf records both halves.
  • When another option became available again in 2024–2026 — the pardons showed a reconciliation door existed and was usable — it was used for the rank and file and refused for the names that would have made it a settlement: Abdulhadi Al-Khawaja, Hassan Mushaima, Dr Abduljalil Al-Singace and Ali Salman remained imprisoned through every pardon wave, and in November 2025 Ebrahim Sharif, the former Wa'ad leader pardoned in 2015, was detained for the tenth time since 2011 (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026). In 2026 the answer to the street and the pulpit was the largest escalation since 2011 (Tests 1, 2 and 4).
  • The 2026 election postponement is a wobble answered by moving the test: faced with a November 2026 vote amid a regional war and domestic arrests, the term was extended a year by royal order rather than the vote being held or the postponement put to any public reckoning beyond "current circumstances" (International IDEA, April 2026).

Why: Twice — 2001 and in commissioning the BICI in 2011 — he considered another option openly and took part of it, and the pardons are genuine, repeated adjustments under pressure. But the street's central demand has been refused for 27 years, the BICI's remedies were mostly not implemented, the reconciliation door was opened for the many and kept shut for the leaders, and the 2026 answer was escalation plus a postponed election. Tactical openness over structural refusal — the same shape as the for-shelf, one size larger. Helped: The prisoners freed in 2001 and in 2024–2025 and their families; Bahrain's international standing, which the BICI's creation and the pardons repeatedly repaired; the cause of a documented record itself — the BICI report remains the authoritative account of 2011 because the King commissioned it. Hurt: The 2011 protesters — more than 50 killed on Freedom House's count, thousands wounded, several thousand dismissed from their jobs; the reconciliation the BICI prescribed, deferred for fifteen years; voters in 2026, whose reckoning was postponed; the four named long-term prisoners and Ebrahim Sharif, for whom the open door was not used.

The no-good-choice clause, applied by its mechanics: Bahrain in 2026 presented a genuine security trap, and it is recorded as one. Bahrain hosts the U.S. Navy's Fifth Fleet headquarters and was, on the documented record, exposed to retaliatory Iranian strikes during the Iran–U.S.–Israel war that broke out in early 2026 (rights-organisation joint statement, October 2026; HRW country page, 2026). The options open to any Bahraini government in March 2026 — tolerate expression of solidarity with an adversary actively striking the region, or police wartime dissent — each carried real harm, and ordinary wartime security measures are not held against the King here. What remains gradable under the clause is the least-harm door: revoking the citizenship of a 19-day-old infant, mass life sentences after a verdict hearing moved at the prosecution's request, and postponing the election were not the least harmful options available, and the trap's origin is itself partly of the seat's making — a majority community excluded from the security forces and from an elected government for decades is the grievance the wartime measures then policed. The clause covers the dilemma; it does not cover the choices taken inside it.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Shia majority, the protesters, women, religious minorities, migrant workers, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Women were counted in at the reign's start and the machinery still runs: Bahraini women received the vote and the right to stand in the 2002 elections — the National Action Charter granted women full political rights — and the Supreme Council for Women, established in 2001 and led by the King's consort, Princess Sabeeka bint Ibrahim Al Khalifa, runs a National Plan for the Advancement of Bahraini Women incorporated into government programmes since 2015, with equal-opportunity committees across public and private sectors and gender-responsive budgeting (CEDAW Committee summary record, 14 February 2023; Bahrain government portal). Labour law amendments prohibit sexual harassment and gender and wage discrimination (CEDAW record, 2023).
  • Small religious minorities are visibly counted in the state's public life: the Shura Council in practice includes members of minority communities (electoral-system record, 2026), Bahrain hosted Pope Francis in November 2022 for a forum on coexistence — cited by Bahrain's own delegation at the UN Human Rights Council — and the government points to churches, a synagogue and temples operating openly (State Department and UN records). Migrant-worker protections were found by Human Rights Watch in 2011 to have improved, and Bahrain's sponsorship reforms were regionally early (Human Rights Watch, via human-rights record compilations).
  • The material count reaches broadly: Bahrain is a high-income state whose rulers built a financial-services and tourism economy with per-capita income at Western European levels, and the Cabinet under the Crown Prince in December 2025–January 2026 held electricity and water tariffs for citizens' primary residences, increased cost-of-living support for low-income families, and directed three job opportunities for every registered jobseeker (CRS, 2025; Cabinet reports, 29 December 2025 and 5 January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The majority community is counted out of the state's coercive and senior arms, on the U.S. government's own reporting: Shia citizens — the majority in the only GCC state with a Shia-majority population — faced widespread employment discrimination in sensitive government positions, notably the managerial ranks of the civil service and the security services, police and military, and integrating Shia into the security forces remains an unimplemented BICI recommendation (CRS, 2025; U.S. Department of State, Country Reports: Bahrain). Electoral districts have been persistently alleged to be gerrymandered to dilute Shia voting strength — an ALLEGATION carried in U.S. Senate and State Department records since 2008 and disputed by the government — and the 2018 isolation laws then barred the dissolved opposition's former members from standing at all (Tests 1–2).
  • The 2026 file is a counting-out file at scale. Since the outbreak of the Iran–U.S.–Israel war, more than 700 people have been arrested, including children, on the count of the 32-organisation joint statement (October 2026); the Bahrain Forum for Human Rights documented, as separate figures, 825 arrests it considers arbitrary and 374 summonses between 1 January and 22 August 2026, including 45 children, 61 Shia clerics and 48 religious reciters or organisers (BFHR submission to the Human Rights Committee, August 2026). In March 2026 a 32-year-old man, named in the rights record as Sayed Mohamed Al-Mousawi, died in custody after being forcibly disappeared for over a week, his body bearing signs of torture — documented by BIRD and Human Rights Watch; the circumstances are ALLEGATION as to individual perpetrators, the death in custody is documented. In April 2026 citizenship was revoked from the group counted separately in Test 1 — 69 including 33 children on one count, 67 mostly children on another. On 9 May 2026 at least 37 Shia clerics were arrested in night raids, expanding to the 69-defendant mass trial that ended on 16 November 2026 with 64 life sentences (BIRD, November 2026; joint statement, October 2026). During Ashura in July 2025, before the war, authorities had already targeted 60 citizens in arrests and summonses marked, on Human Rights Watch's reporting, by unjustified violence (HRW, World Report 2026).
  • Those already counted out stayed out through the mercies: the pardons of 2024–2025 freed hundreds of political prisoners and excluded, every time, the community's and the movement's senior figures — Al-Khawaja, Mushaima, Al-Singace, Salman (Test 3). Death row holds a further counted-out group whose size is printed as separate sourced figures, never blended: 12 inmates at imminent risk of execution per Human Rights Watch (2026); 19 individuals facing the death penalty, 18 with remedies exhausted, per the October 2026 joint letter; 26 men on death row per Human Rights Watch and BIRD's 2022 court-records report, which found the eight cases it examined rested solely or primarily on confessions allegedly coerced through torture — ALLEGATIONS the courts dismissed without investigation, on that report's reading of the records. Bahrain has executed six people since ending a de facto moratorium in 2017; no execution is recorded in 2023, 2024 or 2025, and the last known execution was in 2019 (World Coalition Against the Death Penalty / Cornell Center, updated 16 November 2026). Every death sentence takes effect only on the King's ratification — the count of the condemned ends, by law, on his desk (HRW/BIRD, 2022).

Why: Women, small minorities, and citizens as consumers of subsidies are genuinely counted in — and the country's majority is counted out of the army, the police, the senior civil service, the ballot (by isolation law and postponed election), and, in 2026, in hundreds of cases out of liberty and in dozens out of citizenship itself, including infants. A count that reaches everyone except the majority community, at the points where the state is strongest, is not everyone. Helped: Bahraini women, whose political rights date from this reign's charter and whose advancement machinery is funded and staffed; small religious minorities given public, protected space; low-income citizen households held harmless on utility tariffs and topped up in 2026; the hundreds of families reunited by the pardons. Hurt: Shia Bahrainis as a class — barred in practice from the security forces, their largest society dissolved, their clerics tried en masse in 2026; the more than 700 arrested in 2026 on the joint-statement count, among them children; the families of the April 2026 citizenship group; the man who died in custody in March 2026; the death-row prisoners whose sentences await the King's ratification on trials the court-records report found to rest on allegedly coerced confessions.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 6 March 1999, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The 1999–2001 opening — delivered as announced, at first. The early pledges were kept in the coin promised: political prisoners released, the State Security Court dissolved and the 1974 security decree abolished, exiles returned, the National Action Charter put to referendum on 14 February 2001 and approved by 98.4%, municipal and parliamentary elections held in 2002 with women voting and standing for the first time (Charter and election records). For roughly two years the word and the record ran together, and the shelf records it without discount.
  • The Abraham Accords — a commitment made and held under pressure. Bahrain normalised relations with Israel by the declaration signed at the White House on 15 September 2020; embassies were exchanged, direct flights began, and the commitment was held through the Gaza war despite a parliamentary resolution calling for suspension of ties and documented majority public opposition (Abraham Accords record, 2020; Bahrain–Israel relations record, 2023). Whatever its content is graded elsewhere (Test 4's ledger and the crossings below), as a word given to partners it was kept.
  • The pardons as announced — decreed and executed at the stated scale. The April 2024 decree named 1,584 prisoners and 1,584 were released, with further decrees in June 2024, March 2025 (630) and December 2025 (963) following the same pattern of announcement and execution (OHCHR statement of the UN Resident Coordinator, 9 April 2024; CRS, September 2026; HRW country page, 2026). A pardon promised by decree in Bahrain is a pardon delivered.
  • Economic direction — diversification pursued as pledged. The long-stated goal of reducing oil dependence has been genuinely pursued: non-oil growth of 3.1% and overall GDP growth of 4.0% year-on-year in the third quarter of 2025 were recorded in the government's quarterly report, and a first corporate-profit tax (10% above set thresholds, for implementation in 2027) was referred to the legislature in December 2025 as an income-diversification measure (Cabinet report, 5 January 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The founding assurance — given in writing, reversed within a year. In 2001 the opposition accepted the National Action Charter after Hamad signed a document clarifying that only the elected lower house would have legislative power, while the appointed upper house would have a strictly advisory role. The Constitution he promulgated on 14 February 2002, without public consultation, gave the appointed Shura Council legislative power equal to the elected chamber's — going back on the public promise of 2001, in the assessment the record has carried ever since (Constitution of Bahrain record; openDemocracy, 2026). Four political societies, including Al-Wefaq, boycotted the 2002 elections in consequence. Every later limit on the elected chamber descends from this single reversal: the word was the price of the referendum's 98.4%, and it was not kept.
  • The BICI pledge — implementation promised, mostly not delivered. Having commissioned the BICI and publicly accepted its report in November 2011, the government committed to its recommendations; fifteen years on, the U.S. State Department records that key recommendations remained unimplemented — charges for nonviolent expression not dropped as a class, officers accused of torture not criminally charged above low levels, Shia not integrated into the security forces, no environment for national reconciliation created (U.S. Department of State, Country Reports: Bahrain; BICI follow-up record). The promise was renewed at each international review; the record did not move with it.
  • The Palestinian-state assurance — reversed in sixteen days. On 26 August 2020 King Hamad told the visiting U.S. Secretary of State that Bahrain was committed to the creation of a Palestinian state and dismissed Washington's push for normalisation with Israel; on 11 September 2020 Bahrain announced full diplomatic relations with Israel, signed on 15 September, with Hamad then stressing the need for a just peace and a two-state solution as a strategic option (Bahrain–Israel normalisation record, 2020). The later statement does not erase the earlier one; both are printed, sixteen days apart.
  • The constitutional-monarchy promise in substance — not kept. The Charter was sold, and voted for, as the road to a constitutional monarchy; the opposition's stated goal of a constitutional monarchy in substance "has not been realized," on CRS's flat assessment, and the King retains appointment of the government, command of the forces, the upper house, the judiciary's council and the power — used in April 2026 — to postpone the elected chamber's renewal (CRS, 2018 and 2025). A hereditary seat is not itself a broken promise; promising that the seat would become constitutional in substance, and consolidating it instead — CRS records that since 2020 Hamad has focused on consolidating power among his direct descendants — is the gap this test grades.

Why: The small and medium words — decrees, pardons, a treaty with partners, the first two years of the opening — were kept, and kept exactly. The large words were not: the signed assurance that bought the Charter's referendum, the BICI implementation pledge, the Palestinian-state assurance reversed in sixteen days, and the constitutional monarchy in substance that 98.4% voted for. In this reign the reliability of the King's word runs inversely to its size, and Test 5 grades the whole scale. Helped: The referendum's voters in the opening years, who got the releases, the charter and the 2002 ballot they were promised; Israel and the United States, whose 2020 commitment was held under domestic pressure; prisoners named in pardon decrees, whose announced releases happened at the announced numbers. Hurt: The opposition leaders who accepted the Charter on the signed advisory-Shura assurance and the voters who ratified it — the reversal shaped every election since; the victims of 2011, whose BICI remedies were promised and mostly not delivered; Palestinians and their Bahraini supporters, assured on 26 August 2020 of a commitment set aside on 11 September 2020.
