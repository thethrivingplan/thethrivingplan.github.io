The Ruler's Test — Anura Kumara Dissanayake, President of Sri Lanka

Subject: Anura Kumara Dissanayake — President of Sri Lanka since 23 September 2024; leader of the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP) since 2014 and of the National People's Power (NPP) coalition built around it. He won the presidential election of 21 September 2024 with 42.3 per cent of first preferences and 55.9 per cent after the second-preference count; in the parliamentary election of 14 November 2024 the NPP won 159 of 225 seats on 61.56 per cent of the vote — a two-thirds majority. He also holds the Finance portfolio. He joined the JVP as a school student in 1987, during its second insurgency (see Test 4), and survived the state repression that followed. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Enforcement against the old order has reached further, and landed harder, than under any predecessor in the documented record. On 29 May 2025 the Colombo High Court Trial-at-Bar convicted former sports minister Mahindananda Aluthgamage (20 years' rigorous imprisonment) and former Sathosa chairman and trade minister Nalin Fernando (25 years) in the CIABOC "carrom board" case — charges first filed in 2015, proven beyond reasonable doubt on the court's own judgment (Colombo High Court judgment, 29 May 2025; AFP, 29 May 2025).
  • The Rajapaksa family itself is inside the process. The Attorney General indicted Namal Rajapaksa (February 2025, the Krrish transaction) and Yoshitha Rajapaksa (April 2025, money laundering) in cases begun a decade earlier and previously dropped; in June 2025 former health minister Keheliya Rambukwella was indicted with eleven others over the substandard immunoglobulin procurement, and on 27 March 2026 the Supreme Court ruled that Rambukwella and senior officials had violated the public's fundamental rights in the 2022 medicine procurement and ordered him to pay Rs. 75 million to the state (Supreme Court, SC/FR/65/2023, 27 March 2026). In October 2026 CIABOC arrested former first lady Shiranthi Rajapaksa (CIABOC reporting via Daily Mirror, 7 October 2026).
  • A former president was arrested for the first time in the country's history: Ranil Wickremesinghe was taken into custody by the CID on 22 August 2025 over the alleged misuse of some Rs. 16.6 million of public funds on a September 2023 London stopover for his wife's honorary professorship ceremony, and remanded; a magistrate released him on bail on 26 August on medical grounds, and the case continues (Colombo Fort Magistrate's Court proceedings, August–October 2025). The misuse is an ALLEGATION; Wickremesinghe denies it, maintaining his wife paid her own expenses.
  • The machinery was deliberately re-powered rather than merely unleashed: in January 2025 Dissanayake appointed a retired High Court judge, Ranga Dissanayake, as Director General of CIABOC; police re-established a Financial Crimes Investigation Division the same month; CIABOC has used the 2023 Anti-Corruption Act and the 2025 Proceeds of Crime Act to prosecute directly and freeze assets. The International Crisis Group — no ally of the government — records "noticeably more political space for a range of state institutions to pursue corruption cases free from political interference" (Crisis Group, Asia Report N°356, 16 April 2026). Parliament impeached and removed the Inspector General of Police for gross misconduct in August 2025, and the National Police Commission dismissed the senior officer who had failed to act on advance warnings of the 2019 Easter bombings (July 2025).
  • The law has at least been pointed at his own camp: in August 2025 CIABOC's commissioners formally authorised indicting his sitting Energy Minister, Kumara Jayakody, for alleged misappropriation during a 2015 Fertiliser Corporation posting, and reaffirmed the decision in late February 2026 after legal delay; toward the end of 2025 CIABOC also opened asset investigations into six senior ministers (Crisis Group N°356, citing Sunday Times and Ceylon Today reporting). The government's first Speaker resigned in December 2024 after being unable to evidence a claimed doctorate.

Evidence against:

  • The Jayakody case is also the clearest documented exception. When the opposition moved a no-confidence motion calling on the indicted minister to resign, the NPP used its majority to defeat it — 153 against, 49 for, on 10–12 April 2026 — and kept him in the cabinet. Anti-corruption activists, including prominent NPP supporters, called this a betrayal of the government's founding promise; Crisis Group records that it "badly damaged Dissanayake's and the NPP's credibility as reformers" (Crisis Group N°356, April 2026). As of grading, every person facing a completed corruption case is a loyalist of a previous government or a bureaucrat with no NPP/JVP ties — a pattern Crisis Group's interviewees note expressly.
  • Procurement by his own government is under a documented cloud that has been answered defensively rather than openly: opposition parties and activists point to government purchases of coal (including allegedly substandard shipments), vehicles and passports in which the process was ALLEGEDLY designed to advantage particular companies at a loss to the state (Crisis Group N°356, April 2026). These remain ALLEGATIONs; the graded fact is the absence, to date, of a public accounting that settles them.
  • The Wickremesinghe arrest is CONTESTED as one-law evidence. His supporters and parties across the spectrum called it a political witch hunt designed to humiliate; liberal civil-society figures also questioned it; the government and its supporters answer that it proves "no one is above the law" (Crisis Group N°356; Deutsche Welle, 25 August 2025). Both positions are on the record; the arrest's small sum relative to the Rajapaksa-era files is part of what fuels the dispute.
  • In October 2026 his government enacted the Anti-Corruption (Amendment) Act, No. 25 of 2026, which expands CIABOC's Director General's power to withdraw charges and to spare cooperating accomplices without a magistrate's authorisation, removes the requirement to declare assets of cohabiting persons, raises the state-shareholding threshold for covered companies from 25 to 50 per cent, and criminalises certain uses of published asset declarations. Transparency International Sri Lanka petitioned the Supreme Court against several provisions as weakening scrutiny; the Supreme Court struck down, as unconstitutional, a clause that would have imposed declaration duties on media owners (Supreme Court determination on the Bill, 2026; TISL petition, 31 August 2026; Centre for Investigative Reporting, 2026). The government describes the Act as fixing administrative weaknesses in the 2023 law — its stated position, on the record.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the anti-corruption commission, the central bank, the media and the provinces keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The central bank has operated independently in fact, not only in law. Dissanayake retained Governor Nandalal Weerasinghe — appointed by a predecessor, mid-term under the Central Bank of Sri Lanka Act No. 16 of 2023, which bars the Treasury Secretary from the Bank's boards and protects board members' tenure — and the Bank tightened policy on its own judgment in May 2026, raising its policy rate 100 basis points to 8.75 per cent against an energy shock, and again adjusted reserve requirements in November 2026 (CBSL monetary policy reviews, 2026; Global Finance interview with the Governor, 5 November 2026). A new Monetary Policy Framework Agreement between the Finance Minister and the Governor was signed on 5 October 2026 under the 2023 Act's own procedure.
  • Courts rule against the government's interests and are obeyed: the Supreme Court's March 2026 fundamental-rights judgment against a former health minister and senior officials (Test 1); its determination requiring changes to the government's 22nd Amendment bill before passage (22 September 2026); and its striking of the media-owners clause from the anti-corruption amendment bill. The Chemmani mass-grave exhumation in the north proceeded under court supervision, with the government citing it as the independent judiciary at work.
  • The Constitutional Council — the appointments check — bit in public: it rejected four consecutive Dissanayake nominees for Auditor General as unqualified during 2025, and the president ultimately proceeded through the Council's process, with an appointment approved on 3 February 2026 after the Council's civil-society membership was renewed by agreement between the Prime Minister and the Opposition Leader (Crisis Group N°356, April 2026).
  • Media operate without the documented punishments of earlier eras: no journalist is recorded jailed or an outlet closed under this government, and Sri Lanka's RSF World Press Freedom Index position improved from 150th (2024) to 139th (2025) to 134th (2026), score 40.77 (RSF Index 2024–2026). Opposition parties hold rallies, dominate much of the private press's commentary, and defeated the government in a third of the country's local councils in May 2025 (Test 3).

Evidence against:

  • Oversight commissions have been weakened by vacancy and by money. The Auditor General's post stood empty for most of 2025 amid the nomination standoff — civil-society critics believe the president wanted a loyalist, which is their characterization, on the record. The Right to Information Commission's chair appointment was delayed for months amid reports the president sought a candidate "widely viewed as hostile to information requests"; in August 2025 he accepted the Constitutional Council's nominee only after a public campaign by media and civil society, including NPP-aligned activists. In November 2025 the RTIC itself publicly protested the government's refusal to give it the dedicated funding the law stipulates, saying it operates with "a skeleton staff" despite a rising caseload; journalists argue the Media Ministry's de facto control of the Commission's budget is the lever. CIABOC likewise remains underfunded and understaffed, and anti-corruption campaigners warn that a government claim of finance-ministry control over CIABOC's budget threatens its independence (Crisis Group N°356; RTIC public statement, 20 November 2025; TISL statement, 15 November 2025).
  • In July 2025 the Sunday Times reported government plans to amend the constitution so that members of the Constitutional Council and independent commissions could be replaced whenever a new government takes office; public-interest advocates argue this would fundamentally weaken oversight independence. The plan had not been enacted as of grading, but it has not been publicly withdrawn either (Sunday Times, 20 July 2025, via Crisis Group N°356).
  • State media changed hands with the government in the old manner: the chairmanships of the four principal state-media companies were replaced between 24 and 27 September 2024, within days of his inauguration, and the State Media Monitor's 2026 typology still classifies the four principal outlets as "Captured Public" and the state news agency as "State-Controlled"; RSF finds fewer than one in five citizens has access to politically independent media (State Media Monitor, Sri Lanka country file, July 2026; RSF Index 2026). Ministers and senior officials have also repeatedly asked police to investigate alleged "fake news" and defamation, bypassing the Press Complaints Commission — a practice the Human Rights Commission of Sri Lanka flagged in a statement of 3 February 2026 (Crisis Group N°356).
  • The 22nd Amendment, passed 158–63 on 25 September 2026 with the Judicature (Amendment) Act, raised superior judges' retirement ages (Supreme Court 65→67, Court of Appeal 63→65), capped the Chief Justice's tenure at six years, and enlarged the Court of Appeal from 19 to 24 judges. The Bar Association of Sri Lanka wrote to the President in May 2026, before any bill existed, and the opposition charges that extending sitting judges' tenure threatens judicial independence; the government answers that the changes address a backlog of over a million cases, and the Supreme Court reviewed the bill and required wording changes before passage (ConstitutionNet/International IDEA analysis, 16 November 2026; Parliament of Sri Lanka, certified text, 25 September 2026). The dispute is CONTESTED; the fact graded is that a tenure-extending change to the bench was carried by the government's two-thirds majority over the organized bar's objection.
  • Provincial councils — the constitution's devolved tier — have now had no elected members since their terms expired (the last elections were held in 2014), and all nine provinces are run by presidentially appointed Governors; the government's own timetable for restoring their elections has slipped past its manifesto deadline (graded fully under Tests 4 and 5).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the IMF, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His largest adjustment came within days of taking power and was made openly. Having campaigned to renegotiate the IMF program and win more debt relief, Dissanayake told parliament at its first sitting (21 November 2024) that the economy was "hanging on a thread" and "there is no room for mistakes," retained the officials who had negotiated the agreement, and adopted the program with only marginal adjustments (speech to parliament, 21 November 2024; Crisis Group N°356). The IMF's Executive Board completed the third review on 28 February 2025, releasing $334 million, and praised a government "committed to staying within the guardrails."
  • When the pushback was electoral, he accepted the count. In the local government elections of 6 May 2025 the NPP's vote share fell from 61.56 per cent (November 2024) to about 43 per cent, and it won outright control of only 133 of 339 councils; the results were seated without dispute and opposition coalitions took office where they had the numbers (Election Commission results, May 2025; Crisis Group N°356).
  • On land in the north, he reversed a state move when it was challenged: Gazette Notification No. 2430 of March 2025, under which lands in Jaffna, Kilinochchi, Mannar and Mullaitivu could be claimed as state land if owners failed to file claims by 28 June, was revoked in May 2025 after protests by Tamil politicians and a fundamental-rights application to the Supreme Court (Centre for Policy Alternatives reconciliation tracker, 2025).
  • Adjustment through the front door also marks the crisis year: after Cyclone Ditwah (late November 2025) and the Middle East energy shock, the government renegotiated program parameters with the IMF rather than breaking them — the combined fifth and sixth reviews, approved 27 May 2026 with $695 million disbursed (about $2.4 billion in total), lowered the 2026 primary-surplus target from 2.3 to 1.4 per cent of GDP to accommodate reconstruction and temporary relief (IMF Executive Board decision, 27 May 2026). Between its December 2025 and September 2026 versions, the draft Protection of the State from Terrorism Bill acquired added safeguards that UN experts expressly acknowledged as improvements, even while pressing for more (OHCHR comments, 13 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the promises where pushback came from his own manifesto's readers, the pattern is delay defended rather than adjustment owned: the one-year deadline for provincial council elections passed in September 2025 without a date; the eventual announcement that polls could not be held in 2026 and might occur in 2027 was made not in parliament but by JVP General Secretary Tilvin Silva at a party office opening in Jaffna on 23 May 2026 — a manner the Centre for Policy Alternatives formally objected to, since Silva holds no parliamentary seat or portfolio (CPA statement, May 2026; Lanka Witness, 9 November 2026). The government's reasons — the 2017 electoral-law delimitation problem (real, and inherited) and Rs. 500 billion redirected to Ditwah recovery — are on the record; critics note the recovery money was appropriated by a separate supplementary estimate and that the polls would cost roughly Rs. 10 billion (Lanka Witness, November 2026).
  • The government's reflex under criticism is documented as defensive and moralising. Crisis Group, drawing on interviews across Colombo's political spectrum, records that NPP responses to accusations "have often sounded arrogant and defensive," that ministers react to "any misstep" being seized on by doubling down on the party's self-image as embattled reformers, and that the president's own local-election campaigning included warnings that the centre would be "especially careful" funding councils won by opposition parties — remarks that drew complaints to the Election Commission (Crisis Group N°356; Sunday Times, 20 April 2025).
  • Cyclone Ditwah exposed the same pattern operationally: the administration was widely criticised for poor preparation despite meteorologists' warnings days in advance and for a slow initial response to the country's most destructive recorded disaster (800+ killed, $4.1 billion in immediate losses, World Bank estimate); life-saving alerts were issued only in Sinhala and English, endangering Tamil-speakers in the worst-hit central hills (Crisis Group N°356; Deutsche Welle, 2 December 2025). The government's recovery effort since is documented; the preparedness failure is not answered by it.
  • On the Jayakody indictment (Test 1), pushback from his own anti-corruption constituency was met not with adjustment but with a whipped parliamentary vote to keep the minister — the single clearest double-down in the file.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Tamils and Muslims, the poor, plantation workers, and his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The poor are counted in the fiscal record, within the program's limits. The Aswesuma welfare program was expanded and its targeting improved, with the World Bank noting coverage gains "especially in the estate sector"; social protection received Rs. 230 billion in the 2027 budget, alongside increased allowances for kidney patients and persons with disabilities (World Bank, Sri Lanka Development Update, October 2026). On the Bank's revised series, poverty at the $4.20/day line fell from 20.7 per cent (2023) to 18.4 per cent (2024) and an estimated 16.9 per cent (2025), and real growth reached 5 per cent in 2025 with inflation and interest rates down sharply from crisis levels (World Bank, October 2026; Crisis Group N°356).
  • Plantation (Malaiyaha Tamil) workers received the largest directed wage intervention in the community's history: the 2025 budget proposed raising the daily wage from Rs. 1,350 toward Rs. 1,700, and the 2026 budget set Rs. 1,750 from 1 January 2026 — a Rs. 1,550 basic wage plus a Rs. 200 government-funded attendance allowance, backed by a Rs. 5 billion allocation — the first time plantation wages were raised by budget proposal rather than after union struggle, as the Tamil Progressive Alliance itself acknowledged while supporting the budget (2026 Budget speech, 7 November 2025; Daily Mirror, 15 November 2025). His government also appointed the first-ever woman minister from the Malaiyaha community, and housing programs for estate families (including the Indian-funded 10,000-house scheme) continued.
  • Tamils in the north received real, if partial, land restitution: the president stated on 7 April 2026 that nearly 1,000 acres of private northern land were released in 2025; documented handovers include 234 acres at Myiliddy (2025), 11.23 acres across three Jaffna divisions (October 2026) and 6.97 acres at Keppapilavu (October 2026). He had pledged in Jaffna in January 2025 that "land belonging to the people should rightfully remain with them" (parliamentary committee records and contemporaneous reporting, 2025–2026).
  • The representation count widened in other ways: 21 women were elected to parliament in November 2024, the highest number in the country's history, 19 of them from the NPP; his Prime Minister, Harini Amarasuriya, is a woman academic and feminist activist; and in October 2025 he removed — and in February 2026 police arrested — the former intelligence chief Suresh Sallay in the Easter-bombings investigation, the accountability step the (largely Catholic and Muslim) victims' movement had demanded for six years (Reuters, 25 February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Tamils' political counting is deferred indefinitely. The provincial councils — the one elected institution of the devolution settlement — remain unelected for a twelfth year, run by his appointed Governors, with polls now floated for 2027 "if no further obstacles arose" (Test 3). Reporting in October 2026 records the JVP General Secretary telling a former party leader that the government regards the 13th Amendment framework itself as "a negative" and is developing "its own mechanisms" — a position reported by Lionel Bopage from his own conversation, not an official policy statement, and graded as such (Groundviews interview, 2 October 2026, via Sri Lanka Brief). In October 2025 the government rejected a UN Human Rights Council resolution largely because it extended the UN's Sri Lanka Accountability Project, which it calls divisive; it has made no move toward accountability for wartime atrocities, and neither the president nor the prime minister has met the Tamil mothers campaigning for the fate of the disappeared (Foreign Ministry statement, 6 October 2025; Crisis Group N°356).
  • The security state's treatment of Tamils continues under him and is denied rather than answered: counter-terrorism police and military intelligence "continue to regularly question and intimidate Tamil activists and survivors' groups," and surveillance targets journalists covering the military in the north and east, according to the Adayaalam Centre's February 2025 study and Crisis Group's October 2025 interviews; RSF in October 2025 called for an end to the harassment of Tamil photojournalist Kumanan Kanapathipillai. The government insists state forces are not engaging in these tactics (Crisis Group N°356; RSF, 24 October 2025).
  • Land release, measured against the occupation, remains marginal and conditional: roughly 2,900 acres in Valikamam North alone remain under security-forces control within High Security Zones; some 2025 releases at Myiliddy permit agriculture only, with farmers required to leave by evening; at Keppapilavu some 170 acres are still held against the 6.97 returned; and in July 2026 the Deputy Defence Minister told parliament the Palaly military hospital's 27.9 acres "cannot be returned" (CPA reconciliation tracker, 2025; parliamentary record, 9 July 2026; Tamil Guardian, October 2026).
  • Muslims wait on both of their files: the promised reform of the Muslim Marriage and Divorce Act, backed by Muslim women's groups and by CEDAW's February 2025 concluding observations, has not been enacted, stalled against resistance from Islamic leaders and male Muslim politicians; and the Easter investigation's pace — Sallay arrested but, as of April 2026, not yet indicted — is criticised by the victims' advocates who demanded it (Crisis Group N°356). Sallay's own detention, meanwhile, drew a UN special-procedures urgent appeal (20 July 2026) alleging arbitrary detention under the PTA and torture in custody; the government replied (20 September 2026) that the arrest was lawful, evidence-based and under judicial supervision, and the matter is before the Court of Appeal — ALLEGATION and denial both on the record.
  • The poor's recovery is real but shallow, and its financing is regressive: poverty remains well above the 2019 rate of 11.5 per cent, real wages are about 12 per cent below 2019, child stunting rose from 7.4 per cent (2021) to 10.1 per cent (2025), estate-sector poverty stands at 36 per cent, and two-fifths of the population is poor or at risk of falling into poverty (World Bank, October 2026). Revenue rose from 8 to 15.9 per cent of GDP between 2020 and 2025, but three-quarters of the increase comes from indirect taxes, chiefly VAT, which fall hardest on lower incomes — and the NPP "has given surprisingly little priority" to shifting the burden upward (Crisis Group N°356, citing the World Bank's September 2025 Public Finance Review). The Institute of Policy Studies found only 55 per cent of the poorest quintile of shock-exposed households actually receive social protection.
  • His opponents' voters were addressed as a lesser class of citizen during the May 2025 local campaign, when the president warned that opposition-won councils' funding would be handled "especially carefully" given their parties' history — and after the vote, NPP leaders argued it would be "undemocratic" for smaller parties to combine into majorities on councils the NPP had merely pluralitied, before the NPP itself negotiated such coalitions (Crisis Group N°356; Sunday Times, 20 April 2025).

The party's own past, stated factually where it bears on this test. The JVP — the NPP's core — twice rose in arms against the Sri Lankan state. The 1971 uprising cost, on commonly cited estimates, some 5,000 lives. The 1987–89 insurrection, launched against the Indo-Lanka Accord, saw the JVP's armed wing assassinate politicians, officials, rival leftists and civilians who defied its violently enforced general strikes; the state's counter-insurgency answered with death squads and mass disappearances. Total deaths for 1987–89 are estimated between roughly 30,000 and 60,000, the majority killed by state and pro-state forces; the U.S. State Department's 1998 country profile records an estimated 60,000 dead or disappeared. JVP leader Rohana Wijeweera was killed in military custody in November 1989. The party was banned from 1983; it returned to electoral politics in 1994 and has contested elections since. Dissanayake, who joined as a teenager in 1987 and rose through the post-insurgency party, has led it since 2014; his Foreign Minister told the UN Human Rights Council on 8 September 2025 that the JVP's own experience of "oppression and rights violations" means "we identify with the immense pain and suffering of Sri Lankans who were affected by conflicts on all sides." No JVP leader has been prosecuted for the 1987–89 killings; nor, as the record above shows, has any state official for the counter-insurgency's — the symmetry of that impunity is a documented fact of the system he inherited and now heads.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since September 2024, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Anti-corruption as the governing project — kept in direction and in volume. The manifesto's central promise was to end impunity for the politically connected; the arrests, indictments and convictions in Test 1, delivered through a re-powered CIABOC and a revived Financial Crimes Investigation Division, are the promise being executed against its named targets (Crisis Group N°356).
  • Politicians' privileges — kept, in law. Parliament passed the Presidents' Entitlements (Repeal) Act in September 2025, stripping ex-presidents and their families of official residences and benefits, and the Parliamentary Pensions (Repeal) Act in February 2026, abolishing MPs' pensions (Crisis Group N°356).
  • Transparency about his own wealth — kept. Under the 2023 Anti-Corruption Act, asset declarations of the President, Prime Minister and MPs — including Dissanayake's own — are published on CIABOC's website, a first in the country's practice; CIABOC set a system-wide filing deadline of 30 June 2026 with prosecution warned for non-filers (CIABOC publications, 2025–2026; Fact Crescendo verification, 2025).
  • An independent public prosecutor — begun. The Justice Ministry announced in February 2025 that it had begun establishing a public prosecutor's office independent of the Attorney General, as the manifesto promised and the IMF's governance diagnostic recommended; the Attorney General's Department's collective opposition (November 2025) has slowed but not formally killed it (Economy Next, 24 February 2025; Sunday Times, 16 November 2025).
  • Easter-bombings justice — moved, late. He reappointed the investigators his predecessor's predecessor had sacked, removed Sallay in October 2024 and saw him arrested in February 2026; the senior officer who ignored the warnings was dismissed in July 2025 (Reuters, 25 February 2026; Crisis Group N°356).

Evidence against (promises not kept, deferred, or reversed):

  • Renegotiate the IMF deal and win more debt relief — reversed within two months. The manifesto pledged changes to the reform program and more relief from creditors; in office he adopted the program with "only marginal adjustments" and accepted the IMF's debt-sustainability analysis, which "brought very modest relief from foreign creditors" (Crisis Group N°356). His November 2024 explanation — the economy "hanging on a thread" — is on the record and honest; the promise as made to voters was nonetheless the opposite of the policy delivered. By November 2026 he was presenting a 2027 budget that asks a committee to consider an eighteenth IMF program after the current one ends in March 2027 (budget speech, November 2026).
  • Repeal the Prevention of Terrorism Act — not kept. The manifesto promised to repeal the PTA and other repressive laws. Two years on, the PTA remains in force and in use: 49 people were arrested under it in his administration's first five months alone, and its most prominent current detainee is the former intelligence chief (Sri Lanka Brief count, 15 August 2025; Reuters, 25 February 2026). The replacement offered instead — the Protection of the State from Terrorism Bill, first published December 2025 and gazetted in revised form on 22 September 2026 — retains executive detention orders (up to two months at a time, one year in aggregate) and, per the Centre for Policy Alternatives, "perpetuates the same fundamental flaws of the PTA"; CPA, Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International and UN experts have all called for its withdrawal or deep revision, and CPA's demand is the manifesto's own: repeal unconditionally first (CPA statement, September 2026; OHCHR comments, 13 October 2026).
  • Provincial council elections within a year — not kept. The manifesto (p. 127) promised the indefinitely postponed provincial and local elections "within a year." Local elections were held in May 2025; provincial elections missed the September 2025 deadline, have no date, and were deferred by party announcement to 2027 at the earliest (Lanka Witness, 9 November 2026; CPA, May 2026).
  • Abolish the executive presidency through a new constitution — no motion. The manifesto promised a new charter removing the president's executive powers and devolving power to provinces and local government. Two years in, the government "has yet to put forward a timetable for drafting a new constitution," and the president reportedly assured Tamil legislators the process would begin in early 2026; it has not visibly begun (Crisis Group N°356; The Hindu, 19 November 2025). The constitutional change he has actually carried is the 22nd Amendment on judicial tenure (Test 2).
  • Fix the Online Safety Act — not kept. In opposition the NPP called the 2024 Act a tool to silence dissent; Dissanayake promised to change it so it would no longer restrict free expression. As of November 2026 the original Act remains in force unamended, no replacement bill has been gazetted, and police are still asked to investigate speech — five months after the president told parliament (26 June 2026) that his government did not intend to implement the Act and was preparing a new version (ICNL Civic Freedom Monitor, updated 30 October 2026; Lanka Witness, 16 November 2026).
  • A Rs. 2,000 daily wage for plantation workers — partially kept. The manifesto proposed Rs. 2,000; two budgets delivered Rs. 1,750, of which Rs. 200 is a temporary, attendance-conditioned government subsidy rather than wage — a shortfall plantation-sector analysts document in detail (2024 NPP manifesto; Budget 2026 policy analysis, December 2025).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Anura Kumara Dissanayake is the first ruler in this series whose signature promise — that the law would reach the powerful — has been executed at scale against the people it named: former ministers in prison for decades, a former president arrested, a ruling family's sons indicted, an intelligence chief in a cell. No predecessor in Sri Lanka's documented record let the machinery run that far into the old order, and the independent observers least friendly to his politics confirm the machinery is, for now, genuinely unscripted. But the same record shows a consistent border the law has not yet crossed: it has completed no case against his own camp, and when it reached his own energy minister, his majority voted to keep the minister rather than lose the seat. Around that border, every promise that would constrain the holder rather than the held has been deferred in the same direction — the terrorism law unrepealed and still in use, the devolved tier unelected into a twelfth year, the presidency he pledged to abolish undiminished and untimetabled, the speech law he denounced still on the book, the oversight commissions kept waiting for members and money. His adjustments, when they come, are real — the IMF reversal was owned in plain words within days — but they come where the markets or the voters push, not where the manifesto does. The word, in the end, splits along the same line as the law: kept at full strength against the old order's privileges, approximate wherever it would cost the new order power. Whether the border moves — an indicted minister tried, a terrorism law repealed rather than renamed, a provincial election called — is exactly what this record remains open to correct.
