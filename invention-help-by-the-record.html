Invention help, judged by the record — not the pitch.

You asked for companies that help push an invention for free, or for a fair share. The honest answer, from the companies' own legally required disclosures: no mainstream invention promoter works that way. The big ones are fee businesses whose own numbers show roughly 0.1–1% of customers ever profit. But genuine fair-share and free routes do exist — they're just smaller, pickier, and quieter. This page lays out the record, the maths, and then a bench for growing your own idea the honest way.

$0.40 a unit
What a typical fair royalty actually pays: 5% of an $8 wholesale price on a $20 retail product. Anyone who paid a promoter $10,000 needs ~25,000 units sold just to break even on the fee.
Source: licensing practitioner guides (Core77, Goldstein Patent Law) — typical consumer royalty 3–5% of wholesale.

The one skill that protects you: by law (American Inventors Protection Act, 35 U.S.C. §297), an invention promoter must give you, in writing and before you sign, how many customers they had and how many made a net profit. Divide the second number by the first. That is your approximate chance of coming out ahead — on their own numbers. Then ask why, if that number is under 1%, they need your fee up front instead of living on their share of successes.

Section 1

The promoters, on their own numbers

Researched October 23, 2026. "Verified live" means read on the company's or regulator's own page that day. Company claims are labelled as claims, never as facts.

Fee business

Davison (Pittsburgh, est. 1989)

What you actually pay
$995 pre-development (plus 10% commission) + $3,000–$18,000 for design/development — roughly $4,000–$19,000 before any test manufacturing, which you also fund. From its own published fee schedule.
Its own outcome disclosure
32,198 customers in 5 years; 39 net-profit customers since 1989; 837 license agreements since 1989. Davison states it offers zero evaluations — positive or negative. Its success-stories page carries the line: "The typical Davison client project is not licensed, sold in stores or profitable."
Enforcement record
2006: federal court found deceptive practices, ordered $26 million in consumer redress (FTC, primary). Appealed and settled (~$10–10.7M, secondary sources). Currently the most-listed firm on the USPTO's published-complaints page (complaints dated Apr 2026, Feb 2026, Nov 2024).

Sources: davison.com/legal/aipa (verified live) · FTC judgment PDF

Fee business

InventHelp (Invention Submission Corp., est. 1984)

What you actually pay
Packages typically $10,000–$16,000 (reported range $800–$30,000): a few hundred for a "Basic Information Package," then $8,000–$10,000+ for the submission phase. Customers who hesitated were referred to an affiliated lender. InventHelp publishes no prices on its website.
Its outcome disclosure (as reported)
2022–2024: 3,507 customers, 113 licenses (3.2%), 5 profited (0.14%). 2015–2017: ~6,564 clients, 49 (<1%) earned more than they paid.
Enforcement record
Predecessor settled with the FTC in 1994 ($1.2M set aside for refunds). 2023: $3 million class-action settlement (no admission of liability) — individual claimants received up to ~$250 against typical payments of $10,000+. BBB: A+ local ratings while not BBB-accredited, alongside 15 complaints in 3 years on the HQ profile.

Sources: disclosure analysis · $3M settlement · BBB complaints

The pattern, across 30 years of cases

Regulators keep shutting these firms down and the model re-forms under new names — which is exactly why the law also makes promoters list every promotion company their officers ran in the past 10 years. The script is identical each time:

1. Free kit and a flattering "review" → 2. a paid research report that is almost always positive → 3. a much larger patenting/promotion package → 4. claims of industry relationships and "success stories" → 5. almost no customer profits → 6. resistance to refunds.

The extreme case: World Patent Marketing (shut down by the FTC, 2018) took in ~$26M in about two years charging $9,000–$65,000, with a "review team" and "Harvard/MIT research team" the court found did not exist — and no identifiable customer who profited. Judgment: $25,987,192; the founder was banned from invention promotion. Earlier sweeps: FTC's Project Mousetrap (1997) — in one case, "not one of NISI's 997 Phase Two customers made more money than they had paid." Sources: FTC WPM settlement release · industry enforcement summary

Section 2

Who genuinely works for a share

The honest version has three fingerprints: a small evaluation fee, a high rejection rate, and income stated as a percentage of your royalties — so the firm starves if it says yes to bad ideas. As the FTC itself puts it: reputable licensing agents "depend on royalties from successful clients and not on large advance fees."

Contingency — verified live

Invention City

The gate
Free submission (licensing consideration only, no feedback) — it says it offers deals to about 1%. Or a paid "Brutally Honest Review" from $185 — stated as "the only fee we charge."
If it says yes
It commercialises at its own expense; you pay $0 out of pocket. Deal: a share of net proceeds from sub-licensing, or a royalty on net sales if it makes and sells the product, with performance guarantees and audit rights. The exact % split is not published — it's stated per offer.
Evidence
Publishes named examples of inventions it accepted. No aggregate client-profit rate published — outcomes rest on named examples, not rates.

Source: inventioncity.com (verified live, Oct 23, 2026)

Contingency — historical record

Lambert & Lambert

The gate
~$199 evaluation (patent search + market research, scored on a 16-point "licensability" test). It rejected over 90% of ideas; of those it represented, it claimed 30–40% obtained a license.
The deal
Representation at the firm's expense — travel, marketing, prototype-for-pitching. Paid only from royalties: 25–30%; the inventor kept 70–75%. It did not pay patent costs, and advised a strong provisional filing first.
⚠️ Currency warning
All detailed sources are 2009–2011. Its current status, fee, and percentage could not be verified in 2026 sources. Do not sign based on the historical description — re-verify and demand a current disclosure.

Sources: owner interview · IPWatchdog

Hybrid — read the structure

Enhance & InventionHome

Enhance Product Development
You first buy a design package (prices not published; only Gold/Platinum qualify). Then licensing is contingency: 20% of license income — and, in a genuinely inventor-friendly clause worth demanding anywhere, its 20% only begins after you've recouped what you paid for the design work. The risk: you paid before any market verdict, and the gate is purchase, not merit.
InventionHome
You pay for tangible services (patent search, provisional, website/portfolio); the marketing is claimed on contingency at 20%. Its own service agreement states it "does not evaluate product potential" — and complaints allege the outreach was batch email reported as personal pitching. A monthly report of contacts attempted is not evidence of buyers engaged.

Sources: enhancepd.com/licensing · InventionHome complaint responses

The going rate for a real licensing agent

Typical agent commission: 30–35% of the royalties you earn — not an upfront fee. At a 5% royalty rate, a 30% agent cut leaves you ~3.5% of wholesale. That's normal, and still far better maths than a $10,000 fee. One outlier found: Cad Crowd states a 50% commission on royalties — the top of the range — with no licensed-product outcomes found to verify. Label: claim only.

Sources: LegalClarity agent guide · cadcrowd.com

Cautionary tale: Quirky (defunct)

"Free submission + revenue share" was tried at scale: free ideas, community voting, in-house development, ~$180M raised, real products shipped (the Pivot Power strip did $2M+). It filed Chapter 11 in September 2015, leaving inventors unsure about their IP and royalties. The lesson that shapes the bench below: free + share fails when the gate is popularity instead of purchase intent — votes are not purchases.

Sources: Quirky post-mortem · HBS analysis

Section 3

Actually free — because the buyer is the customer

These cost $0 to submit or ask. The catch is brutal selectivity and silence: most submissions get no reply, and the portals want a finished, protected, priced product — not a raw idea. What you give up is mostly confidentiality and your time; you keep your IP unless a deal is signed.

$0 to submit

Corporate open-submission portals

Walmart Open Call (annual, since 2014)
Free application; products must be made/grown/assembled in the USA and shelf-ready. 2026: nearly 150 entrepreneurs pitched, selected from several thousand applicants; 54 received "Golden Tickets" to sell at Walmart/Sam's Club. No royalty model — you're a normal supplier at wholesale.
LEGO Ideas
Free; your design must reach 10,000 supporters to enter LEGO's expert review. Submission is public — don't submit secrets. If produced: 1% of net sales + 10 copies + creator credit. 72 Ideas sets produced as of May 2025 — a countable outcome list, the gold standard of evidence.
Hasbro Spark · OXO
Both free. Hasbro warns a submission "does not mean it is unique." OXO is starkest: no confidentiality, no NDAs, patent pending/issued required, 6–8 week silent evaluation, contact only on fit. Neither publishes licensed-outcome numbers — could not verify.

Sources: Walmart 13th Open Call · LEGO Ideas outcomes · Hasbro Spark · OXO inventor terms

$0 help

Free advice, patent help & peers

USPTO Patent Pro Bono Program
Volunteer patent attorneys matched with under-resourced inventors — attorney time is free (USPTO filing fees are still yours). Common eligibility: household income under 3× the federal poverty level, plus basic patent knowledge. 60+ law-school clinics similarly draft/file free with supervised students.
SCORE & SBDCs
Free, confidential mentoring (SCORE: 9,000+ volunteers) and free one-to-one business advising — plans, financial projections, market research. Generalist, not invention-specific; one SBDC's inventor guidance states bluntly: "I am not aware of one invention promotion company that I would recommend."
Inventor associations & clubs
The United Inventors Association (501(c)(3), free membership) and local clubs: peer review, speaker nights with real buyers, and hard-won warnings. Volunteer and uneven — nobody pitches or sells for you. That's the honest gap only §2's selective firms, or your own pitching, can fill.

Sources: USPTO Pro Bono (verified live) · USPTO published complaints (verified live)

Section 5

Run the royalty maths yourself

A fair royalty is small: 3–5% of the wholesale price for consumer products (toys & games 5–7%, hardware 3–6%, medical 4–8%; idea-stage deals can be 1–2% — LEGO Ideas pays 1% of net sales). Wholesale is roughly 40% of retail. So always make anyone — promoter, agent, or your own optimism — state the deal in units × dollars per unit. Try it:

$0.40
you earn per unit sold, after any agent cut
25,000
units that must sell before you've earned back the fees alone
$40,000
your total royalties if 100,000 units sell

Now compare: the DIY route's fee is a $65–$130 provisional filing. At $0.40 a unit, that's paid back after ~325 units — vs 25,000 units for a $10,000 promoter package. Same royalty. Very different mountain.

Section 6

Verify any company before you sign

Twelve checks, in the order to run them. Any company that resists check #1 has already answered it.

  1. Demand the AIPA disclosure in writing, before any contract.Evaluations (+/−), total customers (5 yrs), customers who made a net profit, customers who got license agreements, and officers' prior promotion firms (10 yrs). Compute profited ÷ customers. Walk away if they resist, rush you, or the rate needs explaining away.
  2. Price the whole journey in writing.Every phase, patent fees, prototype, packaging, trade shows, financing — and who owns the files and prototype if you quit. Davison publishes $995 + $3,000–$18,000; hold everyone to that standard of candour.
  3. Check the USPTO's published complaints.Search the firm on the USPTO complaints page and read the themes (remember: uninvestigated allegations plus the firm's reply, kept for 3 years).
  4. Check the FTC — including predecessor names.Search ftc.gov for the firm and every affiliate/prior firm from the AIPA list. Project Mousetrap (1997), Davison (2006), and World Patent Marketing (2018) are the templates of what you're looking for.
  5. Check both state attorneys general.Your state and the firm's home state — and whether the firm is registered where state invention-development statutes require it.
  6. Do the BBB check properly.Complaint count and text over 3 years, and accreditation status. An A+ rating can coexist with non-accreditation and a steady complaint flow — the rating rewards complaint handling, not customer outcomes.
  7. Verify "success stories" independently.Is the product on sale now, where — and did that inventor's royalties exceed the fees they paid? Courts have required Davison to disclose exactly this. Choose references yourself from a full client list; never accept only supplied testimonials.
  8. Verify industry relationships.Claimed buyer network or retailer relationship? Ask for names and call two. In litigation, claimed companies were defunct or had never heard of the promoter; Target denied any relationship with World Patent Marketing.
  9. Read the contract for traps.No gag clauses about complaining, a real cooling-off/refund period, no affiliated-lender financing without independent advice, no assignment of your IP, and termination + file-return terms. Have an independent patent attorney — not their referral — read it.
  10. Run the alignment test.Ask: "What do you earn if my invention never sells?" If the answer is "most of your fee," you are the product. Prefer: small evaluation fee + high rejection rate + % of royalties only, recoupment-first if you bought any package.
  11. Run the outreach-evidence test.Will they log and show you named companies, contacts, dates, and replies? "Submitted to our database of 9,000 companies" without a log is not marketing.
  12. Run the maths test.Make them state the expected royalty in units × $/unit at 3–5% of wholesale. If the projection needs a royalty or a volume no licensee pays, leave.
Section 7

Embryo — the Invention Bench

An idea at this stage isn't good or bad yet — it's not-yet-grown. So the bench doesn't judge it; it protects it, nourishes it, and walks it through gates in the order that spends the least before the strongest evidence. The process below is assembled, not copied: the best working parts stripped from every operator in this report — including the ones whose business model failed the record — with every step that takes advantage of the inventor removed.

Embryo is always completely free. No fees, no cut, no share of your royalties — ever. The bench takes nothing off the top because it takes nothing at all. The dollar figures inside the walk are outside costs you might choose to pay others along the way (a USPTO filing fee, prototype materials) — never charged by, or paid to, Embryo. Total outside exposure before a real buyer verdict: roughly $65–$400, against a promoter's $10,000+.

What got stripped out, explicitly: paid "evaluations" that always pass · any phase sold before a buyer verdict · prototypes sold to everyone regardless of merit · "submitted to thousands of companies" with no named log · success stories with no denominator · affiliated financing for fees · gag clauses · votes presented as demand. Good parts, wherever they were found

Davison's one genuinely good idea — design to the licensee's machines, materials and price point — is Stage 5. InventHelp's disciplined drawing → CAD → model order survives too, but only after a buyer asks. A predator can own a good lathe. We keep the lathe.

The gate is the product

Invention City sells a negative verdict with reasons; Lambert's test added licensability — not just "can it be patented?" and "could it sell?" but "would a company need to license it rather than design around it?" Both are built into Stage 2.

The log is the anti-"Data Bank"

Promoters report activity; the bench keeps evidence — named company, contact, date, reply, exact reason (Stage 4). Any intermediary you ever hire must keep the same log and show it to you on demand.
