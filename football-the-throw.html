How this page grades advice

Throwing advice is full of inherited slogans. Each claim here sits on one of three shelves, and contested topics are printed on two shelves at once, at the same size — you decide with both sides in front of you.

What works

Well-established mechanics and footwork — the things sound coaching agrees on and that hold up when you film them. Build your throw on this shelf first.

What really hasn't

Myths and dead ends, named as such: fixes sold as universal that help some throwers and wreck others, and effort cues that feel powerful and throw worse.

What to try

Individual experiments. Legal, plausible, unproven for you until you test them. Try one at a time, film it, and keep it only if your ball flight says yes.

The throw, link by link

A throw is a chain, not an arm swing. Power starts in the ground and arrives at your fingertips in order — feet, hips, shoulders, arm, hand. When one link fires late, early, or not at all, the ball tells on it. Each link below says what it does, and the fault it causes when it's off. Tap a link to open it.

Grip→Feet & base→Hip–shoulder separation→Arm path→Release→Follow-through
1 Grip — fingers on the laces What works Tap to open

What it does

Hold the ball toward its back third, ring and middle fingers across the laces, index finger up near the top point, thumb underneath on the opposite side. The ball sits in your fingers, not buried in your palm — a small pocket of air under the palm is normal. The laces are not decoration: they give your fingers raised edges to pull against at release. That last pull of the fingers over the laces is what spins the ball into a spiral. Smaller hands grip higher on the ball; that's an adjustment, not an error.

When it's off

Palming the ball (ball pressed deep into the palm) kills the finger pull — the ball slips out with a wobble instead of spinning off. Gripping the very tip makes the ball squirt out early and nose-dive. White-knuckle tight from the start tenses the whole arm and slows the chain down; the grip firms up naturally as the throw starts.

Light noteQuick self-check: hold the ball up and look — you should see your ring and middle fingers on the laces and daylight under your palm.
Coach noteWhy the laces matter, mechanically: spin comes from torque — a force applied off-centre. Fingertips on smooth leather slide; fingertips hooked over lace edges can pull. That pull, applied as the ball leaves, rotates the ball around its long axis. No lace contact, no reliable spin — and spin is what keeps the nose pointed where you threw it (see Spiral Science below).
2 Feet and base — the throw starts in the ground What works Tap to open

What it does

Feet about shoulder-width, weight balanced, knees soft. Your non-throwing shoulder points at the target. As the throw starts, you take a short step (about six inches) toward the target with the front foot and drive off the back foot. Every bit of force in the throw is borrowed from the ground through your legs — the arm is the last link, not the engine.

When it's off

Overstriding (a long step) drops your hips, locks the front leg late, and drags the arm behind — balls sail high or arrive late. No step at all leaves the hips closed and forces the arm to do all the work. Feet too narrow makes the base wobble under pressure, and a wobbling base throws wobbling balls.

Light noteIf your throws feel like hard work, film your feet before you touch your arm. The fault is usually downstairs.
Coach noteCoaches call the order of firing the kinematic sequence: ground → legs → hips → torso → shoulder → elbow → hand. Each link adds speed to the next, like a whip cracking in stages. Break the order — arm first, hips later — and you lose both speed and repeatability, because a big muscle (the arm) is now doing a job the whole body should share.
3 Hip–shoulder separation — the wind-up that isn't your arm What works Tap to open

What it does

As your front foot lands, your hips start turning toward the target before your shoulders do. For a moment your hips face the target while your shoulders are still turned away. That twist — separation — stretches the muscles of your trunk like a spring, and their snap-back is where much of a throw's real power lives. The throwing arm simply rides the turn.

When it's off

Everything turning together (hips and shoulders as one block) wastes the spring — the throw becomes arm-only and tiring. Opening too early (shoulders flying open before the front foot plants) leaks power sideways and pulls throws left for a right-hander. Falling away from the target mid-turn does the same leak in the other direction.

Light noteFeel it without a ball: stand sideways, turn just your belt buckle to the target, and notice your chest still points sideways. That gap is separation.
Coach noteThis is the honest answer to "how do I throw farther?" for most developing throwers: not a stronger arm, a better sequence. The trunk muscles are far bigger than the arm's, and separation is how you recruit them. It's also why "just throw it harder" so often makes throws worse — effort usually goes to the arm, which speeds up the last link while the early links stay the same, and the chain desyncs.
4 The arm path — a curve, not a catapult What works Tap to open

What it does

From the carry position (ball up near chest-to-ear height, both hands on it until the throw starts), the throwing hand takes the ball back in a short, curved path — think of the letter C, not a long straight line behind you. The elbow leads the hand forward as the shoulders turn; the hand and ball come last. A compact path is a quick path: less distance to travel means the ball is out sooner after you decide.

When it's off

The long windmill (ball dropping to the hip and looping up) is slow and telegraphs the throw — defenders read it and break early. Pushing the ball (hand behind the ball, shoving it like a shot put) kills spin and floats the ball. The elbow dropping below the shoulder turns the throw sidearm whether you meant it or not, and sidearm balls sail and tail.

Light noteShorten the backswing before you change anything else. Most "slow release" problems are really "long path" problems.
Coach noteOne size does not fit all here: arm slots vary among excellent throwers, and a natural three-quarter slot that repeats is worth more than a textbook over-the-top slot that doesn't. What doesn't vary is the sequence — elbow leads, hand last, ball on a short path. Judge an arm path by whether it's compact and repeatable, not by whether it matches a photograph.
5 Release point — where the ball leaves decides where it goes What works Tap to open

What it does

The ball leaves your hand out in front of your body, at roughly full comfortable reach, with your index finger the last thing touching it. The direction your fingers point at that instant is the direction the ball starts. Release a fraction early and the ball sails high; a fraction late and it dives into the dirt. Same throw, same arm — the release instant is the steering wheel.

When it's off

Releasing beside your ear (too early in the arc, arm still bent) sends balls high and behind receivers. Holding on too long drives balls into the ground in front of the target. Cutting across the ball with the fingers makes the nose wobble off line — a spiral that spins but drifts.

Light noteHigh balls and dirt balls from the same thrower in the same session usually mean one thing: the release point is wandering. Consistency first, aim second.
Coach noteThis is why two throws that "feel identical" can land a metre apart. The arm moves fast enough that a few milliseconds change the launch angle by degrees, and degrees become metres downfield. You can't consciously time milliseconds — but a compact, repeatable chain makes the timing land in the same place by itself. Repeatability is the skill; aim is its side effect.
6 Follow-through — the receipt for the whole throw What works Tap to open

What it does

After release, the throwing arm continues across your body, thumb turning down toward your opposite hip pocket, and your back foot swings through as your weight settles over the front leg. The follow-through doesn't touch the ball — it's already gone — but it proves the chain fired in order and lets the arm slow down gradually instead of braking hard at the shoulder.

When it's off

Stopping dead at release usually means you were steering the ball — decelerating before it left, which steals spin and distance. Falling off to the side points back to a base or separation problem upstream. A follow-through that looks different every throw is a free diagnostic: something earlier in the chain is improvising.

Light noteFilm one throw a week from the side and compare follow-throughs. Same finish, throw after throw, is what "good mechanics" actually looks like.
Coach noteDon't coach the follow-through directly — coach what causes it. It's an effect, not a lever. If a thrower's finish is short and stabby, the fix lives in the step, the separation, or a rushed arm path, and drilling the finish itself just teaches posing after bad throws.

Spiral science, in plain words

Why does a spinning ball fly straight while a wobbling one flutters and dies? The honest answer is one idea: gyroscopic stability.

A football is pointed at both ends, which makes it a poor flyer on its own — left alone, it wants to tumble. When you spin it around its long axis (the line running tip to tip), the spin gives it gyroscopic stability: a spinning object resists being knocked off its axis, the same reason a bicycle wheel or a spinning top stays upright. Air pushing on the ball in flight constantly tries to tip the nose up, down, or sideways; the spin keeps answering "no," so the nose stays pointed along the flight path.

Two honest consequences. First, a tight spiral isn't about looks — a stable nose means the ball presents its smallest face to the air the whole way, so it keeps its speed and holds its line. A wobbling ball shows its fat side to the wind, slows fast, and drifts — the classic "duck." Second, spin can't fix direction: it stabilises the ball around whatever line you released it on. A perfect spiral thrown at the wrong spot is a perfect spiral to the wrong spot. Spin is stability, not steering.

Coach noteOne refinement coaches leave out: on a long throw the nose should gently tip over and follow the arc on the way down, and a good spiral does this on its own — the spin axis stays steady while gravity bends the flight path, and aerodynamic pressure gradually walks the nose around to match. When a deep ball "noses over" and drops in, that's the physics working. When it sails flat and lands on its tail, the release was too high or the spin too weak to let it turn over.

The wider game, in brief

The throw lives inside a simple structure. If you're new to the game, this is the whole frame in one screen.

Downs

The offence gets four tries (downs) to move the ball 10 yards. Make it, and the count resets. Fail on fourth down and the other team takes over. Almost every decision in the sport — including when a quarterback throws short — is this arithmetic wearing pads.

Scoring

A touchdown (ball carried or caught in the end zone) is worth 6, with a short bonus try after. A field goal kicked through the posts is 3. A safety — tackling the ball-carrier in his own end zone — is 2 to the defence.

The quarterback's job

Take the snap, read the defence in about two to three seconds, and deliver the ball to the right teammate — or run, or throw it away safely. The throw on this page is the last half-second of a job that is mostly thinking.

Distance and accuracy, read honestly

What actually adds yards, what actually adds completions — and the most underrated skill in the position: anticipation.

What adds distance What works

  • The chain, in order. Legs and trunk are the big engines; a sequenced throw outthrows a muscled one with less effort.
  • Timing of the step. Front foot planted as the hips fire — the ground's force arrives while the arm is still loading.
  • A clean spiral. A stable ball keeps the speed you gave it; a duck spends it fighting the air.
  • Launch angle. Deep balls need height to travel — roughly a high-30s-to-mid-40s degree arc in plain terms: up enough to fly, flat enough to arrive on time.

What really hasn't "All arm." Trying to throw far by swinging the arm harder desyncs the chain, drags the release point around, and usually costs both distance and accuracy. It also loads the shoulder and elbow with work the legs were built for.

What adds accuracy What works

  • A repeatable release point — built by a compact chain, not by aiming harder.
  • Feet set before the throw whenever the play allows it. Accuracy starts at the base.
  • Throwing to a spot, with anticipation — see below.
  • Knowing your own miss. Every thrower has a habitual miss (high, low, behind). Track yours in the practice record; a known miss is a fixable miss.

What to try Target smaller than the receiver. Some throwers aim at the receiver's far shoulder or a shirt number rather than "the receiver." A smaller aim point tightens groups for some people. Test it over a few sessions and let your hit rate vote.

Anticipation: throwing to where the receiver will be

A football takes real time to arrive — around a second to reach a receiver 20 yards away. In that second the receiver moves several yards. So a quarterback almost never throws at a receiver; he throws to the place the receiver will be when the ball gets there, and often releases the ball before the receiver has even turned or made his cut. That early release is anticipation, and it's why good quarterbacks look like they're throwing to empty grass that a teammate runs into.

The trade-off is honest: anticipation throws are thrown on trust and timing, before the picture is certain. Thrown on time, they beat defences that are watching the receiver. Thrown when the read was wrong, they can arrive exactly where a defender is standing. The trainer at the bottom of this page lets you feel the timing window yourself.

Light noteRule of thumb for practice: if your receiver has to wait for the ball, you were late. If he has to slow down, you were early or under-led. On time, he catches it in stride without breaking step.

The contested corner

Two popular training ideas with real arguments on both sides. Both shelves are printed at the same size, as promised — neither is the "real" answer hiding under the other.

Long-toss: throwing far to throw far

The case for

  • Throwing at gradually increasing distances forces the whole chain to organise itself — the body solves sequencing problems on its own when the target is far.
  • Many throwers report real gains in arm strength and in how "easy" normal distances feel afterward.
  • It's simple, needs no equipment, and the intent — throw with full, athletic effort — carries back to game throws.

The case against

  • Long distances change the throw itself: a higher arc and a different release than a game throw, so some of what you practise isn't the motion you're training for.
  • Max-effort throwing is max stress on the shoulder and elbow. Volume that one arm handles can be the volume that breaks another down — there is no universal safe dose.
  • Dressed up as universally safe and necessary, it oversells. Throwers with sore arms have been hurt by programs that treated distance as a virtue in itself.

Weighted-ball programs

The case for

  • Throwing heavier and lighter balls under supervision is a real training method with studies behind it in baseball, showing velocity gains for some athletes.
  • Overload and underload work is a legitimate strength principle: vary the load, and the body adapts.
  • Structured programs include warm-up, workload limits, and rest — the structure itself protects people who otherwise just throw until tired.

The case against

  • The evidence base is mostly baseball, not football — a football is bigger, gripped differently, and thrown with a different arm path, so the transfer is assumed, not proven.
  • Heavier balls raise stress on the elbow and shoulder on every throw. Injury reports around aggressive weighted-ball work are part of the record, not a footnote.
  • Sold as a shortcut — "add velocity in six weeks" — it skips the boring truth that sequencing and strength built gradually are where most velocity actually comes from.
Coach noteThe honest middle: both methods are tools with a dose. If you try either, the safeguards are the program — gradual build-up, full warm-up, strict throw counts, rest days, and a hard stop at pain (see Arm Care). A thrower whose arm hurts is not "almost through the program"; he's done for the day.

Footwork: the clock the throw runs on

Drops exist to sync the quarterback with the routes. A route that breaks at a certain depth needs the ball out at a certain time — the drop is how the feet keep that appointment.

Drops, matched to routes What works

Quick game (1–3 steps): short, fast routes — slants, quick outs, hitches. The ball is out almost as the feet stop. Deeper drops (5 steps, or 3 from the shotgun): longer routes need longer to develop, so the drop buys time and ends with a short forward "hitch" step as the throw starts. The principle underneath every system: when the back foot plants, the ball should be ready to go. If your feet finish and the receiver isn't ready, the drop was too fast for the route; if the receiver is open and waiting while you're still dropping, it was too slow.

What really hasn't One drop for everything. Using a deep drop for a quick route leaves you throwing late against pressure; using a quick drop for a deep route leaves you waiting, flat-footed, while the rush arrives.

Moving in the pocket What works

The pocket moves, so you move — in small steps, staying in throwing position: climb forward when pressure comes from the edges, slide sideways when it comes up the middle, shoulders staying pointed downfield and two hands on the ball until you throw. The skill is moving without starting the throw over: feet reset, base rebuilt, then the chain fires as usual.

What really hasn't Drifting backward. Backing away from pressure feels safe and is the opposite: it lengthens every throw, shrinks your base, and walks you into the deepest rushers. Almost every back-foot floater starts as a backward drift.

Throwing on the run What works

Rollouts and scrambles are real throws, with rules: get your shoulders turned back toward the target line before you throw, keep the ball up, and throw off the foot that lets your hips open to the target. Throwing toward your running direction is far easier than throwing back across your body — the across-body throw is the one that floats and gets intercepted.

Off-platform throws — and their honest price What to try

Off-platform means throwing without the base: back foot, mid-fall, sidearm on the move. Highlights make these look free. They are not. Without the ground and the chain, the arm does nearly all the work, so distance drops, the release point wanders, and accuracy is the first thing spent. Some elite throwers have grooved a repeatable off-platform throw through years of reps — that's a built skill, not a shortcut. The honest use: a tool for broken plays a few times a game, practised deliberately at low volume — never your default delivery, and never the fix for a base problem.

What really hasn't The sidearm fix for everybody. Dropping the arm slot is sometimes sold as the cure for sailing balls or a slow release. For a thrower whose natural slot is higher, forcing sidearm breaks a working chain to chase somebody else's. Slots are individual; sequence is universal.

Reads and strategy: the thinking game

The throw takes half a second. Choosing it takes the whole play. Coverage in plain words first, then how a quarterback reads it before and after the snap.

Man vs zone — the one distinction under everything

Man coverage: each defender is responsible for a specific receiver and follows him wherever he goes. Beat your man, and you're open. Zone coverage: each defender guards an area of the field and passes receivers between areas. Nobody "has" you — you're open by being in the gap between two areas, or by two receivers overloading one area. Everything below is a variation on these two ideas.

Light noteThe pre-snap tell: send a receiver in motion. If a defender runs with him across the formation, it's likely man. If the defence just shifts and points, it's likely zone.

Progressions and the checkdown What works

A progression is a planned order: first read, second read, third read — decided before the snap from the play design, then confirmed against what the defence actually does. You don't scan the whole field hoping; you check answers in order, on a clock set by the pass rush. When every read is covered, the checkdown — a short, safe throw to a back or tight end near the line — turns a broken play into a small gain instead of a sack or a forced interception. Checking down isn't giving up; it's the discipline that keeps drives alive.

What really hasn't Staring down the first read. Defenders read the quarterback's eyes. Lock onto one receiver from the snap and you've told the defence where the ball is going — the "open" receiver closes fast.

Pre-snap read: the picture before the ball moves

Before the snap you count and locate: How many safeties deep, and where? (One deep middle hints Cover 1 or 3; two deep hints Cover 2 or a split-safety zone.) How are the corners standing — tight on receivers (man, or a press zone) or backed off (zone)? Where's the likely blitz coming from? You're not solving the defence; you're narrowing it to two or three possibilities and picking the progression that answers all of them.

Post-snap read: confirm or throw the picture away

Defences disguise. In the first second after the snap, one defender tells the truth: coaches call him the key — often a safety or a specific linebacker. If the deep safety spins down toward the line, the shell you guessed is wrong; if he bails deep, it's confirmed. The read is a question asked before the snap and answered after it. The mistake isn't guessing wrong pre-snap — everyone does — it's refusing to update when the key defender shows you the real coverage.

Coach noteThe trade-off at the heart of quarterbacking: time versus certainty. Wait longer and the picture gets clearer — and the rush gets closer and the windows get smaller. Every great read is a decision made at the moment the information was good enough, not complete. That's also why anticipation (throwing before the break) and reads belong in the same lesson: the throw often has to leave on the confirmed guess, not the finished picture.

Faults and fixes

Four classic bad balls. Each is a story the chain is telling — what broke, the fix, and a drill that installs it. Tap one to open it.

The nose-down duck — wobbles and dies Tap to open

The story

The ball leaves tumbling or with its nose dropping, flutters, and falls short. Two usual causes, both at the ends of the chain: the grip (ball palmed, fingers off the laces, so no spin is applied) or the finish (fingers sliding off the side of the ball instead of pulling over the top, so the little spin you made points the wrong way). A duck is a spin problem, and spin is made in the last ten centimetres of the throw.

The fix

Reset the grip first — fingers on the laces, pocket under the palm. Then exaggerate the finish for a few sessions: thumb to opposite pocket, index finger pointing at the target as the ball leaves. If the duck survives both, film from behind: a hand coming off the side of the ball is visible in one clip.

The drill

Spin reps: stand 8–10 yards from a partner. Throw easy, perfect spirals — no distance, no speed — twenty throws, counting only balls that arrive spinning true. Ugly ones don't count toward the twenty.

The sailing ball — everything's high Tap to open

The story

Balls keep climbing over receivers' hands. Sailing is a release-point fault with three common drivers: releasing early in the arc (arm still bent, ball beside the ear), overstriding so the hips stall and the arm drags up and over, or leaning back — weight stuck on the back foot launches the ball like a catapult tilted skyward. High balls over the middle are also the most dangerous balls in the sport: a receiver reaching up, exposed, with a defender arriving.

The fix

Shorten the step, get the weight genuinely onto the front foot, and let the release happen out in front at full reach. One cue that works for many: finish with your chest over your front knee. If your chest is behind your hips at the finish, you leaned — and the ball sailed.

The drill

Rope line: string a rope or hold a pool noodle at the receiver's chest height, 15 yards out. Ten throws that must arrive under the rope and on target. The rope makes "high" visible instead of arguable.

The late ball — right receiver, wrong second Tap to open

The story

The receiver was open — a second ago. Late balls come in two kinds that need different fixes. Mechanically late: the read was on time but the ball wasn't — a long arm path, a slow drop, or a double-clutch (starting the throw, stopping, restarting) ate the window. Decision late: the mechanics were fine but you confirmed the read a beat too long, or threw to the receiver instead of the spot, so he had to wait. Behind-the-receiver balls are the signature: he ran to the spot; the ball arrived at where he used to be.

The fix

For mechanical lateness: shorten the path, sync the drop to the route (see Footwork). For decision lateness: throw on the break — start the throw as the receiver plants to cut, trusting the route — and use the anticipation trainer below to calibrate how early "on time" really feels.

The drill

Break throws: a partner runs a simple out route. Your job: the ball leaves your hand as his outside foot plants to break — not after you see him open. Film three; count how many arrived as he turned, not after.

The back-foot floater — no base, no zip Tap to open

The story

Pressure comes, you drift backward, and the throw leaves off your back foot with your weight retreating. The chain never starts — no ground force, no separation — so the arm shoves a ball that floats, hangs, and invites interceptions. This is the fault pressure is designed to cause.

The fix

Move within the pocket instead of out the back of it: climb forward or slide sideways, reset the base, then throw (see Footwork). And learn the honest alternative: sometimes the right play is to throw the ball away — out of bounds, nobody's ball — and take the next down. A throwaway is a completed decision, not a failed throw.

The drill

Climb and fire: a coach or partner points left, right, or forward as you drop; you move that way, reset, and throw to a target. Ten reps. The skill being built is the reset — the throw at the end is just the receipt.

Drills, with honest transfer notes

A drill is a tool with a range. Each one below says what it builds — and what it does not build, because transfer is where practice plans lie to themselves.

One-knee chain drill

Kneel on your throwing-side knee, partner 10–12 yards away, and throw using only separation and the arm path — no legs available. Builds the feel of hips-then-shoulders and a compact path.

Transfer note: teaches sequence feel, not game throwing. Always finish a session standing and throwing full reps, or you'll groove a throw your legs aren't part of.

Target ladder

A target (a tire, a net pocket, a partner's chest) at 10, then 15, then 20 yards. Five throws per distance; move back only when you hit at least four of five.

Transfer note: a still target teaches release consistency, not anticipation. Graduate to a walking, then running, partner — the ladder's job is the release point, and it does that job well.

Drop-and-throw on the route's clock

No receiver: take your 3-step and 5-step drops and throw to a marked spot at the moment the route would break. A partner with a stopwatch — or your filmed reps — checks that the ball leaves as the back foot plants.

Transfer note: high transfer for timing, none for reads. It builds the clock your feet run on; decision-making needs live receivers and defenders.

Partner route timing

The real thing in miniature: a partner runs outs, curls, and go routes at three-quarter speed, then full speed. You throw on the break, not after it. Track completions in stride — those are the on-time balls.

Transfer note: the highest-transfer drill on this page, and the one that needs a second person. Most of quarterbacking is timing with moving humans; this is the only drill here that trains exactly that.

Film yourself — the real check

This page cannot see your throw. Neither can any page. The honest checks are a camera and, when you can get one, a real coach's eye. Three angles cover nearly everything in the chain:

  • From the side (camera on your throwing-arm side, at hip height): arm path, release point, stride length, whether you lean back.
  • From behind, toward the target: the ball's line and spin — a true spiral versus a wobble is unmistakable from here — plus whether your shoulders stay pointed at the target.
  • From the front, three-quarter view: feet and base — width, the short step, weight transfer, the follow-through finishing across the body.

Film five throws, not fifty. Watch them at half speed. Pick one fault — the earliest link in the chain that's off — and work only that until the film changes.

Arm care: workload and pain rules

Your arm is the one piece of equipment you can't replace. These are workload rules, not medical advice — this page never diagnoses anything.

The pain rule, at full size

Pain means stop — and get checked. Sharp pain, pain that changes how you throw, numbness or tingling, weakness, or soreness that lingers for days: stop throwing and see a qualified professional — a doctor, a physiotherapist, an athletic trainer. No drill, program, or page is worth throwing through an injury to finish. "Push through it" is how a sore week becomes a lost season.

Workload, honestly What works

  • Build gradually. More throws, longer distances, and max effort are each a load — add one at a time, week by week, not all on one Saturday.
  • Count your throws. Quarterbacks and coaches who last use a pitch-count mindset: know roughly how many full-effort throws you made today. The trainer below counts for the same reason.
  • Warm up in stages: short and easy, then longer, then full — and cool down the same way in reverse.
  • Rest days are training. Adaptation happens between sessions, not during them. A tired arm throws with borrowed mechanics, and borrowed mechanics are how faults — and injuries — get practised in.

Fatigue is a mechanics problem What works

Watch a tired thrower and you'll see the chain fray in order: the step shortens, separation shrinks, the elbow drops, the release wanders. That's not laziness; it's a system running out of fuel. The lesson cuts two ways — in training, stop the session while the mechanics are still yours to keep; and in the trainer below, the optional tired-arm mode lets you feel how much a wandering release costs, safely, on a screen instead of with your real arm.

Light noteThe throw almost always needs to leave before the break. If you're waiting to see him open, you're already late — that's the whole lesson, and your hands will learn it faster than your eyes.
Coach noteWhy the window is where it is: on the Out and the Curl, the receiver is most open in the first moment after his break, before the defender recovers and before he runs out of field. Ball flight eats most of that moment — so the release has to come during his stem, on trust. The Go route grades differently: there the sin is underthrowing (he has to slow down and let the defender back in) or out-throwing the field. And the session throw counter is deliberate: at 30 throws this page will say what a coach would say — in real practice, this is where somebody starts watching your arm.
