The Ruler's Test — Thongloun Sisoulith, General Secretary of the LPRP and President of Laos

Subject: Thongloun Sisoulith — General Secretary of the Lao People's Revolutionary Party (LPRP) since 15 January 2021 and President of the Lao People's Democratic Republic since 22 March 2021; re-elected General Secretary at the 12th Party Congress on 8 January 2026 and re-elected President by the new National Assembly in March 2026. The seat is graded as it is actually held, and the structure is stated plainly: Laos is a one-party state whose constitution designates the LPRP as the leading core of the political system, and real authority sits with the party's General Secretary, who also holds the presidency. The prime minister administers the government under the Politburo's direction. That premiership has changed hands twice inside the grading window — Phankham Viphavanh (to 30 December 2022), Sonexay Siphandone (30 December 2022 – 5 October 2026), and Saleumxay Kommasith (since 5 October 2026, elevated after National Assembly President Xaysomphone Phomvihane died on 8 August 2026 and Sonexay moved to the Assembly presidency) — while the General Secretary has not changed at all, which is itself the clearest evidence of where the seat sits. Thongloun, born 10 November 1945, previously served as prime minister (April 2016 – March 2021); his premiership is cited as context where the record he now presides over began on his own watch, notably the July 2018 Attapeu dam collapse and his first anti-corruption drive. He is graded on January 2021 – November 2026. A caution on the file itself: Laos has no independent domestic media, foreign reporting is largely exile- and border-based, and several of the gravest items below rest on UN submissions and advocacy documentation that the Lao government disputes or does not answer. Where a grade rests on absence — an investigation never documented, a region never opened — the dossier says so. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The inspection machinery produces real numbers against real officials, published in the state's own forum. The State Inspection Authority's report to the National Assembly (14 November 2025) records 334 individuals implicated in corruption cases between 2021 and 2025 — 213 of them state officials — with losses exceeding LAK 642.93 billion (~USD 30 million) plus USD 66.8 million in foreign-currency losses; 86 individuals were submitted for prosecution, 23 sentenced, 207 given administrative discipline, and LAK 66.4 billion and USD 7.29 million recovered (SIA report via Laotian Times, 14 November 2025). A single provincial audit in Luang Namtha implicated 184 officials, sanctioned 155 administratively, and prosecuted 23, six of them central-level officials (KPL, October 2026).
  • The outcome measure has moved, measurably, the right way: Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index scored Laos 28/100 in 2023, 33 in 2024, and 34 in 2025 (109th of 182) — the highest scores in Laos's CPI history, achieved during this tenure (Transparency International, CPI 2023–2025).
  • The anti-corruption posture is the seat-holder's own stated program, renewed at the top of his second term: addressing the 12th National Organization Work Conference on 18 June 2026, Thongloun called for personnel selection "based on integrity, capability, and demonstrated performance rather than personal connections or patronage," with officials who fail to perform to "make way" under a principle of "entry and exit, promotion and demotion" (KPL, 18 June 2026). In 2026 the leadership of the whole enforcement chain was rotated — a Politburo member, Khamphanh Phommathat, became justice minister; the former justice minister became president of the Supreme People's Court; a new chief prosecutor and a new State Inspection Authority president were installed — and state media and social media carried an announced root-level anti-corruption drive into the autumn of 2026 (Lao Times analysis, 2026; KPL, 2026).
  • Law enforcement did eventually move, at scale, inside the one place Lao law had not run: the Golden Triangle Special Economic Zone. In August 2024 Lao and Chinese police raided the zone's scam compounds, detaining more than 2,000 people and deporting most; Lao officials stated in February 2026 that nearly 2,800 suspects of 27 nationalities had been arrested in the zone since 2023, and joint handovers of fraud suspects to China continued through November 2026 (International IDEA democracy tracker, August 2024; Laotian Times, 6 February 2026; Lao Security News via Chiang Rai Times, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The law's referee is the law's subject. The State Inspection Authority is a party-state organ; its presidents in this period (Khamphanh Phommathat, then Vanxay Phongsavanh) are Politburo members inspecting the party they sit atop. Lao public reaction to the 2026 campaign, documented in social-media monitoring, pairs hope with the specific skepticism "that the Party audits itself" and fear of "selective enforcement against low-level staff while elites escape" (Facebook post monitoring, October 2026). No prosecution of a Politburo member, a minister's family network, or a member of the ruling families appears anywhere in the documented record of this tenure; the prosecuted are provincial, district, and enterprise officials.
  • The seat itself circulates inside a small set of families, on the record: Prime Minister Sonexay Siphandone (2022–2026) is the son of former president Khamtai Siphandone; the National Assembly presidency was held until his death by Xaysomphone Phomvihane, son of the party's founding leader Kaysone Phomvihane. Heredity is not a crime and is recorded here only because Test 1 grades the source: a system in which the top offices pass among the founding families while the inspection authority reports to those families' party is not one law at the source.
  • The Golden Triangle SEZ is the documented exception that ran for a decade and a half. The zone's operator, the Kings Romans group of Zhao Wei, has been under US Treasury sanctions since 30 January 2018 for drug trafficking, human trafficking, money laundering, and bribery; the zone functioned with its own security and immigration arrangements as, in International IDEA's words, a "zone of exception" for scam operations, trafficking, and smuggling. The US State Department's 2025 Trafficking in Persons report — which downgraded Laos to Tier 3, the lowest rank — records that the government "did not investigate, prosecute, or convict any traffickers connected to Laos's SEZs," identified no trafficking victims exploited in SEZs, and reported no investigations of officials for complicity, with "endemic corruption and official complicity" named as significant concerns (US Department of State, TIP Report 2025). The 2024 crackdown followed sustained pressure from China, whose citizens were the scams' main victims; the sanctioned operator was not prosecuted in Laos, and reporting through 2026 records the industry fragmenting and relocating rather than ending (Shan Herald reporting, 2026). Enforcement arrived; it arrived late, from outside pressure, and it stopped below the zone's owner.
  • One law does not reach the security services at all. Fourteen years after Sombath Somphone's disappearance (Test 3), no official has been charged; the UN Human Rights Committee's 2024 follow-up evaluation records the government's position that its task force "is still actively investigating" while listing a dozen further unresolved disappearance cases, including Hmong community members (UN Human Rights Committee, CCPR/C/141/2/Add.4, August 2024). The same penal code that sentences officials for theft sentences citizens for speech: Article 117 (propaganda against the state) carried a five-year sentence for a citizen journalist whose offence was criticizing flood relief (Test 2). Theft by an official and criticism by a citizen are both prosecuted; disappearance by persons unknown at a police checkpoint is prosecuted against no one.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The formal architecture contains the standard guarantees, recorded because the tests grade the record and not the rhetoric alone: the constitution proclaims judicial independence and freedoms of speech and press, and Laos engages the UN human-rights machinery — it underwent its fourth Universal Periodic Review in April–May 2025 and accepted 189 recommendations at the outcome's adoption (UN Human Rights Council, 26 September 2025).
  • The National Assembly is not wholly silent inside the system: it received 731 public petitions and complaints in the first half of 2025 alone (National Assembly reporting, June 2025), its sessions have publicly questioned ministers over inflation and prices, and it was the Assembly — on the prime minister's proposal — that removed a central bank governor mid-crisis (below). These are movements inside a party-led legislature, recorded at that size.
  • The central bank publishes its data openly, including the numbers that embarrass the government: the Bank of the Lao PDR posts its reference rate (22,422 kip to the dollar, 17 November 2026) and monthly inflation (7.7% in October 2026) on its public site (Bank of the Lao PDR, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The grip is constitutional and total in design. The constitution designates the LPRP — the only legal party — as the leading core of the political system; the judiciary, prosecution, and inspection organs are party-led; and the revised constitution adopted in 2025 retained that structure while the party simultaneously ran an administrative streamlining that, per the Lao Times' analysis, is "a deliberate attempt to reassert party control from Vientiane" and claw back authority from the provinces (Lao Times, 2026). No institution in the state is constituted outside the party the General Secretary heads.
  • Elections choose among the pre-approved. In the 22 February 2026 National Assembly election, 243 candidates — all nominated through the party-led Lao Front process — contested 175 seats; the LPRP took 169 and party-vetted independents 6, on officially reported turnout of 97.81%; International IDEA records the vote as held "in a closed autocratic system" and "not considered free or fair" (Wikipedia results compilation from official announcements, March 2026; International IDEA democracy tracker, February 2026). The Assembly then elected the prime minister in October 2026 by a unanimous 158 of 158 votes cast (KPL, 5 October 2026). An electoral body and a legislature that return unanimity are not referees; they are the scoreboard of a decision taken elsewhere.
  • The central bank's autonomy is answered by its personnel file. Governor Bounleua Sinxayvoravong was removed by the National Assembly on 2 July 2024 — on Prime Minister Sonexay's proposal, mid-crisis — and transferred to a provincial post; his deputy served as acting governor, and in the June 2025 reshuffle the governorship went to Bounkham Vorachit, a Party Central Committee member moved directly from the environment ministry (Central Banking, July 2024; KPL reshuffle announcements, June 2025). Whatever the merits of each decision, a central bank whose governor is replaced by political transfer when the currency falls is an arm of the government, not a check on it.
  • Media autonomy does not exist in any documented form. All six mapped Lao media organizations are classified state-controlled; RSF records that all press must follow the party line set by the propaganda authorities, that foreign media have been tolerated since 2016 only under prior censorship, and that the penal code imprisons journalists who criticize the government — a provision extended to internet users in 2014 (State Media Monitor, 2026; RSF country data). Laos ranked 150th of 180 in RSF's 2025 World Press Freedom Index and fell to 154th in 2026 (RSF, 2025–2026). CIVICUS rates Lao civic space "closed."
  • Independence of expression is punished on a continuing record, not a historical one. Citizen journalist Houayheuang "Muay" Xayabouly served a full five-year sentence (2019–2024) under Article 117 for Facebook criticism of flood relief; the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention ruled her detention arbitrary in 2021 and called for immediate release, which did not occur; after her release on 12 September 2024 she remained under monitoring and posted what monitors describe as a forced apology video (Amnesty International UPR submission, October 2024; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). In September 2024 two social-media creators in Bokeo province were arrested for a video mocking local road conditions, put through a "re-education" class, and made to confess and apologize publicly before release (EU delegation reporting via KINU human-rights compilation, 2024). Activist Anousa "Jack" Luangsuphom was shot and wounded in a Vientiane café on 29 April 2023; no arrest in that case is documented, and the impunity is recorded by CIVICUS as continuing into 2026.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The economic crisis — the largest wobble of the tenure — was answered with personnel consequences at the top, twice. Prime Minister Phankham Viphavanh left office on 30 December 2022, officially for health and personal reasons, as inflation ran toward its 41% peak; his successor was approved the same day (National Assembly record, December 2022). The central bank governor followed in July 2024 (Test 2). A further nine-to-twelve-official reshuffle came in June 2025, and the premiership itself changed again in October 2026. The system's answers are internal and unexplained, but the record shows the crisis costing senior people their seats — adjustment of a kind, at the level the system permits.
  • Policy adjusted with the personnel, and the headline numbers moved: after the kip's collapse (a 31% depreciation in 2023 alone, per the World Bank) and inflation's peak of 41% in early 2023, the government abolished private exchange shops, channelled foreign-currency earnings through the banking system, tightened money supply, and serviced and restructured debt — bringing inflation down to 7.7% by October 2026 and official public debt down from a peak of 112% of GDP in 2022 to around 88% in 2025 (Asian Survey, "Laos in 2024"; Bank of the Lao PDR, November 2026; official figures via Lao Times, 2026). The IMF continued to classify Laos as in debt distress through its 2024 Article IV — the adjustment is real and incomplete, recorded as both.
  • External referees were admitted further than before. Laos accepted 189 recommendations at its 2025 UPR, including all 11 recommendations to ratify the enforced-disappearance convention (FIDH statement at adoption, 26 September 2025), and it admitted the UN Special Rapporteur on cultural rights for a country visit in November 2024 — access granted to a mandate-holder whose February 2026 report then documented "strict restrictions on speech, assembly and association" and "harsh reprisals" (UN Special Rapporteur on cultural rights, report to the Human Rights Council, February 2026).
  • Partner pressure on the scam zones produced the August 2024 crackdown (Test 1) — an adjustment, however belated and externally driven, in which the state raided a zone it had licensed and exempted for sixteen years.

Evidence against:

  • The founding denial of the era has never been corrected. Sombath Somphone, the country's best-known civil-society leader, was stopped at a police checkpoint in Vientiane on 15 December 2012 and taken away by unidentified men on CCTV; he has not been seen since. The disappearance predates Thongloun's party leadership but ran through his premiership and now his presidency: the government's answer across fourteen years has been a task force that reports no new evidence, and at the 2025 UPR the government declined the recommendation to investigate his case specifically — a refusal FIDH singled out at the adoption as "particularly disturbing" (UN Human Rights Committee follow-up, August 2024; FIDH, 26 September 2025). A state that will not answer its most documented question has set its answer to every harder one.
  • Pushback from the street and the screen is answered with prosecution and worse. The Muay case (Test 2): a UN body ruled the detention arbitrary; the sentence was served to the last day. The Jack Luangsuphom shooting (April 2023) has produced no documented arrest. Exiled activist Joseph Akaravong was stabbed in France on 14 June 2025; French police arrested four suspects — attribution of the attack to any sponsor is an ALLEGATION, unproven, recorded here because the Lao government's documented response was none (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025; ASEAN Frontier reporting, 2025).
  • The newest wobble was answered with silence on the record assembled here. Sixay Lueangmonetha, an outspoken online critic known as Bao Mo Khaen, was reported taken by soldiers on 14 February 2026 and held incommunicado at Phonthan Prison on witness accounts; his body was found in a forest in Hadxayfong district on 22 February 2026 — election day. The witness accounts are ALLEGATIONs, unverified and unanswered; no official investigation or statement appears in the documented record available to this dossier (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026; HRW reporting via CIVICUS). The dossier records the absence as the answer given.
  • Expert pushback on the flagship development model was heard and overridden. UNESCO's World Heritage Centre and ICOMOS recommended in April 2022 that the Luang Prabang dam be reconsidered and relocated away from the World Heritage city; construction continued (Manushya Foundation submission to the UN Special Rapporteur, April 2025). On the Hmong file (Test 4), the government's answer at the 2025 UPR to recommendations on the ChaoFa Hmong was to deny the group's existence — the maximal form of non-adjustment available to it (UNPO statement at the HRC, September 2025).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — ethnic minorities, the displaced, religious believers, the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The long-run poverty count is real and independently compiled: the World Bank's Poverty & Equity Brief (October 2026) records the national poverty rate falling from 27.6% in 2007 to 15.0% in 2024, poverty at the $4.20 lower-middle-income line falling from 58.5% to 22.5% over the same period, and the Gini index down from its 2018 peak of 39 to 35 in 2024 — driven, the Bank notes, by farm income and remittances (World Bank, October 2026).
  • The development milestone the state set itself was met on criteria that include the flock's condition, not only its income: Laos graduated from UN Least Developed Country status on 24 November 2026, having met all three thresholds — GNI per capita (US$2,375 on the CDP's monitoring figure), the Human Assets Index, and the Economic and Environmental Vulnerability Index — in consecutive reviews (UN Committee for Development Policy monitoring; UN General Assembly Resolution 76/8; UN LDC portal, November 2026).
  • Representation widened inside the system's own chamber: women's share of the National Assembly rose from 22.0% to 29.7% in the February 2026 election (International IDEA, February 2026). The state formally counts 50 ethnic groups within one Lao national community, and its UNGA address in September 2026 recommitted to UXO clearance — the war legacy that still kills and maims rural Lao — as a national development goal (UNGA general debate, 26 September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • One people is counted out by definition. The government denies that Laos has any indigenous peoples, classifying the Hmong among 50 ethnic groups, and at the 2025 UPR it stated that the ChaoFa Hmong have "no presence or recognized existence within the sovereign territory of the Lao PDR" — while declining recommendations to grant UN access to Xaysomboun province and to protect Hmong rights (UNPO/CWHP statements at EMRIP, 17 July 2025, and at the Human Rights Council's 60th session, September 2025). A population the state declares nonexistent cannot appear in its accounting at all.
  • Against that denial stands a documented, contested file. Submissions by UNPO, the Congress of World Hmong People, and Boat People SOS to UN Special Procedures and to the CESCR in August 2025 document alleged May 2025 crackdowns in Bokeo and Xaysomboun provinces — mass arrests, enforced disappearances, military lockdowns of entire villages, and alleged extrajudicial executions — alongside long-documented patterns: forced resettlement to military-controlled villages, uncompensated land confiscation, and the unresolved 2020 disappearance of two Hmong children, a young woman, and their 80-year-old grandfather, raised by the UN Working Group on Enforced Disappearances and never answered (UNPO/CWHP/BPSOS submissions, August 2025; UNPO UPR pre-session statement, February 2025). These are ALLEGATIONs in form — grave, detailed, and unrefuted by any Lao accounting, in a region the state keeps closed to the observers who could settle them. Genocide Watch assessed the Hmong in Laos in 2025 as at critical stages of persecution; the government disputes the premise wholesale. The contest is recorded; the closure that prevents its resolution is the state's own act.
  • The displaced are counted at the price the state sets. The Pak Beng dam will relocate 923 families — about 4,700 people across 26 villages — and affected villagers told reporters the compensation offered was too low (RFA reporting, March 2023, via CIVICUS Monitor). The Luang Prabang dam displaces about 531 families 25 km above a World Heritage city, with villagers in Oudomxay demanding five times the offered compensation and objecting to resettlement sites (Manushya Foundation, April 2025; RFA, February 2023). Consultation on mainstream Mekong dams proceeds without free, prior, and informed consent, with project information barriers documented by International Rivers' submission to the UN Special Rapporteur (May 2025). Behind this tenure stands the file from his premiership: the 23 July 2018 Xe-Pian Xe-Namnoy saddle-dam collapse in Attapeu, which on the government's own count left 71 people dead or missing and displaced thousands, with survivors' compensation and resettlement grievances documented for years afterward — and no senior official or executive held publicly accountable.
  • Religious minorities are counted as a permission, not a right. The state recognizes three churches; an estimated 75% of even the recognized Lao Evangelical Church's congregations lack permanent buildings and meet in homes, which makes their worship an illegal gathering (Open Doors World Watch List 2026). In December 2025 local authorities arrested ten Christians — eight of them teenagers — from a house fellowship in northern Laos and conditioned release on renouncing their faith; four complied, and six remained detained months later, with release then conditioned on a payment of 5 million kip each (Open Doors UK, 2026). Two pastors were killed in this era — Pastor Sy Sengmany in October 2022, his body found showing signs of torture, and Pastor Thongkham in 2024 — with no arrests documented in either case (CSW general briefing, September 2026; Open Doors, 2026). Village-level expulsions of Christian families by local authorities are documented as a recurring pattern (RFA via CIVICUS Monitor, 2023).
  • The crisis years were distributed downward. While the national poverty rate fell across two decades, the World Bank records that growth decelerated, urban poverty is now increasing, and double-digit inflation, currency depreciation, and stagnant wages "eroded household real purchasing power, especially in urban areas"; the ADB documented households reducing food consumption and meal frequency under food-price inflation (World Bank Poverty & Equity Brief, October 2026; ADB reporting, 2024). The adjustment that stabilized the kip (Test 3) was paid for disproportionately by wage-earners and the urban poor, and the labor market's own verdict is the documented exodus of young Lao workers to Thailand.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record since January 2021, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • LDC graduation — the flagship word, kept on the stated date. Graduation from Least Developed Country status was the central development pledge of the era, formalized when the UN General Assembly set the date in Resolution 76/8 (24 November 2021). Laos met all three criteria in consecutive triennial reviews and graduated on 24 November 2026 — days before this grading (UN LDC portal; UN OHRLLS statements, 2025–2026). The 10th Plan now targets upper-middle-income status by 2035 (2026 Round Table Implementation Meeting, 19 November 2026).
  • The growth target — kept, at the lowered bar he set. The 9th Five-Year Plan's growth target, revised down to 4% for the crisis years, was exceeded on the government's own final accounting: average GDP growth of 4.24% for 2021–2025 (Prime Minister's report to the 12th Party Congress, 6 January 2026). The revision itself is part of the record: the original ambition was higher, and the target was moved before it was met.
  • Macroeconomic stabilization — the crisis pledges, substantially delivered late. The commitments made to the National Assembly through the crisis — inflation down, currency stabilized, debt ratio falling — stand largely delivered by late 2026: inflation 41% (early 2023) to 7.7% (October 2026); official public debt from 112% of GDP (2022) to ~88% (2025); the IMF's "in debt distress" classification stands as the counterweight on the same shelf (Bank of the Lao PDR; IMF Article IV 2024; official figures via Lao Times).
  • Land-linked — in motion in steel. The strategy of converting Laos from land-locked to land-linked, restated at the 11th Congress, acquired its spine on schedule: the Laos–China railway opened in December 2021, in the first year of this tenure, and cross-border freight and passenger traffic have run since (project record; KPL).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or kept only in the letter):

  • The income promises inside the growth promise — missed by a quarter. The same final accounting that reports growth of 4.24% reports GDP per capita of USD 2,138 against a plan target of USD 2,880, and GNI per capita of USD 1,985 against USD 2,280 (Prime Minister's report to the 12th Party Congress, January 2026). The kip's collapse — from roughly 9,700 to the dollar in early 2021 to 22,422 in November 2026 (Bank of the Lao PDR rate history) — is the mechanism: the plan's targets were denominated in a currency that lost well over half its dollar value on this watch, and the miss was absorbed by households, not announced by the state.
  • The anti-corruption word — spoken since 2016, structurally unkept. Thongloun built his rise on an anti-corruption drive as prime minister from 2016; analysts assess that drive as having failed swiftly, and a decade on the CPI stands at 34/100 — improved, and still deep in the corrupt band — while the inspection system remains party-internal and the SEZ exception ran until 2024 (Lao Times analysis, 2026; Transparency International CPI 2025; US TIP Report 2025). The June 2026 renewal of the same pledges, in nearly the same words, is recorded as the current state of the promise: restated, not yet redeemed.
  • The disappearance-convention word — accepted four times, never performed. Laos signed the International Convention for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance in 2008 and accepted UPR recommendations to ratify it in 2010, 2015, 2020, and again in 2025 — accepting all 11 such recommendations in the latest cycle, with no timeframe. Seventeen years after signature, ratification has not occurred (FIDH statement at the UPR adoption, 26 September 2025; UN Human Rights Committee follow-up, August 2024). A word accepted in every cycle and performed in none is the series' clearest case of a promise kept as rhetoric.
  • The rights language of his own constitution — not kept. The constitutional guarantees of speech, press, and belief stand beside Article 117 prosecutions, a press ranked 154th of 180, and detention conditioned on renouncing a faith (Tests 2 and 4). The gap between the state's paper word and its practice is the oldest item in this dossier and the least changed.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

The Lao seat is held the way the Berdimuhamedow seat is held — by a structure, not a moment: a party that is constitutionally the state, headed since 2021 by a man who is simultaneously its General Secretary and the republic's president, re-elected to both in 2026 without a contested vote at any level, while three prime ministers have rotated beneath him. Under that roof the record is genuinely two-sided in the economic lane and one-sided everywhere else. The state inspected and prosecuted hundreds of its own officials, improved its corruption score to the best in its history, survived a debt-and-inflation crisis that cost a prime minister and a central bank governor their jobs, brought inflation from 41% to under 8%, and delivered — on the exact date promised to the United Nations — graduation from least-developed-country status, the era's flagship word, kept. Against that stands the rest of the accounting: a sanctioned criminal zone exempt from Lao law for sixteen years and raided only when Beijing pressed; an inspection authority that is the ruling party inspecting itself, which has never reached the ruling families or the security services; a journalist who served five years for criticizing flood relief while a UN body called her detention arbitrary; a civil-society leader fourteen years disappeared from a police checkpoint, uninvestigated; Christians released from detention on condition they renounce their faith; and a whole people, the ChaoFa Hmong, whose existence the state denied at the United Nations in the same year that detailed submissions documented military crackdowns against them in provinces closed to every observer who could settle the question. The wobble grade is mixed because the adjustments were real — in the economy, in personnel, in the slow opening to UN review. It is not higher because every pushback that touched power itself — a disappearance, a dialect of dissent, a people's name — was answered with denial, prosecution, or silence, and the last of those answers was given on election day 2026, over a critic's body in a forest outside the capital, with no investigation documented since. We do not pick who lands where they land. On the documented record, this seat manages competently, counts selectively, and grips completely — and its word is good exactly where keeping it does not loosen the grip.
