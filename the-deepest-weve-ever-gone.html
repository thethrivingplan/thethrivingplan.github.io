The Deepest We've Ever Gone

Every person who ever went to the bottom of the ocean came back — all of them. The machines' record stands beside theirs on this page, including the ones that stayed down.

The honest headline, up front: no secret or hidden dive footage turned up anywhere in the hunt behind this page. The genuinely hidden-away facts run the other way:

1. The most famous dive of all — Trieste, 1960 — produced no photograph or film of the bottom itself. The silt saw to that.

2. Kaikō filmed a plastic bag at 10,898 m in 1998 — the deepest human-made debris ever recorded — and that footage sat in JAMSTEC's archive for about 20 years before anyone catalogued it.

Trieste — the first people to the bottom

23 Jan 1960 · Challenger Deep, Mariana Trench (Western Pool) · Jacques Piccard & Lt. Don Walsh, US Navy (Project Nekton)

First humans to the bottom of the ocean. Both survived — as has every crewed descent on this page.

The depth of it — tap to open

What does NOT exist: no photograph or film of the bottom itself exists from this dive. On landing, Trieste stirred the silt — Walsh described it as like being in a bowl of milk, the viewport painted white — and they got no pictures of the bottom. The famous “flatfish” was seen on approach, never photographed, and is now thought likely to have been a sea cucumber.

The US National Archives (RG 428) holds Navy motion-picture material from the Nekton era, largely licence-gated or undigitised — archived, not secret. No item-level free streaming copy of bottom footage was found in this hunt — and per the silt account, bottom footage from the dive itself does not exist to be found.

Ocean — the uncrewed full-depth vehicles

The machines that went where no rescue could follow — and the two that never came home.

Kaikō — the deepest uncrewed dive on record

24 Mar 1995 · 10,911.4 m, Challenger Deep · JAMSTEC (Japan), ROV

The deepest uncrewed dive ever made, and the most accurate measurement of the Deep then taken — the first visit since Trieste. Lost at sea in 2003; it stayed down.

The depth of it — tap to open

Kaikō returned in Feb 1996 (10,898 m, sediment and microbe sampling) and May 1998, made 250+ dives, and collected 350 species. It shot video and photographs at the bottom — benthos including tubeworms and shrimp, per JAMSTEC's accounts.

On dive 73, 20 May 1998, Kaikō filmed a plastic bag at 10,898 m — the deepest human-made debris ever recorded — catalogued only when JAMSTEC opened its public Deep-Sea Debris Database in March 2017, some 20 years later.

Fate: lost 29 May 2003 off Shikoku Island during Typhoon Chan-Hom, when the secondary cable to its launcher snapped. Never recovered.

Honest limit: no free full-length Kaikō bottom video was found in this hunt; JAMSTEC's dive imagery lives in its institutional archives and database rather than on an open video channel.

Deepest life

Life was not the only thing that got there first — but it got astonishingly deep on its own.

The deepest fish ever filmed — a snailfish at 8,336 m

Filmed Aug 2022 · 8,336 m, Izu-Ogasawara Trench · Minderoo-UWA Deep Sea Research Centre / Tokyo University of Marine Science and Technology, aboard DSSV Pressure Drop

A juvenile snailfish (Pseudoliparis sp.), filmed by a baited lander — just past the long-predicted ~8,200–8,400 m biochemical limit for fish. Announced April 2023; Guinness-recognised.

The depth of it — tap to open

The caution, printed: some secondary pages misplace this record in the Mariana Trench or date the filming to 2023. The expedition's own reporting is clear: Izu-Ogasawara Trench, filmed Aug 2022, announced Apr 2023. The previous record — a Mariana snailfish filmed at 8,178 m — is the one those pages are blurring it with.

On the same expedition, two Pseudoliparis belyaevi were caught at 8,022 m in the Japan Trench — the first fish ever caught below 8,000 m. The limit the fish sits just past is the TMAO limit: past a certain depth, the chemistry that keeps a fish's cells stable against the pressure stops working.

Documented merpeople?

Reports: 1493–2012 · REPORTS — DOCUMENTED BEINGS — NO EVIDENCE FOUND

Documented reports exist, in ships' own logs. Documented beings: none. This card prints both halves, graded — and it is the bridge into Wing 2: here is where the documented record ends and the stories begin.

The depth of it — tap to open

The reports. Columbus's journal, Jan 1493, off Hispaniola: three “mermaids” that rose out of the sea — “not so beautiful as they are painted,” their faces with masculine traits. Now attributed to manatees. And Hudson's crew, 15 Jun 1608, in the Arctic — a detailed log entry by named sailors, Thomas Hilles and Robert Rayner: woman-like above the navel, white skin, long black hair, a tail “like the tayle of a porposse, and speckled like a macrell.” Both halves printed: no sea mammal of those waters matches it well — and the account rests on two men's word.

The beings. None. Every “mermaid body” ever examined was manufactured — monkey-and-fish constructions, the Japanese ningyo tradition, Barnum's 1842 Feejee Mermaid — or a manatee or dugong.

The modern chapter. Animal Planet's Mermaids: The Body Found (27 May 2012) was scripted docufiction with actors; enough viewers believed it that NOAA's National Ocean Service published an official Ocean Facts answer (27 Jun 2012): “No evidence of aquatic humanoids has ever been found.”

The deepest-parts bottom line: nothing humanoid is documented at hadal depths; the deepest genuine mystery on record is the unidentified animal at 9,137 m, already on this page.

Kola Superdeep Borehole — 12,262 m, drilled, not visited

Begun 24 May 1970 · record depth 1989 · Kola Peninsula · Soviet scientific drilling (SG-3)

The deepest hole ever dug — deeper below the surface than Challenger Deep is below sea level. Diameter ~23 cm: no person went down it. It is a drilled depth, not a visited one.

The depth of it — tap to open

Drilling stopped in 1992 after heat (~180 °C) far above predictions made the rock behave plastically.

Both records printed: Kola was the world's longest borehole by measured depth until an Al Shaheen (Qatar) oil borehole passed it in 2008 — but that is a length-along-the-bore record; SG-3 remains the deepest by true vertical depth.

Inward — into matter

Depth in the other direction entirely: how small a scale we have directly probed. This section's own measure is metres, going down.

The Large Hadron Collider — the smallest scales directly probed

CERN · structure resolved down to roughly 10⁻¹⁸ m

Proton beams at 6.8 TeV each, colliding at 13.6 TeV. Stated plainly: we have directly tested matter about a million times smaller than an atomic nucleus — and found quarks and electrons still point-like, with no deeper layer showing itself yet.

The depth of it — tap to open

Precision measurements and compositeness limits probe effective scales toward ~10⁻²⁰–10⁻²¹ m.

Explicitly NOT claimed: the floor of the map is the Planck length, ~1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m — theory's edge, nowhere near experimentally reached. This page does not print it as a probed depth.

Anchors and reflections

Michel Siffre — going deep into the self, and into time

1962 · 63 days alone, 130 m down, Scarasson abyss (Maritime Alps) · and 1972 · 205 days, Midnight Cave, Texas

The founding experiment of human chronobiology: no watch, no calendar, no daylight. When told on 14 Sep 1962 that his time was up, Siffre believed it was 20 Aug — his inner calendar had drifted 25 days.

The depth of it — tap to open

Counting to 120 at one number per second took him five minutes — he was living five real minutes as two. In 1972, six months (205 days) in Midnight Cave, Texas: without time cues his sleep-wake cycle stretched toward a 48-hour rhythm. He emerged from the work only partially recovered, by his own account and his biographers'.

Why it anchors the wing: it is the documented case of a person descending with no depth to conquer except his own sense of time — and coming back changed in ways that were measured, not imagined.

  • FREE NOW New Scientist — “This man spent months alone underground and it warped his mind”

The 1968–69 Golden Globe Race — the long solo endurance

1968–69 · the first solo non-stop round-the-world race · nine starters, one finisher

Robin Knox-Johnston finished. Bernard Moitessier, positioned to win, sailed on past the finish to Tahiti — 37,455 miles, still cited as the longest recorded non-stop solo voyage — choosing the voyage over the prize. Donald Crowhurst's boat was found empty and drifting.

The depth of it — tap to open

Crowhurst, alone in the Atlantic, falsified his positions for 240 days; his logbooks were left behind. The documentary Deep Water (2006) tells it; it is a commercial film.

Why it anchors the wing: three men, the same ocean, the same solitude — one came back, one sailed on, one didn't. The depth that decided it was not the water's.

REFLECTION — NOT A RECORD

The trench as a mirror

Everything that goes to Challenger Deep goes where no rescue can follow. Past a certain depth there is no help from above — you carry your air, your power, and your way back with you, or you don't come back. That is true of the dive. People recognise their own hard places in it because it is also true of some of theirs.

REFLECTION — NOT A RECORD

Pressure

At the bottom the pressure is more than a thousand times the surface's — and the animals there are not armoured against it; the deepest fish is soft, gelatinous, almost transparent, built to let the pressure pass through rather than fight it. The record-holder survives by not resisting. (The biology is Wing 1, Deepest life; the lesson drawn from it is this wing's.)

REFLECTION — NOT A RECORD

Darkness and what comes back changed

Trieste came back with no photograph of the bottom — the most important dive in history returned almost empty-handed, and it still changed everything, because now we knew a person could go and return. Siffre came back 25 days out of sync with the world. Crowhurst didn't come back. What a deep place gives you is rarely a picture. It's the knowledge of whether you can be there and still return as yourself.
