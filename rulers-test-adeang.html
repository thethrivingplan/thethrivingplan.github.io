The Ruler's Test — David Adeang, President of Nauru

Subject: David Ranibok Waiau Adeang — President of the Republic of Nauru (since 3 August 2026 styled the Republic of Naoero) since 30 October 2023. The seat graded is the presidency, and in Nauru that is the whole executive: the president is both head of state and head of government, chairs the Cabinet, and is elected by and from the 19-member Parliament, not directly by voters. Adeang, born 24 November 1969, is Nauru's longest-serving current MP, first elected in 2001, and a former Speaker, Minister for Finance, and Minister for Justice. He took the seat without an election: Parliament passed a vote of no confidence in President Russ Kun on 25 October 2023, deadlocked in its first ballots, and elected Adeang 10–8 over Delvin Thoma on 30 October 2023; he was sworn in on 31 October 2023 and appointed the ousted Kun a deputy minister. He holds the presidency personally together with the Finance and Sustainable Development portfolio, among others. After the general election of 11 October 2025 — in which he topped the Ubenide constituency poll with 837.298 preference points — the new Parliament re-elected him unopposed, unanimously, at its inaugural sitting on 14 October 2025 (Government of Nauru official site; PASAI Congress 2026 biography; PINA, 14 October 2025). Premise check for November 2026: the seat has not changed hands. Government Gazettes of 3, 9, 10 and 11 November 2026 are signed by Adeang as President, and the 11 November 2026 Gazette records Vice President Lionel Aingimea acting only during Adeang's official travel (Republic of Nauru Government Gazettes Nos. 375, 392, 395, 397 of 2026). He is graded on just over three years in office. His long ministerial record before 2023 — above all his tenure as Minister for Justice in 2014 — is used only where it is his own documented hand in the same system he now heads, and it is labeled as pre-presidency wherever it appears. Two naming notes: in 2026 his government renamed the country Naoero (below, Tests 3 and 5); this dossier keeps "Nauru," the name in the series' seat list and on most of the record graded. Reports conflict on the next election date — 2028 in some wire-derived reporting, 2029 in reporting that counts the four-year term Parliament enacted in March 2026 — and the conflict is printed, not blended, where it matters. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one; the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as two independent axes, and all four crossings are named where they occurred. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier says so, names the options that actually existed, and grades the choice made in the moment rather than holding the trap itself against the ruler. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The fiscal law he administers has been applied as written, to his own government first. The IMF's 2025 Article IV consultation recorded continued compliance with Nauru's fiscal responsibility ratios and a government contribution of A$22.6 million (8.5 percent of GDP) to the Intergenerational Trust Fund in FY2025, with Fund investments reaching A$421.8 million at 30 June 2025 — the rules binding the executive's own spending before anyone else's (IMF Country Report No. 25/275, September 2025; Intergenerational Trust Fund Annual Report 2025).
  • The electoral machinery ran under its own rules against no one: the Electoral Commission conducted the 11 October 2025 election and referendum, published full preference counts constituency by constituency — including the referendum result his own government did not want (below, Test 3) — and Parliament seated the members the count produced (Nauru Electoral Commission results via Government Gazette, October 2025).
  • No documented case in his presidential tenure shows a prosecution, tax action, or licensing decision aimed at an opponent as an opponent. The prosecutions that scar Nauru's record — the "Nauru 19" protest case — belong to the Waqa government of 2015–2019, before his presidency, and are not charged to this seat's tenure here.

Evidence against:

  • The law has never reached his own circle, and a monitor downgraded Nauru for exactly that. Freedom House's Freedom in the World 2025 report cut Nauru's anti-corruption safeguards score from 3 to 2 for one stated reason: "no efforts have been made to investigate persistent allegations of bribery and corruption involving prominent politicians" (Freedom House, Nauru country report, 2025). The persistent allegations are specific: ABC reporting in 2015 and 2016, based on leaked emails and bank records, alleged that Australian phosphate company Getax paid hundreds of thousands of dollars to Nauruan politicians while they were in opposition — including transfers in 2008 totaling some A$60,000 into the ANZ account of Madelyn Adeang, the late wife of David Adeang, then an opposition MP — allegedly to install a government that would let Getax buy phosphate below market value (ABC 7.30, June 2015 and September 2016; Uniting Church submission to the Australian Senate, January 2020, summarizing the ABC record). These are ALLEGATIONs: the Australian Federal Police investigation could not continue for lack of jurisdiction, no charge followed, and Adeang has never been convicted of anything. What is documented — and what Test 1 grades — is the other half: in more than a decade since the allegations were aired, and three years with Adeang himself holding the presidency and the finance portfolio, no Nauruan investigation of them appears in the record.
  • A second, current file sits in the same place. In November 2025, Greens Senator David Shoebridge read into the Australian Senate Hansard an unreleased AUSTRAC information report covering January–September 2020, which described transactions across accounts held by Adeang, then-President Lionel Aingimea, and associates as "indicative of money laundering and corruption" — including 15 Osko payments worth A$113,797 to Adeang, three of them from LRC Car Rentals and Construction, a company that had received A$580,277 from the Nauru government (Senate Hansard, 25 November 2025, as reported by AAP, the ASRC, and the Sydney Morning Herald, November 2025). AUSTRAC reports are intelligence alerts, not findings of guilt; the ABC noted it had not independently verified the report; AUSTRAC and DFAT declined to comment; and the Nauru government did not answer questions about it. No charge has been made public. All of that is recorded — and so is this: the subject of the alert is now the president, the alert concerns the years immediately before his presidency, and no Nauruan inquiry into it is documented either. An allegation the system will not even open is a second law in operation, whatever the allegation's merits.
  • The structure of his own administration puts family inside the money line. Australian Home Affairs officials confirmed in Senate evidence in May 2026 that the Secretary of Finance — the departmental counterpart for the offshore-processing money — is Damon Adeang, the President's son, serving under his father, who is himself Minister for Finance, and that the son is also a trustee under the relevant trust arrangement (Australian Senate Legal and Constitutional Affairs Committee evidence, May 2026, via AAP and Senate footage). The same evidence trail put a documented figure on insider-adjacent contracting: as at 31 October 2025, A$761,370 had been paid by the detention operator MTC Australia under a subcontract to Chazaq Corporation, owner of LRC Car Rentals — the company linked in the AUSTRAC reporting to the President and to the former president's family (AAP, 5 May 2026). The payments are documented; that they were improper is an ALLEGATION, contested by Home Affairs officials who told the committee due diligence had been done. A president who is finance minister, whose son is finance secretary and trustee, and whose government's largest revenue stream flows past companies linked to his own family, has built a source at which one law cannot be seen to operate — and visibility at the source is what this test grades.

Why this grade: One law means the rules reach the ruler's own side first. On the documented record, the fiscal rules do bind his government's spending — but every allegation touching the President himself or his family, across ten years and two separate files (Getax, 2008–2016 reporting; AUSTRAC, 2020 transactions, surfaced 2025), has met the same response: no Nauruan investigation, no public answer, no charge and no clearance. A system that investigates neither guilt nor innocence for its head is running two laws, and the second one is for insiders.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the Adeang family and the insider network around the processing-centre economy, who hold the finance secretaryship, a trusteeship, and documented subcontract income (A$761,370 to Chazaq/LRC as at October 2025) without documented scrutiny; future Nauruan generations, genuinely, through the trust-fund discipline recorded on the FOR shelf. Hurt — ordinary Nauruans and phosphate landowners, whose national asset generated the payments at issue in the Getax ALLEGATIONs and who have never had an official accounting of them; Australian taxpayers, whose offshore-processing money flows through the structure described above; and every honest Nauruan official, who works under a ceiling the record makes visible.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The referee that seated him twice acted independently of him. The Electoral Commission ran the October 2025 election and referendum, published a referendum result his government opposed, and Parliament — not the executive — chose the president, in 2023 by a contested 10–8 ballot after first deadlocking, and in 2025 unanimously (Electoral Commission results, October 2025; PINA, October 2025). Nauru still rates "Free" at 75/100 in Freedom House's 2026 report, which finds Nauruans "generally enjoy political rights and civil liberties" (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026).
  • The courts still sit and still rule on election disputes under the constitution, and in 2026 Parliament used formal constitutional process — bills tabled in January, a 90-day layover, two rounds of voting — for its amendments, rather than ruling by decree (PINA, April 2026, on the vice-presidency amendment; Government statements, 2026).
  • One genuine institutional addition was made openly: a Vice Presidency was created by constitutional amendment, with Lionel Aingimea sworn in as its first holder on 16 April 2026, giving Nauru a defined succession where none existed (PINA, 17 April 2026; Government Gazette Extraordinary, 16 April 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Parliament, the referee that elects and can remove him, no longer contains anyone outside his government. On 10 November 2026 Adeang announced that all remaining opposition MPs had joined his government — the last two, Pyon Deiye and Jason Bingham Agir, by being appointed deputy cabinet ministers, Deiye taking Police, Justice and Border Control and Agir taking Sport and the state holding company Eigigu — leaving the 19-member Parliament with no formal opposition for the first time since independence in 1968 (Associated Press, November 2026; Islands Business via AP, November 2026). The MPs' moves are recorded as voluntary; no coercion is documented, and in a party-less 19-member chamber, bloc-switching is normal Nauruan politics. What Test 2 grades is the mechanism and the result: the executive absorbed its own checker by handing out executive posts, and the body that alone can move no-confidence in a Nauruan president now consists entirely of his appointees and ministers. The announcement itself reached the public only through government Facebook posts — "the sole source of information about it," AP noted — because of the next point.
  • The media referee is owned by the player. All news outlets in Nauru are government-owned; all journalists are employees of the Nauru Media Bureau, classified as public servants who take an oath of allegiance to the government, and content is rarely critical of the government (UN Country Team reporting ahead of Nauru's Universal Periodic Review, January 2026; CIVICUS Monitor, 2025–2026; ABC International Development Pacific media study, March 2025). There is no freedom-of-information law (UNESCO, October 2025). Foreign journalists face a non-refundable media visa application fee of A$8,000 — raised from A$200 in early 2014, when Adeang was a senior minister in the government that raised it, and condemned at the time by Reporters Without Borders as "nothing less than a ban on foreign media visits" (RSF, 2014; CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). The fee stands unchanged through his presidency; at the January 2026 UPR, member states formally recommended Nauru review it and let foreign journalists in. No review is documented.
  • The judiciary carries his fingerprints from before the presidency, and nothing in his tenure has undone them. In January 2014, as Minister for Justice and Border Control, Adeang issued deportation orders against two expatriate businessmen; Resident Magistrate Peter Law granted interim injunctions against them; Adeang complained to Law by SMS that he had "gone against the will of Cabinet"; and on 19 January 2014 the government removed and deported Law without notice or stated reasons, ignored Chief Justice Geoffrey Eames's injunctions against the deportation — a police officer told the court's emissary, "I don't take orders from the Chief Justice" — and cancelled Eames's visa, effectively removing him, although the constitution allowed removal only on a two-thirds parliamentary vote for proven misconduct or incapacity (Chief Justice Eames's own account, speech of 24 October 2014 and Senate submission of 20 July 2015; International Commission of Jurists letter to President Waqa, copied to Minister Adeang, 3 March 2014). The government then retrospectively invalidated the court's orders by legislation (Immigration Act 2014, s.35). This was President Waqa's government, and Waqa bears its head-of-government responsibility; but the deportation orders were Adeang's, the SMS was Adeang's, and the portfolio was Adeang's. He now heads the state whose highest court his ministry once deported its way past, and the record shows no reckoning with that episode from him.
  • In September 2026 he withdrew Nauru from the one external court that remained: at the UN General Assembly on 22 September 2026, hours after President Trump urged all member states to quit the International Criminal Court, Adeang announced Nauru would withdraw from the Rome Statute for the ICC's "increasing irrelevance," and deposited the instrument of withdrawal, confirmed received by the UN Secretary-General on 23 September 2026 — the only one of the Court's 125 member states to follow Trump's call at that Assembly (Reuters, 24 September 2026; Parliamentarians for Global Action statement, 7 October 2026). Withdrawal takes effect 23 September 2027. A sovereign state may lawfully leave the ICC, and Venezuela, Chad, Niger, Burkina Faso and Mali also deposited withdrawals in 2026; the act is graded here only for what it does to the board: it removes the last referee seated outside his own system.

Why this grade: A holder-without-gripping leaves the referees able to rule against him. By November 2026 the documented board reads: a Parliament with zero members outside his government, a media wholly state-owned and oath-bound, a foreign-press door priced at A$8,000 non-refundable, a judiciary whose defining modern episode is a removal his own ministry executed, and an international court membership resigned within hours of a foreign president's request. The Electoral Commission's independence and the formal correctness of his constitutional processes are real, and they are on the FOR shelf; they do not outweigh a board on which every standing referee is either his employee, his appointee, or gone.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Adeang himself and his government, who now govern without a parliamentary opposition, a critical domestic press, or an external criminal court; his deputy ministers Pyon Deiye and Jason Bingham Agir, who received portfolios in the November 2026 absorption. Hurt — Nauruan voters, whose only constitutional route to removing a president mid-term (a parliamentary no-confidence vote) now runs through the president's own ministers; Nauruan journalists, employed and oath-bound by the state they would report on; foreign journalists and, through them, everyone outside Nauru who needs independent reporting from the island — including about the detention and resettlement operations Australia runs there; and the asylum seekers and transferees on Nauru, for whom outside scrutiny is the main documented protection they have.

The no-good-choice clause, applied where it genuinely fits: the banking file. At the start of 2024 Nauru's only remaining bank, Bendigo, was withdrawing — a state of 12,000 people faced losing banking altogether. The options that actually existed were: accept the December 2024 Australia–Nauru Treaty, with its A$100 million budget support over five years, A$40 million for policing and security, a guaranteed Commonwealth Bank branch — and an Australian veto over Nauru's engagements with any other state in security, banking, telecommunications and critical infrastructure; or refuse, and face debanking with no documented alternative partner at the table (the Bank of China had signed only an agreement to explore opportunities, after the January 2024 recognition switch). He took the treaty. The constraint is recorded, not held against him: a president who lets his country lose its last bank harms everyone on the island. What remains gradable, and is graded in Tests 3 and 4, is what he did with the room the treaty bought — not the fact that he bought it.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • His government does explain itself in public when it reverses course, rather than pretending nothing changed. When the promised referendum on the country's name was dropped in 2026, the government said so openly and gave its reason on the record: "The decision not to pursue a referendum was reached after thoughtful deliberation that Naoero is not a new name seeking acceptance by the people… While a referendum is intended to determine the will of the people, the name Naoero has never been lost, but rather waiting to be fully embraced" (Government of Nauru statement, July–August 2026, via Associated Press). Explanation is more than denial, and the shelf records it — the substance of the reversal is graded on the against shelf and in Test 5.
  • Faced with the debanking wobble of 2024 — an event, not an opponent — he adjusted the country's whole alignment to meet it: the Australia–Nauru Treaty of 9 December 2024, signed in Canberra with Prime Minister Albanese, secured banking continuity and A$140 million over five years, and Adeang defended the trade in public: "This treaty strengthens our own economy, enhances also our mutual security and addresses critical challenges like debanking and ensuring inclusive growth and resilience for our own people" (Reuters and AP reporting, 9 December 2024). Per the no-good-choice clause in Test 2, the trap itself is not held against him.
  • On climate, the pushback of geography itself, his government has adjusted policy rather than denying the fact: an updated Nationally Determined Contribution covering 2025–2035 was submitted to the UNFCCC, built around the Higher Ground Initiative to move homes and infrastructure to the island's mined-out interior plateau, and his climate minister told the UN Ocean Conference in June 2025 that the initiative "is our survival strategy" (Republic of Nauru national statement, UNOC3, June 2025; Nauru updated NDC, UNFCCC, 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The voters pushed back directly, and he answered by changing the rules so their answer would not stand. On 11 October 2025, in a referendum held alongside the general election, Nauruans voted on his government's proposal to extend Parliament's term from three to four years: 3,714 Yes (44.67 percent) to 4,601 No (55.33 percent) — a majority against, and far short of the two-thirds the constitution required, so the amendment failed (Electoral Commission of Nauru results, October 2025; RNZ and PINA reporting, 13 October 2025). The documented response was not to accept the answer or to argue the case again at the next election. Adeang introduced a further constitutional amendment in December 2025 creating a new Article 84A, under which Parliament may recall and pass a referendum-failed amendment that won at least 40 percent support; Parliament passed that device, then recalled the term bill and enacted the four-year term unanimously on 26 and 31 March 2026 (PINA, 7 April 2026). The sequence matters more than the term length, which is a legitimate policy either way: the voters were asked, the voters said no by a majority, and within six months the same change was law, by the votes of the very members whose terms it extends. That is the wobble answered by removing the wobble's effect.
  • The name change followed the same shape in miniature. Parliament passed the Naoero amendment on 13 May 2026 and the government announced a referendum to confirm it; by July 2026 the referendum had been dropped as "unnecessary," and the name took effect on 3 August 2026 without the vote that had been promised (Associated Press, August 2026; Government statements, May–August 2026). The pushback here was anticipatory — no vote had yet gone against him — but the pattern is the documented one: where a popular vote stands between the government and a settled intention, the vote is the thing adjusted.
  • When the January 2026 Universal Periodic Review put documented recommendations in front of his government — ratify the ICCPR, establish a national human rights institution, review the A$8,000 foreign-journalist visa fee, facilitate entry for foreign journalists and human-rights defenders (UN Human Rights Council UPR of Nauru, 20 January 2026, via CIVICUS Monitor) — if another option becomes available, this test grades whether it was at least considered openly. No documented consideration, acceptance, or reasoned refusal of these recommendations appears in the record assembled here. The same silence meets the UN Human Rights Committee's January 2025 decisions (Test 4): a new, documented option — independent oversight of the offshore arrangements — was available from that moment, and the documented response was to expand the arrangements instead (the August 2025 NZYQ memorandum) without any public engagement with the findings. Refusing to look is the failure this test names; considering and declining with stated reasons would not have been.
  • Pushback from Australia's own Senate met the same wall. When the AUSTRAC reporting was read into Hansard in November 2025 and a Senate inquiry into the offshore arrangements followed, the Nauru government's documented response to questions about the President's own file was no response at all — the government "didn't answer questions about it" (ABC reporting, November 2025). On the one wobble that touched him personally, the record shows neither adjustment nor explanation.

Why this grade: The wobble test asks what happens when the world says no. In this tenure the two clearest noes — a referendum majority against his term extension, and a promised referendum on the national name — were both answered by re-routing around the voters, one by a newly invented constitutional device (Article 84A) built for the purpose, the other by cancelling the vote as unnecessary. International pushback (UPR, Human Rights Committee) was met with documented silence, and personal pushback (AUSTRAC) with no answer. His genuine adjustments — the banking treaty, the climate program — were answers to events, not to being told no by people with the standing to tell him. A ruler who adjusts to weather but routes around voters is doubling down where it counts.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Adeang and all 19 MPs, whose terms now run four years instead of three and whose next accounting to voters is a year further away (next election reported as 2028 on the old cycle, 2029 on the new — the conflict is printed in the Subject line, not resolved here); his government, which kept both its term extension and its name change. Hurt — the 4,601 Nauruans who voted No in October 2025, a majority of those who voted, whose answer was enacted-against within six months; every future Nauruan voter, whose referendum verdicts now carry a documented precedent — fail to reach two-thirds with 40 percent support, and Parliament may pass the measure anyway; and the dei-Naoero whose name was changed, in the end, without the confirming vote they had been told to expect.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the minorities, the protesters, the poor, the people who did not elect him — and the people on his soil who cannot vote at all — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Future Nauruans are counted with unusual literalness. The Intergenerational Trust Fund exists to replace phosphate and processing revenue after 2033 for education, health, environment and infrastructure; his government contributed A$20.2 million of the A$21.1 million paid in during FY2025, budgeted a further A$24.3 million (10.1 percent of adjusted revenue) for FY2026–27, and the Fund passed A$400 million for the first time, reaching A$421.8 million in investments at 30 June 2025 (Trust Fund Annual Report 2025; IMF Article IV, September 2025; Adeang's Appropriation Bill 2026–2027 second reading speech, 20 July 2026). The FY2026–27 budget he presented was balanced — revenue of $359.2 million against expenditure of $359 million — in a state whose revenue depends on fishing licenses, phosphate, and the processing centre (Appropriation Bill speech, July 2026).
  • The whole present population is inside the climate accounting. The Higher Ground Initiative aims to relocate roughly 90 percent of residents from the flood-prone coastal fringe to higher ground, with climate-smart housing, water recycling, renewable energy and restored land, and the updated NDC carries it as the centerpiece of national policy (UNOC3 statement, June 2025; Princeton LISD policy brief, June 2026, summarizing the program). The citizenship-by-investment program created to help fund it — the Nauru Economic and Climate Resilience Citizenship Program, launched January 2025 — completed its first full year in FY2025–26 with 103 applications and $3.7 million received against a $9 million budget (Appropriation Bill speech, July 2026). The figures are his government's own and are printed as such.
  • The name change, whatever its process (Tests 3 and 5), was framed and received as an act of counting the indigenous identity in: restoring the name the island's own language uses, already on the coat of arms, for all dei-Naoero (Government statements, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The people on Nauru's soil who are not Nauruan are counted as revenue, not as people. The Regional Processing Centre population stood at about 91–100 people through 2025 (163 transferred since May 2022), held under arrangements the UN Human Rights Committee ruled on in January 2025: in two decisions covering 25 people, including 24 unaccompanied minors transferred in 2013–2014, the Committee found arbitrary detention in the Nauru RPC — overcrowding, insufficient water and sanitation, inadequate healthcare, no individualized assessment of less restrictive measures — and held Australia responsible because Australia exercises effective control (UN Human Rights Committee decisions, published 9 January 2025; UNHCR and OHCHR releases, January 2025). Australia's responsibility is the Committee's finding, and it stands; but the centre sits on Nauru, operates with Nauru's consent and Nauruan participation, and is forecast to contribute about A$135 million in government revenue in 2024–25 (Australian official figures via Reuters, December 2024). Nauru's consent is the load-bearing wall of the arrangement, and consent renewed is a choice counted in this test.
  • In February–August 2025 his government signed Nauru up to receive a second, distinct counted-out group: up to 354 members of Australia's "NZYQ cohort" — non-citizens released from indefinite detention after the Australian High Court's 2023 ruling — under a confidential memorandum signed 29 August 2025 paying A$408 million upfront and about A$70 million a year for 30 years, roughly A$2.5 billion in total, with no minimum number of visas Nauru must issue (Australian Home Affairs statements and Senate evidence, 2025; RACS submission to the Senate inquiry, March 2026). The memorandum remains secret; there is no independent oversight or monitoring of the treatment of those sent (Externalizing Asylum analysis, April 2026; Senate inquiry submissions, 2026). The first transfer took place on 24 October 2025; six people had been transferred by 10 February 2026, and eight by May 2026, on Senate evidence — the counts are printed separately because the record gives them at different dates, not blended (Senate evidence via Externalizing Asylum, 2026). Some A$2.5 billion over 30 years, for a group in single digits actually transferred: the accounting that produces those numbers counts the transferees as a revenue line, and counts Nauru's own citizens as beneficiaries of it. Both halves of that sentence are this test's subject.
  • The President's own words counted the cohort out inaccurately. In a February 2025 interview in Nauruan with a government information officer, Adeang repeatedly described the cohort as "not refugees" — "ordinary individuals" whose history "is that they have been to jail" — and floated that if Nauru found "a solution to return them home," they would not complete their 30-year visas (independently verified translation obtained by the Asylum Seeker Resource Centre, read into the Australian Senate Hansard by Senators Shoebridge and Pocock, 24 November 2025; Law Council of Australia submission, 13 February 2026). The translation is contested terrain and is printed as such: the Australian government holds its own translation, refused to release it, and obtained a court suppression order over it, arguing release would damage foreign relations. On the merits, the Law Council's submission records that Australia owes protection obligations to around 83 percent of the cohort — which would make "not refugees" wrong for most of them — and that returning a refugee through a third country is chain refoulement, prohibited under the Refugee Convention to which Nauru itself is a party. No return is documented as having happened; what is documented is the head of the receiving state describing a protected group as unprotected, on the record, while negotiating their reception.
  • Nauru has no national human rights institution, has not ratified the ICCPR, and at its January 2026 UPR received recommendations to build both — the machinery by which a state counts the people who cannot count themselves in its politics (CIVICUS Monitor on the UPR, 2026). The counted-out on Nauru have no domestic referee of their own; from September 2026 they also begin losing the international criminal court (Test 2).

Why this grade: The split is real and it runs along the citizenship line. Inside the line, this government counts everyone it can see — present citizens through a balanced budget and a climate-relocation plan for 90 percent of the population, future citizens through a trust fund it feeds at 10 percent of revenue. Outside the line, the roughly one hundred asylum seekers at the RPC and the handful of NZYQ transferees are held inside a system a UN committee has found wanting, under a secret memorandum, with no independent oversight, described by their host president in terms the Law Council's figures contradict. A test that asks "does the accounting include everyone?" cannot pass a seat whose budget is materially built on people it does not count — and cannot wholly fail a seat that counts its own people's grandchildren by name in a trust fund. MIXED is the honest grade, with the hurt side printed in full.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Nauruan citizens, present (budgeted services, banking continuity, climate adaptation) and future (a A$421.8 million trust fund); the Nauruan treasury, which gains a forecast A$135 million a year from the RPC and A$408 million upfront plus A$70 million a year under the NZYQ memorandum. Hurt — the asylum seekers and refugees at the Regional Processing Centre (about 91–100 people in 2025; 25 of their predecessors' cases produced UN findings of arbitrary detention); the NZYQ transferees (six by February 2026, eight by May 2026, on Senate evidence), sent under a secret memorandum to a country none had a connection to, with a UN Human Rights Committee interim request against one removal issued in August 2025; and, in the longer ledger, Nauruan citizens themselves, whose state's income now depends on the continued exclusion of both groups — a dependence the trust fund is meant to outlast but has not yet replaced.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 30 October 2023, with dates. Nauru has no political parties and no election manifesto; Adeang's word is found in his budget speeches, his treaty signatures, his government's published undertakings, and his statements to Parliament and the UN — and it is graded on those.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The trust-fund commitment — kept, on schedule, three years running. His budgets promised sustained contributions to the Intergenerational Trust Fund as the state's long-term fiscal anchor; the record shows A$22.6 million contributed in FY2025 on the IMF's figures (A$20.2 million from Nauru in the Fund's own annual report, the small difference being the reports' different scopes, printed separately rather than blended), the Fund reaching A$421.8 million, and a further A$24.3 million — 10.1 percent of adjusted revenue — budgeted for FY2026–27 in his July 2026 budget speech, which named reaching the Fund's target by 2033 "our key long-term fiscal anchor" (IMF, September 2025; Trust Fund Annual Report 2025; Appropriation Bill speech, 20 July 2026). Under the mandate rule, delivering the fiscal platform he was re-elected on is FOR-shelf evidence, and this is the clearest instance of it.
  • The Australia treaty — signed as announced, and its banking promise performed. The December 2024 treaty committed Australia to budget support and to ensuring Nauru kept a bank as Bendigo withdrew; the Commonwealth Bank arrangement proceeded under the treaty, and the treaty's five-year contribution framework underwrote the FY2025 trust-fund payment his government made (Trust Fund Annual Report 2025; ASPI Strategist analysis, 2024). The word given to Canberra was kept — the hurt side of that keeping is recorded in the crossings below, on its own axis, as the method requires.
  • The NZYQ undertakings — the machinery delivered as agreed with Australia. The February 2025 interim arrangement and the 29 August 2025 memorandum committed Nauru to issue long-term visas and receive transferees; Nauru issued the visas, the first transfer occurred on 24 October 2025, and transfers continued into 2026 (Home Affairs statements; Senate evidence, 2026). As a word given to a partner government, it was performed. What that performance meant for the people transferred is graded in Test 4 and in the crossings — not netted against the keeping here.
  • The climate word — stated, then filed. His government said Nauru would submit an updated NDC built on the Higher Ground Initiative; the updated NDC covering 2025–2035 was submitted to the UNFCCC (UNFCCC registry, 2025). The citizenship program launched in January 2025 to fund it reported its first-year figures publicly in the July 2026 budget speech — 103 applications, $3.7 million received against a $9 million budget — a shortfall published by the government itself rather than hidden.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The name referendum — promised, then withdrawn. In May 2026, when Parliament passed the Naoero amendment, the government announced the change would proceed to a national referendum to complete the process — the government's own May 2026 statements said the proposal "will now proceed to a national referendum which is required to complete the constitutional amendment process" (Government statements via FRCN/Pacific reporting, 14 May 2026). In July 2026 the referendum was dropped as unnecessary, and the name took effect on 3 August 2026 (Associated Press, August 2026). The reason given — that Naoero "is not a new name seeking acceptance by the people" — is a reason for not needing a vote, offered after the vote had been promised as required. That is a broken word, of the milder kind: no one was harmed in their rights or property by it, and the change itself honors the country's own language; but the promise was specific, public, and unmet.
  • "Proper treatment and long-term residence" vs. the February 2025 interview. The August 2025 memorandum, as Australia's government described it, "contains undertakings for the proper treatment and long-term residence of people" received in Nauru (Home Affairs statement, 29 August 2025). Seven months earlier, in the February 2025 interview, Adeang had described the same cohort as "not refugees" and raised returning them to their home countries if "a solution" were found — a course the Law Council of Australia submits would breach the Refugee Convention for the roughly 83 percent of the cohort owed protection (verified translation read into Hansard, 24 November 2025; Law Council submission, 13 February 2026). The translation dispute is named in Test 4: Australia's own translation remains suppressed. On the translation in the public record, the President's public description of the deal to his own people contradicts the undertaking his government signed with Australia. No refoulement is documented; the words, as they stand in the record, do not match each other.
  • The referendum principle — the word underneath the words. No single speech promised "we will abide by referendum results." But his government put the term extension to a referendum under a constitution requiring two-thirds, campaigned for it as the people's decision, and then — after 55.33 percent voted No — built Article 84A to pass it anyway (Test 3). A government that asks the people and then legislates past their answer has broken the undertaking that made the asking meaningful. This is recorded here as well as in Test 3 because Test 5 is where the series keeps the word ledger: the promise was procedural, and it was not kept.

The two axes, printed independently — all four crossings: 1. Kept his word AND it helped people: the trust-fund contributions — promised in his budgets, paid in FY2025 and budgeted again for FY2026–27, for the stated benefit of future generations' education, health, environment and infrastructure. 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt people: the NZYQ memorandum — performed exactly as agreed with Australia (visas issued, transfers received from October 2025), and the keeping is credited here; the hurt — a secret, unmonitored 30-year reception of people Australia owed protection duties toward, on the UN and Law Council record — is recorded in Test 4's ledger in full. The promise's content, not its keeping, is what Test 4 grades. 3. Went against his word AND it helped people: not documented. No instance appears in the record assembled here of a broken commitment that benefited people; the category is printed, as the method requires, and left empty rather than filled by invention. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt people: the referendum undertakings — the promised name referendum withdrawn (July 2026), and the term referendum's No majority legislated past (March 2026). The hurt is constitutional rather than material: 4,601 No voters' answer set aside, and every dei-Naoero given a national name without the confirming vote they were told would complete the process.

Why this grade: His word is good where it is written in budgets and treaties and bad where it is given to voters about their own say. The fiscal word — the trust fund, the balanced budget, the treaty machinery — has been kept with dates and figures that check out against the IMF and the Fund's own reports. The democratic word — referendums promised, referendum results rendered advisory after the fact — has been broken twice in a single year, openly, with explanations that concede the facts. Neither shelf cancels the other; that is what MIXED means, and the crossings above keep the axes separate as the method requires.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — future Nauruan generations (trust fund, kept); the Australian government, which got the partner performance it paid for; Nauruan public finances in the present (treaty support, processing revenue). Hurt — Nauruan voters as promise-holders (the two referendum files); the NZYQ transferees, who were promised "proper treatment and long-term residence" by an undertaking their host's president publicly described in terms that point the other way; and anyone who must decide how much a Nauruan government commitment is worth, who now prices in the referendum precedent.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

David Adeang came to the presidency the Nauruan way — not elected by the people to the office, but chosen 10–8 by a Parliament that had just removed his predecessor, then re-elected unopposed in October 2025 after topping his constituency poll. Three years on, the record divides cleanly in two. As a fiscal manager he is the strongest part of his own file: the Intergenerational Trust Fund fed at roughly a tenth of revenue and past A$421 million, a balanced FY2026–27 budget, an IMF consultation that welcomed his government's prudence, a banking system saved from collapse by the December 2024 treaty — a treaty signed, per the no-good-choice clause, from a position where the documented alternative was losing the country's last bank, and therefore not held against him. As a holder of power he fails the Test's first three questions on documented facts, not interpretations: allegations touching himself and his family — Getax in the last decade, AUSTRAC in this one, both labeled ALLEGATION throughout this dossier, neither charged nor cleared — have never met a Nauruan investigation, while his son sits as Secretary of Finance under his father's finance ministry (Test 1, why: one law stops at the insider's door); by November 2026 his Parliament contains no opposition at all, absorbed through deputy-ministerial appointments, his media is wholly state-owned, the foreign-press visa stands at A$8,000 non-refundable against a January 2026 UPR recommendation, and the ICC membership was resigned within hours of a foreign president's call (Test 2, why: every standing referee is his employee, his appointee, or gone); and when the voters themselves pushed back — 55.33 percent No to his term extension in October 2025 — he answered not by adjusting but by inventing Article 84A and passing the extension anyway in March 2026, then dropping the promised name referendum three months later (Test 3, why: the wobble is answered by re-routing around the people who made it). The people inside his citizenship line are counted with real care, including generations not yet born; the roughly one hundred asylum seekers and the first eight NZYQ transferees on his island are counted as the revenue that pays for that care, under a secret memorandum, described by him in words the Law Council's figures contradict (Test 4, why: the accounting is complete inside the line and stops at it). His word follows the same split — kept to the dollar in budgets and treaties, broken twice in a year where voters were the promise-holders (Test 5, why: the fiscal word checks out; the democratic word does not). The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether any Nauruan body ever opens the insider allegations, whether an opposition re-forms before the next election (reported as 2028 or 2029 — the record conflicts, and the conflict is printed), whether the NZYQ memorandum is ever published or independently monitored, and whether a referendum verdict is ever again allowed to be final.
