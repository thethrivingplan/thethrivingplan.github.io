The Ruler's Test — Surangel Whipps Jr., President of Palau

Subject: Surangel Samuel Whipps Jr. — President of the Republic of Palau since 21 January 2021, now in his second term. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: in Palau the President is both head of state and head of government — there is no separate prime-ministerial seat to grade. The premise was verified before grading. Whipps, born 9 August 1968, a businessman who ran the family company Surangel & Sons for over 30 years and served as a senator in the Olbiil Era Kelulau (OEK, the National Congress) from 2009 to 2016, was first elected in 2020. He was re-elected in the general election of 5 November 2024, defeating his predecessor and brother-in-law, former President Thomas Remengesau Jr., by 5,626 votes (57.74%) to 4,103 (42.11%), on Palau Election Commission figures, and he took office for his second term on 16 January 2025 (Palau Election Commission results via 2024 election reporting; Reuters, 13 November 2024; Australian Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade country brief, 2026; PalauGov president's page, 2026). No premise correction to the holder is needed: he remained president through every 2026 check assembled here — delivering the State of the Republic Address on 22 July 2026, taking over the chairmanship of the Pacific Islands Forum at the 55th Leaders Meeting in Koror on 1 September 2026, and visiting Australia on 11–17 October 2026 (State of the Republic Address, 22 July 2026; Pacific Islands Forum reporting, September 2026; DFAT country brief, 2026). Presidents are limited to two consecutive four-year terms, so this is his final consecutive term; the next election is due in November 2028. He is graded on the full documented record of his presidency, 2021 to November 2026, with the second term weighted most heavily where the record allows the terms to be separated. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test is printed here verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Every test closes with the reason for its grade in plain words and a named ledger of who was helped and who was hurt. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His flagship fiscal reform applies, in its design, to everyone in the same way. The tax reform that took effect on 1 January 2023 replaced the old gross-receipts system with a 10% Palau Goods and Services Tax (PGST) and a 4% business profits tax, broadening the base across businesses and visitors alike. The IMF's 2025 Article IV consultation — concluded by the Executive Board on 26 January 2026 — commended the "successful implementation of tax reform," recorded that deficits had turned to surpluses on the strength of the recovery, increased grants, the reform itself, and spending restraint, and recorded public debt falling from a peak of 81% of GDP to 67% by the end of FY2024 (IMF mission statement, 19 November 2025; IMF Executive Board conclusion, 30 January 2026). A broad-based tax that the outside referee credits with fixing the books is one-law evidence, whatever its distributional cost (graded in Test 4).
  • The relief built into the same system was aimed by income, not by connection: a tax-refund programme provided full income-tax refunds to individuals earning up to US$15,000, benefiting about 70% of Palau's workforce (PINA re-election reporting, November 2024).
  • On the minimum wage, his version of the law was the more universal one on the table. In the 2024 wage debate, the House bill would have applied the increase only to Palauan citizen employees; President Whipps' proposal extended it to all workers. The joint bill that passed applied the phased increase generally, and he signed it as Public Law 11-34 on 10 June 2024 (PINA, March 2024; Marianas Business Journal, June 2024). The distinction matters to this test: when a narrower, citizens-only version was available, the president's stated position was the one-law version.
  • The election that returned him was run as one contest under one count: he defeated a former president — a member of his own extended family — by a margin counted and conceded in the open, 5,626 to 4,103 (Palau Election Commission figures, November 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The family's company sits inside the state's contracting economy, and the documented count is specific. Surangel & Sons — founded by his father, run by Whipps Jr. as CEO until he became president in 2021 — won, through its construction arm, at least 37 US defence contracts valued at more than $5.8 million during his first term (Guardian reporting from Palau, November 2024). His opponent Remengesau called this a "conflict of interest" and said there seemed to be "no equal opportunities or opportunities for other companies to get the benefits of government projects or military projects" — an opponent's characterization, recorded here as an ALLEGATION-level claim, not a finding. Whipps denied wrongdoing on the record: "We have never cheated, never taken advantage of any situation that we've been in, from when we started our business 40 years ago, until now" (Guardian, November 2024). No court or audit finding against him or the company on these contracts appears in the record assembled here; what is documented is the overlap itself — the president's family firm is the country's largest private company, and it kept winning state-adjacent work while he held the seat.
  • The tax file produced a second, sharper version of the same question. In October 2024 a leaked tax report concerning Surangel & Sons became an election issue; the company condemned the leak as a privacy violation and a smear campaign, and then sued the newspaper that reported it (graded for its press-freedom effect in Test 2). For this test the entry is narrower: when the tax system touched the first family, the response documented on the record was litigation and a public complaint by the president that the paper should have explained the tax process instead of reporting the leak (CIVICUS Monitor, covering October 2024 – March 2025) — a different register of response than the one ordinary taxpayers receive.
  • No documented prosecution or sanction of a senior insider for corruption under his government appears in the record assembled here. The one senior corruption action of the period was taken not by Palau's own machinery but by Washington: on 10 February 2026 the US Department of State publicly designated Senate President Hokkons Baules — a political opponent of Whipps on the deportee file, not an ally — "for his involvement in significant corruption on behalf of China-based actors," stating that he "abused his public position by accepting bribes" (US Department of State designation, 10 February 2026; Reuters, 10 February 2026). Baules has dismissed corruption allegations against him as a smear campaign (RNZ Pacific reporting, February 2026). The designation is a foreign government's administrative finding, contested by its subject, and is recorded here as such — not as a Palauan court verdict, and not as an act of Whipps' government in either direction.

Why: The grade is MIXED because the law he wrote is genuinely general — a broad-based tax the IMF credits, relief targeted by income, a wage law he wanted applied to all workers — while the law's application around his own family is genuinely unproven in his favour. Thirty-seven defence contracts for the family firm during his presidency is a documented fact; favouritism in their award is an allegation he denies and no referee has sustained. One law is visible in the statutes; at the source, where his family stands, the record shows the question asked and never independently answered.

Helped: Low-income workers, who received full income-tax refunds under the up-to-$15,000 programme (about 70% of the workforce); all workers covered by the minimum-wage law, which his proposal would have — and the enacted law did — apply generally rather than to citizens only; the public finances themselves, with debt down from 81% to 67% of GDP by end-FY2024 on the IMF's figures. Hurt: Smaller Palauan companies competing for government and military work, who on Remengesau's stated account — an allegation, not a finding — could not get equal access to tendered projects; ordinary taxpayers without a family company's legal resources, whose dealings with the tax system are not met with the response the leak file received. No individual is named on this shelf as a proven wrongdoer: the contract overlap is documented, the favouritism is not.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The referees' outside scores stayed at the top of the scale across his tenure. Freedom House rates Palau "Free" at 92 of 100 in the Freedom in the World assessments covering 2024 and 2025 (Freedom House, 2025 and 2026 editions). The US State Department's Palau 2024 Human Rights Report records no credible reports of significant human rights abuses and no reports of the government committing arbitrary or unlawful killings (US Department of State, Palau 2024 Human Rights Report).
  • The courts heard a case against the president himself, brought by the legislature, and ruled on it in the open. In February 2026 the Senate and two private citizens sued President Whipps over the deportee memorandum (Tests 3 and 4), arguing it was effectively an unratified treaty requiring legislative approval; on 20–21 February 2026 the Supreme Court denied the Senate's application for a stay order, and screening under the agreement later began while the lawsuit remained active (RNZ Pacific, 20 February 2026; Island Times reporting via Standing Record, April 2026). A court that can be asked to stop the president, and that answers publicly either way, is not a gripped court on this evidence — and the president submitted to its process rather than ruling around it.
  • Critical media operate and publish against his government without documented state retaliation: the Island Times — the paper his family's company sued — continued publishing through 2026, including coverage critical of the deportee agreement and the hospital-site dispute, and Palauan and regional outlets covered the Senate's lawsuit, the chiefs' objections, and the sanctuary controversy at length (Island Times reporting, January–June 2026; RNZ Pacific and PINA, 2026). Palau scored 70 points in the RSF-based press-freedom comparison for 2026 (RSF World Press Freedom Index data, 2026 compilation).
  • Palau has no standing military and its currency is the US dollar, so two of this test's usual referees — an army and a central bank — do not exist as domestic institutions for him to grip; that structural fact is recorded so the grade is not credited for restraint he was never positioned to exercise.

Evidence against:

  • The deportee memorandum is the central gripping entry in this file, and it is documented step by step. The US proposal to transfer third-country nationals to Palau was put to Palau's leadership in July 2025; a letter dated 21 July 2025, signed by Senate President Hokkons Baules and House Speaker Gibson Kanai, told the president Congress "cannot accept this specific proposal" (Reuters, July 2025). The Council of Chiefs also rejected the proposal, and, on the compiled record, Congress and the Council of Chiefs had twice rejected it, with Baules warning again in a letter of 3 October 2025 (Standing Record compilation, 2025–2026; Reuters, July and December 2025). On 24 December 2025 Whipps announced the memorandum of understanding anyway: up to 75 third-country nationals, case-by-case acceptance, in exchange for US$7.5 million and a US commitment toward a new hospital (US Department of State readout, 24 December 2025; Reuters, 24 December 2025). Whatever the Supreme Court later made of its legality, the sequence is the executive proceeding over the stated, repeated objection of both the elected legislature and the traditional chiefs — the two checking institutions Palau's system has.
  • The press-freedom entry is a lawsuit from inside the first family. On 31 October 2024 — five days before the election — Surangel and Sons Co., the company owned by the president's father, filed a civil defamation and negligence suit against the Island Times, its publisher, and its editor Leilani Reklai over an article drawn largely, on the paper's account, from the company's own press release; the company sought compensatory and punitive damages and stopped selling the paper in its stores. Reklai warned the suit could bankrupt the paper and chill media freedom; the Palau Media Council called it an assault on press freedom, and the Pacific Freedom Forum and the International Federation of Journalists called for its withdrawal (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025; BenarNews, 2 November 2024). The suit is a private company's act in law; its weight comes from whose company it is, and from the president's own public intervention backing the complaint against the paper's reporting (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025).
  • The Senate's January 2026 response shows how far the institutional pushback had to go: senators passed a bill to block the deal, declared the country "not suited to serve as a country of deportation, relocation, or resettlement" given its size and resources, and voted to seek a national referendum (Senate bill reporting, January 2026). The House of Delegates did not approve the blocking legislation (RNZ Pacific, February 2026) — recorded here because it cuts both ways: the legislature was divided, not unanimous, even as its Senate and its presiding officers stood against the president.

Why: The grade is MIXED because Palau's referees are demonstrably alive — a free election conceded by the loser, a Supreme Court that heard the Senate's case against the president and ruled in public, a press that prints his government's failures, a 92-of-100 Free rating held across his tenure — and because, on the largest institutional question of his second term, he proceeded anyway after both checking bodies had said no, twice, in writing. He did not capture the referees. On the deportee file he played on after they had blown the whistle, and let the court sort it out afterwards.

Helped: The judiciary and the Senate as institutions, whose authority was exercised and publicly answered rather than abolished — the Senate's suit was heard, the court's ruling was reported, and the referendum demand entered the public record; readers of an independent press that survived its lawsuit and kept publishing through 2026. Hurt: The Olbiil Era Kelulau and the Council of Chiefs as checking institutions, whose twice-stated rejection of the deportee proposal was overridden by executive signature on 24 December 2025; the Island Times and its editor Leilani Reklai, who bore a damages suit from the president's family's company days before an election, with the chilling effect on Pacific media named by the Palau Media Council and the Pacific Freedom Forum; Palauan citizens who sought a referendum on the deportee question and, on the record assembled here through November 2026, had not been given one.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, the ruler at least considered it openly. Considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The marine sanctuary file shows a real, documented adjustment over time. The Palau National Marine Sanctuary — legislated in 2015 under his predecessor and in full effect from 1 January 2020 — closed 80% of Palau's exclusive economic zone to commercial fishing and mining, reserving 20% for domestic fishing. Whipps first proposed doing away with the sanctuary altogether after the COVID tourism collapse; he then moderated, on the record, to shrinking the no-take area: "Let's use science. Let's use data. Let's talk to the community. Let's calm down. Let's really evaluate what is the best practice" (NPR reporting, August 2024). The process that followed was public and consultative in form: a marine spatial planning process to assess the conservation measures' impact on local communities, criticism aired openly from environmental groups and from Remengesau, and traditional leaders' position — stated by High Chief Reklai in 2025 — that the sanctuary is both an enacted law and a bul (a traditional closure) changeable only by Palau's leadership collectively (PINA / Earth.Org interview reporting, October 2026; sanctuary review reporting, 2025–2026). By his State of the Republic Address of 22 July 2026, the proposal sent to the OEK was a "rightsizing" to a protected core of roughly 30% of the EEZ — the 50 nautical miles around the reefs — with 100% management of the whole zone, argued from a new Ocean Nexus / Nippon Foundation study of official catch data (State of the Republic Address, 22 July 2026). If another option becomes available, this is what considering it openly looks like over six years: the maximal position was abandoned, the reasons were stated in public each time the position moved, and the final decision was left where it belongs, with the legislature that enacted the sanctuary.
  • Foreign pushback of the hardest kind — economic coercion by Beijing — was answered openly and at stated cost, not quietly accommodated. Whipps told the Associated Press in 2024 that a Chinese ambassador had pledged, in 2020, to flood Palau with a million tourists if it dropped Taiwan, that "that continues to be the overture," and that he refused: "We don't need a million tourists. It's not always about how much money we get" (Associated Press interview, August 2024). He stated the penalties in public as they landed — Chinese visitor numbers halved in 2024, Macau conference visas denied to Palau tourism representatives, Chinese state-media safety warnings — and attributed a May 2024 cyberattack that stole some 20,000 government documents to China while stating plainly that the claim was unproven (Associated Press, August 2024). The wobble was named, the price was named, and the policy did not move.
  • The fiscal wobble of his first term — a pandemic-collapsed economy and debt at 81% of GDP — was answered with a reform he defended in public against direct criticism that it burdened low-income households, and then adjusted at the margin with the low-income refund programme recorded in Test 1 (Guardian and PINA campaign reporting, 2024; IMF, January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the deportee file, pushback from every domestic direction was met not with adjustment but with signature. Congress said no in July 2025, the Council of Chiefs said no, the Senate president warned again in October 2025 — and the memorandum was announced on 24 December 2025 (Reuters, July and December 2025; Standing Record compilation). His office's documented defence was procedural: that Palau would accept or decline each individual case by case, and that the Senate and traditional leaders had declined attempts to meet for discussions (president's office statements, July 2025 and February 2026, via Reuters and RNZ Pacific). That last claim is contested by the sequence itself — the chiefs' documented position is that they were "caught off guard when we learned about the signed MOU" (Council of Chiefs letter to the president, reported January 2026). Both accounts are printed; the disputants are the president's office and the Council of Chiefs. What is not in dispute is that no referendum was held before signing, though the Senate later voted to seek one.
  • A quieter wobble went unanswered for over a year on the documented record. Koror State — the state containing most of Palau's population — adopted a resolution in April 2024 offering several sites for the relocated national hospital and asking for dialogue; its governor, Eyos Rudimch, stated in a letter of 7 October 2025 that Koror had received no response from the president for over a year, while rumours circulated of the hospital going to Airai or Ngatpang instead (Island Times, 8 January 2026, reporting the Koror State resolution and the governor's letter, with petitions signed by hundreds of Koror residents attached). A state government and hundreds of petitioners asking where the national hospital will go is pushback in its most ordinary form; the documented answer, for over a year, was silence.
  • The sanctuary adjustment, credited above, also carries its counterweight: the direction of travel never changed, only its speed and its stated basis. From abolition, to 50%, to roughly 30% — each "adjustment" still landed on a smaller sanctuary than the law his predecessor left, against the stated opposition of the traditional leadership's senior voice and of conservation groups, in a debate in which Japan's lobbying for more fishing ground was itself a documented factor in 2025 (Island Times, October 2025). The dossier credits the openness of the process; it records that the process's destination was set at the start.

Why: The grade is MIXED because both patterns are documented at full strength. Where the pushback came from voters, scientists, fishers, and a coercive foreign power, he adjusted in the open or held his position with its price stated aloud. Where the pushback came from his own legislature and chiefs on the deportee memorandum, and from a state government on the hospital, he did not adjust at all — he signed over the objection, and in the hospital case did not answer. The wobble he answers best is the one arriving from outside Palau; the wobble he answers worst is the one arriving from inside it.

Helped: Palauan fishing communities and households, whose case against the 80% closure — tuna catch and licence revenue collapsed on the figures the government cites (Test 5) — was heard, studied, and carried into a reform package rather than dismissed; the public record itself, by a president who stated China's overture and its price in his own words; Taiwan, whose diplomatic position in the Pacific held. Hurt: The Council of Chiefs and the Senate majority, whose objections on the deportee file were overridden rather than answered; Koror State's government and the hundreds of residents who petitioned on the hospital site and received, on the governor's documented account, no reply for over a year; conservation constituencies — including traditional leaders who regard the sanctuary as a bul — who watched a six-year process move in only one direction.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the outer states, the foreign workers who are a large share of the population, women, the poor, and the people his agreements bring to Palau's shores — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The wage and subsidy record counts the bottom of the economy first, and counts foreign workers in. The minimum wage rose from $3.50 to $4.25 an hour on 1 October 2024 and to $5.00 on 1 October 2025, under Public Law 11-34 which he signed — the first increases since 2013 — and the IMF recorded that the increases "have helped real wages recover to their pre-pandemic trend" (Marianas Business Journal, June 2024; IMF mission statement, November 2025). His March 2024 supplemental budget package also created the Child Raising Subsidy — the first of its kind in Palau — and raised monthly stipends for people who are blind or use a wheelchair, alongside two $75-cent-equivalent government pay increases totalling a $3,120 annual raise for public employees over two years and a one-time $480 payment for retirees (Marianas Business Journal, 2024; PINA, November 2024).
  • The health file counts the whole country in its intent: more than $6 million invested in upgrades to Belau National Hospital and community health centres, electronic medical records added, a feasibility study for a new hospital under way, and more than $4.6 million in assistance budgeted for 2025 for disabled residents, children, and retirees (State of the Republic reporting via Marianas Business Journal, 2025). The deportee memorandum itself was tied, on the US readout, to a commitment toward a new hospital — a benefit his office presented as accruing to Palauans (US Department of State, December 2025).
  • The climate and ocean file counts the most exposed first in its stated logic. Palau under his presidency was the first country to ratify the UN High Seas Treaty (2023), maintained its opposition to deep-sea mining with a call for a moratorium while Cook Islands, Nauru, Tonga, and Kiribati explored the industry, kept the Palau Pledge — the visitor eco-pledge in passports since 2017, a predecessor's policy he maintained rather than dismantled — and carried the small-island case to the UN General Assembly in September 2025 in the first person plural: "Our shores are being invaded by seawater. Our homes are being blown away by storms… This is our daily battle" (PINA / Earth.Org, October 2026; UN General Assembly address reporting, September 2025). As Pacific Islands Forum chair from September 2026 he convened the region on the same file (Forum Leaders Meeting, Koror, September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Foreign workers — roughly a third of Palau's population are noncitizens, on Freedom House's earlier accounting — are counted in his wage proposal and counted out in the law's own carve-outs and in enforcement. The US State Department's 2024 Human Rights Report records that the minimum wage does not apply to foreign migrants working as agricultural labourers or domestic workers, to students, or to workers under 21; that there were continued reports of mistreatment of foreign workers by employers, with noncitizens especially at risk — domestic and farm workers, waitresses, cashiers, beauticians, karaoke-bar hostesses, massage-parlour workers, and construction workers named; that the government "did not effectively enforce" the wage and conditions law and "did not take a proactive approach to inspecting labor conditions," with eight labour officers for the country (US Department of State, Palau 2024 Human Rights Report). Palau remains Tier 2 in the US Trafficking in Persons assessment — not fully meeting minimum standards, with light penalties for trafficking-permissive violations recorded (US Department of State, Trafficking in Persons Report, Palau). None of this began with Whipps; all of it continued, unremedied on this record, through his presidency.
  • The PGST's incidence fell where its critics said it would. His opponent's central 2024 charge — that the 10% tax worsened the burden on low-income households amid a cost-of-living crisis in a country importing most of its goods — was made by Remengesau and his supporters and echoed in voter interviews across the campaign (Guardian, November 2024; PINA, March 2024). The refund programme in Test 1 is the documented offset, and it is real; it is also partial — it refunds income tax to those in the income-tax system, while the PGST is paid at the till by everyone, including those outside that system.
  • The 75 people at the centre of the deportee memorandum are counted in the deal as a number, with their protections thin on the documented record. Palau is not a signatory to the 1951 UN Refugee Convention; the US State Department described the transferees as third-country nationals "with no known criminal histories" and Palau's right to accept or decline each case individually (US Department of State readout, 24 December 2025; Reuters, December 2025). The first transferee documented under the agreement — a Vietnamese man who arrived in May 2026 — left Palau within about two weeks (Island Times reporting via Standing Record, June 2026). What status, work rights, or path any of the up-to-75 would hold in Palau is not documented in the record assembled here; they are recorded as counted in the agreement's arithmetic and not yet counted in any documented Palauan framework for their protection.
  • Women appear in this record mainly as beneficiaries of others' action: the paid-maternity-leave laws he praised in his annual address were passed by the Koror and Airai state governments, not by his national government (Marianas Business Journal annual-address reporting, 2025). No Whipps-specific national initiative on the documented foreign-worker abuse file — whose named at-risk occupations are overwhelmingly women's jobs — appears in the record assembled here; it is recorded as an absence, not an act.

Why: The grade is MIXED because the counting is genuine where his own signature policies run — the wage rises reached all covered workers including noncitizens, the child subsidy was created from nothing, the refunds reached most of the workforce — and stops where the law's own exclusions and the enforcement gap begin. A third of the people who live in Palau work under carve-outs his government did not close and inspections his government did not make, and the newest people his government agreed to receive arrive with no documented protective framework at all. Palauans are counted, increasingly well. Everyone in Palau is not yet the same statement.

Helped: Minimum-wage workers, Palauan and foreign, whose pay rose from $3.50 to $5.00 an hour in a year; parents, who gained Palau's first Child Raising Subsidy; blind residents and wheelchair users, whose stipends rose; retirees, who received government pay-parity increases and a one-time $480 payment; future generations of Palauans, whose ocean estate his deep-sea-mining moratorium position and High Seas Treaty ratification protect. Hurt: Foreign agricultural and domestic workers, excluded from the minimum wage by law and exposed, on the State Department's reporting, to continued mistreatment with ineffective enforcement; low-income households paying the 10% PGST at the till, only partly offset by income-tax refunds; the up to 75 third-country nationals under the deportee memorandum, whose documented protections in Palau amount, on this record, to a case-by-case right of refusal exercised by the government receiving them, not by them.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 21 January 2021, with dates. The two axes are graded independently: word-keeping is graded here; who was helped and who was hurt is graded in the ledgers, and neither axis launders the other.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Taiwan and the United States — the central foreign-policy word, kept at a stated price. On taking office in 2021 Whipps vowed to maintain Palau's recognition of Taiwan and to oppose illegal fishing and trespassing in Palauan waters. Through November 2026 Palau still recognizes Taiwan — one of only three Pacific states that do — despite the documented million-tourist overture from Beijing, the halving of Chinese visitor numbers in 2024, and the cyberattack he publicly attributed to China (Associated Press, August 2024; Lowy Institute Forum preview, August 2026). Under the mandate rule, this is a platform carried out as run on, and it is evidence on this shelf.
  • The Compact — the money word, delivered. He made securing the renewed Compact of Free Association a centrepiece of his first term. The Compact Review Agreement was initialled on 17 May 2023 at $889 million over 20 years — more than double the previous trajectory — and signed into US law on 9 March 2024 as part of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2024, valued at about $890 million for FY2024–FY2043 (Marianas Business Journal, May 2023; US House Natural Resources Committee, March 2024; DFAT country brief, 2026). When US congressional funding stalled in early 2024, his letter to US senators warning that "every day it is not approved plays into the hands of the CCP" was part of the documented pressure that carried it (VOA reporting, February 2024).
  • The minimum wage — a dated promise, met on its dates. He called for the increase from early in his presidency; Public Law 11-34, signed 10 June 2024, delivered $4.25 on 1 October 2024 and $5.00 on 1 October 2025, exactly the phased schedule his March 2024 proposal set out (PINA, March 2024; Marianas Business Journal, June 2024).
  • Tax reform and debt — delivered as described. The 2023 reform he defended as more equitable and easier to enforce than the gross-receipts system it replaced was implemented, broadened the base, and is credited by the IMF with helping turn deficits to surpluses and cut debt from 81% to 67% of GDP (IMF, January 2026; Guam Business Magazine reporting). Growth followed on the same referee's figures: 12% in FY2024 and an estimated 6.7% in FY2025 (IMF, November 2025).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The pension and social-security word — six years in, still a warning. A Social Security bill was first introduced in 2022. In his State of the Republic Address of 22 July 2026 the president was still "advancing" reforms — raising the retirement age to 65, adjusting contributions and benefit formulas — against an unfunded liability of roughly $300 million, warning that without action "the funds will become insolvent, and the promises we made to our elders and to younger workers will be broken" (State of the Republic Address, 22 July 2026). The IMF's January 2026 conclusion likewise urged "swift implementation of pension reforms" (IMF, 30 January 2026). The diagnosis has been his own throughout; the legislation has not passed on the record assembled here. A $20 million US commitment toward the pension fund, secured weeks before the 2024 election, is a documented down payment, not the reform (PINA, November 2024).
  • The new hospital — promised in pieces, unbuilt, its site unanswered. A feasibility study was under way in 2025; the deportee memorandum of December 2025 added a US commitment toward a new hospital; Koror State's documented account is that its offered sites and its request for dialogue went unanswered by the president for over a year (Island Times, January 2026). No completed — or, on this record, sited and started — new national hospital exists at grading.
  • The sanctuary word moved three times. Abolition, then 50%, then a rightsizing to roughly 30% with the reform package sent to the OEK in July 2026 and not, on this record, enacted by November 2026 (NPR, August 2024; State of the Republic Address, July 2026). Each position was stated openly (Test 3 credits the openness); a word restated at three different numbers across six years is, on this test's axis, a word that has not yet landed anywhere. Overall Summary

Surangel Whipps Jr. is graded on nearly six years in a seat that is both head of state and head of government, re-elected in November 2024 by 57.74% to 42.11% over the predecessor whose signature policies — the 80% marine sanctuary above all — he has spent his presidency revising. On one law, the grade is MIXED because the statutes he wrote are genuinely general — a broad-based PGST the IMF credits with cutting debt from 81% to 67% of GDP, refunds reaching about 70% of the workforce, a wage law he wanted applied to all workers — while the documented overlap of at least 37 US defence contracts worth over $5.8 million won by his family's company during his presidency stands with favouritism alleged by his opponent, denied by him, and decided by no referee: helped, low-income workers and the public finances; hurt, smaller firms on the allegation's account, and taxpayers who watched the tax system meet the first family in court. On gripping, the grade is MIXED because the referees demonstrably live — Freedom House at 92 of 100 Free, a Supreme Court that heard the Senate's suit against him and ruled in public in February 2026, a press that kept publishing through a defamation suit from his father's company — and because on the deportee memorandum he signed on 24 December 2025 over the twice-written rejection of both Congress and the Council of Chiefs: helped, the institutions whose authority was exercised rather than abolished; hurt, those same institutions as checks, the Island Times and its editor, and citizens refused a referendum. On the wobble, the grade is MIXED because the sanctuary position adjusted openly across six years — abolition abandoned for a science-argued rightsizing sent to the legislature — and China's coercion was answered with its price stated aloud, while the deportee pushback from inside Palau was overridden and Koror State's hospital-site letters went unanswered for over a year on its governor's account: helped, fishing communities whose case was studied and carried; hurt, the chiefs, the Senate majority, and Koror's petitioners. On counting everyone, the grade is MIXED because his own policies count the bottom first — the wage doubled-stepped to $5.00 for all covered workers, Palau's first Child Raising Subsidy created, disability stipends raised, a deep-sea-mining moratorium held — while foreign workers, a third of the population, remain under statutory minimum-wage carve-outs with enforcement the State Department calls ineffective, and the up-to-75 deportees arrive with no documented protective framework in a state that has not signed the Refugee Convention: helped, wage earners, parents, disabled residents, and the ocean estate; hurt, foreign domestic and farm workers, till-payers on low incomes, and the transferees themselves. On his word, the grade is MIXED because the stance-promises were kept in full — Taiwan held against a million-tourist overture, the Compact's roughly $890 million delivered, the wage paid on its dates — while the schedule-promises recede: pension reform advancing since 2022 against a $300 million unfunded liability, a hospital promised and unsited, a sanctuary restated at three numbers and not yet enacted at any of them.
