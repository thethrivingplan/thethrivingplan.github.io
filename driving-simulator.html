The Open Road

A driving simulator that teaches the whole craft — not just which pedal to press, but why every rule is what it is: the stopping-distance maths with real numbers, why you scan a junction the way you do, why a two-second gap is physics and not politeness. Drive the scenarios, and every mistake comes back as a lesson with the real-world rule and the numbers behind it.

Honest limits — read this first

This page teaches knowledge and judgement. It does not teach car control. No screen can put the feel of a clutch, a wet brake pedal, or a real blind spot into your hands. The real door to driving is real lessons with a qualified instructor in a real car, on real roads, with real practice between them. Treat this page as the theory half of learning to drive — the half you can master here, so the hours in the car are spent on skill, not on rules.

Laws vary by place. Speed limits, right-of-way details, signs, and test standards differ between countries, states, and cities. This page teaches widely shared principles of safe driving. Your own local rules — your highway code, driver handbook, and instructor — are the authority. Where this page and your local rules disagree, your local rules win.

The traffic in the simulator follows simple stated rules (printed with each scenario). Real drivers are less predictable. That gap is itself a lesson: drive for the driver who might do the wrong thing.

The stake: everybody gets home

There are no points to buy anything with here, and no money anywhere in this game — there never was, so there is no money switch to turn off. The stake of every scenario is people arriving safely. Some runs are driven for somebody: a neighbour who needs the pharmacy before it closes, a cousin who cannot be late and cannot be terrified either, your dad and his train. Their trust is the standing you earn or lose — smooth, lawful, unhurried driving earns it; harsh braking, near misses, and collisions cost it, and the debrief grades it in plain words.

What passing unlocks is not currency — it is capability and trust: harder roads, worse weather, and being the driver the people in this game's world ask for by name. Your safety standing (in Your Record, below) rises and falls only on the record's actual evidence — scenarios passed clean, hazards spotted early, collisions and their causes. It cannot be bought, and it is never dressed up as a score in disguise: it is a reputation, and it is earned the slow way, like the real one.

The stopping-distance table — and the maths underneath it Tap to open · the single most important numbers in driving

Your total stopping distance is two distances added together:

Thinking distance — how far the car travels while you see the hazard, decide, and move your foot to the brake. At a typical 1-second reaction, that is simply your speed converted: 1 mph ≈ 0.447 metres per second, so thinking distance in metres ≈ speed (mph) × 0.45. Tired, distracted, or on the phone, reaction time stretches — and this distance stretches with it, at full speed.

Braking distance — how far the car travels while the brakes scrub off speed. Here is the part that surprises everyone: braking distance grows with the square of speed. Double your speed and you need four times the road to stop; triple it and you need nine times. The physics: kinetic energy is ½mv², and brakes can only remove energy at a limited rate, so distance ≈ v² ÷ (2 × deceleration). With firm dry braking (about 6.5 m/s²), braking distance in metres ≈ (0.447 × mph)² ÷ 13.

Try the maths yourself

Why the following-distance rule is what it is: the “two-second rule” is this table turned into something you can measure without a tape. Pick a fixed point, and when the car ahead passes it, count “one-thousand-one, one-thousand-two.” If you pass the same point before you finish, you are closer than two seconds. Two seconds at any speed gives you roughly your thinking distance plus a braking margin — and because it is measured in time, it scales automatically with speed, which a fixed number of metres never could. In the wet, double it to four seconds: the table shows why — your braking distance just doubled.

Right-of-way in plain words Tap to open · who goes first, and why the rule exists

Right-of-way is not a prize and it is never “taken” — it is given. The rules exist to make every driver's next move predictable, because crashes happen when two people predict differently. The widely shared principles:

  • Traffic lights: red means stop behind the line; amber means stop unless you are so close that stopping would be unsafe; green means go if the way is clear — green is permission to proceed, not a promise that nobody is coming.
  • Stop signs: a full stop, every time, even at 3 a.m. on an empty road. Why not slow-and-look? Because the stop buys the one thing a roll cannot: a stationary look in both directions, with time to judge speed — judging an approaching car's speed from a moving car is one of the hardest perceptual tasks in driving.
  • Uncontrolled junctions (no signs or lights): yield to traffic already in the junction, and generally to traffic approaching from your right in right-hand-traffic countries (the mirror image where traffic keeps left — check your local rule). When in doubt, the driver who can stop most safely does.
  • Pedestrians: people on foot at crossings have priority in most places, and a pedestrian already crossing your path has it everywhere that matters. A person is unprotected; the rule prices that in.
  • Emergency vehicles with lights and sirens: move aside and let them through, without breaking other rules to do it.
  • The golden override: if taking your right-of-way would cause a crash, give it away. Being right is no comfort in a wreck. Defensive driving means treating every green light and every priority as a claim to check, not a shield.

Why you scan a junction the way you do — left, right, left again (mirror it where traffic keeps left): the last look goes to the side danger reaches you from first. Between your first look and your move, a fast car can cover a lot of road; the final check is timed to the moment of commitment, not the moment of arrival. In the simulator's junction scenario, cross traffic runs the same simple rule you do — in life, scan as if it might not.

Before you move: mirrors, signal, blind spot Tap to open · the discipline that prevents the “I never saw them” crash

Your mirrors do not show everything. Each side mirror leaves a blind spot roughly beside your rear doors — big enough to hide a whole car, and easily a cyclist or motorbike. That is why the check sequence exists, in this order:

  1. Mirror — interior, then the side you're moving toward. What's behind, and how fast is it closing?
  2. Signal — early enough to be information, not decoration. A signal is a promise about your next few seconds; other drivers plan around it.
  3. Shoulder check — a quick glance over your shoulder into the blind spot, every lane change and every pull-away where something could be alongside. Quick is the point: your eyes are off the road ahead for about a second, which at 30 mph is 13 metres — another reason the mirror work comes first.
  4. Move — smoothly, one lane at a time, and re-check mirrors once you're there.

Before pulling away from parked: all-round check — mirrors, blind spot, and a look for the things mirrors never catch: a child behind the car, a cyclist filtering up the inside, a door about to open on the car in front. Before opening your own door after parking, check the mirror and over your shoulder for passing cyclists — reach across with the far hand and your body turns, making the look almost automatic (often called the Dutch reach).

The pairing principle: mirror + shoulder check go together the way lettuce goes with tomato — each covers exactly the other's weakness. The mirror gives you history and speed; the shoulder check gives you the present tense in the one place the mirror is blind. Either alone is half a check.

Bad-weather theory Tap to open · rain, standing water, fog, ice — and what changes

Rain: grip falls most in the first minutes of rain, when water lifts oil and dust off the surface into a slick film. Braking distances at least double (see the table). The correct response is not “brake harder” — it is slow down earlier and leave a bigger gap, because the gap is the distance you have already bought. In the simulator, switch Weather to Wet and feel the same brake pedal do less.

Hydroplaning (aquaplaning): at speed on standing water, the tyre must shove water out of the way through its tread. Past a speed the tread can no longer clear — roughly when water pressure lifts the tyre onto a film of water — steering and braking simply stop working until speed bleeds off. Worn tyres lower that speed sharply. The defence is all before the puddle: slow down, avoid deep water at speed, keep tyres legal and inflated. If it happens: ease off the gas, hold the wheel straight, do not brake or steer sharply, and let the tyres find the road again as speed falls.

Fog: your limit is not the speed limit — it is stopping within what you can see. If you can see 30 metres, your total stopping distance must fit in 30 metres, which the table says means about 30 mph or less. Use dipped headlights (high beam reflects back and blinds you), fog lights where your local rules allow, and follow road markings rather than the taillights ahead, which may be leading you off the road.

Ice and snow: stopping distances can reach ten times the dry figure. Everything becomes gentle: accelerate, brake, and steer as if there is a full cup of coffee on the dashboard. If the wheels lock or slide, steering input does nothing until grip returns — which is why prevention (speed, gap, gentleness) is the whole game.

Tired driving: the fatigue science Tap to open · the knowledge behind the Long Drive variant

Fatigue is impairment, not weakness. Researchers comparing tiredness with alcohol find that being awake for roughly 17–19 hours slows reactions about as much as a blood-alcohol level near common legal limits — and the tired driver, unlike the drinker, usually believes they are fine. That false confidence is part of the impairment.

Microsleeps: past a certain tiredness the brain sleeps in slices of a few seconds at a time, eyes open, hands on the wheel, nobody home. At 60 mph a four-second microsleep is about 107 metres driven completely blind — longer than the total stopping distance at that speed. You cannot feel one coming reliably, which is why “I'll push through” is not a strategy.

What does not work: opening the window, turning the radio up, singing, slapping your face. These raise alertness for minutes at most and change nothing underneath. Caffeine helps briefly and is a bridge to a stop, not a substitute for one. The only cure for sleepiness is sleep — even a 20-minute nap in a safe parking place measurably restores you.

The two-hour rule: plan a real break at least every two hours of driving — out of the seat, walking, fresh air, 15–20 minutes. It exists because alertness decays on roughly that curve whether you notice it or not; the rule is timed to the biology, not to the fuel gauge. In the Long Drive variant above, the rest area works exactly this way: pulling in is a driving decision, and the game rewards it because the road does.

Capacity, honestly: your energy is a real budget, and it caps how much help you can give. On a long-drive day there are only so many trips in you before the safe answer becomes “not this one, not by me, not now.” This game models that limit through the one long drive rather than a queue of errands — the budget and the choosing are the lesson; a driver who knows their limit is part of how everybody gets home.

Seeing early: hazard perception and the 12-second eye Tap to open · why spotting it early is the whole skill

Official hazard-perception tests (the form this page's Hazard Round copies) score you on when you respond to a developing hazard: click as it starts to develop and you score full marks; click only when it is upon you and you score little or nothing. The scoring is not pedantry — it is the stopping-distance table in disguise. Spotting a hazard two seconds earlier at 30 mph gives you 27 extra metres to work with: often the whole stopping distance.

The technique: rest your eyes 12–15 seconds ahead of the car — at 30 mph that is about 180 metres — and let your gaze sweep: far, near, sides, mirrors, repeat. Hazards announce themselves in clues, not appearances: brake lights two cars ahead, a ball rolling into the road (a child owns that ball), a parked car's wheels turned outward, a pedestrian looking over their shoulder at traffic, a bus at a stop (someone is about to step out from behind it), exhaust vapour from a parked car on a cold morning (its driver is about to pull out).

Cover the brake: when a clue appears but the hazard has not yet developed, move your foot to rest over the brake pedal without pressing. It costs nothing and cuts your reaction time roughly in half — because the slowest part of reacting is not deciding, it is moving your foot.

Parking, step by step Tap to open · bay parking here; parallel parking in the videos below

Bay parking (the simulator's rung): slow to walking pace before the turn — nearly all parking damage happens because entry speed left no time to judge. Signal, check mirrors and blind spot (car parks are full of pedestrians walking behind reversing cars), pick your reference point, turn smoothly, and straighten the wheel the moment the car is parallel with the bay lines. Stopped, centred, handbrake on, then check before opening the door.

Why slow is precise: steering geometry needs distance to change the car's angle. At walking pace you get many small corrections per metre; at speed you get one big, wrong one. Parking is the one driving task where slower is literally more accurate, not just safer.

Parallel parking is taught in the verified video below, in three steps — watch it, then hold the shape of it: alongside, back on lock to the angle, straighten and settle. On the road, add: signal, a mirror-and-shoulder check before swinging out, and never rush because someone is waiting — a hurried park costs more than a waited one.

Strategies — more than one good way, with the trade-offs printed

Keeping your distance

Two-second rule: simple, works at any speed, needs no instruments. Trade-off: it is a minimum for dry roads and an alert driver — it assumes your reactions and brakes are good.

Three-to-four seconds: the wet-weather and tired-driver setting; costs you nothing but a few car lengths of road and buys back the doubled braking distance. Trade-off: in dense traffic, others will fill the gap — let them, and re-open it. The gap is yours to remake, not to defend.

Fixed distance (“a car length per 10 mph”): easy to picture. Trade-off: it scales linearly while braking distance scales as a square — at high speed it quietly under-buys. The time-based rules win because physics is time-shaped.

Approaching a junction

Cover the brake + scan cycle: foot hovering, eyes left-right-left. Costs a little speed early; buys reaction time and a final check at the moment of commitment.

Commit early and briskly: when a gap is clearly there, taking it promptly keeps traffic predictable. Trade-off: “clearly” is doing all the work — a gap you must hurry for is not a gap. If you cannot cross at a calm pace, wait for the next one.

Creep for a view: where parked cars block sightlines, edge forward slowly until you can see. Trade-off: your nose enters the road before your eyes do — creep at walking pace, ready to stop instantly, and never let impatience turn a creep into a lunge.

Merging onto a highway

Match speed on the ramp: use the slip road to reach traffic speed, then slot into a gap. This is the standard strategy and the simulator's: merging slowly forces highway traffic to brake, and a speed difference is itself a hazard — closing speeds are what make collisions violent.

Yield and wait: if no gap exists, the ramp's end is a yield, not a launch. Trade-off: stopping on a ramp feels wrong and invites a rear-end risk from behind — which is why matching speed early, while you still have ramp left, is the main skill.

When a hazard develops

Brake in a straight line: maximum braking happens with the wheels pointing straight; braking hard and swerving splits the tyres' grip between two jobs and does both worse (grip is a budget — steering and braking spend from the same account).

Swerve instead: at low speed, or when stopping distance plainly exceeds the distance available and a clear escape exists, steering around can be the right call. Trade-off: you are betting on the escape route staying clear — check it is real (mirror history, shoulder knowledge) before you take it.

The horn is a tool, not a verdict: a short alert can freeze a pedestrian in place or wake a drifting driver. Use it to inform, early — not to complain, late.
