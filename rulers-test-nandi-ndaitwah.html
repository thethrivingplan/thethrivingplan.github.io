The Ruler's Test — Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah, President of Namibia

Subject: Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah — President of the Republic of Namibia since 21 March 2025. The seat graded is the presidency, which in Namibia is both head of state and head of government: the president appoints and dismisses the prime minister, ministers, deputy ministers and regional governors, commands the executive under Article 32 of the constitution, and — the file that dominates this dossier — is seeking through legislation to become the licensing authority for the country's upstream oil and gas sector. Nandi-Ndaitwah, born 29 October 1952 at Onamutai, is a SWAPO liberation-movement veteran who served as foreign minister and, from February 2024, as vice president under Nangolo Mbumba after President Hage Geingob's death in office. She won the presidential election of 27–30 November 2024 with 683,560 votes (58.7%), ahead of Panduleni Itula of the Independent Patriots for Change (IPC) at about 25.8% (Wikipedia tabulation of official results; Britannica gives "more than 57 percent"), becoming Namibia's fifth president and its first woman president. The election itself was troubled: ballot shortages and technical failures forced voting to be extended over up to three days, the IPC and another party challenged the results, and opposition parties alleged — an ALLEGATION recorded here as theirs — that the failures were intentional; the Supreme Court dismissed the challenge on 28 February 2025, and she was inaugurated on 21 March 2025, the 35th anniversary of independence. SWAPO retained a reduced National Assembly majority (51 of 96 elected seats). She is graded on roughly twenty months in office, against a SWAPO era that supplies the context several tests turn on: the party has governed without interruption since 1990, and the two largest corruption files in the country's history — the Fishrot fishing-quota scandal (arrests in 2019, still untried) and the Namcor fuel scandal (losses exceeding N$1.2 billion in 2022/23) — are inheritances from that era, as are a public debt above N$200 billion (about 70% of GDP), a youth unemployment rate of 44.4% (Namibia Statistics Agency), and the worst drought in a century, declared a national emergency by her predecessor in May 2024. Her predecessors Geingob (2015–2024) and Mbumba (2024–2025) appear as context where the record she inherited, or the promises the seat carried into the election, bear directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic of the Test, printed above the five tests: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one — the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what she ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Marks are lost only for how it is done: a second law for her own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect her (Test 4). Test 3 also grades whether, when another option became available, she at least considered it openly; considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence — refusing to look at it is. Under every test, a plain-words why and a helped/hurt ledger close the section: who her actions on that test helped, and who they hurt, named as the record allows. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes, and the Overall Summary names which of the four crossings occurred. Where the record shows a situation in which every available option carried real harm, the dossier says so, names the options and why each was bad, and grades the choice as the best possible available in the moment — while still grading whether she built the trap herself.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The first minister she lost was her own pick, and she removed him herself, fast. Mac-Albert Hengari, appointed Minister of Agriculture, Fisheries, Water and Land Reform on 22 March 2025 from among her own top-eight parliamentary nominees, was arrested on 26 April 2025 after allegedly attempting to bribe a complainant to withdraw a rape case against him; he faces 11 charges including rape, kidnapping and defeating the ends of justice, all ALLEGATIONs he denies, with bail refused (The Namibian and NBC reporting, April 2025; presidency statement, 27 April 2025). When he did not resign as requested, she dismissed him under Article 32(6) effective 23 April 2025 and withdrew his National Assembly seat — the law applied at full strength to a man she had personally elevated five weeks earlier (Presidency statement via X, 27 April 2025).
  • The second dismissal was also inside her own house, and it enforced a rule she had set for everyone. Natangwe Ithete, her deputy prime minister and Minister of Industry, Mines and Energy, was dismissed on 26 October 2025 after he approved the renewal of Paragon Oil & Gas's PEL 37 exploration licence in July 2025 — a renewal her predecessor's minister had refused, made during a moratorium she had imposed and in breach of her instruction that no licence be renewed or block awarded without consultation with her: "I told the minister, 'Let me first understand – there should be no renewal, there should be no oil block giving, unless we consult one another'" (The Namibian, October 2025 and 2026 reporting; Africa Risk Consulting, November 2025). The Namibian reported the firing as over alleged misconduct and cabinet interference — ALLEGATIONs, officially unconfirmed, the presidency having given no reason beyond coordination (The Namibian, 27 October 2025).
  • The inherited flagship corruption case has been prosecuted on her watch without visible interference from her office. In July 2025 the Anti-Corruption Commission (ACC) arrested and charged the former leadership of Namcor — former managing director Immanuel Mulunga and former managers and business associates, 13 to 14 accused in all — over the 2022 Enercon filling-station transaction (N$53.2 million paid for assets never delivered) and fuel-credit losses; the accused include figures of the SWAPO-era state apparatus, six were refused bail by the Windhoek Magistrate's Court in September 2025, and the case proceeds through ordinary court process, every charge an ALLEGATION until verdict (Confidente, July 2025; Windhoek Magistrate's Court reporting, September 2025). The conduct predates her presidency; the prosecution's freedom to run does not.
  • She subsequently ordered the questionable licence decision itself reviewed through legal channels: in 2026 she requested the attorney general's legal opinion on the PEL 37 renewal — its four-year term against a two-year regulatory limit for a third renewal, and the roles of both the dismissed minister and Petroleum Commissioner Maggy Shino (The Namibian, 2026 reporting).
  • One directive of her first year put officials under the same roof as citizens: she directed that public servants use public health facilities — the same state hospitals ordinary Namibians use — a rule whose existence is documented by the March 2026 teachers' protest that listed it among its grievances (Windhoek Observer, March 2026). In October 2026 she publicly called on traditional leaders to combat corruption and advance national development (The Namibian, 20 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The conflict-of-interest question sits at the center of the seat and remains answered only by her own denial. In February 2026 The Namibian published "President under fire for family oil interest," reporting concerns that her family was moving to position itself in the oil sector; she responded categorically — "my children have no interests, direct or indirect, in the oil and gas sector" — and demanded a retraction and public apology, or credible verifiable evidence (presidential statement, February 2026). In October–November 2026 IPC leader Panduleni Itula renewed the charge in parliament's oil-bill debate, citing Tradeport Namibia, a company he said is led by one of her sons and active in the petroleum logistics chain, and the First Gentleman's patronage of the Namibia Youth Energy Forum — concluding "the presidency controls who gets oil blocks… this is not governance" (IPC press conference, reported November 2026). These are ALLEGATIONs by her principal political opponent, contested by her on the record, and no independent investigation documented in the record assembled here has resolved them either way; the dossier records the file as open, and grades Test 1 on the fact that the only resolution offered so far is the interested office's own word (see also Test 2, on the journalist removed for asking the question).
  • The outcome measure moved the wrong way, to the worst reading in the country's history. Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025 scored Namibia 46 of 100 (65th of 182), down three points from 49 — the largest single-year decline Namibia has recorded (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released February 2026). The index measures perception, not conduct, and its period straddles her first year; it is recorded as the environment's verdict, which she herself lamented in Parliament (ISS Africa, 2026).
  • The referee institution for corruption remains unreformed on her watch. The ACC is described by Namibia analyst Henning Melber as "a toothless lion — understaffed and mainly focused on small fry instead of big fish"; when the IPC called for more ACC funding, she answered that "millions and millions" would not deliver clean government without a change of mindset — a stated reason, recorded as such, whose institutional consequence is that the commission enters the oil era with the same structure that failed the fishing era (ISS Africa, 2026). The Fishrot case — the largest corruption file in the country's history, its accused arrested in 2019 — remains untried seven years on; the delay is the judiciary's and her predecessors', but the IPC's charge that her posture is to "look forward and not back" is recorded as the opposition's characterization, unanswered in substance in the record here (ISS Africa, 2026).
  • The newest file is unanswered at grading. In November 2026 Affirmative Repositioning leader Job Amupanda alleged irregularities in fuel supply to the defence ministry by August 26 Holdings, the military-owned company — an unlicensed fuel role, intermediary companies, a foreign truck delivering to a military base without transport authorisation (Windhoek Observer, November 2026). August 26 denied the claims, announced an independent internal investigation, and tightened its own procurement rules (company statements, November 2026). No ACC action or presidential response appears in the record assembled here; the file is days old at grading and is recorded as an open file, not a finding.

Why this grade: Inside her own cabinet the law has been one law, enforced at personal cost — she fired her own agriculture minister on criminal charges and her own deputy prime minister for breaking her licence rule, and let the ACC's biggest prosecution run against the state-oil old guard. That is a stronger FOR shelf than most first-year files in this series. What keeps the grade from HOLDS is the center: the one conflict question that touches her own family has been met with denial and a retraction demand rather than any independent process that could clear it, the perception index fell to a record low on her watch, and the anti-corruption commission goes into the oil era unreformed. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the complainant in the Hengari case, whose alleged bribery approach was answered with the minister's removal rather than protection (the criminal charges remain ALLEGATIONs before the courts); the Namibian public's claim on its oil licences, protected in the one documented instance where a minister broke the renewal rule; taxpayers, in whose name the Namcor accused now answer in open court. Hurt — Mac-Albert Hengari and Natangwe Ithete, removed from office by her hand (the first on charges unproven, the second on reasons never officially stated); the officials and firms under the Namcor cloud, whose cases proceed slowly through bail refusals and postponements; and public confidence itself, which the CPI's record-low reading measures falling while the family-interest question stands unresolved.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The court that seated her was not hers, and it ruled on her own election before she held any power over it. The Supreme Court heard the IPC's challenge to the November 2024 election — an election run by an Electoral Commission whose logistical failures were documented by all sides — and dismissed the challenge on 28 February 2025; she took office under that judgment, and no judge has been removed, transferred or publicly threatened by her since (Britannica, 2025). No electoral commissioner, prosecutor-general, central bank governor or ACC director-general has been dismissed or punished in the record of her twenty months.
  • Parliament checks her in the open, and its machinery works against her preferences. Her State of the Nation addresses include unscripted question sessions in which the PDM's McHenry Venaani pressed her on youth unemployment and called the genocide settlement "apartheid-style" to her face (Windhoek Observer, April 2026). Opposition lawmakers walked out of the petroleum bill debate and remain free to call for a vote of no confidence, as Itula did in February 2026 (Africa Press, February 2026). In September 2026, when her government's first nominee to chair the Land Tribunal did not meet the criteria, the nomination was withdrawn and an alternative candidate approved by both sides of the House (National Assembly proceedings, September 2026).
  • The critical press operates, and publishes the hardest stories about her personally. The Namibian — the country's leading independent paper — published the family-oil-interest investigation in February 2026 and the PEL 37 reporting that underpins this dossier; the Windhoek Observer, Confidente and the Namibian Sun cover her ministers' failures weekly. Namibia's civic environment remains comparatively open, with no internet shutdowns or systematic censorship (Hivos Enabling Environment Snapshot, 2026; Intelwatch media-threats mapping, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The petroleum file concentrates the referees into her own office, by design. The Upstream Petroleum Unit was created inside the Presidency in May 2025, with special advisors Kornelia Shilunga and Carlo McLeod appointed by her to run upstream oversight (The Brief, October 2025). After dismissing Ithete in October 2025 she assumed the Industry, Mines and Energy portfolio herself — placing defence minister Frans Kapofi in interim oversight on 28 October 2025 — and no substantive successor appears in the record at grading, a year later (Africa Risk Consulting, November 2025; ministry listing, 2026). The Petroleum (Exploration and Production) Amendment Bill would complete the structure in law: licensing, renewal, revocation and royalty decisions move from a minister answerable to parliament to a unit in the Office of the President whose director-general she appoints and directs. Former prime minister Nahas Angula — of her own party — called it "a big risk" that weakens appeals and technical vetting; analysts note the bill sets no fixed terms, removal grounds or qualifications for the unit's heads (The Namibian via The Free Library, 2026; Cliffe Dekker Hofmeyr analysis, 2026). The National Assembly approved the bill at second reading on 20 October 2026; it is not yet law at grading, and its passage runs through parliament rather than decree — which is why this shelf records a documented centralization in progress, not a completed seizure (Namibia Oil and Gas, November 2026).
  • The media's access to her personally has narrowed to the point of physical removal. On 2 February 2026, journalist Jemima Beukes — editor of The Whistle Namibia — attempted to question the president at State House about the alleged family oil interests and was escorted out by security officers; Beukes states that officers photographed her car, threatened her with arrest and followed her vehicle, and the Namibia Media Professionals' Union described "physical manhandling and the confiscation of a colleague's phone" (CPJ statement, 4 February 2026; Nampu statement, February 2026 — Beukes's account, which CPJ could not independently verify in all parts). The Presidency defended the removal on protocol grounds and reaffirmed its commitment to media freedom (Presidency statement, February 2026). The Editors Forum of Namibia has noted that she had not held a press conference since taking office (Intelwatch, 2026). The African Media Barometer's overall score for Namibia declined from 3.1 (2022) to 2.7 (2026), a decline its authors attribute to structural pressures rather than a collapse of freedom (African Media Barometer, June 2026).
  • A minister's attack on a journalist stands uncorrected in the record. In January 2026 Urban and Rural Development Minister James Sankwasa subjected The Namibian journalist Tracy Tafirenyika to xenophobic remarks following her reporting on his outstanding municipal bills; CPJ grouped the incident with the Beukes removal as raising "concerns about their willingness to face public scrutiny" (CPJ, February 2026; Hivos snapshot, 2026). No public discipline of the minister by the president appears in the record assembled here — from a president who has dismissed two ministers for lesser-documented causes.

Why this grade: No existing referee has been gripped: the courts, the electoral commission, the prosecution and the press all still act against her interests in public, and the one completed transfer of power in this file — hers — ran through a court judgment she did not control. What blocks HOLDS is the direction of construction. In the sector that will define the country's next fifty years, she has built — administratively first, legislatively in progress — a structure in which the licensing referee sits inside her office, under her appointees, while she personally holds the parent ministry; and at her own door, the journalist who asked the conflict question was removed by security rather than answered. The grip is prospective and sectoral, not general; it is also deliberate and documented. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the presidency's own coordination, which gains a single desk for oil decisions ahead of the Venus final investment decision expected in late 2026; investors seeking one accountable counterpart, a benefit TotalEnergies and industry bodies have publicly valued (African Energy Chamber, 2026). Hurt — parliament, whose power to question the licensing authority is designed out of the petroleum bill; future applicants and the public, whose appeals route runs to the office that decided; Jemima Beukes and the colleagues whose phone was confiscated, on the union's account; and Tracy Tafirenyika, left without a presidential word after a minister's xenophobic remarks.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The genocide file shows an inherited position adjusted under pressure, in the open. The 2021 Joint Declaration with Germany (€1.1 billion over 30 years) was negotiated by her predecessors and rejected by major descendant bodies from the start. Under her, Namibia rejected the declaration's chapter-closing clause — "there was article 21 that was talking about closing the chapter. And that's where we say, 'No, this we cannot'" — secured revisions (implementation shortened toward 23 years; the €1.1 billion "not necessarily the final amount," April 2026), promised that the revised text "will be tabled before the National Assembly prior to signing," and carried the reparations demand onto the international stage at a 2026 Accra conference on colonial reparations (State of the Nation Address Q&A, April 2026; Africa Briefing, November 2026). The first Genocide Remembrance Day was observed on 28 May 2025, two months into her tenure.
  • When her own minister's licence decision became the wobble, she answered with process rather than only with the dismissal: the attorney general's legal review of the PEL 37 renewal, covering the dismissed minister's and the petroleum commissioner's roles, was sought and confirmed by State House as running "through the appropriate institutional and legal channels" (The Namibian, 2026).
  • The free-education announcement met capacity and funding pushback and was adjusted in the open rather than abandoned or denied: implementation was phased — first-time undergraduates at public institutions, tuition and registration covered, accommodation, meals and postgraduate study excluded — defended by her personally at the second State of the Nation address with a N$663 million allocation and an argument ("we cannot prevent people from proceeding because there is no space. We have to create that space") rather than a retreat (Windhoek Observer, April 2026; NSFAF 2026 application round).
  • Parliament's jobs challenge was answered with a clarification, not a denial: pressed by Venaani on the 500,000-jobs pledge, she stated openly that "not all of these jobs are going to be created by the government" and that skills come first (SONA Q&A, April 2026). The street's pushback has likewise been processed inside the rules: the Teachers' Union of Namibia's 21 March 2026 protest — against the five percent salary increase and her public-health-facilities directive — was approved by police with a negotiated route to Parliament, and proceeded (Windhoek Observer, March 2026).
  • She accounts on a schedule she set herself: a public report on her first three months in office, delivered with the NDP6 launch on 21 July 2025, and a monitoring dashboard for cabinet decisions announced at her first cabinet meeting (Presidency statement, 21 July 2025; Windhoek Observer, March 2025).

Evidence against:

  • On the petroleum structure — the loudest institutional pushback of her tenure, from the opposition, from analysts, and from a former prime minister of her own party — she has not adjusted at all. The presidency "rebuked" the concentration claims, restated the efficiency rationale, and carried the bill through its second reading on 20 October 2026 with the appointment and oversight design unchanged in the record (Namibia Oil and Gas, November 2026). Another option — a statutorily independent regulator, fixed terms for the unit's heads, a parliamentary confirmation step — was available and publicly proposed by critics; the record shows it neither adopted nor answered on its merits.
  • The family-interest wobble was answered with a denial plus a demand that the newspaper retract and apologize, and the follow-up question at State House was answered with the questioner's removal (Test 2). A denial is an answer; a retraction demand backed by a security escort is pressure on the referee, and the distinction is recorded here rather than merged.
  • The ACC funding question was declined with stated reasons — mindset over money — which this Test does not count as refusal to look; what is recorded is the outcome left standing: the commission's structure, staffing and "small fry" docket unchanged a year later, while the president's own speeches escalated the rhetoric to treason (ISS Africa, 2026).
  • Her signature land reform has drifted rather than adjusted. The Land Bill — consolidating land law, banning foreign acquisition of communal and commercial land, creating compulsory-acquisition powers with just compensation — was tabled on 30 September 2025 and remained at second-reading debate on the National Assembly order papers of 30 September and 1 October 2026, thirteen months later, not law (National Assembly Order Papers, 2026; conveyancing review, October 2026). The ancestral land rights and restitution legislation recommended by the 2021 commission of inquiry remains unenacted.

Why this grade: The pattern splits cleanly by whose ox is gored. Where events, inherited negotiations or her own ministers push back, she adjusts in the open — a clause struck from the genocide text, a legal review commissioned, a flagship policy phased with reasons given, a jobs pledge clarified to her critics' faces. Where the pushback targets the presidency's own new powers or her family's proximity to them, the answer is rebuke, retraction demands and an unchanged bill. Both halves are documented; neither cancels the other. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — genocide descendant communities who demanded the chapter stay open, and parliament, which gains a promised tabling before signature; students and institutions, who got a funded, phased scheme instead of a collapsed announcement; the public record itself, which holds her three-month accounting and her SONA answers. Hurt — the descendant factions (the Okandjoze Chiefs Assembly, the Nama Traditional Leaders Association, Chief Mutjinde Katjiua's Ovaherero Traditional Authority) who remain outside the negotiating room and in the High Court against the declaration even after the adjustments; critics of the petroleum bill, whose proposed safeguards went unanswered; and The Namibian's editors, answering a retraction demand for a story no authority has disproven.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the descendant communities, the San and other marginalised groups, the landless, the unemployed young, and the regions that did not vote for her — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The first structural count of her presidency was of women, into power itself. Her cabinet of 14 ministers and 7 deputy ministers — cut from 21 ministries — carries a majority of women across its portfolios, with Lucia Witbooi as vice president, the first woman to hold the presidency's deputy office in Namibia's history, and women at finance (Erica Shafudah) and education (Sanet Steenkamp) (Britannica, 2025; Presidency statement, 21 July 2025). "My presidency will be for all Namibians," she pledged as president-elect, regardless of political affiliation (SABC interview, December 2024).
  • The flagship social policy is universal within its stated bounds: from the 2026 academic year every eligible first-time undergraduate at a public university and every trainee at a public vocational centre pays no tuition and no registration fees — up to 134,000 students in 2026 on the education ministry's estimate — with NUST refunding registration fees already paid by eligible students (SONA, April 2025; NSFAF and university reporting, 2026). The National Youth Development Fund, capitalised at N$500 million with N$257 million allocated for 2025/26, had disbursed N$62.3 million to 140 youth projects across the regions by March 2026 (NAMPA, March 2026).
  • The hungry were counted through the drought she inherited: the emergency relief declared by her predecessor in May 2024 ran under her government until August 2025, and the measured outcome improved sharply — 456,000 people (15% of the analysed population) in IPC Phase 3 or above in July–September 2025, down from 1.15 million in the same period of 2024, an improvement the IPC attributes mainly to above-average rainfall, with government and partner interventions contributing (IPC acute food insecurity analysis, October 2025; WFP Annual Country Report 2025). The counts are printed separately and not blended: 456,000 current (Jul–Sep 2025); 612,000 projected for the October 2025–March 2026 lean season; 408,000 projected for April–June 2026; the prior year's figures, 1.15–1.26 million, belong to the drought peak under her predecessor.
  • The genocide dead were counted into the national calendar: Genocide Remembrance Day, 28 May, was observed for the first time on 28 May 2025 under her presidency, and her government kept the descendant communities' core demand — that the chapter not be closed — inside the negotiation (Test 3).

Evidence against:

  • The drought-relief decision is this test's no-good-choice file, and it is graded by the clause's mechanics. The options as they stood in mid-2025: continue universal countrywide relief indefinitely — against a public debt above N$200 billion (about 70% of GDP), a relief programme designed as an emergency measure, and the best harvest in years already in; or end the programme and accept the lean-season rise the IPC then projected. She ended it in August 2025. The trap was inherited — the drought, the debt and the emergency were her predecessor's — and the constraint is recorded here, not counted against her. What remains on the ledger is the choice's arithmetic: the IPC projected 612,000 people (20%) in Crisis or worse for October 2025–March 2026, naming the relief discontinuation among the causes, with Kunene, Kavango West and Zambezi in Phase 3 — and no targeted bridge for those regions appears in the record assembled here (IPC, October 2025).
  • The young who elected her are counted in her speeches and not yet in her outcomes. Youth unemployment stands at 44.4% (NSA); overall unemployment 36.9%, above 50% in some regions (IPC, 2025). In April 2026 the police inspector-general reported more than 53,000 applications for 2,000 police posts (NAMPA, April 2026). Her own second SONA counted the delivery: 1,503 jobs created by 13 operational investment projects, 1,934 more from projects in progress, 722 from the Youth Fund's first disbursements — real counts, honestly stated, and a small fraction of the constituency that marched, applied and waited (Windhoek Observer, April 2026; NAMPA, March 2026).
  • The descendant communities remain outside the room where their file is decided. The Okandjoze Chiefs Assembly and the Nama Traditional Leaders Association rejected the Joint Declaration again in November 2026 — "nothing about us, without us" — presenting their own N$12.9 trillion reparations valuation in September 2026; Chief Katjiua's faction litigates against the declaration in the Windhoek High Court; UN special rapporteurs have criticized the process's exclusion of affected communities (NAMPA, November 2026; Bundestag reporting, 2026). Her adjustments (Test 3) improved the text; they did not change who sits at the table.
  • The landless are still waiting on the law that counts them in. Twenty-four farms were acquired for redistribution in 2025/26, and of the 176 resolutions of the 2018 Second National Land Conference, 25 are fully implemented and 134 ongoing, on her agriculture minister's own count to parliament (Ministry budget motivation, April 2026). The Land Bill that would ban foreign land acquisition and empower compulsory purchase remains unpassed (Test 3), and the ancestral restitution act recommended in 2021 remains unenacted — for the communities whose dispossession is the country's founding grievance, the count is still a promise.

Why this grade: Her counting machinery is genuinely universal where it has been switched on — free public tertiary education for every eligible student, a youth fund reaching all regions, a cabinet that counts women into the majority, a calendar that counts the genocide dead. The failures are of reach and of room: a relief programme switched off ahead of a projected lean-season rise (graded with its inherited constraint recorded), a youth constituency counted in rhetoric at a scale the job numbers do not yet approach, and the two constituencies with the oldest claims — the descendant communities and the landless — still outside the negotiating room and the statute book respectively. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — up to 134,000 students and trainees whose fees fell away in 2026, and their households; the 140 youth projects funded across the regions; women entering the executive at majority scale; households carried through the drought's tail by relief that ran until August 2025. Hurt — the projected 612,000 people in the lean-season count after relief ended, concentrated in Kunene, Kavango West and Zambezi (a projection, printed as one, in a separate count from the measured 456,000); the unemployed young — 44.4% of their cohort — for whom the counted jobs so far number in the low thousands; the Ovaherero and Nama descendant bodies still litigating for a seat; and the landless waiting on an unpassed bill.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 21 March 2025, with dates; her predecessors' completed words stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The lean government — delivered on day one, as specified. She campaigned on ending "business as usual" and restructuring the state; on 22 March 2025 she appointed a cabinet of 14 ministers and 7 deputy ministers, down from 21 ministries, with deputy-minister posts slashed and all 14 regional governors appointed under the new structure (Presidency statement, 21 July 2025; Britannica, 2025).
  • Free tertiary education — delivered, in a narrowed form recorded in full below. Announced in her first State of the Nation Address on 24 April 2025 — "from the next academic year, commencing 2026, tertiary education will become 100% subsidised by the government" — the scheme opened with the 2026 academic year: N$663 million allocated, NSFAF applications run, registration fees refunded at NUST, up to 134,000 students eligible (SONA, 24 April 2025; Windhoek Observer, April 2026).
  • The delivery discipline — machinery built and used. Ministers were directed to sign performance agreements aligned to NDP6; she warned public servants publicly, "Before I lose my job as president, because Namibians voted for me and have the power to fire me, I will fire you first for not doing what needs to be done" — and two of her own ministers were gone within eight months, one on criminal charges and one for breaking her licence rule (Tests 1–2). She reported publicly on her first three months on 21 July 2025, a self-imposed accounting no predecessor in the seat had made a habit of (Presidency statement, July 2025).
  • The genocide word — kept in its specific. She said the chapter would not be closed and the revised declaration would come to parliament before signature; the closing clause was struck, the text revised, and at grading it remains unsigned and untabled — the promise's letter intact, its completion pending (Test 3).
  • The predecessors' words, as context for the seat: Geingob's government negotiated the 2021 Joint Declaration and the 2018 land conference whose 176 resolutions her minister now counts at 25 implemented; Mbumba declared the drought emergency in May 2024 and held the seat through an orderly transfer. The seat she holds was passed to her, on schedule and by the book, twice in thirteen months — a kept institutional word that is SWAPO's strongest exhibit and the baseline her own tenure is graded against.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The 500,000 jobs — off its own arithmetic at twenty months. The campaign pledge, repeated at the Public-Private Forum in October 2025: "Our goal is bold yet achievable, to create 500,000 jobs over the next five years," backed by a promised N$85 billion in programme spending. The term runs to 2030 and the word is not yet formally broken; the trajectory is.
