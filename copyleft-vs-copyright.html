One page, both sides — nothing hidden

Copyright fences it in.
Copyleft keeps it free.

Both are built on the same law. Copyright says “ask me first.” Copyleft uses that same power to say the opposite: “use it, change it, pass it on — just don’t lock it up.” This page lays out the real pros and cons of each, including the hard ones for copyleft.

The two ideas, in plain words

If you only read this section, you’ll have the whole argument.

The fence

Copyright — “All rights reserved”

When you make something original — a song, a book, a photo, a design, code — copyright arrives automatically. You don’t have to file for it.

  • What it gives you: the legal right to decide who may copy, change, sell, or build on the work.
  • How long it lasts: in the U.S., usually the creator’s life plus 70 years.
  • The default: nobody may use it without permission, unless an exception applies.

Derivative work — that phrase means a new work built from an old one, like a remix, a sequel, or a product built from a blueprint.

The ratchet

Copyleft — “These freedoms travel”

Copyleft is not the opposite of copyright and it is not “no copyright.” It is a copyright licence — a set of permissions the creator attaches to the work.

  • What it gives everyone: permission to use, copy, change, and share the work — and to sell a physical thing built from it.
  • The one condition: anything you pass on must carry the same freedoms. You can’t take it private.
  • Why “ratchet”? A ratchet only turns one way. Freedom can stay or spread. It can’t be removed.

Permissive licence — a different kind of free licence (like MIT or BSD for code) that lets you pass work on without keeping it free. Copyleft supporters and permissive supporters disagree about that one condition — see the honest cons below.

The line that sums up copyleft:

“A copylefted design literally cannot be sold — because it is already free.” You can sell the build, the materials, the craft, and the service. You can’t sell exclusive ownership of the design itself.

The pros and cons — both shelves, full

A fair page names the other side’s best points before answering them. Tap any line to open it.

Copyright — what it’s genuinely good at

Pro: Earning leverage for a single creator+

Inside the system as it stands, owning the rights means you can charge for copies, licences, and adaptations. For an individual artist or inventor with no other income, that leverage can be the difference between making a living and not.

Pro: Control over how a work is used+

The owner can stop a use they find harmful, misleading, or against the work’s purpose, and can choose who represents it.

Pro: A clear, familiar legal tool+

Courts, publishers, and businesses everywhere understand it. That familiarity makes deals, insurance, and investment easier today.

Copyright — where it costs everybody

Con: It encloses the commons+

The commons means the shared pool of ideas, knowledge, and culture everyone can draw from. Every fence around a work shrinks that pool a little, even when the work was built from the pool to begin with — and every work is.

Con: Permission culture+

When the default is “ask first,” a lot of good uses never happen: the owner can’t be found, doesn’t reply, or says no for no stated reason. Teachers, builders, and small creators feel this first.

Con: Orphan works nobody may touch+

An orphan work is a work still under copyright whose owner can’t be identified or found. Libraries and archives hold millions of them — preserved, but legally unusable.

Con: Terms that outrun any incentive+

Life plus 70 years is far longer than any creator needs as an incentive to create. For most of that time the work isn’t earning for the creator at all — it’s earning rent for whoever holds the rights, or earning nothing while locked away.

Con: Takedown abuse+

Automated and mistaken takedowns regularly remove lawful uses — criticism, teaching, parody, and even creators’ own work. Fighting a bad takedown costs the small person more than the large one.

Copyleft — what it’s genuinely good at

Pro: Freedom that can’t be revoked downstream+

Ordinary generosity can be undone by the next person in line. Copyleft can’t. Because the condition travels with the work, nobody downstream can turn a free design into a private monopoly.

Pro: Everyone can build, fix, and improve+

When the design is free, the best fix can come from anywhere — not only from the original maker. Improvements flow back into the shared pool instead of dying inside one company.

Pro: It works inside today’s law+

Copyleft doesn’t wait for copyright law to be reformed. It uses the law that already exists, today, with a licence anyone can attach in minutes.

Pro: Proof it scales+

Linux, Wikipedia (CC BY-SA), the RepRap 3D-printer project (GPL), and Arduino’s open designs all grew under copyleft or share-alike terms. This is not a theory that has never been tested.

Copyleft — the honest cons

“I don’t see any cons” is a good instinct to test, not a conclusion to print. Here are the cons on the record — most of them are cons on the current system’s terms, plus one restriction that is deliberate.

Con 1: Enforcement costs time and money+

A licence doesn’t enforce itself. Software history is full of copyleft violations that were only fixed because somebody noticed, documented, and pushed — sometimes in court. Small creators have the least capacity to do that pushing.

Con 2: It restricts one freedom — the freedom to enclose+

Permissive-licence advocates make this as a principled objection: “You call it free, but you force sharing.” The copyleft answer is plain: yes — it restricts the freedom to restrict others. A fair page prints the objection before the answer.

Con 3: Friction and compatibility mazes+

Different free licences don’t always combine cleanly, and some companies refuse to touch copylefted code at all. That can slow adoption in the world as it is, even when the work itself is better.

Con 4: It governs copying, not conduct+

Copyleft controls the terms of copying. It does not, by itself, stop a bad actor building something harmful from a free design. That needs other layers — law, norms, and design choices — not just a licence.

Con 5: “So how does the creator eat?”+

The first question every listener asks, and a real tension under the current system. The answers that exist: sell the physical build, the craft, the support, the teaching, the service around the free design. Red Hat built a major company selling support around free software. But the answer has to be said out loud — a creator who only knows how to sell copies is being asked to learn a new way to eat.

Con 6: The structural irony+

Copyleft rides on copyright. Abolish copyright tomorrow and copyleft’s lever disappears — though so would most of the need for it. Copyleft is best understood as a bridge built from the materials of the system it’s trying to outgrow.

Which copyleft licence for what?

Three names you’ll see on free work. Each explains itself here — you don’t need a glossary.

CC BY-SA 4.0

For: writing, pages, images, designs, documentation.

Plain meaning: Creative Commons Attribution–ShareAlike. Use it and adapt it, credit the source, and share your version under the same licence.

GPL

For: code and software.

Plain meaning: the GNU General Public License — the original copyleft licence, written by the Free Software Foundation. Distribute the code, and the freedoms (including source code) go with it.

CERN-OHL-S

For: hardware designs and blueprints.

Plain meaning: CERN Open Hardware Licence, strongly-reciprocal version. Build and sell the physical thing freely; the design files stay free and travel with it.

What is not granted

A name or brand is separate from the work. Copylefting a design does not give away the project’s name, and does not let anyone claim their version is the original.

What if somebody actually did it?

“Wow — it would be a real shock to the system if somebody copylefted every blueprint they could think of.”

Here’s the thing worth saying out loud: it wouldn’t be a hypothetical. Under the standing copyleft rule for The Thriving Plan, every blueprint, page, and publication released is already copyleft — writings and pages under CC BY-SA 4.0, hardware blueprints under CERN-OHL-S, code under GPL. Anything made from that work carries the same freedom. The name — The Thriving Plan — is not part of the grant.

That’s the hint worth dropping, in the video and anywhere else: not “imagine if somebody did this,” but “somebody is doing it, the designs are already free, and here they are.” A shock to the system lands harder when it’s not a wish — it’s a link: The Thriving Plan ↗

For the video: a spine you can shoot from

Kept short on the face. The fuller reasoning is in the pros and cons above — one tap up.

  1. Open on the fence: “Copyright’s default move is a fence — all rights reserved, ask me.”
  2. Show the ratchet: “Copyleft uses that same law to do the opposite. Use it, change it, build on it, sell what you build — just don’t lock it up.”
  3. Name the best objection before anyone else can: “So how does the creator eat?” — then give the build / craft / service answer.
  4. Deliver the punchline: “A copylefted design literally cannot be sold, because it is already free.”
  5. Close with the shock — as a fact, not a tease: “It would be a real shock to the system if somebody copylefted every blueprint they could think of. So we did. They’re free. Go build one.”

The bottom line

Copyright’s strongest case is a single creator’s earning power and control inside the system as it stands.

Copyleft’s strongest case is that knowledge and designs grow fastest — and help the most people — when nobody can fence them in, and that its few real cons are mostly the friction of doing a free thing inside an unfree system, plus one deliberate restriction: you may not take away anyone else’s freedom.

We believe it can be done. We believe we know how. And we need everyone’s help to get us there.
