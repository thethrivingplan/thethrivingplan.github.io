Why? — What it is

Charge is a property of matter itself, as basic as mass. Electricity is what happens when charge is pushed and flows. Every power technology ever built is a way of doing that pushing — so this is the sheet underneath all of them.

How? — What it means

Three ideas run everything: voltage (the push), current (the flow), resistance (the drag) — tied together by one short law, V = I × R. If you hold those three apart in your mind and never blur them, you understand electricity. The rest is detail.

Act. — What it could mean for you

You can read a battery label, judge whether a charger matches a device, understand your power bill's kilowatt-hours, and know — precisely, not vaguely — why the breaker box is not a toy and when the only correct move is calling a licensed electrician.

01 / FIRST, AT FULL SIZE

Safety — Electricity Kills Quietly

This section is first because with electricity the order of knowledge is the order of survival — and because electricity gives none of the warnings other dangers give. It is invisible. It makes no sound and no smell. A wire that can kill you looks exactly like a wire that can't. That is not a scare line; it is the documented reason behind every rule below.

It takes very little current

What harms a person is current through the body — and the amounts are small. Around a thousandth of an amp (1 milliamp) is a tingle. Around ten milliamps, muscles can clamp and a person may be unable to let go of the thing shocking them. A few hundredths of an amp across the chest can throw the heart into fatal fibrillation. A household circuit carries fifteen to twenty amps — hundreds of times the lethal amount, delivered without hesitation.

The breaker does not protect you

A circuit breaker's job is to protect the wiring — to trip when a circuit overloads, before the wires in the wall overheat and start a fire. It trips at 15 or 20 amps. A lethal current is a small fraction of one amp. A breaker can sit happily closed while a fatal current passes through a person. The device that does guard people is the GFCI (ground-fault interrupter, called an RCD in much of the world): it watches for current leaking where it shouldn't — through you, for instance — and cuts power in a fraction of a second. Both devices matter. They do different jobs. Neither makes live wiring safe to touch.

Water changes everything

Dry skin resists current, somewhat. Wet skin barely resists at all — the same voltage that tingles through dry hands can drive a dangerous current through wet ones. That is the whole reason for the oldest rule in the house: electricity and water stay apart — no appliances at the bath's edge, no cords across wet floors, no handling plugs with wet hands. The rule is not fussiness. It is Ohm's law, which you will meet in §③, applied to your own body.

Treat every wire as live. Every one. Every time.

That is the working rule of every electrician who lives to retire, and it is the only attitude this sheet teaches toward real wiring. A wire is not safe because a switch is off — switches get wired wrong, breakers get mislabeled, circuits get shared in ways nobody documented. Professionals test before they touch, with instruments, every time — and even they treat the test as a check, not a promise.

The honest limit, printed plainly: this sheet teaches understanding — principles, history, and why. It contains no instructions for working on live or mains wiring, and none for building electrical devices, and it never will. The inside of a breaker panel, the wiring of an outlet, the repair of anything that plugs into the wall: that is licensed electricians' work, learned in apprenticeship and under code, and the correct use of this blueprint — the Act — is to understand enough to respect it, to spot when something is wrong, and to make the call. Knowing how electricity works is exactly what tells you where your part ends.

02 / THE THREE IDEAS

Charge, Push, Flow, Drag

Everything electrical is built from one fact and three ideas. The fact: electric charge. The ideas: voltage, current, and resistance — three genuinely different things that everyday speech blurs into one word, "electricity." Unblurring them is most of understanding the subject.

Documented

The fact underneath: charge

All matter is atoms, and atoms are built from particles that carry charge: protons, with positive charge, locked in the atom's center — and electrons, with negative charge, around the outside. Charge is as basic a property as mass: things don't get charge from anywhere; they are made of it. Like charges push each other apart; opposite charges pull together. That push and pull is the only force electricity ever uses.

Charge is measured in coulombs. One coulomb is the charge of about 6.24 billion billion electrons — an enormous crowd, which is why the numbers in §③ will look small: even a modest current is a staggering number of electrons on the move. In a metal wire, the electrons are the ones free to move; the protons stay put in the metal's structure. So an electric current in a wire is a slow drift of electrons through a metal that is, in a sense, already full of them.

One honest wrinkle, labeled: the push travels fast — near the speed of light — while the electrons themselves drift at millimeters per second. Think of a tube already packed with marbles: push one in at your end and one pops out at the far end almost instantly, though no single marble traveled the tube. The signal is fast; the carriers are slow. Both halves are true.

Idea one

Voltage — the push

Voltage measures how much energy each unit of charge carries — how hard the charge is being pushed. It is measured in volts (V), and it exists between two points: across a battery's terminals, across a bulb. A AA battery pushes at 1.5 volts. A car battery, 12. A wall outlet in North America, about 120; in much of the rest of the world, about 230. Voltage alone harms no one and lights nothing — it is pure potential, the height of the hill, whether or not anything rolls down it.

Idea two

Current — the flow

Current measures how much charge passes a point each second. It is measured in amperes (A) — amps — and one amp is one coulomb per second: that crowd of electrons, streaming past. Current is what does the work and, per §①, what does the harm. It flows in a circuit — a complete loop — or it does not flow at all. Break the loop anywhere and the flow stops everywhere in that loop, instantly. No loop, no current, whatever the voltage.

Idea three

Resistance — the drag

Resistance measures how hard a material fights the flow. It is measured in ohms (Ω). Copper barely fights — that is why wires are copper (see the Mining sheet: the wire begins at a mine mouth). Rubber and plastic fight so hard they are used as insulation. Resistance depends on the material, on length (longer wire, more drag), on thickness (thinner wire, more drag), and on temperature. A light bulb's filament is deliberate resistance: force current through it and the drag turns electrical energy into heat and light.

An analogy — labeled as one

The water-in-pipes picture — and where it breaks

The classic teaching picture: voltage is water pressure, current is the flow rate, resistance is a narrow stretch of pipe. Raise the pressure or widen the pipe and more water flows — raise the voltage or lower the resistance and more current flows. As a first grip on three separate ideas, it earns its place, and this sheet uses it.

Where it breaks — printed at the same size: Cut a pipe and water spills out onto the floor; cut a wire and the current simply stops — charge does not leak into the air. A pipe can stand empty; a wire is never empty — it is always full of electrons, and what travels is the push, not a delivery of fresh electrons from the power station. Water flowing downhill is used up at the bottom; charge is never used up — it circulates, delivering energy and coming back around. And for alternating current (§⑤), the picture gives up entirely: household AC would be water sloshing back and forth in the pipe sixty times a second, which no plumbing does and no pipe could show you. Use the analogy to get the three ideas apart. Then set it down.

Tap to openTap to close Which way does the current flow? — the Franklin wrinkle

Here is a documented oddity worth knowing, because it confuses every beginner who meets it: in circuit diagrams, "current" is drawn flowing from positive to negative — but in a metal wire, the electrons actually drift from negative to positive. Both statements are in every textbook, and both are used without apology. The reason is history: Benjamin Franklin, working before anyone knew about electrons, had to pick a direction for his "electrical fluid," and he picked the one we now call conventional current. When the electron was identified in 1897 and turned out to carry the charge the other way, the convention was already load-bearing in a century of books and equations — so it stayed. The math works identically either way, because what matters is consistent bookkeeping. Engineers think in conventional current; the electrons, unbothered, drift the other way. Knowing this once saves a lifetime of small confusion.

03 / ONE LAW, THREE IDEAS

Ohm's Law — and Power

In 1827 the German physicist Georg Ohm published the relation that ties the three ideas together — and was initially dismissed for it, which the record also shows; his law is now the first equation of the whole subject. It says the three ideas are never independent: voltage = current × resistance — V = I × R. Fix any two and the third is not a choice; it follows. That is not a slogan. Try it:

Power — measured in watts (W) — is the rate of doing work: how fast electrical energy becomes heat, light, or motion. One watt is one joule per second. Run 1,000 watts for one hour and you have used one kilowatt-hour — the unit on the power bill. The bill is not charging you for electricity; it is charging you for energy, counted in those hours of watts.

Documented

Why the law matters more than it looks

Ohm's law is a constraint, not a suggestion — and it quietly governs every safety rule on this sheet. It is why wet skin is dangerous (§①): same voltage, lower resistance, so the law — not bad luck — raises the current through you. It is why a short circuit is violent: resistance near zero means current limited by almost nothing, and the wiring itself becomes the filament. And it is why §⑤'s war was won by transformers: losses in a wire grow with the square of the current, so sending power at high voltage and low current loses almost nothing on the way. One short equation; the whole industry is its footnotes.

Documented

Power: P = V × I

Multiply the push by the flow and you get the rate of work — power, in watts. A 60-watt bulb on 120 volts draws half an amp; the lab above will confirm it if you set V = 120 and R = 240. Power is the number printed on appliances because it is the number that predicts heat, brightness, and the bill. Current alone doesn't tell you the work (a huge flow at a tiny push does little); voltage alone doesn't either (a huge push with no flow does nothing at all). The product is the story.

04 / TWO WAYS TO WIRE A LOOP

Circuits — Series and Parallel

A circuit is a complete loop from a source, through the things that use the power, and back. There are exactly two basic ways to arrange the users on the loop, and the difference between them decides how your whole house behaves. Tap between them — then tap the burn-out button and watch what each arrangement does with a failure.

Documented

Why your house is wired parallel

Run the comparison above and the answer explains itself. In a house wired in series, every device would share one path: each would get only a slice of the voltage, the fridge would dim when the kettle started, and one burned-out bulb — or one switched-off lamp — would cut power to everything downstream. In parallel, every outlet and fixture sits on its own branch across the full supply: each device gets the full voltage it was built for, each works or fails on its own, and each branch can carry its own switch and its own breaker. That independence is not a luxury feature; it is what makes a building's wiring usable at all. The trade, printed honestly: parallel branches each draw their own current, and the currents add on the shared main line — which is why overloading one circuit with too many devices trips its breaker. The breaker is counting exactly that sum.

05 / THE GREAT ARGUMENT

AC vs. DC — and the War of the Currents

Current can flow two ways. Direct current (DC) flows steadily in one direction — what batteries give, and what Edison sold. Alternating current (AC) reverses direction many times a second — 60 times a second in North America, 50 in much of the world — and is what the grid delivers. In the late 1880s these two systems fought for the future of electrification, in what newspapers called the War of the Currents. The history is graded here the way this tree grades everything: both shelves, at full size.

The technical shelf

Why AC won the grid — a physics result, not a popularity result

The problem was distance. Power lost in a wire grows with the square of the current, so the trick to sending power far is to send it at high voltage and low current — then bring the voltage back down at the far end. AC can do that trick and DC of the era could not, because of one device: the transformer, two coils sharing an iron core, which steps AC voltage up or down with almost no loss — and works only on current that keeps changing. A transformer fed steady DC does nothing at all.

So Edison's DC stations, generating at the ~110 volts his lamps used, could serve only about a mile around the plant before the losses ate the power — a station on nearly every neighborhood, burning coal in the middle of the city. Westinghouse's AC could be generated, stepped up to thousands of volts, sent for miles on thin wire, and stepped down at the destination. The economics were not close, and in 1893–96 the demonstration closed the argument: Westinghouse lit the Chicago World's Columbian Exposition with AC, and AC generated at Niagara Falls reached Buffalo, some 26 miles away — a distance DC could not dream of. The grid has been AC ever since.

The other shelf — the smear

How the losing side fought — documented, and named

Edison's companies did not only argue; they campaigned. In 1888 the engineer Harold P. Brown, working with support from Edison's laboratory, staged public electrocutions of dogs and other animals using alternating current, to brand AC as a killer current unfit for homes. Edison's side spread word of fatal AC accidents, lobbied against AC in state legislatures, and backed using a Westinghouse AC generator for New York's new electric chair — so that "being Westinghoused" would enter the language as a word for execution. The first execution by electric chair followed in 1890. None of this was hidden at the time; it was done in front of reporters, on purpose.

The honest corrections, at the same size: the war was fought between companies — Edison's against Westinghouse's — with Nikola Tesla's AC patents licensed to Westinghouse; the schoolbook version of a personal duel between Edison and Tesla is a later simplification. Edison's safety argument was not pure invention, either: high-voltage AC genuinely is dangerous — §① of this sheet says the same of all current — and his side had a real point, which lost to a bigger one. The famous electrocution of the elephant Topsy at Coney Island in 1903 was not part of this war: it came a decade after the fight ended, was not organized by Edison, and was merely filmed by a company bearing his name. And the story's true ending is ironic and documented: in 1892 Edison's own company merged with a rival AC firm to form General Electric, Edison was eased out of control — and GE sold AC. DC never died, either: it remains what batteries, electronics, and solar panels produce, and modern high-voltage DC lines now carry power over some of the longest distances on Earth. The war decided the grid, not the physics.

Tap to openTap to close Why AC won motors too — Tesla's actual contribution

Lighting alone might have kept DC competitive in dense city centers for years — and in fact downtown DC service lingered for decades. What made AC irresistible was the motor. Tesla's documented contribution was the polyphase AC system and the induction motor (patents granted 1888, licensed by Westinghouse): a motor with no brushes and no electrical connection to its spinning part, driven by a rotating magnetic field created by AC itself — rugged, simple, and cheap to build. Factories ran on motors, not lamps. Once AC could turn the machines as well as light the rooms, the contest was over in industry as well as in distance. Tesla's royalty contract on those patents, incidentally, was later torn up at Westinghouse's hour of financial need — a documented act, whether you grade it as generosity or folly; the record carries both readings.

06 / WHY THIS SHEET IS THE FLOOR

Every Power Technology Ends Here

Open any other energy sheet on this tree and follow it to its end. However exotic the front of the machine is — a splitting nucleus, a star in a magnetic bottle, a mountainside of mirrors — the back of the machine is the same, and it is this sheet. Almost every power source humanity uses finishes by doing one of two things: spinning a generator, or pushing charge directly. The generator itself is one experiment, scaled: Michael Faraday showed in 1831 that moving a magnet through a coil of wire induces a current in the wire. Every generator on Earth — coil turning inside magnets, or magnets turning inside coils — is that experiment wearing a bigger coat.

Power technologyWhat it actually does at the endThe honest label
Nuclear Fission Splitting atoms releases heat; the heat boils water; the steam spins a turbine; the turbine spins a generator. Strip the reactor away and the back half is a steam engine turning Faraday's coil. Documented, operating worldwide for decades.
Nuclear Fusion The plan is identical: fusion heat → steam → turbine → generator. The front of the machine is the hard part; the back is this sheet, waiting. Documented as a design. No fusion plant feeds a grid yet — printed plainly, as the Fusion sheet itself prints it.
Solar (photovoltaic) The exception that proves the rule: no spinning at all. Light strikes silicon and knocks electrons loose — the photovoltaic effect — pushing charge directly. Panels produce DC, which an inverter converts to AC for the grid. Documented, operating worldwide.
Wind Moving air turns a rotor; the rotor spins a generator — directly, or through a gearbox. The wind does the pushing; Faraday does the rest. Documented, operating worldwide.
Water (hydro) Falling water is the most direct spinner of all: the river's weight turns the turbine, the turbine turns the generator. Niagara's Adams station (§⑤) was exactly this, at the dawn of the grid. Documented, operating worldwide for over a century.
Coal, gas, geothermal Different fires and heats, same ending: steam, turbine, generator. A coal plant and a fission plant are near-identical machines from the boiler onward — only the furnace differs. Documented, operating worldwide.

That is why this sheet sits where it sits on the tree. Fission and Fusion are not separate magics; they are two very different ways of making heat for the same steam-and-coil ending. Solar skips the spinning and pushes charge outright. Wind and water spin the coil with weather and gravity. Learn voltage, current, and resistance once, here, and you hold the working end of every one of them. The sheets differ in how they push. This one is about the push.

07 / THE DOCUMENTED RECORD, IN BRIEF

How We Got Here

The history of electricity is unusually well documented — and unusually mythologized. This timeline prints the record, with the legends labeled as legends where the record says they are.

1752 · FRANKLIN

The kite — story and record

Partly legend
Benjamin Franklin proposed drawing "electrical fire" from a storm cloud to prove lightning is electricity. The experiment was performed in France in May 1752 — by Thomas-François Dalibard, working from Franklin's published proposal — using a tall iron rod, not a kite. The famous picture of Franklin himself flying a kite with a key, in a storm, rests on a much thinner record than the picture books suggest, and historians grade the details as unverified. What is fully documented is Franklin's real work: the terms positive and negative charge, the principle that charge is conserved, and the lightning rod — which protected buildings for real, whatever the kite did.

→
1800–1831 · VOLTA, ØRSTED, FARADAY

Current, then the generator

In 1800 Alessandro Volta stacked metal discs and brine into the first battery — the first steady source of current, the thing that made electricity an experiment instead of a spark. In 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted noticed a current deflecting a compass needle: electricity and magnetism were connected. In 1831 Michael Faraday — a bookbinder's apprentice turned the era's greatest experimentalist — closed the loop: a changing magnetic field induces current in a wire. That single discovery is the generator, the transformer, and the motor in embryo — the whole of §⑥. Faraday, who had almost no mathematics, found it with his hands; the mathematics came next.

→
1865 · MAXWELL

The deep why

James Clerk Maxwell gathered everything known about electricity and magnetism into a small set of equations — and the equations said more than anyone had put into them. They described electric and magnetic fields endlessly creating each other, traveling as waves at one particular speed: the speed of light. Light itself, Maxwell's math announced, is an electromagnetic wave. This is the deep why under this entire sheet: the generator, the transformer, the motor, radio, and light are one phenomenon, and Maxwell's equations are its complete description at the level every technology on this tree uses. You do not need to solve them to honor them: everything above this paragraph is those equations, behaving.

→
1882 → NOW · THE SPREAD

Electrification

Edison's Pearl Street station opened in New York in 1882, serving a few dozen customers within about a mile. Then the §⑤ war settled the system, and the spread began: cities first, industry alongside, farms last — in the United States, rural electrification was still incomplete enough in 1936 to need a federal Rural Electrification Act. Within two generations, electricity went from a novelty for the rich to the assumed background of ordinary life in the industrialized world. The honest remainder, printed at full size: the spread is not finished. Recent International Energy Agency estimates put the number of people still without any access to electricity at roughly three-quarters of a billion. The floor this sheet describes is laid under most of the world — not yet all of it.

Tap to openTap to close Why the history is on a blueprint sheet at all

Because the pattern in it is a tool. Every stage of this history is the same move repeated: someone noticed the push could be made steadier (Volta), stronger (Faraday), farther (the transformer), cheaper (the grid). The technologies on this tree that are still concepts — fusion above all — are waiting on their version of one of those moves, and their sheets say which one. A reader who knows how electricity's bottlenecks actually fell can grade a new claim about any power technology by asking the historical question: which bottleneck does this remove — the push, the flow, or the distance? If it removes none, it is not a power technology yet. That test is this section's gift to the rest of the tree.
