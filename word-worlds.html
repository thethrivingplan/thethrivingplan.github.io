The Full Why

Why this works, what it honestly cannot do, and the different ways people learn — one tap down, nothing hidden.

Why playing beats memorising listsTap to open · Tap to close

Comprehensible input, in plain words: you learn a word fastest when you can already almost understand it — you see the apple, you hear manzana, and the two click together. The picture carries the meaning, so the word never floats free as a bare spelling to be memorised.

Retrieval practice, in plain words: reading a word is a weak pass. Pulling it back out of your own memory — seeing the picture and finding manzana yourself — is the pass that makes it stick. That is why the matching game flips direction, and why a word only counts as “known” here when you get it right again in a later round.

Using it is the third, strongest pass: buying the apple in the errand makes the word a tool, not a fact. Three passes — see it, retrieve it, use it — beat thirty readings of a list.

Honest grading: what a game can and cannot doTap to open · Tap to close

A game starts a language. It does not finish one. Word Worlds gives you first words, first sentences, polite forms, and the habit of reaching for Spanish — a real start, and a real achievement. Fluency is a longer road: real conversation with real people, listening that runs at full speed, and months of small daily use. Any page that promises fluency from a game alone is selling you something. This one would rather hand you a true beginning.

Two honest strategies, with trade-offs: Picture-first learning (see the thing, attach the word) is fast and friendly for objects, but weak for ideas like “please” that have no picture. Sound-first learning (hear it, say it) builds an ear and a mouth early, but can leave spelling behind. Whole phrases (“Quiero la manzana, por favor”) get you understood on day one but hide how sentences are built; word by word is slower but shows you the machinery. This game mixes all of them on purpose, and the sentence builder shows you the machinery inside the phrases.

Where Spanish order differs — and where it doesn't: in Spanish the describing word usually comes after the thing: la manzana roja, “the apple red.” English puts it before. But much is the same: Yo quiero el pan runs in the same order as “I want the bread.” The sentence game points at each case in the moment, honestly, either way.

How the game is shaped: ramps, limits, and stakesTap to open · Tap to close

Difficulty ramp: word sets start small and concrete (food, colours) and grow toward family, the street, and full numbers 1–20; sentences lengthen; errands chain — the café errand reuses the market's polite words, so earlier learning carries weight.

Capacity, named as a variant: a day's errands are finite — three, and then the market day ends. That is a chosen limit, not a fact of the world: it gives a session a satisfying ending and a reason to come back tomorrow. If you would rather practise without the day-shape, the other three games never end.

Stakes beyond money: there is no money score anywhere on this page. Coins exist only as drawn props inside the pretend of buying. What grows is what matters: errands done, people helped, sentences built, and a phrasebook you can watch filling up.
