The Ruler's Test — Steve Scalise, House Majority Leader of the United States House of Representatives

Subject: Steve Scalise (R-LA, Louisiana's 1st district) — House Majority Leader since 3 January 2023, serving in the 118th and 119th Congresses. The seat graded is the majority leader's office itself, and its framing is stated plainly: the majority leader is not the chamber's presiding power. He serves under the Speaker's authority, is elected by secret ballot of the majority party's conference — not by the whole House — and cannot sign, veto, preside, or enact anything alone (CRS, Party Leaders in the House, RS20881). What the office actually holds is floor schedule management under the Speaker (he is charged with scheduling legislation for floor consideration and planning the daily, weekly, and annual legislative agendas), consultation with members to gauge sentiment and urging colleagues to support or defeat measures, close watching of floor activity and the opposition's parliamentary maneuvers, guidance to committee chairs on policy priorities, and — working with the Speaker and the Majority Whip, Tom Emmer — the vote-counting and persuasion operation that decides when a measure is ready for a vote and whether it passes (CRS RS20881; Scalise official biography, 2026; Washington Post reporting on the whip operation, September 2026). He is graded on his exercise of those powers, not on his whole career as a member or as the Republican whip (2014–2023), except where that record bears directly on the office. Premise verified for November 2026: Scalise still holds the office today. The House History office lists him as Majority Leader for the 119th Congress (2025–2027), and the House leadership listings as of 24 November 2026 show Speaker Mike Johnson, Majority Leader Steve Scalise, and Majority Whip Tom Emmer (House History, 119th Congress profiles; House leadership directory, November 2026). The midterm elections of 3 November 2026 removed his party's majority — the Associated Press count reported by the Wall Street Journal on 10 November 2026 gave Democrats at least 255 seats to at least 167 Republicans, with some seats still uncalled, against a current 219–214 Republican majority with two vacancies; other outlets' running totals differ because they call races at different times, and the figures are printed here as separate sourced counts, never blended (WSJ/AP, 10 November 2026) — but the 119th Congress sits until noon on 3 January 2027, and Scalise is its Majority Leader through that hour. Two November leadership elections decide what happens next, and they are different offices: on 4 November 2026 Scalise announced he would run for House Minority Leader of the 120th Congress after Johnson stepped back from leadership, and on 10 November 2026 House Republicans elected Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio their leader for the next Congress — the minority leader-elect — defeating Scalise 106–73 in a secret ballot (AP via Gray/WVUE, 10–11 November 2026; WSJ, 10 November 2026). Jordan has exercised none of that office's powers and is not graded here. Scalise's own office passes not to Jordan but across the aisle: on 18 November 2026 the Democratic Caucus elected Rep. Katherine Clark of Massachusetts, unopposed, as Majority Leader for the 120th Congress — the first woman chosen for the post — and she appears in the record as "Majority Leader-elect," having released the 120th Congress calendar on 20 November 2026 (Democratic Whip's office, 18 and 20 November 2026; Ballotpedia, 120th Congress leadership elections). She takes the office on 3 January 2027 and has exercised none of its powers in this Congress; she is named as the successor, not graded. Scalise himself was re-elected in his own district on 3 November 2026 with 60.7% (159,896 votes) against Lauren Jewett's 34.0% (Louisiana Secretary of State unofficial returns via AP, November 2026), so he remains a member in the 120th Congress; on 11 November 2026 he declined to commit to serving its full term and said his focus was the lame-duck weeks — "I've got a lot more gas in the tank… We still have work to do right now" (Fox News reporting via Shreveport Times and WVUE, November 2026). Because his tenure as Majority Leader is effectively complete — just under four years, with only the lame-duck weeks left — this dossier grades the substantial, essentially finished record, in the series' completed-tenure handling, and states that timing plainly. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is NOT inherently a failure and is never graded as one — carrying out that platform is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for HOW it is done: a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source. For this seat, the source is the floor schedule: whose bills get a rule, whose get a suspension slot, and whose never get either.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The schedule itself is published, in advance, to everyone on the same terms. The Majority Leader's office issues the weekly schedule and the annual legislative calendar publicly — Scalise released the 2026 calendar for the second session of the 119th Congress on 18 November 2025 — and announces the week's business, including the complete list of suspension bills, by the close of business before the week begins, in the long-standing colloquy practice the office maintains with the minority (Majority Leader's office, November 2025 and November 2026; House schedule records). A minority member can read the same lookout, on the same day, as a majority member; the calendar is not a private list.
  • The suspension calendar — the one door this office opens to bills without a rule — ran broadly bipartisan product all the way through the lame duck. In the week of 16 November 2026, under Scalise's published schedule, suspension bills passed 396–14 (the Earth MRI Reauthorization Act) and 401–0 (the Preventive Health Savings Act), and the Major Richard Star Act for combat-wounded veterans passed 392–18 on 12 November 2026 (House Clerk roll calls 324–327, November 2026, via CiVL reporting; House voting records, November 2026). Whatever else the schedule favored, it was not run as a loyalty list in its open lane.
  • His stated rule for floor access is one rule, stated the same way to his own side and the other: asked in 2026 about members circumventing leadership with discharge petitions, Scalise — identified in the reporting as the leader "responsible for setting the floor schedule" — answered, "I don't support that process… As the Majority Leader, when people come to me and they want a bill moved the first thing I always tell them is, go talk to the chairman, work through the committee process. That is what the regular order is around here" (WCIA/The Hill reporting, 2026). Committee-first is a neutral-sounding gate, and on the documented record he applied the same answer to Republican petition-signers (seven Republicans signed the labor-contracts petition he was criticizing) as to Democrats.

Evidence against:

  • The bills that matter most to the majority moved under a different, closed law. His own signature bill, the Rescissions Act of 2025 (H.R. 4), came to the floor under a closed rule (H.Res. 499, adopted 213–207 on 11 June 2025) that waived all points of order against the bill and its provisions, provided one hour of debate, and allowed no amendments — the Democratic amendments submitted to the Rules Committee, including ones striking the public-broadcasting and PEPFAR rescissions, were never made in order (House Rules Committee record on H.R. 4, June 2025). The One Big Beautiful Bill Act moved the same way in May and July 2025. A schedule with an open lane for consensus bills and a sealed lane for the majority's program is two laws of access, sorted by whose bill it is.
  • One bipartisan transparency bill could not get his schedule at all and had to take it. Through 2025 the Epstein Files Transparency Act — bipartisan, majority-supported in the country and eventually passed 427–1 — was kept off the floor by the leadership of which Scalise is the schedule-keeper, until a discharge petition reached 218 signatures over that leadership's opposition and forced the vote on 18 November 2025; Scalise confirmed he would vote yes that morning and said the vote would "continue to show the transparency that we've delivered," while joining the suggestion that the Senate revise a bill the Senate's leader then judged "sufficient" unamended (Politico congressional reporting, 18 November 2025; The Banner, November 2025). The motive critics alleged for the wider delay — protecting the president — is recorded in the Speaker's dossier as an ALLEGATION disputed by leadership; what is documented for this office is simpler: the schedule's door stayed shut to this one subject until the House's rarest end-run opened it, and it reached 218 signatures again, on a second enforcement petition, in November 2026.
  • The schedule's priorities in the session's final weeks were the majority's, including the symbolic: the post-shutdown schedule Scalise previewed in November 2025 front-loaded D.C. crime bills overriding the District's own enacted policies and a resolution denouncing socialism, while the bipartisan one-year extension of the expiring Affordable Care Act credits — the substantive dispute of the shutdown — received no slot on his schedule and reached the floor only later, by discharge petition, in January 2026 (Fox News Digital reporting of Scalise's schedule preview, November 2025; Congress.gov, H.R. 1834 status). Scheduling is this office's whole power; what it makes time for, and what it never makes time for, is the law it gives.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because Scalise's personal one-law record is thinner, and less differential, than the Speaker's: the documented acts that most clearly applied two laws to two members — the fifty-day swearing-in delay, the Ethics intervention — were the Speaker's hand, not the schedule-keeper's, and this dossier does not transfer them. What is his is the access structure itself: a genuinely open, published, bipartisan suspension lane, a stated committee-first rule applied in words to both parties — and, above it, a closed-rule lane for the majority's program and a shut door, until a petition forced it, for the one transparency bill the leadership opposed. The rule is even where the stakes are low and uneven where they are high, and the grade says exactly that.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: members of both parties whose consensus bills moved under suspension, including the veterans of the Major Richard Star Act (392–18, 12 November 2026); every member and staffer planning around a published calendar; majority members spared amendment votes on the Rescissions Act and the reconciliation bill.
  • Hurt: Epstein survivors and the public seeking the files, whose bill needed a discharge petition rather than a schedule slot; Democratic members whose Rescissions Act amendments were submitted and never made in order; District of Columbia residents, whose local policies were scheduled for override while their district has no voting member to object.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized? For this seat the referees within reach are narrower than the Speaker's: the discharge petition, the committee process he invokes, the scorekeeper whose numbers his bills must carry, and the conference ballot that made and can unmake him.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Twice, the conference ballot went against the thing he wanted most, and twice he took its answer in the open. On 11 October 2023 the Republican Conference nominated him for Speaker, 113–99 over Jim Jordan; when a day of meeting holdouts left him short of the 217 floor votes he needed, he withdrew on 12 October 2023 rather than forcing a losing floor fight or bargaining the office's powers away — "I never came here for a title. I've had some great titles. I am the Majority leader of the House, and I love the job I have… it's much bigger than me, and it's much bigger than anybody else" (Majority Leader's office transcript, 12 October 2023; NPR/WAMC reporting, 12 October 2023). On 10 November 2026 he lost the minority-leader ballot to Jordan 106–73 and congratulated the new leadership team the same evening — "I look forward to supporting my colleagues and continuing to fight for the American people" (Scalise post via Shreveport-Bossier Advocate and WVUE reporting, 10–11 November 2026). A leader who wanted a handle on the count had his chances; the record shows him declining them.
  • The discharge petition was opposed in words and never abolished in fact. Scalise said plainly, "I don't support that process" (WCIA/The Hill, 2026) — and the instrument nonetheless survived his tenure intact and was used successfully against his leadership's position repeatedly in the 119th Congress, a modern record of forced votes including the Epstein files release (House Democrats' count of seven discharge-petition bills, 13 November 2026; see the Minority Leader's dossier). Gripping would have been a rule change against the counter; criticizing the counter while leaving it standing is pressure, not capture, and the shelf records the distinction.
  • No documented act in the record assembled here shows Scalise defying a court order, lobbying the Ethics Committee, threatening the Congressional Budget Office, or retaliating against an election official or a news outlet's coverage. CBO's adverse scores of the bills he marshaled — $3.4 trillion added to deficits, 10 million more uninsured (CBO, July and August 2025) — were published, debated, and answered with argument (his 2.6% growth-assumption defense, May 2025), not suppressed.

Evidence against:

  • He authored the bill that defunded the public-media referee. The Rescissions Act of 2025 — introduced by Scalise personally on 6 June 2025 as H.R. 4 — rescinded $1.1 billion from the Corporation for Public Broadcasting alongside $7.9 billion (as enacted) from international assistance, and his stated ground was editorial: taxpayers should not subsidize "woke public broadcasting, including NPR and PBS," outlets he described as carrying "raging political bias" and, in the passage statement, "radical media" (Scalise statements, 6 and 12 June 2025). Passing a law through both chambers is not censorship, and the platform point is graded at Tests 4 and 5, not here; what belongs on this shelf is the office's weight used, by the schedule-keeper's own bill, to withdraw the federal funding of news organizations whose coverage the majority disliked — a grip on a media institution's independence by its budget line, openly avowed as a response to its content.
  • The House's statutory check on the executive was kept off his schedule for eleven months. The rules that froze the National Emergencies Act fast-track clock in 2025 (H.Res. 211 and successors, declaring that days "shall not constitute a calendar day" for the tariff-emergency termination vote) were leadership rules that reached the floor through the schedule this office keeps; when the clock finally ran, the House voted 219–211 on 11 February 2026 to end the emergency, and on 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that the emergency law had not authorized the tariffs at all (Rules Committee record, March 2025; House vote, 11 February 2026; Supreme Court ruling, 20 February 2026 — full record in the Speaker's dossier). The Speaker owned the decision; the Majority Leader's office was the mechanism that kept the vote unscheduled, and this dossier charges the mechanism its share, no more.
  • During the 43-day shutdown of October–November 2025, the schedule he publishes simply had no House in it: the House was out of session from 19 September to 12 November 2025 — 54 days without floor business on the nonpartisan count of the backlog reporting — while the Senate and the White House negotiated the ending (Washington Examiner reporting, November 2025; House record, October–November 2025). A schedule-keeper cannot outvote his Speaker, and Johnson's stated rationale (pressure on Senate Democrats) is recorded in the Speaker's dossier; but the office graded here is the one whose product is the calendar, and for six weeks the calendar was empty by design, removing the chamber itself as a referee in its own funding fight.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because the two halves of the record are different in kind. On every referee that votes on him — the conference ballot, twice — Scalise submitted completely and publicly, and the House's internal end-run against his schedule survived his open opposition untouched; no court, scorekeeper, or ethics process bears his fingerprints in this record. Against that stand two uses of the schedule-and-bill machinery against referees that check his side: a media institution's funding withdrawn by his own bill for its coverage, and a statutory emergency-check vote kept unscheduled for eleven months, inside a six-week stretch in which the House was not scheduled at all. He holds the referees he answers to without gripping, and grips, through the calendar, some of the ones that answer to no one. MIXED is the honest sum.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: the House as an institution in October 2023, spared a doomed floor spectacle by a nominee who withdrew when the count said no; Jim Jordan and the incoming leadership team in November 2026, congratulated rather than contested; future minorities of either party, whose discharge-petition precedent this tenure left intact.
  • Hurt: NPR- and PBS-affiliated stations and their audiences — most exposed, on the documented record of the rescissions debate, the small and rural stations for which the federal share is largest — whose funding his bill rescinded for their coverage; House members denied the tariff-emergency termination vote for eleven months; the House's own bargaining position during the 43-day shutdown, surrendered with its calendar.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, he at least considered it openly. Considering it and declining with stated reasons is NOT against-evidence. Refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The defining wobble of his tenure in this office was answered by stepping back, in public, within a day. The October 2023 speakerless House was his party's crisis and his nomination; when the votes were not there he withdrew openly, gave his reasons to reporters the same night, and returned to the majority leader's desk he still held, from which the House eventually elected Johnson on 25 October 2023 (Majority Leader's office, 12 October 2023; NPR, 12–13 October 2023). No denial that the count had failed, no attempt to hold the nomination hostage — the adjustment was the withdrawal itself.
  • Inside his signature fight, the adjustments were open, named, and traded in the open. When Budget Committee holdouts balked at the reconciliation bill in May 2025, Scalise described the internal settlement on the record — the conference "settled" on a 2.6% growth assumption for scoring, "we agreed to limit it for the budgeting purposes to 2.6% growth" — and when the Freedom Caucus pressed in the final week, leadership sped up the Medicaid work-requirements date and accelerated clean-energy credit phaseouts in a manager's amendment rather than denying the pushback existed; he called the balance "very delicate" between moderates and conservatives and said so on camera (Scalise on Fox Business, 16 May 2025; Washington Times reporting of the 21 May 2025 White House meeting). The bill passed the House on 22 May 2025, 215–214.
  • When the Senate changed his own bill, he took the changed bill. His Rescissions Act as introduced cut $9.4 billion including PEPFAR funds; the Senate amended it to protect $400 million of PEPFAR, exempt major disease, nutrition, and maternal-and-child-health programs, and protect aid for Jordan and Egypt — and Scalise's House agreed to the Senate amendment on 18 July 2025, 216–213, sending the smaller bill ($7.9 billion of aid rescissions as enacted) to signature on 24 July 2025 (Senate amendment record and House concurrence, July 2025). If another option became available — a rescissions law that kept the AIDS-relief money — he considered it, accepted it, and whipped his chamber to concur.
  • The shutdown's end was answered with his office's one real tool, used at full stretch. Before the House had even voted to reopen, Scalise told Fox News Digital he had "rework[ed] the schedule to create more time to make up for what happened during the shutdown," with a five-day legislative week, evening votes, and extended committee time "for the next few weeks until we catch up," and repeated it after reopening: "It's full steam ahead… with late night votes and a fully packed schedule" (Fox News Digital, November 2025; Washington Examiner, November 2025). The schedule was the adjustment, and it was his to make.
  • The voters' verdict of 3 November 2026, and his conference's verdict of 10 November, were both accepted without denial or maneuver: he congratulated Jordan's team the night he lost (see Test 2), took public stock of the defeat — "There's clearly a lot of weight lifted now… these leadership jobs take a lot of time" — and redirected to the lame-duck work rather than contesting either result (WVUE/Fox News reporting, 11 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The shutdown wobble itself was doubled down on for its whole length. For 43 days the pushback was specific — Senate Democrats and House Democrats demanding negotiation over the expiring ACA credits, a bipartisan one-year extension in existence — and Scalise's public answer never moved from attribution: the closure was "the Democrat Shutdown," Democrats were "holding Americans hostage," and the House's clean continuing resolution of 19 September 2025 was the only door (Scalise statements and leadership statements, October–12 November 2025). If another option became available — a House-negotiated extension, a shorter stopgap with talks attached — the record shows it was not considered openly in his chamber, for the mechanical reason in Test 2: his schedule had sent the chamber home. The reworked catch-up calendar (FOR shelf) answered the backlog; it did not answer the refusal that created the need for it.
  • On the Epstein files, his adjustment followed the petition and the president's reversal rather than preceding either: months of a shut schedule door (Test 1), then a yes vote and a transparency claim on 18 November 2025, after President Trump had told Republicans to vote yes and the discharge petition had made the vote unavoidable (The Banner and Politico reporting, November 2025). And in 2026, faced with a new wave of discharge petitions succeeding against his schedule, his stated position had not adjusted — "I don't support that process" (WCIA/The Hill, 2026). The wobble was answered by the institution; the office's own position is on record as unchanged.
  • The lame-duck attendance collapse was met, from his office, with a schedule that surrendered to it. In the week of 16 November 2026, with 14 Republicans absent on Wednesday and 21–22 on Thursday on the Clerk's counts as reported, the rule his office had scheduled (for the American Franchise Act and the Direct Seller and Real Estate Agent Harmonization Act) never reached the floor, Friday's votes were canceled, and the week ended Thursday morning (House Clerk roll calls, November 2026, via CiVL and USA Today reporting; Speaker Johnson's attendance letter, 20 November 2026). The letter pleading for attendance was the Speaker's; the canceled Friday was the schedule's — the one lever this office wholly owns, used to end the week rather than to force the question. Rep. Mike Simpson's warning that the remaining agenda could be squandered stands in the Speaker's dossier; its scheduling half stands here.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because Scalise's wobble record splits cleanly by whose pushback it was. Pushback from inside his own conference — holdouts on his nomination in 2023, holdouts on his bill in 2025, a Senate amendment to his own legislation, a conference ballot against him in 2026 — he answers fast, openly, and at cost to himself, up to and including surrendering a speakership nomination and a leadership future. Pushback from outside it — the other party in a shutdown, the petition-signers forcing his schedule, his own members simply not showing up — he answers with attribution, an unchanged stated position, or a canceled Friday. The same man who withdrew in a day rather than deny a count spent 43 days denying the other side's premise, and both halves are documented at full strength.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: the House Republican Conference in October 2023, freed to find a Speaker it could elect; PEPFAR and global-health program beneficiaries, whose funding the Senate's option — which he accepted and carried through his chamber — protected; committee chairs and members with stacked-up bills in November 2025, given five-day weeks and evening votes to clear the backlog.
  • Hurt: federal workers and benefit recipients who went 43 days unpaid or unserved while the only House answer was attribution; ACA marketplace enrollees, whose extension option was never looked at in his chamber during the closure; the lame-duck agenda itself in November 2026, whose scheduled rule died of absences his schedule accommodated rather than confronted.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include those who did not elect the majority — the minority party's voters, the poor, the unrepresented, the recipients abroad — or are some counted out? The platform itself is not graded here; the counting is.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The completed FY26 appropriations cycle — all 12 bills passed by the House in January 2026 through the committee-led process the Speaker's dossier documents — funded the whole government's services for every district of both parties, on a schedule his office kept; regular full-year funding is the baseline form of counting everyone, and in the final cycle this leadership delivered it (House Appropriations Committee, January 2026).
  • His schedule carried, and his conference passed, measures whose beneficiaries sit squarely outside his electorate: the Major Richard Star Act, restoring full benefits to combat-wounded veterans medically retired before 20 years, passed 392–18 on 12 November 2026; and when the Senate's rescissions amendment protected PEPFAR and maternal-and-child-health programs, his House concurred rather than insisting on the deeper cuts he had introduced (House votes, 18 July 2025 and 12 November 2026).
  • Inside the signature law he marshaled, he publicly defended the $50 billion rural hospital fund as the answer to the charge that the bill abandoned rural care — citing it, during the shutdown, against Democratic efforts he said would "gut" it (Scalise on CNBC, 12 November 2025). Rural hospitals serve counties of both parties; the fund is a real entry on the counting side of the ledger, and he put it there.

Evidence against:

  • The signature law he helped marshal to the floor — holding the July 2025 vote open for over six hours alongside Emmer while holdouts were worked, per the Washington Post's account of the persuasion operation — carries the neutral scorekeeper's distribution, and it runs downward. CBO's distributional analysis of the enacted law (Public Law 119-21, 11 August 2025) puts the bottom tenth of households at roughly $1,200 a year worse off on average over 2026–2034 and the top tenth roughly $13,600 a year better off; CBO's estimate as enacted projects 10 million more people without health insurance in 2034, 7.5 million of them from the Medicaid changes alone, and $3.4 trillion added to deficits (CBO, July and 11 August 2025). The mandate rule is honored here: extending the 2017 tax cuts and tightening Medicaid eligibility were the platform, and the platform is not the failure. What Test 4 grades is that the law's net accounting, on the official numbers, takes most from the households with least — most of whom never voted for his majority — while his public framing sold the bill as protecting "American workers" and delivering for "hardworking families" generally (Scalise statements, May–July 2025). The counted-out were described as counted in.
  • His own bill counted out two populations by name. The Rescissions Act he introduced and celebrated rescinded, as enacted, $7.9 billion in international assistance — programs whose recipients, in the main, are patients, farmers, and aid partners abroad with no vote anywhere in his system — and $1.1 billion from the Corporation for Public Broadcasting, whose local stations serve every district but whose smallest rural audiences depend on the federal share most (Rescissions Act of 2025, Public Law, 24 July 2025; Scalise statements, June 2025). His stated accounting for both was not that the recipients did not need the money but that the spending was "woke foreign aid" and "radical media" — the recipients appear in his ledger as a saving, described by a label, which is the definition this test grades.
  • The shutdown's costs fell, as they always do, on people with no seat at his table — federal employees working unpaid or furloughed for 43 days, and the marketplace enrollees facing the subsidy cliff — while his chamber, the one whose schedule he keeps, was absent for the whole negotiation (shutdown record, October–November 2025; Tests 2–3). The District of Columbia's roughly 700,000 residents, who have no voting member in his chamber at all, were scheduled for governance from above in the post-shutdown priority list he previewed (Fox News Digital, November 2025).

Why: The grade lands at FAILS because, unlike the Speaker's file — where the counting failure rides on a law the Speaker championed — the heaviest items here carry Scalise's own name: he introduced the rescissions bill whose ledger is a list of the counted-out, and he was, on the documented account, one of the two or three men who physically worked the floor for six hours to pass a law whose official score concentrates its losses on the poorest tenth. The FOR shelf is real — full-year funding for everyone, a veterans' bill at 392–18, PEPFAR protected by his concurrence, a rural hospital fund he defended — but it is general where the against shelf is specific, and a majority leader counts everyone only if the people who cannot remove him still appear in his accounting as costs. In his two biggest files they appear as savings, under labels.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: higher-income households, who gain most under the enacted law on CBO's table (top tenth +$13,600 a year on average); taxpayers and service users in all districts from the completed FY26 funding; combat-wounded veterans under the Major Richard Star Act; rural hospitals covered by the $50 billion fund; PEPFAR patients protected by the Senate amendment he carried.
  • Hurt: the bottom tenth of households (−$1,200 a year on average, CBO, August 2025) and the 10 million projected to be uninsured by 2034; recipients of the $7.9 billion in rescinded international assistance and the audiences of the stations losing $1.1 billion in public-broadcasting funds; federal workers during the 43-day shutdown; District of Columbia residents governed over without a vote.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 3 January 2023, with dates. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes: keeping a word can still hurt, and breaking one can still help, and both are recorded in full.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The 2024 platform, delivered on the deadlines he named — the mandate rule's core case. Republicans won the 119th Congress on President Trump's America First platform, and Scalise's dated predictions for his own office's product were kept to the week. On 16 May 2025 he said the One Big Beautiful Bill would pass the House "next week… Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, somewhere in there"; it passed on Thursday 22 May 2025, 215–214 (Scalise on Fox Business, 16 May 2025; House vote, 22 May 2025). The leadership's promise of a signed bill by 4 July was met on 3 July 2025, 218–214, after the six-hour held-open vote he worked on the floor (Washington Post whip-operation account, September 2026; House vote, 3 July 2025). On 6 June 2025 he introduced the Rescissions Act and said he looked forward "to seeing this legislation come to the floor next week"; it passed the House on 12 June 2025, 214–212 (Scalise statement, 6 June 2025; House vote, 12 June 2025). A schedule-keeper's word is his calendar, and on the tenure's biggest items the calendar he announced is the calendar that happened.
  • The process word — kept in form. He told members seeking floor time, of both parties, that the road ran through the committee chairmen and regular order (WCIA/The Hill, 2026), and the final appropriations cycle ran exactly that way, committee-led, with all 12 FY26 bills passed individually in January 2026 (House Appropriations Committee, January 2026). His 2026 legislative calendar was released on 18 November 2025, as the office's standing practice promises the minority and the members a year of dates in advance (Majority Leader's office, 18 November 2025).
  • Crossing 1 — kept his word AND it helped people: the FY26 appropriations completion. The leadership promised regular order and full-year funding; the 12 bills passed; funding certainty helped every agency, worker, and service user in both parties' districts.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Crossing 2 — kept his word AND it still hurt people: fiscal responsibility. "Restore fiscal responsibility" was the stated promise of his rescissions bill and of the platform generally (Scalise statement, 6 June 2025). He kept the platform word — the tax law and the $9 billion rescissions package were the promise, delivered — and CBO scores the signature law alone at $3.4 trillion added to deficits over 2025–2034, revised in CBO's February 2026 outlook to $4.7 trillion over 2026–2035 with growth effects counted, before some $3.0 trillion of tariff offsets in the same outlook; his own $9.4 billion rescissions figure, as introduced, became $9.0 billion as enacted ($7.9 billion aid + $1.1 billion broadcasting), a kept word at one-three-hundredth of the scored addition (CBO, July 2025 and 11 February 2026; Rescissions Act as enacted, July 2025). The keeping earns its credit on the FOR shelf; the hurt — the debt service and the uninsured count at Test 4 — is recorded in full, not netted away. His related public prediction in May 2025 that growth would run "at least 3%," above the 2.6% the conference had settled on for scoring, is recorded as a campaign-grade forecast the dossier does not grade as a governing pledge.
  • Crossing 3 — went against his word AND it helped people: the PEPFAR reversal. The bill he introduced on 6 June 2025, and celebrated on passage, cut into the President's Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief as part of its $8.3 billion (as introduced) foreign-aid rescissions. The Senate struck $400 million of PEPFAR cuts and exempted the major disease and maternal-and-child-health programs, and on 18 July 2025 Scalise's House concurred in the smaller bill rather than holding his introduced text (Senate amendment and House concurrence, July 2025). The break from his own bill's scope is recorded here in full — and the help is recorded in full: the AIDS-relief money his original text reached for stayed appropriated.
  • The majority word — the outcome reversed. A majority leader's standing promise to his conference and his voters is to keep and grow the majority. Scalise's own accounting of the effort, in his 4 November 2026 letter seeking the minority leadership, was $282 million raised across his leadership career and $67.9 million this cycle, $23.7 million of it given directly to House campaigns and the NRCC (Scalise Dear Colleague letter, 4 November 2026, majorityleader.gov). The voters' count went the other way: from a 219–214 majority (WSJ/AP, November 2026) to at least 255 Democratic seats against at least 167 Republican on the AP count reported 10 November 2026, with other outlets' uncalled-race totals differing and printed separately in the Subject line. Effort and money are documented; the promised outcome is the opposite of the record, and his conference then voted 106–73 that someone else should lead the recovery — a judgment on the word's delivery rendered by the word's own audience.
  • The transparency word — a claim his schedule contradicts. Scalise said on 17–18 November 2025 that the Epstein vote would "continue to show the transparency that we've delivered" (The Banner, November 2025). The same record shows the bill reaching his floor only by discharge petition over his leadership's opposition, and his stated position a year later still that he does "not support that process" by which it arrived (Tests 1 and 3). This is filed as a characterization at war with the record rather than a dated pledge broken, and weighted accordingly.
  • Crossing 4 — broke his word AND it hurt people — is not documented as a personal, dated pledge of Scalise's in the record assembled here: his clearest shortfalls are an outcome the voters reversed (the majority) and a characterization the record contradicts (transparency), neither of which is a promise he made and then personally broke to someone's harm. The dossier records the absence rather than inventing the crossing, as the method requires. His forward words in the 4 November 2026 letter — that as Republican Leader his "number one goal" would be to win back the majority in 2028 and that the conference should hold him accountable to it — were a candidacy's promises for an office he did not win on 10 November 2026; they are recorded so the record holds them, and they cannot be graded against a tenure that never began.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because Scalise's word-record is the mirror image of his office: exact where the word is a schedule, unreliable where the word is an outcome. Every dated prediction about his own product — the reconciliation bill next week, the rescissions bill next week, the 4 July signing, the calendar a year ahead — was kept, some of them at one-vote margins he personally worked on the floor; that is a genuinely strong FOR shelf, and the mandate rule credits the platform delivery in full. Against it: the fiscal-responsibility word kept in form and reversed in the scorekeeper's arithmetic (crossing 2), his own bill's deepest cut withdrawn under Senate pressure to other people's benefit (crossing 3), and a majority promised, funded at $67.9 million for the cycle on his own count, and lost by some fifty seats on the voters'. A leader whose calendar never lies and whose outcomes do is a MIXED file, and the crossings — not the letters alone — are the finding.

Steve Scalise held the House's second office for just under four years under two Speakers, and the dossier's through-line is the difference between the levers that were his and the ones that were not.
