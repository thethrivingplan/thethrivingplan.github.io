A worked hypothetical, by the record

If we flipped the switch tonight

The question, asked plainly: if we switched to a no-money system right now, what would step one be — and what are the honest odds? Here is the simple answer first. Everything underneath it is yours to open, in your own time.

1

Step one: guarantee the floor before you touch the switch

Do not start by abolishing money. Start by making sure nobody needs money in order to live:

  • Keep the old system running while the new one is built. Every payment, ledger, and supply line stays up during the changeover. A switch you cannot reverse is not a plan; it is a cliff. How, step by step
    1. Step one: announce the changeover publicly, with dates, before anything moves — nobody should learn about the switch from a rumor.
    2. Freeze nothing. Wages, pensions, debts, rents, and prices keep working exactly as they did while the new system is built beside them.
    3. Keep every supply contract and import paid in money until the replacement has actually delivered — not merely been designed.
    4. Run the old and new systems side by side, and publish how each one is doing, so the comparison is a fact and not an argument.
    5. Retire a money function only after its replacement has met its target through several full delivery cycles in a row.
  • Count what is actually there. Food, fuel, medicine, homes, spare parts — and the people who know how to run things. Money can hide a shortage for a while. An honest inventory cannot. How, step by step
    1. Step one: count the physical stocks — food in store, fuel in tanks, medicine on shelves, homes standing empty or occupied, spare parts on hand.
    2. Count capacity, not just stock: what the farms, bakeries, clinics, and workshops can produce or move each week.
    3. Count the people and the skills: who can farm, repair, nurse, drive, teach — the retired and the part-time included.
    4. Count the need: how many people, of what ages, with what health conditions, in what kinds of households.
    5. Put the two counts side by side — what exists against what is needed — and publish the gaps by name.
    6. Re-count on a fixed schedule, and mark every figure with when it was counted. An old inventory presented as current is a shortage waiting to surprise you.
  • Give everyone a direct claim on the essentials — food, shelter, heat, basic care, local transport — that does not pass through a price. If the floor is real, everything above it can be tested. If it isn't, the test fails on day one. How “everyone's needs met” actually happens, step by step
    1. Step one: write the essentials list down — staple foods, shelter, heat and power, water, basic medicine and care, local transport — and publish it where everyone can read it. “Needs” stops being a slogan the moment it is a list.
    2. Set the amount per person from the inventory count, not from a promise: what exists, divided honestly, with the arithmetic shown.
    3. Issue the claim to every person directly, in their own name — a card, a book, a ledger entry — with no employer, landlord, or official standing in between.
    4. Make the claim work at the point of need: the storehouse, the clinic, the bus. Shown, recorded, done — no price, and no fresh application every single time.
    5. Add the need-based extras openly, the way wartime rationing did: more for children, pregnancy, illness, disability, and heavy physical work — the rules printed, never decided case by case behind a counter.
    6. Build the fix-it door before launch: a claim that is wrong, lost, or short can be challenged, and corrected by a named person within a set time.
    7. Audit in public: publish what was claimed against what was actually delivered, so a gap between the promise and the shelf shows up in numbers, not rumors.

Why this order Money is not the goods. It is the claim-check system for the goods. Tear up the claim checks before another way of handing things out exists, and people do not get equality — they get queues, favors, barter, black markets, and whoever holds the keys to the storehouse.

What “success” means on this page

Five years in: the basics reliably reach essentially everyone; people can question a decision, appeal it, and leave; and no shadow currency has quietly become the real system. A system that only holds together by locking the doors fails this test even if its shelves are full.

Go big — the working underneath

The simple answer above stands on its own. What follows is the machinery: what money actually does all day, the strongest case for replacing it, the coldest water from the record, the exhibits both sides stand on, and a way to test your own version of the switch.

01 What money actually does — the four jobs any replacement must cover ▸

“Abolish money” sounds like removing one thing. It is removing four systems at once — and a no-money world does not get to skip any of them; it has to do each job some other way.

The jobWhat it does nowThe replacement must…The hard part
Medium of exchange Lets strangers trade without finding someone who wants exactly what they have. Match needs to offers directly, or through a shared ledger everyone trusts. Matching millions of unlike needs, in real time, without the match itself becoming a currency.
Unit of account One ruler for comparing unlike things — an hour of nursing against a gearbox against a ton of wheat. Compare them in some other unit: hours, calories, need, ecological budget. Every proposed ruler distorts something. Hours say a surgeon's hour equals a sweeper's; need is real but hard to measure honestly.
Store of value Carries today's work into next year — savings, pensions, promises. Guarantee the future some other way: a standing claim on care, housing, and food in old age. The guarantee is only as strong as the future society's willingness and capacity to honor it.
Scarcity signal A rising price tells millions of strangers “use less of this” without anyone issuing an order. Publish quantities, wait times, and limits that people believe and act on. A signal nobody trusts gets gamed, hoarded around, or ignored — and then the shortage arrives unannounced.

The first three jobs are the standard textbook list — see OpenStax, “Defining Money by Its Functions.” The fourth is Hayek's 1945 argument about prices as information — influential, and still argued over; see the Federal Reserve's summary in “Hayek and the price system.”

02 The strongest case for — and the coldest water — at equal size ▸

The strongest honest case for

  • The goods are real; money is only the ticket. A harvest does not shrink because a currency does. When homes stand empty while people sleep outside, the failure is in the claim system, not the bricks — so a different claim system is at least thinkable.
  • Large parts of life already run without prices. Households, volunteers, congregations, open-source software, and Wikipedia coordinate real work through duty, love, reputation, and interest. Money is one motivator among several — not the only one ever observed. Settled record that this works at scale for information; Debated how far it stretches to steel, surgery, and shipping.
  • A floor can be lifted out of the market on purpose. Public health care and schooling already work this way in many countries. UCL's Universal Basic Services proposal costed free housing, food, transport, and communication for the UK at about 2.3% of GDP — a worked proposal, not a proof, but a serious one. UCL, 2017
  • We can count things directly now. Modern logistics already moves goods on quantities, forecasts, and shelf counts inside firms. Shared ledgers could publish scarcity itself — stock on hand, wait time, energy budget — instead of a price that mixes scarcity with bargaining power. Debated whether that data stays honest without prices checking it.

The coldest water from the record

  • Prices compress knowledge that nobody holds whole. Hayek's 1945 point: knowledge is dispersed across millions of people, and a price tells all of them “use less tin” without anyone needing to know why. A replacement must move that much truth, that fast, without a center that can be captured, fooled, or simply wrong. Federal Reserve summary
  • Equal shares bend behavior. Kibbutz research names the standing temptations — shirking, attracting more takers than makers, and losing the most skilled to the outside. The kibbutzim answered with screening, peer pressure, and making exit costly: cures with a price of their own. Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2011
  • Trust does not scale by itself. Reciprocity runs on memory and reputation. Among millions of strangers, claims need records, audits, and enforcement — and a trusted, transferable record of claims is most of the way back to money. Argentina's barter créditos became exactly that, and then were forged and over-issued until the goods behind them ran out.
  • The switch itself is the most dangerous moment. Wages, pensions, debts, rents, and import contracts are all written in money. Cancel the unit before a replacement carries those promises, and the first casualties are the people with the least cushion — the opposite of the point.

Neither column wins by volume. The case for is strongest about the floor and about small, chosen communities. The case against is strongest about scale, speed, and the morning of the switch itself. The exhibits below are where both columns get their evidence.

03 The switch, sequenced — six steps in the only order the record supports ▸

A step that only names the destination is not yet a plan. Each step below carries its own substeps — the concrete moves, in order — because every problem, this one included, has to bottom out at a step one somebody can actually take.

  1. Guarantee the floor Essentials as a direct claim, before anything else changes. Why first: it is the only step that makes every later step survivable if it fails. How, step by step
    1. Step one: write the essentials list down and publish it — staples, shelter, heat, basic care, local transport.
    2. Set a starting amount per person from the real inventory count, and show the arithmetic.
    3. Issue the claim to everyone by name, before any other rule changes.
    4. Use the distribution points that already exist — shops, clinics, depots — so day one feels ordinary.
    5. Run the floor for a full season while money still works, and publish the delivery numbers.
    6. Repair every miss in the open before moving on. The floor earns its trust by being fixed where people can see it.
  2. Map the real economy Inventories of capacity — fields, factories, skills, spare parts — not bank balances. Why: you cannot allocate what you have not counted, and money's numbers stop describing reality the moment the switch is announced. How, step by step
    1. Step one: walk the ground — warehouses, farms, workshops, clinics — and count what is physically there.
    2. Record capacity alongside stock: what each site can make, repair, or move in a week.
    3. Register the skills: who can repair, nurse, drive, build, teach — retirees and part-timers included.
    4. Trace the supply lines: what arrives from outside, from whom, how often, and what would stop it.
    5. Publish the map, with every number carrying how it was counted and when.
    6. Re-count on a fixed cycle. A stale map is worse than no map, because it still looks certain.
  3. Build the claim system before removing the old one Entitlements, needs assessment, a public ledger, and a real appeals process. Why: Argentina's lesson — claims without a trustworthy record collapse into forgery and rumor. How, step by step
    1. Step one: choose the record — a public ledger, on paper, digital, or both — that anyone may inspect.
    2. Give every claim an owner, an amount, and a date. No anonymous claims, ever.
    3. Build in the duplicate check: one person, one claim. This is Argentina's lesson, applied on purpose.
    4. Write the appeals process before launch: who hears it, how fast, and what evidence counts.
    5. Attack your own system before strangers do: try the forgery, the double claim, the lost record — and fix whatever breaks.
    6. Staff a help door with a real person who can correct a wrong entry the same day it is reported.
  4. Pilot small and chosen One town, or one sector, with volunteers — and money still legal everywhere outside the pilot. Why: bounded and chosen is where every durable case in the record lives. How, step by step
    1. Step one: pick one bounded place — a town, a campus, a cooperative — where the volunteers are the clear majority.
    2. Write the pilot's rules, measures, and end date down before it starts, and publish them.
    3. Keep money legal inside the pilot and everywhere outside it. The exit door stays open and visible.
    4. Measure from day one: deliveries made, waits, complaints, and how many people leave.
    5. Publish the pilot's numbers on a schedule — the good ones and the bad ones in the same table.
    6. Let the pilot vote, at the end date, on whether to continue. Consent is renewed, not assumed.
  5. Move sector by sector, easiest first Start where supply is plentiful and demand is steady — staple foods, local transit, broadband. Leave the scarce and specialized — transplants, jet parts — for last. Why: rationing worked on a defined list of essentials, not on everything at once. How, step by step
    1. Step one: rank the sectors by two questions — is supply plentiful, and is demand steady?
    2. Start at the top of that ranking: staple foods, local transit, broadband.
    3. Move one sector at a time. The previous sector must be running stable before the next one starts.
    4. Keep the money fallback alive inside each sector until its replacement has a track record, not a promise.
    5. Leave the scarce and the specialized — transplants, aircraft parts — for last, or for a permanent hybrid.
    6. After each sector, publish what changed, what broke, and what it cost to fix.
  6. Pre-commit the way back Publish the failure triggers in advance — missed deliveries, a growing shadow currency, rising exit requests — and honor them. Why: a reversible experiment can afford to be honest. An irreversible one has to lie to survive. How, step by step
    1. Step one: write the failure triggers down before launch — missed deliveries, a growing shadow currency, rising exit requests.
    2. Put numbers on them in advance: what counts as “missed,” and how much shadow trade is too much.
    3. Name the watchers: people with no stake in the switch continuing, so the reading stays honest.
    4. Publish the trigger readings on a schedule, whether or not any of them fire.
    5. If a trigger fires, the rollback starts automatically — no emergency meeting called to save face.
    6. Keep the old system's records archived and restartable, so the way back is a working door, not a theory.

What can I do right now?

The switch above is a whole society's job, and it is not yours to carry alone. But every step in it has a version one person can start today, at the scale of one household and one street:

  • Count what is actually in your house. Pantry, medicine cabinet, tools, spare parts — the inventory step, done at home. Most people have never taken it; it takes an hour.
  • Write your own essentials list. What you truly need to live for one month, on paper. That is the floor, defined for one household — the same list step one asks a whole society to write.
  • Learn the names and one skill each of your nearest neighbors. Who can fix a pipe, who can sit with a child, who grows tomatoes. That is the skills register, at the only scale it has ever started.
  • Give one hour before you give anything else. An hour of help to a neighbor, a timebank, or a mutual-aid group — the forty-year-old exhibit on this page runs on exactly that, and it is still running.
  • Pass one surplus thing directly to the person who needs it. The spare coat, the second ladder, the extra meal — a direct claim, settled between two people, no price in between.

That's it. Nothing to join, nothing to buy, nothing to wait for. The switch at the only scale that starts today: yours.

04 The evidence ledger — six exhibits, what each can and cannot prove ▸

Every side of this argument reaches for history. Here are the exhibits both sides use, weighed the same way: what happened, what it teaches, and — just as important — what it cannot prove.

Cambodia — the imposed abolition 1975–1979 Settled record — the events Debated — the causes' weights

The Khmer Rouge abolished money, markets, and private property outright, emptied the cities, and collectivized farming by force. At least a quarter of Cambodia's population died in under four years, from starvation, disease, overwork, and execution.

What it teaches

Total + imposed + overnight + no exit is the most dangerous combination there is. When the only distributor is the state and complaint is treason, a distribution failure has no correction mechanism — it has a body count.

What it cannot prove

That a chosen, gradual, reversible system would fail. Money's abolition arrived in the same decree as forced evacuation, collectivized labor, and terror; the record cannot separate their effects, and honest argument doesn't pretend otherwise.

Sources: Documentation Center of Cambodia · U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum

Argentina — the trueque collapse 1995–2003 Settled record — the rise and fall Debated — who broke it

During Argentina's economic collapse, barter clubs grew into a national network whose paper créditos were used by millions at the 2002 peak (estimates vary). Within about a year the system fell to a fraction of its size: the créditos were massively forged and/or over-issued, their value collapsed, and the clubs emptied of goods — in one organizer's words, “full of notes and empty of goods.”

What it teaches

The moment a community issues claims, it has rebuilt money's hardest problem — a record everyone can trust. Issuance discipline, audit, and goods actually standing behind the claims are not bureaucracy; they are the system.

What it cannot prove

Anything direct about a claim-free system: the trueque recreated money-like scrip rather than abolishing claims. And whether forgery was organized sabotage or the network's own over-issuance is still disputed by the participants' own accounts.

Sources: Gómez, “What triggers a large complementary currency system?” (IMF conference paper) · University of Florida archival collection, Red Global de Trueque

New Harmony — the chosen community that folded 1825–1827 Settled record

Robert Owen bought a ready-built town in Indiana and invited anyone to join a fully communal society: no private property, work credited at the communal store, necessities supplied in common. Within about two years it had dissolved — poorly organized and managed, production lagging, short of skilled farmers and craftspeople, with no unity among members recruited by open invitation.

What it teaches

Chosen is not enough. Goodwill does not substitute for governance, a workable match of skills to needs, and an answer to “who decides, and who does the work nobody wants?”

What it cannot prove

That chosen communities generally fail — this was one founder-led experiment with no screening of members, in 1825, without any of the tools a modern community would have. It is a warning about management, not a law about human nature.

Sources: National Park Service, “Secular Utopias in America” · Encyclopedia.com, “Owen Model Communities”

The kibbutzim — the durable ones 1910–present Settled record

Israel's kibbutzim ran the real thing for decades: equal sharing, no private property, a noncash internal economy, communal dining, collective production — roughly 120,000 members in some 270 communities at their height. After a debt crisis in the 1980s, most moved away from full equal sharing toward salaries and private budgets; the communities themselves largely endure.

What it teaches

A noncash economy can last generations — under specific conditions: small scale, members who chose the life and were screened for fit, strong shared purpose, intense mutual knowledge, and a surrounding money economy to trade with and, ultimately, to leave for. Its survival tools (peer pressure, costly exit) carry real costs in freedom and privacy, which the record should name alongside the achievement.

What it cannot prove

That the model scales to a nation of strangers. Every condition that made it work — chosen, small, screened, surrounded — is a condition a national switch does not have.

Source: Abramitzky, “Lessons from the Kibbutz on the Equality–Incentives Trade-Off,” Journal of Economic Perspectives (2011)

Timebanks — the quiet survivors 1980–present Settled record — that they persist Debated — how much they achieve

Edgar Cahn founded time banking in 1980: give an hour of help, earn an hour of credit, every hour equal. Timebanks have operated in communities worldwide for over forty years, mostly exchanging care, errands, repairs, and companionship. Research finds real social and wellbeing benefits, though the evidence base is largely qualitative and thin on long-term measurement.

What it teaches

A reciprocity ledger can run for decades at neighborhood scale and dignify work the market prices at nearly nothing — care above all. People will contribute for credit that is explicitly not money, when the circle is human-sized.

What it cannot prove

That the model can run supply chains. Timebanks trade services among members; they do not grow food, refine fuel, or stock pharmacies — and their hour-credit is itself a small currency, not the absence of one.

Sources: TimeBanks.org, Dr. Edgar Cahn · Systematic review of community exchange and time currencies, Public Health (2020)

Wartime rationing — the floor that held Britain, 1940–1954 Settled record

Facing genuine national shortage, Britain issued every person a ration book: set allowances of meat, fats, sugar, cheese, and more, with extra for children and expectant mothers. Coupons were required alongside money. The system was broadly accepted as fair, ran for fourteen years, and is credited with keeping the whole population fed through the shortage.

What it teaches

A needs-based entitlement layer can distribute essentials legitimately to an entire nation — when the list is defined, the allowances are visibly equal, need-based extras are open, and a competent state runs the logistics behind it. This is the strongest evidence anywhere for step one of this page.

What it cannot prove

That a moneyless economy works. Money never left; rationing rode on top of it, under wartime unity and emergency powers that a peacetime switch could not — and should not — assume.

Source: Imperial War Museums, “Rationing in World War 2”

Debated — a pattern, not a law
Read across these six exhibits, one pattern keeps its shape: every arrangement that lasted was chosen or consented to, bounded in scope, and left people a way out — or a working money economy around it. Every fast, total, imposed one broke, and broke people. The exhibits differ in a hundred other ways, so treat the pattern as a guide for designing a fair test — not as a proof about all possible worlds.

05 The capacity already on hand — technology we have and underuse ▸

One objection follows the switch everywhere, from New Harmony's collapse to the comments under every proposal since: fine — but who does the necessary-but-mundane work? This section weighs a fact that cuts into that objection. A large share of the mundane is already technically doable by machines, and some of it is already done by them. Deployment lags capability — for documented, mostly unglamorous reasons. No hype in either direction: the measurements below include the ones that embarrass the technology.

AI doing the mundane tasks — the headline exhibit 2023–present Settled record — the trials themselves Debated — what they generalize to

AI assistants now draft, summarize, sort, transcribe, fill in forms, write routine code, and answer first-pass questions — the paperwork layer of modern work, and much of what people mean by “mundane.” The controlled measurements point both ways, and the disagreement is itself the finding. In a 2023 controlled trial (Peng and co-authors, Microsoft, GitHub, and MIT), programmers given an AI assistant finished a set task 55.8% faster. In METR's 2025 randomized trial, experienced developers working in their own large, mature codebases took 19% longer with AI allowed — while believing, afterwards, that it had sped them up. Both are real measurements. They measured different work: bounded tasks with a clear finish line versus deep-context work where reviewing the machine's output costs more than it saves.

The deployment gap is wider than the capability gap. The McKinsey Global Institute's current analysis holds that today's technologies could theoretically automate more than half of US work hours — and names the binding constraint as workflow redesign, investment, and policy, not capability. Estimate — judgment MIT's Project NANDA (2025) found roughly 95% of enterprise generative-AI pilots producing no measurable profit-and-loss effect, with the failures traced mostly to workflow fit and budgets aimed at visible tools rather than back-office plumbing — a figure other analysts dispute as too narrow a definition of success. Debated

What it teaches

A large slice of the mundane is a deployment decision, not a law of nature. The hours exist; whether they are spent by people or machines is currently decided by budgets and org charts, slowly.

What it cannot prove

That AI does this work reliably everywhere — the METR result stands against that — or that the saved hours reach anyone's floor. Today, saved hours accrue to whoever owns the workflow. Capacity is not distribution.

Sources: Peng et al., “The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity” (2023) · METR, “Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity” (2025) · McKinsey Global Institute, “Agents, robots, and us” · An independent read of MIT Project NANDA's “State of AI in Business 2025”

The warehouse that already runs on robots — one of them 2012–present Settled record — the deployment

In July 2025 Amazon announced its one-millionth warehouse robot, spread across more than 300 facilities, with robots assisting the large majority of its deliveries. The mundane work of hauling boxes — for a century the picture of human drudgery — is, at the largest logistics operator on Earth, substantially mechanized today. Not in a forecast: in operation.

What it teaches

Physical drudgery at scale is already optional where the building is designed for the machines. “Someone has to carry it” stopped being a complete argument the day the shelves started walking to the picker.

What it cannot prove

That this generalizes cheaply. The million robots belong to a single company with standardized buildings and enormous capital; the rest of the world's shelves are still stocked mostly by hand. And every fleet creates its own new necessary work — maintenance, supervision, repair — done, for now, by people.

Source: Coverage of Amazon's announcement, Retail Customer Experience (July 2025)

The lag itself — the productivity J-curve Documented across a century of technologies Settled record — that the lags happen Debated — how long, and for whom

Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson document why powerful technologies underdeliver for years: the machine is the cheap part. The expensive part is intangible — retraining, reorganized workflows, new management habits — investment the statistics barely count, so measured productivity first dips and only later climbs. A J-curve. Electricity and computers followed the same script over decades before their gains showed up in the numbers.

What it teaches

“We have it but don't use it” is the normal early state of a general-purpose technology, not a special failure of this one. Underuse is evidence about organizations, not about what the technology can do.

What it cannot prove

That the payoff always arrives, or arrives for everyone. A lag explains delay; it does not guarantee the destination, and it says nothing about who collects when the curve turns up.

Source: Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson, “The Productivity J-Curve” (BFI Working Paper 2019-33)

The other column gets its full say — what “mundane” hides

Everything above is true, and it is not the whole truth. The word “mundane” does quiet work in this argument: it describes a task from the outside, by someone who doesn't have to live in it.

  • For the person doing it, a mundane job is a livelihood. Acemoglu and Restrepo's study of US labor markets (1990–2007) found each additional robot per thousand workers associated with the employment-to-population ratio falling by about 0.2 percentage points and wages by about 0.42% in exposed areas — Settled record — the study with Debated size: German research found jobs reshuffled between sectors more than destroyed in total. Under money, the job is the holder's claim-check on the goods. Remove the burden and the claim in the same motion and you have not freed the person; you have stranded them. That is why this page's step one replaces the claim before celebrating any removed task.
  • Work is also identity and belonging, and the record says so plainly. Panel-data research (Winkelmann & Winkelmann, on the German Socio-Economic Panel) attributes the large majority of unemployment's cost to wellbeing to the job loss itself rather than the income loss — unemployment still hurts when the money is replaced. Jahoda's Marienthal study, following a factory town's closure in the 1930s, named what a job quietly provides besides pay: a structure for the day, contact beyond the family, a status, a purpose shared with others. A task can be mindless and still be the place where someone is known, needed, and missed when absent.
  • Some “mundane” tasks are care wearing chores' clothing. Cooking the meal, cleaning the room, driving someone to the appointment — filed as drudgery, experienced as love. A machine performing them changes what they mean, not only who does them. A society that automates its care by default has answered a question it may never have meant to ask.

So this section's verdict is narrow, and both-sided on purpose: the capacity is real, underused, and growing — and the “who does the drudgery” objection is smaller than it sounds. But capacity was never the whole objection. The switch still has to answer who decides what the machines do, who holds a claim when a task disappears, and what people are for on the days the chores do themselves.

    07 What the record could not verify ▸

    Every record page in this house ends with its gaps named. Here are this one's:

    • A voluntary, moneyless modern nation. No verified case exists — of a country choosing a moneyless system freely and sustaining it. The denominator is empty, so no honest historical success rate can be computed; every forward-looking rate on this page is a labeled judgment, nothing more.
    • Whether computing can replace price signals at national scale. The pieces exist — logistics software, real-time inventories, demand forecasting — but a whole-economy demonstration does not. Claims that “the computers can just plan it now” are Story until somebody runs one.
    • How much of Cambodia's catastrophe belongs to abolishing money itself. Forced evacuation, collectivized farming, and terror arrived in the same decrees. The record cannot separate their effects, so this page assigns the warning to the combination — total, imposed, overnight, no exit — and not to the single policy.
    • A trustworthy survival rate for intentional communities in general. Counts vary widely depending on who is counted, what counts as “communal,” and over how many years. The confident figures repeated in popular writing could not be verified against a solid census, so the community estimate above leans on the best-documented case — the kibbutzim — and says so.
    • What people would actually choose. No population has ever been offered a worked, reversible version of this switch with the floor guaranteed first. Opinions about an undefined version — for or against — are not evidence about a real one.
