IN THREE LAYERS

What it is · what it means · what it could mean for you

WHAT IT IS

A full bar built into a 7 × 16 ft tandem-axle enclosed cargo trailer (7,000 lb GVWR class). It tows to an event behind a half-ton-class truck, levels on stabilizer jacks, opens a curb-side serving hatch, and serves a complete bar where it stands — draft beer on four taps, bottled and canned drinks cold, mixed drinks from a proper well — on shore power where the venue offers it, or on its own water tanks and battery bank where it doesn't.

WHAT IT MEANS

A bar is normally a room, and a room can't come to the wedding, the festival, the ranch, or the parking-lot reunion. This design separates the bar from the building: everything a bartender's hands expect — the well, the rail, the ice, the taps, the cold — arranged in the same working order as a fixed bar, inside a box that has wheels under it. What changes is the address. What doesn't change is the pour.

WHAT IT COULD MEAN FOR YOU

A bar that goes where the people already are, owned outright, owing nothing to a venue's house bar or a rental company's weekend rate — and a second income or a community asset that parks in a driveway between events. Every part is a shelf part or a standard trade job with a named source, so the distance between this sheet and the object is measured in work and one licensing errand, not in wishes.

A trailer tows true when 10–15% of its loaded weight rides on the coupler. The floor plan is drawn to land in that band — kegs, water, and batteries sit forward of the axles; the grey tank rides empty to the event and fills only where it stands. The estimate is a drawing's promise, not a fact: the built trailer is weighed on a scale and its tongue on a tongue scale, and the load is rearranged until the numbers are the numbers (Candidate to Test 2).

The tow rule, printed as a rule

The tow vehicle's rated towing capacity is at least the loaded trailer weight ÷ 0.8 — here ≈ 5,500 lb rated — so the rig tows at 80% of rating or less, with the rest held as margin for hills, heat, and honesty. Half-ton-pickup territory. The vehicle's door-sticker payload rating must also carry the tongue weight plus passengers and gear — both stickers are read, not assumed. Ice is not towed: 150 lb of bagged ice is bought at the event end and loaded where the bar stands.

Two big loads, one rule

The point-of-use water heater draws 1,440 W while heating and a blender ≈ 1,200 W for seconds at a time. There is deliberately no ice machine — a 300–500 W continuous draw plus a water budget, spent making on-site what a bag from the store already is. Bagged ice is the ice system.

  • Shore, 30 A / 120 V = 3,600 W: everything at once peaks ≈ 2,915 W. It fits, with margin.
  • Battery, 2 × 100 Ah LiFePO₄ ≈ 2,000 Wh usable: ≈ 7.3 hrs at base load; the printed service window is 6 hours — the difference is margin the sheet refuses to spend on paper.
  • Inverter, 2,000 W pure sine: base + heater ✔ · base + blender ✔ · base + both ✘. The one-big-load rule is printed on the power panel, not left to memory.
  • Generator, 2,200 W inverter class: same arithmetic — base plus one big load — with run time set by its fuel tank.
  • Solar, 400 W roof: ≈ 1,800 Wh a Texas day — a top-up that stretches the battery, never counted as a source that replaces shore or generator.

All-electric, by printed choice

There is no propane aboard. No gas flame means no pilot lights in a moving box, no propane leak class at all, and a simpler fire sheet — at the price of the water heater's electric appetite, which the count pays openly in the one-big-load rule. A builder who wants gas heat is designing a different sheet and should write one; this one states what it does.

    IDEA 1 · P1–P3

    The Platform

    The trailer that tows true and parks level. A bar is a precision instrument — draft beer foams on a slope and refrigeration sulks off-level — standing on a vehicle. The platform is a standard enclosed cargo trailer, chosen in the tandem-axle class for two plain reasons: the weight count needs two axles' worth of GVWR with margin, and a tandem trailer tows steadier and survives a single tyre failure as a limp instead of a wreck.

    Parked, the trailer stops being a vehicle in four moves: chocks at the wheels, the coupler off the ball, stabilizer jacks down at four corners until the frame — not the suspension — carries the building, and the level checked in both axes. Levelling runs PLATINUM — align exactly, 1:1: the bubble is centred, in both directions, checked against the counter the drinks are poured on. The reason is mechanism, not fussiness: a draft system balanced for a level pour foams on a tilt, a keezer's condensate drains where gravity says, and a guest's drink slides off a sloped counter with complete impartiality. The platform's whole job is to make the ground irrelevant.

    P1The trailer›
    WHAT IT IS

    The platform itself: a 7 × 16 ft enclosed cargo trailer, tandem 3,500 lb axles (7,000 lb GVWR), electric brakes on both axles with a breakaway kit — a battery and lanyard switch that apply the trailer's own brakes if it ever separates from the tow vehicle — a 2-5/16 in coupler on an A-frame tongue, safety chains, a tongue jack, and a rear personnel door. Interior height in the 6.5 ft class, so a bartender stands upright at the well; the clear height after lining (P3) is measured and printed at Stage 1, because a bartender who has to duck at their own station is a design failure, not a posture.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    A title and registration in the builder's state — the trailer is a road vehicle before it is a bar, so its paperwork is a build part — tyres within their age and load ratings, and brakes and bearings serviced on the maker's schedule.

    MAKE / FIT

    Bought, not built — the cargo-trailer industry already builds this box by the hundred thousand. The builder's work is selection and inspection: frame straight, roof seams sound, floor solid, brakes working, and the manufacturer's GVWR and axle ratings read off the trailer's own VIN plate and written into the count in place of the class estimates.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Trailer dealers in every region and their manufacturers (the Haulmark / Cargo Mate / Pace American class of makers and their peers), new and used. A used trailer is a legitimate door — with the inspection above as its price. The VIN plate's printed ratings govern; a trailer whose plate disagrees with the count gets a new count, not an argument.

    P2The levelling and stabilization set›
    WHAT IT IS

    Four corner stabilizer jacks (scissor or telescoping class, rated well past their corner's share of 4,400 lb), wheel chocks for both sides, a pair of levelling blocks, a tongue-wheel or foot, and a two-axis level mounted where the person levelling stands — plus a small torpedo level that lives on the bar counter, because the counter is the surface the level is for.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Ground pads under the jacks on soft ground (a jack on grass without a pad is a slow drill), and the discipline printed in the operating order: chocks first, jacks second, level third, hatch last.

    MAKE / FIT

    The jacks bolt or weld to the frame's four corners by the trailer trade's standard mounts; every fastener is the Universal Screw (P19) where the mount takes a bolted fitting. The set is fitted once and lives on the trailer — nothing in it is rented, borrowed, or improvised per event.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Stocked RV-and-trailer parts from trailer dealers and RV suppliers, in rated classes with the rating printed on the part. The builder buys against the printed rating, per corner, with a wide margin — because ground, not weight, is the variable.

    P3The build-out — insulation, lining, floor, and the hatch cut›
    WHAT IT IS

    The box made into a room: rigid-foam insulation in walls and ceiling, washable FRP wall lining (fibreglass-reinforced panel — the smooth white sheet of commercial kitchens), a seamless washable floor with sealed edges where floor meets wall — and in the working zone, the floor is specified from the bartender's feet up: non-slip when wet, an anti-fatigue mat run the length of the station, and a floor drain at the well plumbed to the grey tank, the floor sloped a whisper toward it, so a night's melt and spills drain where they land instead of standing under the bartender's shoes. And the serving hatch: a framed opening in the curb-side wall, roughly 8 ft wide × 3.5 ft tall, sealed against weather open or shut.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Foam board and FRP by the sheet, flooring by the roll, aluminium angle stock, RV-roof-class sealant, and the Universal Screw (P19) throughout — a panel that cannot come off with the one driver is a future repair made miserable.

    MAKE / FIT

    The hatch cut is the one irreversible cut in the build, ordered like one: the opening is framed before it is cut — header and legs fitted to carry the wall's load around the hole — then cut, then sealed, tying into two studs each side minimum. Insulation and lining follow; the floor goes in last. A builder who has never cut a trailer hires this one cut to a trailer or RV shop and does the rest — a respectable division of labour, not a confession.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Foam board, FRP, and sheet flooring are commodity building stock (home-improvement and commercial-kitchen suppliers); aluminium angle from metals suppliers; RV-class sealants from RV suppliers. The hatch hardware itself is P4.

    IDEA 2 · P4–P9

    The Bar

    The serving machine. The guest side is a counter under an awning. The working side is a well — the bartender's station — laid out in the sequence of a drink's life: ice, bottle, mixer, glass, garnish, serve. The sequence runs one direction only: nothing backtracks, and nothing crosses the bartender's body to get made. The speed rail holds the most-poured bottles at the well's front edge, on the bartender's dominant-hand side; the ice well sits at the centre of the station, against the bartender's body, because ice is the ingredient in nearly every drink. Where the house bartender's dominant hand is the left, the station is fitted mirrored, and the rail order card is printed to match.

    The reach rule is printed as a rule: everything a normal service motion touches is within one step or a pivot of the bartender's stance — a turn, never a trip. Nothing in a standard drink requires walking the trailer's length, crouching to a floor bin, climbing for a shelf, or turning a back on the crowd. The service flow runs GOLDEN — spread, never align: order and payment at one end, pickup along the counter's length, the taps visible down the line so the next guest is deciding while the current drink is made. A single-file line at one register jams; the counter's layout is the fix, built in wood. The draft system runs PLATINUM — align exactly, 1:1 (see P7): a pour is a balance of pressure, temperature, and line resistance that either matches or foams. There is no spread setting on a regulator.

    P4The serving hatch and counter›
    WHAT IT IS

    The hatch door: the wall panel above the opening, hinged at its top edge and lifted on two gas struts to become the awning — one part doing both jobs, shut wall and open shade. Below it, the serving counter (stainless or sealed solid surface) running the opening's width, with a fold-out extension shelf on the guest side. The counter's heights are set from the bartender's side of the wall: the guest face at the standard 42-inch bar class, the interior work surface at a standing elbow's height less a hand's breadth, confirmed against the house bartender's own body at fit-out. A drink is built and set down inside one natural arm motion. A menu board mounts on the awning's face. The hatch seals weather-tight shut — the bar drives through rain at highway speed between events.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    The framed opening (P3), counter stock, gas struts rated for the panel's weight with margin, trailer-class hinges and latches, and gasket stock.

    MAKE / FIT

    The panel is the wall section P3 framed and cut — the hole's own piece becomes the door. Struts are sized from the panel's weighed weight, fitted so the awning holds in a working breeze; a prop pin backs the struts up at every event, because a strut that tires mid-service should meet a pin, not a guest. The counter fixes through the wall framing with the Universal Screw (P19).

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Gas struts are a stocked hardware class sold by length and force rating (hardware and RV suppliers); hinges, latches, and gasket are trailer-hardware stock from the same dealers who sell P1. Counter stock from restaurant-supply and building suppliers.

    P5The well — speed rail, ice well, garnish station›
    WHAT IT IS

    The bartender's station, in stainless, laid out in the order a drink is made — and fitted mirrored whole when the house bartender's dominant hand is the left. A speed rail at the well's front edge, dominant-hand side, inside a forearm's reach with no lean and no step. An insulated ice well of about 100 lb working capacity centred on the bartender's body, drained to the grey tank — and refillable without leaving the well: a lidded backup ice chest under the counter holds the night's reserve, so topping up is a crouch at the station, never a walk with a bucket; the scoop lives in the well. Garnish trays in standard gastronorm pans at the sequence's end. The tools of the motion racked at the well itself — jigger, shaker tins, strainer, bar spoon, pour spouts, towels — because a tool stored across the trailer is a tool the drink waits on. Under it all, the anti-fatigue mat and the draining well floor (P3).

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Stainless work of the restaurant class, bagged ice bought at the event end, garnish pans in standard gastronorm sizes so any kitchen supplier's pan fits, and the drain run to P10's grey side.

    MAKE / FIT

    The well is fitted as a unit against the hatch wall, plumbed to the grey tank before the counter closes over it. The speed rail's bottle order is the house's choice, printed on a card inside the well — the card also states which hand the station is fitted to, so the fitting is a fact on the record, not a surprise to the next bartender.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Speed rails, ice bins, and condiment rails are stocked restaurant-supply parts (the WebstaurantStore / KaTom class and every local restaurant-supply house), in standard widths. Gastronorm pans are an international standard size — gettable on any continent, which is the point of specifying the standard.

    P6The back bar and bottle storage — with transport restraints›
    WHAT IT IS

    The display-and-storage wall behind the bartender: bottle shelving with a raised lip rail on every shelf, glassware racks beneath, dry storage, and the reserve stock — arranged from the bartender's turn. The service backups stand directly behind the well, on the shelf the bartender's pivot faces, so a bottle that empties mid-rush is replaced from inside the station's turn. Working stock lives in the strike zone between knee and shoulder height — nothing poured all night is stored overhead or on the floor. And its second, defining feature: everything on it is restrained for the road — bottles in divided racks or behind retaining bars, glassware in its rack or a padded crate. The back bar is designed twice, once for the guest's eye and once for the pothole, and the pothole's design wins every tie.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Shelving stock, rack dividers, retaining bars with the Universal Screw (P19) or thumb-latch fittings, and a padded crate for the glass that matters.

    MAKE / FIT

    Shelves are fitted with lips and rails as one job, not as a later apology. The load check is part of Stage 3: the fitted bar is towed over the builder's worst local road, empty-stocked then full-stocked, and whatever moved is re-restrained before the first event. Movement found on the test road is information; movement found at an event is breakage.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Restaurant-supply and bar-supply houses stock bottle racks, glass racks, and shelf hardware; dividers and retaining bars are shop-made from P3 stock. The padded crate is a stocked bar-supply item.

    P7The draft system — keezer, kegs, gas, lines, tower›

    Why it runs PLATINUM. A keg pours at the pressure that keeps its carbonation in the beer (roughly 10–14 psi at serving temperature for most ales and lagers — the beer maker's own figure for the beer being poured, not a house guess). That pressure, unopposed, would blast beer out as foam. The line opposes it: beer line resists flow in proportion to its length and narrowness, and the line is cut to the length whose resistance matches the serving pressure, so the beer arrives at the tap at a near standstill and pours as beer. Pressure, temperature, and resistance, three numbers aligned 1:1. Warm beer holds less gas and the balance breaks — so the keezer holds 36–38 °F, with the controller's probe riding in a water bottle inside the cabinet so it reads beer temperature, not air swings.

    WHAT IT IS

    Four taps of draft beer, served cold and clean. The keezer — a chest freezer converted to a keg cabinet, its lid fitted with a wooden collar carrying the taps' shanks, run on an external temperature controller — stands at the well's end, inside the bartender's station. That placement is an ergonomics decision before a plumbing one: a keg change mid-service is a coupler swap, not a keg move — all four kegs ride cold in the cabinet, and when one kicks, the bartender swaps the coupler without leaving the station. Kegs are loaded at load-in: a full half-barrel is 161 lb, and loading one is a two-person lift or a dolly-and-ramp job, printed as an operating rule. The cabinet also rides at the axles' forward side, where the tongue-weight band wants the weight. The gas: one 10 lb CO₂ cylinder in a chained, upright mount (a gas cylinder is never a free object in a moving vehicle), two-gauge regulator, four-way manifold with shutoffs per keg. The lines: food-grade beer line cut to the balance length, labelled at both ends with their keg's name. The tower: four faucets through the collar, over a drip tray drained to the grey tank.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Kegs — the breweries' own property, obtained on deposit through the distributor (the keg is borrowed, its beer is bought, and the deposit is printed as part of the part). CO₂ refills by exchange or fill. Line and hardware by the foot and piece, and a spare coupler and faucet in the tool kit — draft hardware fails at events, by a law older than this sheet, and the spare is the fix carried in advance.

    MAKE / FIT

    The collar is shop carpentry; the controller is a plug-in unit — no freezer wiring is cut. Lines are measured, cut to the balance calculation for the beers actually poured, and labelled so a line can be traced by eye in ten seconds. The system is cleaned on the draft trade's own schedule — a dirty line foams and flavours exactly like a broken balance, and the sheet does not let dirt impersonate physics.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Couplers, regulators, line, faucets, and controllers from draft and beverage suppliers (the KegWorks / Micromatic class and every local draft supplier); chest freezers from appliance suppliers; CO₂ from welding-gas and beverage-gas suppliers. Kegs from the beer distributor, on deposit, returned the same door.

    P8Bottle and can refrigeration›
    WHAT IT IS

    An undercounter refrigerator — glass-door or solid, 5–6 cubic foot class — under the back-bar counter, holding bottled and canned drinks, wine, mixers, and garnish backup at food-safe cold. It is the bar's second cold box, separate from the keezer on purpose: the door that opens a hundred times a night is not the door the kegs' balance depends on staying shut.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Its share of the power count (75 W averaged), ventilation clearance at its coils per its maker's manual, and a latch or strap for the road — a fridge door that opens at a pothole empties itself with enthusiasm.

    MAKE / FIT

    Fitted under the back-bar counter on a level pad, strapped for transport, its temperature checked against a thermometer that lives in it — the display is a claim; the thermometer is the record.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    A stocked restaurant-supply class (beverage centres and back-bar coolers from the True / Beverage-Air class of makers and their peers), sold by every restaurant-supply house.

    P9Glassware, plasticware, and the point of sale›
    WHAT IT IS

    Glassware in its racks for the services where glass belongs; plasticware as the standing backup and the default where the venue, the ground, or the crowd says glass is a hazard — the choice made per event, in advance, on the event checklist, not at the first broken pint. And the POS point at the counter's order end, inside the serve motion: the drink is set down and the payment taken in the same stance — card reader facing the guest, tablet facing the bartender, cash drawer at the bartender's hand — so no sale asks the bartender to walk the counter or turn their back on the crowd. A printed price list sits on the counter where the guest can read it before ordering: a price you learn after the pour is leverage wearing an apron.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    The tablet's connectivity (venue Wi-Fi or a phone hotspot) — and an offline mode that keeps selling when the signal dies, with card sales queued, because a field's signal is a rumour — a small battery bank for the tablet's own day, and the cash float.

    MAKE / FIT

    The POS mounts at the counter's end on a swivel the guest can read; its cable runs with P12's power. The price list is printed per event from the house's own list — the sheet specifies the list's existence and placement, the house sets its prices, and licensing rules about drink pricing or specials, where they exist, are a Stage 0 check (P18), not a sheet assertion.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Tablet POS systems are a stocked service class (the Square class and its peers) — the reader is bought, the service subscribed to; the sheet names the class, not a brand loyalty. Glassware and plasticware from bar-supply and restaurant-supply houses.

    IDEA 3 · P10–P16

    The Systems

    Water is a closed loop with two tanks: potable water comes aboard through a food-grade hose into a 40-gallon tank, a 12-volt pump delivers it to the sinks on demand, and everything that goes down a drain lands in the 42-gallon grey tank — sized a step larger, on the printed rule that you can never put back more than you took out, and the grey tank must always be able to swallow the potable tank's whole contents plus the ice well's melt. The grey tank is emptied at an approved dump point — an RV dump station or a venue's approved drain — never on the ground, never in a storm drain.

    Power arrives by three doors, in a stated order: shore first (P12), generator second (P14), battery underneath everything (P13) — the battery carries the cold chain through every changeover, so the kegs never learn that the power source changed. Solar (P15) feeds the battery when the sun is up.

    P10The water system — tanks, pump, plumbing›
    WHAT IT IS

    A 40-gallon potable tank and a 42-gallon grey tank, both food-grade polyethylene, mounted low and forward (potable) and low (grey); a 12 V demand pump of the 3 GPM class with an accumulator — a small pressure vessel that smooths the pump's pulses, so the tap runs steady and the pump cycles gently; a food-grade fill hose and lockable fill port; a filter on the sink feed; tank gauges or sight tubes on both tanks, mounted on the aisle wall where the bartender reads them at a glance in passing, because a budget you cannot read is a surprise scheduled for mid-event; and drain valves that empty the whole system for winterizing and for the tow-home-light rule (grey travels empty).

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Water itself — filled from a potable source through the food-grade hose, never a garden hose that lives who-knows-where — and sanitation: tank and lines sanitized on the tank maker's printed schedule and dilution, with the sanitizing log kept as a dated page in the bar's own book.

    MAKE / FIT

    Tanks strap to the floor frame in fitted cradles — 334 lb of water that can surge is a load case, and the cradle is designed for the surge, not the still weight. Plumbing is PEX with crimp fittings, run inside the lining where freezing and boots can't reach it, every run accessible at a panel — the one-driver rule (P19) applies to the panels. The pump mounts on a rubber pad; a pump bolted hard to a floor is a drum.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Tanks, pumps, and accumulators are stocked RV-and-marine parts (the Shurflo class of pumps; both tank sizes are standard catalogue items) from RV and marine suppliers. PEX and fittings are commodity plumbing stock everywhere. Food-grade fill hoses are an RV-supplier staple.

    P11The sinks and the water heater — the sink question, answered honestly›

    The honest part, at full size. Whether a mobile bar must carry a three-compartment sink, how large, with what water temperatures, and under whose inspection, is a health-code question whose answer is set by the jurisdiction the bar serves in — in Texas, by the state health rules and the local health department that permits mobile food-and-drink operations — and this sheet does not assert an answer it has not been given by that authority. What the sheet does is fit the larger provision now, because a sink fitted at build time is carpentry, and a sink demanded at inspection time is surgery. The exact requirement is a step-to-verify at Stage 0 (P18); if an authority requires more, the requirement, once verified, outranks the sheet.

    WHAT IT IS

    Two sink provisions, both fitted in the build. A dedicated handwash sink — small, at the well end of the back bar, one pivot-step from the bartender's station, with its own faucet, soap, and paper towels, used for hands and nothing else, and kept clear by rule: nothing stored in it, on it, or in front of it, because a handwash sink a bartender has to excavate before using is a decoration shaped like hygiene. A compact three-compartment sink across the rear wall — wash, rinse, sanitize — sized to the bar's largest washable item, with drainboards. And the heater: a 2.5-gallon point-of-use electric water heater (1,440 W class) under the sink bay, feeding both sinks.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Its share of P10's water and P12's power (the heater is the count's big load), sanitizer of the food-service class with its test strips — the sanitize compartment is a measured solution, tested with the strip, not a hopeful splash — and hot-water rationing discipline in off-grid mode.

    MAKE / FIT

    The sinks are restaurant-supply stainless, fitted into the rear counter run with drains manifolded to the grey tank and traps accessible. The heater mounts under the bay with its relief valve plumbed to a visible drain — a relief valve that drips where nobody sees it is a rumour, and the design routes it to be seen.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Compact three-compartment and handwash sinks are stocked restaurant-supply items in standard sizes; point-of-use heaters are a stocked plumbing class (the Bosch / Rheem class and their peers) from plumbing suppliers. Sanitizer and test strips from restaurant-supply houses.

    P12Shore power — inlet, panel, protection›
    WHAT IT IS

    The plugged mode's whole front door: a 30 A, 120 V RV-style inlet on the trailer's street side; a small breaker panel inside; GFCI protection on every receptacle — the ground-fault interrupter that cuts power in milliseconds when current leaks toward a person, the standing rule wherever water and electricity share a building, and this building is full of water; a surge protector at the inlet, because venue power is a stranger; and the printed power panel card: the base load, the two big loads, and the one-big-load rule, laminated where the bartender can read it in the dark.     WHAT IT NEEDS

    The venue's receptacle — 30 A where offered; the design carries a 15 A adapter for ordinary outlets, with the count re-run honestly at 1,800 W (base + one big load, nothing more, and the heater's duty watched). What a venue's circuit can actually carry is asked at booking, in the open, and written on the event sheet.

    MAKE / FIT

Inlet, panel, and every receptacle are the RV/marine classes of the same parts, so the shore system is one coherent class, not a salvage collage.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    RV inlets, panels, GFCI receptacles, and cords are stocked by RV suppliers and electrical distributors everywhere. The electrician is found the way electricians are found — licensed, local, and told exactly what the box is before they quote it.

    P13The battery bank and inverter›
    WHAT IT IS

    The self-contained mode's heart: two 12 V, 100 Ah lithium-iron-phosphate batteries in a vented, strapped battery box low and forward; a 2,000 W pure-sine inverter/charger — one unit that inverts battery to AC, charges the bank from shore when plugged, and transfers between them without a blink the cold chain can feel; a battery monitor with a real state-of-charge gauge at the bar; and 12 V distribution for the pump (P10), lights (P16), and controls, so the bar's DC citizens never pay the inverter's toll.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    The bank's own care rules, printed on the battery-box lid: LiFePO₄ is the thermally steady lithium class, it is not charged below freezing, and its terminals are covered — a wrench across 200 amp-hours is a welder nobody ordered. The bank is sized in the count; a builder who adds load re-runs the count.

    MAKE / FIT

    The battery box is shop carpentry in P3 materials, vented, batteries strapped against the road's opinion. Cabling is the marine class — thick, fused at the battery, routed where feet and kegs don't go. The inverter/charger mounts with air around it per its manual; a smothered inverter quits at the hot hour of the event, by long tradition.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    LiFePO₄ batteries and inverter/chargers are stocked classes from battery and solar suppliers (the Battle Born / Victron class of makers and their peers) and RV suppliers. Nothing here is exotic; the specification is capacity, sine purity, and transfer behaviour, and any maker meeting it serves.

    P14The generator door — and the carbon-monoxide facts›

    The CO facts, printed plain, as facts. A generator's exhaust is carbon monoxide — colourless, odourless, and lethal in enclosed and semi-enclosed air. The generator never runs inside the trailer, never under the awning, never in a tent or garage, and never where its exhaust can drift into the bar or the crowd; the public-health guidance distance — 20 feet from people and openings, exhaust aimed away — is the design's minimum, not its aspiration. A battery CO detector lives inside the trailer regardless (P17), because the design does not ask memory to do a sensor's job.

    WHAT IT IS

    The second power door: a 2,200 W inverter generator — the quiet class, chosen because a bar is a conversation and a construction generator is an argument — carried in the tow vehicle's bed, not in the trailer: the generator is a fuel-burning engine, and it sleeps with the truck. At the event it is set on the ground, exhaust pointed away from every crowd and opening, and cabled to P12's inlet like any shore source. Fuel is carried in an approved container, in the truck, never in the trailer's living space, and the generator is cool before it is refuelled.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Fuel for the event's hours plus the honest reserve, the shore cord (P12) as its cable, and a chain and lock — a quiet generator at an event is a desirable object, and the design treats theft as a load case too.

    MAKE / FIT

    No fitting: the generator is a bought appliance with a ground position marked on the event checklist — downwind, 20 ft, exhaust away, on the street or rear side, never on the hatch side, so its noise never competes with an order spoken at the counter. The checklist carries two questions asked out loud at setup: which way is the wind, and can the bartender hear an order at the hatch over it — the second judged from the bartender's ears, standing at the well, with the generator running.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Inverter generators are a stocked appliance class (the Honda / Yamaha class and their peers) from equipment and outdoor suppliers. Buy against noise rating and the 2,200 W class figure — and read the noise figure's fine print, because generator noise is quoted at part load at a distance; the rule is to compare like with like.

    P15The solar top-up›
    WHAT IT IS

    400 W of photovoltaic panels — two rigid panels — flat-mounted on the trailer's roof, a charge controller, and the run down to P13's bank. Solar's job is exactly the one the count gives it: it returns roughly 1,800 Wh across a Texas day (estimate), stretching the battery through a parked weekend and keeping the cold chain's bank topped between events. A top-up with a named size, not a promise of independence. This is the most optional part on the sheet — the bar is complete without it, and the sheet says so.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Roof area the hatch and vents don't use (the roof plan is drawn before panels are bought — the awning's arc and the roof vent get first claim), sun where the bar parks — and the honest admission printed here: a bar that works evenings, parked in a venue's chosen spot, does not always get to choose its shade. The count assumes the sun; the checklist asks about the trees.

    MAKE / FIT

    Panels mount on the RV class of roof mounts, sealed with the RV-roof class of sealant, cabling through a gland fitting. Flat mounting costs some yield against tilted — the count's 4.5 peak-sun-hours already prices flat, temperate-latitude reality, which is why the figure is an estimate and not a brochure number.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    Panels and controllers are stocked solar-supplier classes everywhere; the specification is wattage, footprint, and the controller's lithium profile. P15 is fitted when the builder wants the weekend stretch, not because the design limps without it.

    P16Lighting and ventilation›
    WHAT IT IS

    LED lighting in three circuits, every one aimed from the bartender's eyes outward. The work light over the well and back bar — bright, colour-true, mounted above and behind the bartender's shoulder line and shielded, so the light lands on the rail, the ice, and the glass and never in the bartender's eyes when they look up at a guest. The guest light under the awning — warm, dimmable, aimed outward and down at the crowd's side, never back through the hatch into the well.     IDEA 4 · P17–P19

    The Rules

    Licensing, safety, and the checks that make a patch of ground legal to stand on. A fixed bar's licence hangs on its wall, and the wall never moves. A mobile bar's wall is wherever it parked — so the questions a fixed bar answers once, this bar answers as a discipline: is this operation licensed, is this ground legal to serve on, is this event covered, are the people serving qualified. The design's answer is procedural, printed here at the same size as the draft system's: the licence file is a build part (P18), it is built first, and no event is served that the file doesn't cover. Where this sheet's knowledge ends, it prints UNKNOWN and names the authority who holds the answer — the fine print law of this house is both halves: read the fine print on anything coming at us, and justify every bit of the fine print we write.

    P18The licence file — Stage 0, built before the trailer›
    WHAT IT IS

    A physical and digital file, built before any build-out money is spent, containing the verified answers — from the authorities themselves, in writing where they give it — to the questions this design cannot answer from its own knowledge. The alcohol licence: in Texas, the door is the Texas Alcoholic Beverage Commission (TABC). Which licence or permit class covers selling and serving alcohol from a trailer, whether and how a trailer can stand as a licensed premises, what event-by-event permission looks like, and what the application costs and takes — all UNKNOWN to this sheet, settled by asking TABC and writing down what TABC says. Wet and dry ground: Texas sells alcohol by local option — whether the specific ground an event stands on is legal for the specific drinks served is a check run per event, against the authority's own records, its answer written on the event sheet before the trailer rolls; “the venue surely handles that” is the one sentence this design never believes. The health permit: the sink, water, and tank requirements (P11) as the permitting health department actually states them. The servers: what training or certification the jurisdiction requires of the people pouring (in Texas, TABC seller/server training is the named door — its exact requirement is part of the Stage 0 verification, not a sheet assertion), plus the house's own stricter rules: ID checked as the verified rules require, no service to anyone the law or the house says no to, and a named sober person in charge at every event. The venue and the locality: the venue's own permissions, local ordinances on noise, hours, and signage, and the fire authority's view of a crowd around a trailer.

    WHAT IT NEEDS

    Postage, patience, and the fee schedule as the authorities publish it — the file's costs are whatever the authorities charge, learned at Stage 0, and budgeted like a part, because they are one.

    MAKE / FIT

    Stage 0's method is the house method: ask the authority, in writing where possible; file the answer with its date and the name of the office it came from; where two offices disagree, the stricter answer governs until the disagreement is resolved in writing. The build does not proceed past Stage 0 on a verbal “should be fine” from anybody — including anybody's memory of how it worked somewhere else.

    WHERE TO GET IT

    From the authorities themselves: TABC's published licensing channels for the alcohol questions; the state and local health departments for the permit questions; the venue and the locality for the event questions. The resource is obtained by asking, and the asking is the rung of the ladder this part exists to print.

    SAFETY FACTS

    Printed plain, as facts

    • Carbon monoxide. The generator rules at P14 are facts, not advice: never inside, never under the awning, 20 feet and downwind, exhaust away, detector aboard and tested. CO is the one hazard at an event that gives no warning of itself; the design's whole answer is distance, direction, and a sensor.
    • Towing. The breakaway battery is charged and its lanyard clipped on every tow, without exception — a runaway trailer is the worst object this design can make. Safety chains crossed under the tongue. Tyres are replaced on age as well as wear; a trailer tyre that looks fine and is seven years old is a blowout with a date on it, and the date is read at every Stage-4 checklist.
    • Alcohol and people. The bar serves a drug that impairs the people it serves, in a place with vehicles. The design's facts: the house service rules at P18 are part of the build, the person in charge is sober, and the event plan includes how guests who drank get home — asked at booking, in the open, like the power question. A bar that plans its drinks and not its departures has done half its job.
    • Water discipline. Potable and grey are different colours of hose, different fittings, and different ends of the trailer, and they never meet: the fill hose fills, the dump hose dumps, and neither ever does the other's job. Cross-contamination is prevented by making it physically awkward, not by remembering hard.
    • The crowd side. The awning is propped (P4), the counter is solid, cords are ramped, and the ground is walked (P17). A bar's guests are, by the design of the evening, progressively worse at watching their step; the setup is done by the sober, in advance, for the people they will become.

    This sheet's bartender-first claims — the reach rule (one step or a pivot), the well refilled from its own backup chest, the keg change as a coupler swap at the station, the POS inside the serve motion, air moving at the bartender's stance — are design claims, not measurements. If the drill shows a station element belongs somewhere else, the station moves: the bartender's body outranks the drawing.

    THE LOGIC

    The logic behind all of it

    • Why a trailer, and not a truck or a tent: a tent bar rents its weather and owns nothing; a truck bar ties the bar's life to an engine's. A trailer is the bar as a pure room — towable by whatever the house already drives, parkable in a driveway, sellable or lendable as a thing in itself. The bar outlives any vehicle that ever tows it.
    • Why the licence is Stage 0 and not a later errand: because every other stage spends money the licence might forbid spending that way. A premises rule can change the trailer; a health rule can change the sinks; a commissary rule can change the business. Paper first is not caution — it is the cheapest order of operations, and this house builds in the cheap order.
    • Why two cold boxes: because the kegs' temperature is a leg of a physical balance (P7) and the bottle fridge's door is a social object opened all night. Separating them means the social door never spends the physical balance. One box doing both jobs would fail at both, politely, all evening.
    • Why all-electric, no propane: one fuel fewer is one hazard class fewer — no leaks, no pilots, no flame in a moving wooden box — and the cost is printed where it belongs, in the count's one-big-load rule, instead of being hidden in a gas bill nobody itemized. The sheet trades a convenience for a hazard class and says so.
    • Why the counter spreads the crowd (GOLDEN) while the level and the pour align exactly (PLATINUM), on the same sheet: because each spec takes the mode its mechanism demands. People are a flow, and flows jam at points — so the crowd spreads. A pour is a balance, and balances match or fail — so the draft aligns. A level is a fact, and facts don't average — so the bubble centres. The sheet stamps no mode where the mechanism doesn't call for it, and the whole unit — exact in its working parts, spread across any ground it is invited to — is the weave of the two: DIAMOND is the trailer itself, the bar that aligns locally and travels globally.

    • ✔ 6The logic behind all of it — the why printed under the whole design and under each major choice: the trailer, Stage 0 first, two cold boxes, all-electric, and the modes each spec runs.
    • ☐ 7The will to make it happen — this one the reader supplies. The sheet carries everything else; the will is what a builder brings. The contract is open — anyone may build it, and what is built is free — so the sheet's whole job is to make the will enough: no gap in the paper should ever be the reason a willing builder stopped. When you pick this up, the seventh line checks itself.

    What are we missing? Every blueprint has a blind side. If a part is underspecified, a number disagrees with its part class's published figure, or an open box is missing from the shelf — say so. The sheet gets better in the open or not at all.

    These ideas look the best on paper. When we know better, they change. Two things don't: be good — and everything is free. Those aren't rules. They just are.
