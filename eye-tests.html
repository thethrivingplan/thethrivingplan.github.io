This is a screening game. It is NOT an eye exam, and it cannot diagnose anything.

Screen size, brightness, colour settings, viewing distance, room light, and tired eyes all move your result — sometimes a lot. A phone held close in a dark room and a big monitor across a desk will not agree, and neither one is a measurement.

A real measure of your eyes needs an eye professional with calibrated equipment. If anything here worries you — or your vision has changed, or one eye sees differently from the other — book a real eye exam. Treat every result below as a reason to look closer, never as a verdict.

1 · Hidden in the dots

Why? → Some eyes separate colours that other eyes blend — a hidden number shows you which.

A number or shape is hiding in this field of dots. The dots that form it are a different colour family from the rest — but close in brightness, so you cannot cheat by looking for “the darker ones.” Every plate is generated on the spot; press New plate and a different one appears.

Hint
Hold your phone at a comfortable reading distance and look at the whole circle, not one dot. If nothing appears after ten seconds, that itself is information — answer “I see nothing.”
The full why
Plates like this work because the figure and the background differ mainly in hue (which colour) while their brightness is scrambled. If your colour vision separates that hue pair easily, the figure pops out. If it doesn’t, the dots merge into one speckled field and there is genuinely nothing to see — the number hasn’t faded, your visual system never received the signal that separates it. The Learn section below explains the three cone types behind this.

The vanishing plate — the trick, explained · Tap to open

One plate in the rotation is built to vanish for red-green-deficient eyes. Its figure dots are reds and its background dots are greens chosen to sit on the same “confusion line” — pairs of colours that look different only if your red and green cone signals are both working and distinct. The brightness of every dot is randomised, so brightness gives no clue.

To typical eyes: a clear number. To red-green-deficient eyes: the red dots and green dots produce nearly the same signal, so figure and background arrive as one undifferentiated field. The number isn’t hidden better — for those eyes, the difference it is drawn with does not exist.

Use the simulation button above on any plate to watch this happen from the outside. And remember the limits at the top: a screen can imitate the trick, but only a professional test can say anything about your eyes.

3 · Letters that fade away

Why? → Sharpness isn’t everything — eyes also have to pull faint things out of fog, dusk, and glare.

Each row is printed fainter than the one above. Find the faintest row you can still read with confidence, tap its button, then type its letters to prove it. Contrast fades for everyone eventually — where it fades is the interesting part.

The full why
Contrast sensitivity is a separate channel from acuity: you can read tiny black letters and still struggle with grey text on a grey day. Cataracts, glare, and some eye conditions lower contrast sensitivity before they blur sharp vision — one more reason a game like this can only ever be a pointer toward a real exam, never a substitute.

4 · The shape in the static

Hint

How colour vision works

Why? → The games above stop being magic once you know the machinery.

Three kinds of colour sensors · Tap to open or close

What it is: The back of your eye holds about six million cone cells, in three kinds. One kind responds most to long-wavelength light (we call its signal “red”), one to medium (“green”), and one to short (“blue”). You never see wavelengths directly — your brain compares the three signals and builds colour from the differences. A ripe tomato isn’t red in itself; it reflects light that drives the long-wave cones hard and the others weakly, and “red” is the name your brain gives that pattern.

What it means: Colour is a comparison, not a label stuck on objects. Change any one of the three signals and the whole comparison shifts — which is exactly what happens in colour-vision deficiency.

What it could mean for you: Two people can look at the same dot plate and honestly see different things, because their hardware sends different comparisons. Neither is “not trying.”

Red-green deficiency, in plain words · Tap to open

What it is: The most common kind of colour-vision difference, affecting roughly 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women. Either the green-sensitive cones respond to light shifted toward red (so red and green signals overlap more than usual), or one cone type is missing its usual pigment altogether. Reds, greens, browns, and oranges that look obviously different to typical eyes can produce near-identical signals.

What it means: Certain red-green pairs become confusion pairs — different in the world, the same in the signal. That is the whole trick of the vanishing plate above: draw a number using only a confusion pair, scramble the brightness, and the number exists for one visual system and not the other.

What it could mean for you: It’s a difference, not broken eyes — acuity is usually normal. But it matters practically: ripe vs. unripe fruit, some wiring and map colours, some jobs’ colour tests. A professional screening can say which kind and how strong; this page cannot.

Blue-yellow deficiency, in plain words · Tap to open

What it is: Much rarer, and it affects men and women equally. Here the short-wave (“blue”) cone signal is weakened or missing, so blues slide toward green and yellows toward pinkish-grey. The confusion pairs are blue-with-green and yellow-with-violet/pink.

What it means: A plate drawn in blue against a teal-yellow field can vanish the same way the red-green plate does — one of the plates in the rotation above is built on exactly this pair.

What it could mean for you: Because it’s rare and can also be acquired — some eye diseases and medicines affect blue-yellow vision first — a sudden change in how blues and yellows look is a “book a real exam” signal, not a game result.

Why the hidden number vanishes — the one-paragraph version · Tap to open

Your brain finds the number by finding a boundary — a place where the signal changes. The plate offers only one kind of change: hue, red-family on one side, green-family on the other, with brightness deliberately scrambled so it draws no boundary of its own. If your red and green cone signals are distinct, the boundary is loud and the number stands on it. If those two signals arrive nearly identical, there is no boundary anywhere in the field — not a faint number, not a blurry number, no number at all. The information was never in the signal your brain received. That’s why these plates are a fair test of the signal, and why “try harder” is never the answer to one.
