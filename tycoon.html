Pro tools

Tap to open: Margin vs markup — the two numbers people mix up

Markup is what you add on top of cost: sell at $5 what cost $2, and your markup is ($5 − $2) ÷ $2 = 150%.

Margin is the share of the selling price you keep before rent and wages: ($5 − $2) ÷ $5 = 60%.

Same sale, two different numbers. Margin is the one that has to cover your rent, wages and loan interest — which is why the Coach always quotes margin, and why a "100% markup" can still lose money in a shop with high rent.

Tap to open: Cash flow vs profit — why profitable shops go broke

Profit counts a sale the moment it happens. Cash counts money the moment it moves. Buy $800 of stock on Monday and it leaves your till at once, but it only becomes "cost" in the profit column as it sells, week by week.

So a shop can show a profit every week and still run out of cash — usually because it bought stock, repaid a loan, or expanded faster than the till refilled. Watch both numbers in the HUD. Cash is the one that ends games.

Tap to open: Why expanding on debt breaks people

A loan adds two things: cash today, and a fixed interest bill every week whether trade is good or bad. Rent and wages are also fixed. Stack enough fixed bills under a business whose sales swing with the seasons, and one slow month puts cash under zero — even if the business is "profitable on average."

The test the Coach uses on your books: could you pay all your fixed weekly bills for four straight weeks with zero sales? If not, you are one bad month from the edge, whatever the profit column says.

Tap to open: Pricing — both directions are real strategies

Price lower and more customers come, but each sale keeps less. Works when your margin is fat and your shelves are full — volume pays the rent.

Price higher and each sale keeps more, but fewer customers come. Works when your product is distinctive and your costs are low — this is the premium niche.

There is no universally right price. There is only the price whose maths — margin × number of sales — beats your fixed bills. The Coach works that maths on your actual shops, above.

Three honest ways to play

More than one strategy can win. Each has a real trade-off — anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

1 · Slow and solvent

Never borrow. Grow only from profit, keep a cash cushion of at least four weeks of bills, expand one shop at a time.

What you gain: almost impossible to go bankrupt; a slow season is an inconvenience, not an ending.
What it costs: slow. A borrower who survives will reach the goal weeks before you.

2 · Borrow and grow

Take a loan early, buy a second and third shop while prices are fair, and let three tills pay the interest. Repay aggressively in good weeks.

What you gain: the fastest route to a big worth goal — more shops earning at once.
What it costs: fixed bills stack up. One slow season plus one breakdown can put you under before the growth pays off.

3 · Premium-price niche

Keep one or two shops, price well above the street average, run lean staff, and live on fat margins from fewer, happier-to-pay customers.

What you gain: high profit per sale, low stock costs, small wage bill — very resilient per shop.
What it costs: a low ceiling. Fewer sales means worth grows slowly, and a price-sensitive event hits you hardest.

About & the real history

Market Street is an original game — its name, shops, prices, events and words are all its own. It belongs to a documented kind of game: the tycoon game, a business simulation where you build and run an enterprise against costs, prices and luck.

The form got its name from a 1990 railroad-building classic — a game about laying track, running trains and out-trading rival railroad companies — whose title put the word "Tycoon" on the genre for good. Dozens of business simulations followed in the decades after, covering everything from theme parks to pizza chains, all built on the same honest loop this page uses: buy, price, staff, stock, borrow or don't, and live with the ledger.

The business lessons in the Coach are real and standard: margin and markup, cash flow versus profit, fixed costs, break-even, and the danger of expanding on debt. The money here is play money — but the maths is the maths.

How a week is worked out: each shop has a fair street price. Price below it and more customers come (with diminishing returns); price above it and they drift away. Staff set how many customers you can actually serve; stock sets how many you can sell to. Food stock spoils a little each week. Events — a street festival, a slow season, a supplier putting prices up, a breakdown, a kind review — are drawn honestly at the frequency you chose, and the ledger tells you when one lands.
