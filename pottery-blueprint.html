Pottery

Clay + water + fire = the first material humans manufactured that nature doesn’t make.

Stone tools are stone, reshaped. A wooden spear is wood, reshaped. But a fired pot is a new substance — rock that people un-made and re-made into something no riverbank ever produced. This sheet holds the whole working knowledge: the clay, the forming, the drying, the fire, the glaze, and the smallest real pot you can start with today.

Why?

Harvests rot, water runs through baskets, and cooked food has nowhere to live. A settled people needs containers that hold, keep, and cook — and don’t rot, leak, or burn.

→
How?

Earth that turns soft in water can be shaped by hand — and fire changes it back into stone, permanently. Shape it wet, harden it with heat. That one discovery is the whole technology.

→
Act.

Dig clay, clean it, wedge it, pinch a pot, dry it slow, fire it. The starter practice at the bottom of this sheet is a real pot, not a model of one.

Layered surface · the same fact at three depths

Pottery in three layers

Layer 1 · What it is

Shaped earth, hardened by fire

Pottery is clay — fine particles of weathered rock — mixed with water until it can be shaped, dried until it holds that shape, then heated until the change becomes permanent and the piece will never soften in water again. Low-fired unglazed clay is terracotta, “baked earth”; hotter firings make denser stoneware, and the hottest make porcelain.

Layer 2 · What it means

The first manufactured material

Everything before pottery was found material, cut down. Pottery is synthesis: people took the most common substance on earth — dirt — and gave it properties it never had in nature. It appears in the record before farming in some places (the oldest known pots, from Xianrendong Cave in China, date to roughly 20,000 years ago), and it becomes universal once farming does, because the two technologies feed each other.

Layer 3 · What it could mean for you

A container you can make from the ground itself

The raw material is under your feet in much of the world, the tools are your hands, and the entry skill — a pinch pot — takes an afternoon to learn. What it asks in return is respect for the fire: the temperatures that make clay permanent are temperatures that burn, and this sheet treats the safety steps as part of the blueprint, not fine print.

On the tree · Pottery sits beside Agriculture

What pottery unlocked

Pottery is Agriculture’s partner node. Farming creates a new problem — a surplus that arrives all at once and must last all year — and pottery is the technology that makes that surplus keepable.

Storage

Grain, water, and oil keep in fired clay: vermin can’t chew through it, it doesn’t rot like wood or leak like a basket, and a lidded jar holds a harvest from autumn to spring. Surplus stops being a race against spoilage.

Cooking in vessels

A pot lets food simmer in water — grains and legumes that are near-inedible raw become soft, safe, and digestible, and boiling makes questionable water safer. Whole cuisines, and soft weaning foods for infants, start here.

A surplus you can count

Jars are measurable, stackable, tradable units. Stored surplus can be carried, shared, traded, and counted. Pottery shards are among the most common finds in archaeology precisely because this node underwrote the settled world.

Stage 1 · The material

Clay: weathered rock, ready to work

Clay is what rock becomes after water and time have their way with it. Granite and feldspar-rich rocks break down over thousands of years into particles so fine they behave like no other earth: add water and they slide over each other; dry them and they lock.

Plasticity, in plain words

Clay particles are flat plates, thinner than anything you can see. Water slips between the plates like oil between sheets of glass, so the mass can bend, stretch, and be pinched into shape without cracking — that quality is plasticity. Take the water away and the plates grip; the shape stays. Add too much water and you have slip (liquid clay, used as glue and paint); too little and the clay tears instead of bending. Workable clay feels like firm cheese and bends before it breaks.

Finding it

  • Where: riverbanks and creek bends, road cuts, construction digs, lake edges, and the subsoil under the topsoil in much of the world. Clay layers are often a different colour from the soil above — grey, rust-red, tan, or blue-grey.
  • The squeeze test: wet a handful, roll it into a coil as thick as a pencil, and bend it around your finger. Good clay bends without deep cracks. Sandy or silty soil crumbles or tears.
  • The settle test: shake a lump in a jar of water and let it stand overnight. Sand sinks in minutes, silt settles in hours, true clay stays cloudy longest and settles last, on top.
  • Ask and respect: dig only where you have permission, refill your hole, and never dig under a bank that could collapse on you.

Cleaning it

Wild clay carries stones, roots, and sand. Two honest routes: dry processing — dry the lumps fully, crush them, and sieve out the grit; or slaking — soak lumps in water until they fall apart into slip, stir, pour through a sieve into a bucket, let it settle, pour off the clear water, and dry the slip on a plaster slab or cloth until it reaches working stiffness. Slaking gives the smoothest, most even clay and is how most potters have cleaned clay for centuries.

Wedging: the step nobody may skip

Wedging is kneading for clay, and it does three jobs at once: it evens out wet and dry patches so the wall of a pot dries and fires evenly; it works out air pockets, which expand in the fire and can blow a piece apart; and it aligns the clay plates so the clay is strong and cooperative. Push the lump down and away, fold it back, turn, repeat — fifty to a hundred turns for a fresh batch. Properly wedged clay cuts clean with a wire, with no bubbles or streaks inside.

Bought clay vs. dug clay Tap to open

Bagged clay from a pottery supplier is cleaned, blended, and consistent — the honest recommendation for a first firing, because it removes one whole variable while you learn the fire. Dug clay is free, local, and teaches you what clay actually is, but every deposit is different: test-fire small pieces before committing a piece you care about, and expect some dug clays to melt, bloat, or crack at temperatures a commercial clay takes easily. Neither is cheating. They are different lessons.

Stage 2 · The shaping

Forming: four ways, four whys

Every forming method is an answer to a different problem. All four are still in daily use somewhere in the world; none of them is a primitive draft of another.

Pinch

Oldest · needs nothing but hands

Roll a ball of clay, push your thumb into the centre, and pinch the walls upward between thumb and fingers, turning as you go, until the wall is even.

Why it works: the hand is both mould and measure — your fingers feel thickness no tool can report. Why choose it: small cups, bowls, and first pots; zero equipment; the fastest way to learn how clay behaves. Its limit is size and symmetry — a pinched wall is only as even as your attention.

Coil

The storage-jar method

Roll clay into ropes, stack them in rings on a flat base, and weld each ring to the one below by smearing clay across the join, inside and out. Smooth the walls or leave the coils showing.

Why it works: you build in courses, like masonry, so the clay only ever has to hold up one new ring at a time. Why choose it: this is how you make vessels too big to pinch — the great grain and water jars of the ancient world are coil-built. The one law: joins must be truly welded, or the pot splits along them in the fire.

Slab

The flatwork method

Roll clay flat between two guide sticks so the sheet is one even thickness, cut pieces from the sheet, and join them at the edges — scoring both surfaces and painting the join with slip so the two pieces fuse into one.

Why it works: rolling controls thickness exactly, and flat sheets make shapes pinching can’t: boxes, tiles, angular vessels, flat bases. Why choose it: anything with flat sides or sharp corners. Its law is the same as coil’s, doubled — every seam is a future crack until it is scored, slipped, and pressed into one piece of clay.

Wheel

A later node on the tree

On a spinning wheel head, centre a lump of clay, open it, and pull the walls up between your hands. Rotation does in seconds what pinching does in minutes, and every vessel comes out round and even-walled.

Why it works: the wheel trades hand-pressure for centrifugal steadiness — the clay passes under your fingers hundreds of times, so small corrections compound into symmetry. Why it is a later node: the wheel itself is a machine that must be invented and powered (kick wheel or motor), and centring is a real skill that takes weeks of practice. It belongs to its own sheet — and to this blueprint’s hands-on kin, the Pottery page (The Wheel). The hand methods above are not its rough draft; they stand on their own.

The even-wall law

Whatever the method: even thickness is survival. A thick base under thin walls dries unevenly, shrinks unevenly, and heats unevenly — three chances to crack. Aim for walls like a coin’s edge to a finger’s width, the same all over, and hollow out any part of a sculpture thicker than about 2.5 cm (1 inch).

Stage 3 · The waiting

Drying: slow and even, because steam is the enemy

As clay dries it shrinks — typically around 5% for common clays — and it shrinks around whatever water is left. Dry the outside fast and it shrinks over a still-wet, still-full inside: the surface tears. That is the whole science of drying, and every rule below is that one fact wearing work clothes.

The stages you will handle

  • Soft: just formed. Too wet to hold detail; a rim will flop if you rush it.
  • Leather-hard: firm as cheese or stiff leather, still cool and damp to the touch. This is the working window — trim bases, carve, attach handles and lids now. Too dry and a join won’t fuse; too wet and the piece deforms in your hands.
  • Bone dry: room-temperature to the touch, lighter in colour, no coolness left. Only bone-dry work goes into a fire. Bone-dry clay is also at its most fragile — it has the strength of chalk, and it is thirsty: a splash of water can crack it.

The working rules

  • Dry slow: cover work loosely with plastic and let it dry over days, not hours. Uncover a little more each day.
  • Dry even: rims and thin edges dry first. Wrap rims in a strip of plastic or a damp cloth while bases catch up; turn pieces over or dry them on a porous board so the base dries too.
  • Keep it out of moving air and sun until it is leather-hard all over. A draught is a one-sided dryer.
  • Test before firing: hold the piece against your cheek. Any coolness means water remains. Give it another day. This old test has saved more pots than any instrument.

Why “bone dry” is a safety rule, not a style tip

Water trapped inside clay turns to steam in the fire — and steam takes up far more room than the water it came from. A piece fired still damp inside can explode in the kiln, scattering hot shards that wreck neighbouring work and can injure anyone who opens the kiln. Slow, complete drying is the first safety step of firing, and it happens on the shelf, days before any flame.

Stage 4 · The transformation

Firing: what the fire actually does

Firing is not drying-with-more-heat. It is a one-way chemical change. Up to a few hundred degrees the piece is still, in principle, mud that could slake back down in water. Past that point the clay’s chemistry itself changes, and past a higher point its particles begin to melt into each other — vitrification — and the pot becomes stone for good.

The chemistry, in plain words

  1. The water you can feel leaves first (to ~200°C). The mixing water between the clay plates evaporates. Go slowly here: this is the steam-explosion zone, and kilns “candle” — hold at a low heat for hours — to walk damp ware through it.
  2. The water you can’t feel leaves next (~450–600°C). Clay minerals hold water inside their very structure, as part of the mineral. Heat drives this chemical water out, and the mineral is changed forever — this is the moment clay stops being clay. After it, no soaking will ever turn the piece back into workable mud.
  3. Quartz flips at 573°C. The silica in clay suddenly expands as its crystals change form (the quartz inversion), and flips back, shrinking, at the same temperature on the way down. Heat and cool gently through 573°C both ways, or the sudden size change cracks the work.
  4. The clay begins to melt into itself (~900°C and up). Fluxes in the clay start to form glass, which flows between the remaining particles and glues them into a single stony mass — vitrification. A little glass makes strong, still-porous earthenware; more glass makes dense, waterproof stoneware. Too much heat and the whole piece slumps and melts. Every clay has its window, and firing inside it is the potter’s central skill.

Temperature landmarks

Potters measure heat-work with pyrometric cones — small pyramids of ceramic material, numbered, that soften and bend at known combinations of temperature and time. A cone that has bent says “this much heat-work happened here,” which a thermometer alone can’t: clay responds to heat and time together. Cone numbers run from 022 (coolest) up through 01, then 1, to 10 and beyond; a leading zero means the cool end of the scale. Temperatures below are the standard equivalents at a typical firing rate — treat them as landmarks, not promises.

Kiln vs. open firing — the honest comparison

 Open / pit firingKiln firing
What it is Ware set in a shallow pit or barrel with wood, dung, or sawdust, covered and burned. The oldest method on earth — and still living practice in many pottery traditions. An insulated chamber (electric, gas, or wood) that holds heat in and lets the potter control the climb, the peak, and the cooling.
Heat reached Roughly 600–900°C, unevenly. Enough to make the chemical change permanent — real pottery — but not enough to vitrify. As high as the clay needs, evenly, on a schedule. The only route to stoneware and porcelain.
The ware Porous earthenware: beautiful smoke patterns and flame marks no kiln can fake, but it weeps water, chips more easily, and is not for long simmering. Stronger, denser, repeatable. Glazes fire true. Two firings (bisque, then glaze) are routine.
The honest cost Cheap and immediate — but high losses to cracking and uneven firing are normal, the fire is open and outdoors, and smoke is part of the bargain. Costs money and power, demands ventilation and respect (see Safety), and an electric kiln’s elements and a gas kiln’s burner are real equipment with real manuals. Control is what you are paying for.

Neither is the “real” one. Pit firing made the pottery of most of human history; the kiln is what happens when potters needed ware stronger, bigger, and the same every time. Choose by what the vessel must do.

Stage 5 · The skin

Glaze: glass, worn by clay

A glaze is a coat of glass fused onto the clay in the fire. The recipe family is always the same three jobs: silica to make the glass, a flux to make that glass melt at kiln temperatures instead of the far higher heat pure silica needs, and alumina to keep the melted glass from running off the pot. Colour comes from metal oxides — iron for ambers and browns, cobalt for blue, copper for greens.

What glaze is for: it seals porous earthenware so it can hold liquid; it makes a surface that can be washed clean, which is a food-safety matter and not just tidiness; and it carries colour and finish. Stoneware vitrified in the fire is waterproof without glaze, but a glazed interior is still easier to keep clean. Glaze is usually applied to bisque-fired ware — by dipping, pouring, or brushing — then fired a second time to melt it. Glaze and clay must also fit: if the glass shrinks more than the clay as they cool, it crazes into a network of cracks; if less, it can shiver off in flakes. Fit is tested, not assumed.

The safety line, at full size: some glazes are poison

Some traditional and commercial glazes contain lead — historically prized because it melts low and shines bright — and other toxic materials, including cadmium and barium in some formulations. Lead can leach out of a glazed surface into food and drink, especially acidic food, and lead poisoning is cumulative and serious, above all for children.

So the rule of this blueprint is plain: a vessel meant for food or drink gets only a glaze sold and labelled as food-safe, fired exactly to its maker’s schedule, on the clay it was tested for. A beautiful old recipe, a market glaze with no label, or a finish you cannot identify is decoration for a shelf piece — never a cup. This sheet prints no recipes for toxic glazes, and no firing trick makes a lead glaze into a dinner-safe one.

Dry glaze materials are also dusty chemistry: mix and sand them only with a proper respirator, keep them out of food areas, and treat unknown inherited powders as hazardous until identified.

Act · The starter practice

Your first pot: the pinch pot, honestly

The smallest real version of this entire technology is a ball of clay and your two hands. Everything on this sheet — cleaning, wedging, even walls, slow drying, the fire — is present in it at full strength, at hand scale.

  1. Wedge a ball of clay about the size of a tennis ball until it cuts clean with no bubbles (see Stage 1). If it sticks to the table, it is too wet; if it cracks when bent, too dry.
  2. Open it: cradle the ball in one palm and press your other thumb into the centre, stopping about a finger’s thickness from the bottom. That stop is your base — feel it, don’t guess it.
  3. Pinch the walls up: pinch between thumb inside and fingers outside, turn the pot a little, pinch again slightly higher. Work in spirals from base to rim, several gentle passes rather than one hard one. Keep the wall one even thickness.
  4. Set the rim and base: tap the base flat on the table; smooth the rim with a damp fingertip. A crack at the rim now is a crack in the fire later.
  5. Dry it slow under loose plastic for several days, then uncovered until it passes the cheek test — no coolness anywhere (Stage 3).
  6. Fire it — in a community kiln’s bisque firing if you can (cone 06 is the usual ask), or leave it unfired and read the honest table below before deciding what it is.

Part of the blueprint · Not fine print

Safety: kilns and fires are real hazards

Documented craft knowledge includes the ways the craft hurts people. These steps are load-bearing parts of the blueprint — a firing that skips them is not a bolder firing, it is an unfinished one.

  • Heat: a kiln at bisque temperature will cause deep burns instantly, and ware stays dangerous for hours after firing. Open kilns and move ware only at the maker’s stated temperatures, with kiln gloves and tongs — and assume any piece near a kiln is hot until you have proven otherwise.
  • Never look into a firing kiln unprotected: use the shaded viewing lenses made for it; the light at temperature can damage eyes.
  • Ventilation: firings release fumes — from the clay, from glazes, and from anything that shouldn’t have been in the kiln. Kilns belong in ventilated spaces with the maker’s venting fitted; gas and wood kilns also produce carbon monoxide and belong outdoors or under proper flues, never in living space.
  • Fire discipline outdoors: pit and barrel firings are open fires. Clear ground, no firing in dry windy weather, water at hand, local fire rules obeyed, and the fire attended or fully cold before it is left.
  • Dust: dry clay and glaze dust contain silica, which damages lungs over time. Clean with wet methods, not dry sweeping; wear a fitted respirator when mixing dry materials or cleaning a kiln.
  • Electricity and fuel: kiln wiring, elements, and gas burners are installed and serviced to the manufacturer’s instructions and local code. If that sentence is beyond the setup you have, a community studio’s kiln is the honest route — it is also how most potters in cities actually fire.
  • Food ware: the glaze rule from Stage 5 stands at full size — food-safe labelled glaze only, fired to schedule, or the piece does not touch food.
