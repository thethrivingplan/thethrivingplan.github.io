The Hub-Fed Machine

Put the snow or fog machine up high, where its output covers the whole yard. Keep the juice inside, in a reservoir hub you can reach in your socks. A pump and one feed line connect them — so refills happen where it's warm, in any weather, and the machine on the roof never needs you to climb up and feed it.

"A snow machine that I can put on the roof — but I don't have to feed the juice [up there]. I feed the juice inside somewhere, so if it's raining or it's cold I don't have to go outside and refill the machine itself. I just pour it in something in the hub and it works — it's fed there."

— TRISTAN, THE IDEA AS GIVEN, NOV 20, 2026
01

What it is

Three parts, one idea: separate the machine from its tank, and put each where it belongs.

The emitter — up high

A snow, fog, or haze machine mounted at the eave, gable, or roofline. Height is coverage: a pro snow machine rated for the stage covers about a third more ground mounted 16 feet up than sitting on the floor. Gravity and fall time do the spreading for free.

The hub — inside

A reservoir (10–25 litres) in a garage, laundry room, or closet, with a filler neck you can pour a jug into without spilling, a level you can read at a glance, and the pump. This is the only part you ever touch day to day.

The line — between them

One small-bore feed line, 6–8 mm, run through the wall and up to the machine — and plumbed so that when the system shuts off, the line drains itself back into the hub. Nothing sits outside to freeze, go stale, or grow anything.

What it means

The machine stops being a chore you visit and becomes an appliance the house has — like a furnace with a thermostat. You pour juice in when you think of it, inside, and the roof takes care of itself. What it could mean for you: the Christmas snow, the Halloween fog, the summer mist — all of it runs from one indoor pour, and the ladder stays in the garage.

1 · The hub tank — indoors, on purpose

A 10–25 L reservoir where you live, not where the machine lives. Wide filler neck, a sight gauge or translucent wall so the level reads at a glance, and a lid that seals — dust and juice don't mix. Every refill for the life of the system happens here, at waist height, indoors.

03

The two rules that make it work

Everything else in this blueprint is shopping. These two are engineering — get them right and the system behaves; skip either and it fights you.

RULE ONE

Push, don't pull

An effect machine's own pump is a sipper, built to drink from a tank sitting right beside it. Ask it to suck juice 10–20 feet straight up and it starves — air locks, weak output, a pump chewing itself dry.

So the lifting is done by a diaphragm pump at the hub — the same pump family that runs misting systems, self-priming and rated around 125 psi, happy to shove fluid uphill all day. It pushes juice up the line to a small header tank beside the machine, kept topped up by a float switch. The machine then drinks from that header tank exactly the way it was designed to: from a full tank, at its elbow. Each pump does the job it was built for.

RULE TWO

Empty line when it's off

Fluid standing in an outdoor line is a problem waiting for a cold night: it can freeze, thicken, or just sit there going stale between seasons.

So the line is plumbed to drain back to the hub when the system shuts down — a drain valve at the low point by the tank and a vent at the top let gravity pull every drop home. Off means empty. Empty means nothing outside can freeze, burst, or spoil — and the next start primes in seconds because the pump, not the weather, decides when the line fills.

Parts list — all of it buyable today

No custom fabrication. Every line below is a stock part from the stage-effects, misting, or RV-plumbing shelf.

P1

Reservoir — the hub

Sealed HDPE tank with a wide filler neck and quick-connect outlet. Martin's JEM external reservoir is exactly this part at 25 L; a food-grade water container does the same job for less.

10–25 L · HDPE
P2

Diaphragm transfer pump

The lifter. Self-priming, pressure-switch type (RV / misting class), so it runs when the header tank calls and rests when it's full. MistKing-class misting pumps are rated 125 psi and tolerate running dry — an empty hub won't burn one out.

12/24 V DC · ~125 psi class
P3

In-line filter

A small mesh filter between tank and pump. Snow and fog fluids are clean, but one crumb in a 6 mm line is a clog at the worst height. Cheap insurance, cleaned at the hub.

mesh, serviceable
P4

Feed line

Flexible silicone or polyurethane tube, the same stock the pro external-reservoir kits ship with — theirs is 1.5 m; yours runs hub to eave, typically 5–10 m. UV-stable where it exits the wall.

8 mm OD / 6 mm ID
P5

Header tank + float switch

A 1–2 L tank mounted beside the emitter. The float switch tells the hub pump when to top it up. The machine's own feeder tube drops into this tank instead of its stock one — an unmodified machine, drinking normally.

1–2 L · float switch
P6

Drain-back valve + top vent

A small solenoid or manual valve at the hub end of the line, and a vent/check fitting at the header tank. Shutdown opens the path home; the line empties by gravity.

12/24 V solenoid
P7

The emitter

Any machine that drinks from an external or drop-in tank — which is most pro snow and fog machines already. Mounted on its hanging bracket at the eave or gable, aimed out over the yard, on a GFCI-protected circuit with its wireless remote or DMX doing the talking.

see research, §06
P8

Control

The machine's own wireless remote, timer, or DMX — plus one smart plug or switch at the hub that powers the pump and drain valve together. One switch runs the whole system; one switch empties it.

remote / timer / DMX

The gap, stated plainly Unfilled

Search the residential market and you find machines, tanks, and pumps — never the system. No maker sells a home kit that puts the emitter on the roof and the refill indoors, with a pump sized for the lift and a line that drains itself. The pro world solved each piece for stages and film sets, at stage prices, and stopped a metre and a half short of the house. This blueprint is the last stretch: longer line, a pump that pushes, a drain that empties it, and a tank where the people are.

One distinction the research forces: foam snow is not snowmaking. The Entour and Boreas make light foam flakes that fall, look like snow, and evaporate — they work at any temperature, indoors or out, and drink bottled fluid, which is what makes them hub-feedable. Real snowmaking (SNOWatHOME's SG6 package, from $2,118) freezes actual water with compressed air, needs it at or below about 29 °F, and is plumbed to a tap and a pressure washer — a different machine with a different blueprint. If what you want is the look of falling snow over the yard in December in Texas, foam is the one that shows up on demand.

07

One hub, a family of machines

The blueprint doesn't care what the juice makes. Anything that drinks fluid and throws its output over a wide area fits the same drawing — swap the emitter and the fluid, keep the hub, pump, and line.

  • Snow (foam) — the original case. Roofline mount, December, refills from the hallway closet.
  • Fog & low fog — a fogger at the top of the yard or over a porch, rolling fog across the whole scene for Halloween, fed from indoors instead of kneeling in the dark with a jug.
  • Haze — the quiet one: a hazer up in a garage or venue corner, sipping so slowly (tens of ml a minute) that a 25 L hub is effectively a season's supply.
  • Cooling mist — in summer the same line carries plain water to misting nozzles under the eave. The hub-and-push architecture is a misting system; the snow machine was the winter tenant.
  • Bubbles — a bubble machine over a patio or dance floor, drinking from indoors instead of being babysat with a dip tray.

That's the pattern underneath the idea, and it's the same one running through Tristan's other builds: the hub holds the supply; the edge does the show. Feed the edge from the hub and nobody ever has to go out to the edge to keep it alive.

08

Build sequence

  1. Pick the emitter and the high spot

    Choose the machine for the effect you want first (§06), then the mount: eave or gable bracket beats roof penetration — no holes in the roof, and the machine hangs over the yard, not over the shingles. Confirm the spot can take 30–35 lb plus wind, and that a GFCI circuit can reach it.

  2. Set the hub

    Tank and pump indoors, low, near a wall the line can climb. Fill it, and bench-test the pump and header tank on the floor before anything goes up — prime it, watch the float switch cycle the pump, drain it back. Every behaviour of the system, proven at ground level.

  3. Run the line

    Through the wall with a sealed pass-through, up to the header tank, secured so it can't chafe or sag into traps. Slope matters more than neatness: the drain-back only works if the line falls toward the hub with no low pockets holding fluid hostage.

  4. Hang the machine, drop in its feeder

    Machine on its bracket, header tank beside it, the machine's own feeder tube and filter sitting in the header tank. From the machine's point of view, nothing unusual has happened — it's drinking from a tank, as designed.

  5. Commission it

    Power the hub: pump fills the line, header tank tops up, float cuts the pump. Run the effect, confirm output and coverage, then shut down and watch the line drain home. Time the prime on the next start — it should be seconds. Label the hub switch. Done: refills are a jug, indoors, from now on.

09

Honest limits & safety

Read before building

  • Roof work is roof work. The mount goes up once — ladders, height, and 30+ lb of machine. If that's not your day, a handyman hangs the bracket in an hour; the hub side is all indoor, low-voltage, and forgiving.
  • Power stays protected. The emitter runs on mains power outdoors: GFCI protection, outdoor-rated cable and connectors, and no extension-cord improvisation at height.
  • Foam snow leaves a film. Evaporating flakes are a soap solution — decks, steps, and patios under the fall zone can get slick while it's running and leave residue after. Aim the fall at lawn and beds, not the front steps.
  • Use the fluid the maker approves. Machines are tuned and warrantied for their own fluid; the wrong juice clogs, corrodes, or voids the warranty. ADJ's snow fluid is water-based, non-toxic, and non-flammable — keep it that way.
  • Never let the emitter's own pump run dry. The header tank exists partly to prevent exactly this — the float switch and hub pump keep it fed. Machines like the Entour carry a low-fluid shutoff; don't treat it as a strategy.
  • This is a blueprint, not a product listing. Specs and prices quoted are the makers' published figures, checked November 20, 2026, and linked below. The hub-fed residential system itself remains unbuilt and unsold — that's the finding, not a footnote.
