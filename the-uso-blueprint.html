The USO

A craft designed water first — at home under the surface before anything else — built to the characteristics in the USO reports: fast under water, quiet, and able to cross between water and air without slowing down.

“An unknown is not a no.”— the stance, and the rule this whole shelf of blueprints is built under. An unknown is an open how on the shelf, not a refusal.

The honesty frame — read this first, at full size

“USO” — unidentified submerged object — names a class of reports, not a proven craft. What is documented is this: navies and sailors have reported objects under water moving at speeds and in ways that public submarines don't, and objects passing between water and air without slowing. Those reports are graded on this page as reports — real reports, honestly labeled, not proof of what was in the water.

This blueprint designs a craft to the reported characteristics and labels every gap an OPEN HOW. It never claims such a craft exists. A drawing is a design, not a sighting.

WHY?

The reports describe a craft that treats the ocean's surface as no barrier at all and the deep as home. If that is the reported shape of the thing, then an honest design exercise starts from the reports — graded as reports — and asks what a craft would have to be to do what they describe.

HOW?

Water first: real submarine engineering as the proven base — pressure hull, ballast, quiet propulsion — then the design's candidate answers on top: the absorption field asked to take the ocean's steady squeeze, and to smooth the crossing. Every candidate is labeled design, not fact.

ACT.

The blueprint is released. Read it, test the pieces small and shallow, build on it, improve it. What is not yet engineered is labeled OPEN HOW at full size — those are the doors, and they are open on purpose.

The Standing Laws of This Design

Two house laws govern every sheet in this family. This section states what they honestly mean for this craft — no more, and no less.

The Three Modes — set down November 21, 2026, under copyleft (CC BY-SA 4.0) — rule every spec that distributes, spaces, proportions, or flows. Golden = spread, never align: each new element into the biggest gap, at the golden angle 137.5077641°. Platinum = align exactly: equal, on the line, 1:1 — the right mode where alignment is the function. Diamond = the weave: align locally, spread globally. The full treatment lives on the Super Suit sheet (System 9) and on Sheet № 0 (§14). Here is this craft's own grading:

Golden — the absorption field

The field takes the ocean's squeeze across the whole covered hull — spread over the entire surface, never borne at one point. A crash's energy is spread the same way. Distribution is the field's whole job, so where this craft distributes, it runs Golden.

Platinum — ballast, the vertical drive

Buoyancy is exact equality: heavier than the water it displaces to sink, lighter to rise, exactly even to hover. Trim tanks fine-tune the balance to the same exactness. Hover is not approximate — it is 1:1, held. Where this craft balances, it runs Platinum.

Diamond — the craft as a whole

Each system is fitted exactly where it belongs — a round hull holding its shape, ballast balanced to the kilogram, the crossing system waiting on its answer — and one field spreads over all of it at once. Local exactness woven under a global spread: the whole design runs Diamond.

Nothing else on this sheet carries a mode, and that is printed on purpose. The report shelf is graded evidence, not an arrangement. The pressure hull's round shape is geometry, not an arrangement. The build order is a sequence, not a spacing. And the crossing carries no mode at all — its mechanism is unwritten, an open how, and a mode cannot be graded on a mechanism that does not exist yet. The modes' own law governs the modes too: use it when it works — don't force it.

Adapt and overcome — the second standing law, and a top law: learn your lessons and grow. This design is built to take in new knowledge and get better. The sheet already says it in pieces — 'build on it, improve it'; when a step fails, that failure becomes the next version's first item; and anyone who closes an open how improves the blueprint for everybody. The law names the whole of it: today's sheet is the floor, not the ceiling. When better knowledge arrives — an open how closed, a capacity measured, a crossing proven — the design is revised to fit it in: fitted when verified better, never forced, and never a from-scratch rebuild of what already works.

01

The Report Shelf — graded as reports

Everything this design is built to match lives on this shelf, and everything on this shelf carries the same label: REPORT — something people said they saw, heard on sonar, or caught on a sensor. A report is evidence that a report was made. It is not, by itself, proof of a craft.

Speed under water REPORT

Sailors and naval crews have reported sonar contacts and submerged objects moving far faster than public submarines travel, and changing depth and direction in ways public submarines don't. No public navy has produced one for inspection. Graded: reported, repeatedly, by trained observers — and unverified as a craft.

Between water and air REPORT

Objects have been reported entering the water from the air, and leaving the water for the air, without slowing — no splash-down pause, no laboring climb-out. This is the report the whole design turns on, and it is the least explained: everything human-made pays a heavy toll at that boundary.

Shag Harbour, 1967 REPORT — OFFICIALLY SEARCHED

Witnesses in Nova Scotia reported a lit object entering the water; Canadian authorities mounted a search and recovered nothing conclusive. It stands on the shelf as what it is: a documented report, officially investigated, never resolved.

The 2013 Puerto Rico video REPORT — VIDEO EXISTS

A US Customs and Border Protection aircraft camera recorded an object near Aguadilla that appears to cross into the water and back out without slowing. The video is real; what it shows is contested. Graded: sensor record of something, interpretation open.

The design rule follows from the grading: design to the reported characteristics, claim only the reports. If a report is wrong, the blueprint is a design exercise that still had to solve real problems — pressure, quiet speed, the crossing. If a report is right, the blueprint is a first sketch of the shape of an answer. Either way, nothing on this page needs the reports to be more than they are.

02

Water First — the design starts under the surface

This craft is designed as a creature of the water before it is anything else. The air is a place it visits. The deep is home — and home is the hardest environment on Earth to live in, because of one thing: pressure.

The pressure problem. Water is heavy, and it stacks. Every 10 metres down adds roughly another atmosphere of pressure — another full weight of Earth's whole sky, pressing on every square centimetre of hull, from every direction at once, forever. And the bigger the craft, the more hull surface that pressure gets to push on: pressure is the ocean's version of the square-cube problem, the same law that governs the Ship blueprint, wearing a different uniform. A hull comfortable at 50 metres faces roughly ten times the pressure at 500 — about fifty atmospheres in all. Crushing force grows with depth, and hull — or field — must answer it.

Read the second row slowly: at just 100 metres down, the sea leans on a 30 cm window with the weight of several cars — and it never lets up, never gets tired, and pushes from all sides at once. That steady, patient squeeze is what the hull — and the field, if it can — has to answer.

The proven base: real submarines. None of this is new territory for engineering. Submarines are the most pressure-honest machines humans build, and their answers are the base shelf of this design: DOCUMENTED ENGINEERING

  • The pressure hull — a thick cylinder or sphere of high-strength steel or titanium. Round shapes spread the squeeze evenly; a sphere is the strongest shape of all, which is why the deepest craft ever built, like the bathyscaphe Trieste that reached the bottom of the Mariana Trench in 1960, put their crews in a ball.
  • Ballast — tanks that flood with seawater to sink and blow empty with air to rise. Buoyancy is a submarine's vertical drive: it doesn't fly up or down, it balances — heavier than water to descend, lighter to ascend, exactly even to hover.
  • Quiet propulsion — the propeller is a submarine's loudest part. Spin it too fast and the water itself boils into bubbles on the blades — cavitation — which is loud, erosive, and a hard speed limit. Real submarines trade speed for silence on purpose.

The design's candidate: the field as pressure answer. This blueprint asks the absorption field — the same field the Suit and the Ship carry to take crash energy — to also take the ocean's squeeze: a cushion around the hull that bears the pressure so the hull doesn't bear all of it. That is a design candidate, printed as one. OPEN HOW And the honest catch is printed with it: steady pressure is a different ask than impact. A crash is a spike — enormous energy, gone in a moment. Depth is a siege — smaller force, applied forever, from every side. A field that swallows a spike may or may not hold a siege. That difference is exactly what the Open Hows shelf below is for.

Propulsion in water. The reported craft move fast under water and quiet — and those two words are the whole problem. Every known way of pushing water gets loud or tears itself apart at speed: propellers cavitate, jets churn. So this design's propulsion is specified by its requirements, not by a mechanism it doesn't honestly have: quiet, no cavitation, at reported speeds. OPEN HOW Whatever answers that — a field-driven push, a way of moving water without blades, something not yet thought of — is the single biggest door in the underwater half of this design.

WHAT IT IS

A water-first design: a proven submarine base (pressure hull, ballast as the vertical drive, quiet-running practice) with one candidate added — the absorption field, asked to share the pressure load.

WHAT IT MEANS

Depth is not a detail to be handled later; it is the environment itself. Every other system on this craft is designed inside the pressure answer, the way every system on a submarine is. Get pressure wrong and nothing else matters. Get it right — hull alone, or hull plus field — and the deep opens.

WHAT IT COULD MEAN FOR YOU

The provable work starts shallow: ballast control, hull strength, and field-under-pressure tests can all be run in tens of metres of water, uncrewed, cheaply. The Build Order below is written that way on purpose.

03

The Crossing — the hardest door in the design

Printed at full size, as promised: the surface crossing is the hardest problem on this page. Water is about 800 times denser than air. A craft built for one is a stranger in the other — and at the boundary, for one violent moment, it is in both at once.

This is where air-designs and water-designs both break. A submarine that breaches the surface arrives as a falling building: tonnes of water shedding off it, no lift, no control authority, gravity collecting its bill. A flying craft that hits water at speed meets something closer to concrete — the surface does not care how sleek the nose is; at speed, water does not get out of the way fast enough, and the impact forces arrive like a wall. Everything human-made pays at this door: seaplanes land on water slowly and gently, submarines surface slowly and carefully, and nothing crosses at speed. The reports say the reported craft cross without slowing. That sentence is the headline characteristic of this design — and its hardest door.

The candidate thinking — labeled design, not fact. This blueprint's candidate is the field again, asked to do a third job: smooth the crossing. If a field surrounds the craft, then what meets the surface is not hull but field — and a field, in this design's terms, could shape the meeting: parting the water ahead of the craft instead of slamming into it, carrying a shaped pocket of one medium into the other, spending the boundary's violence the way it spends a crash's. OPEN HOW That is design thinking, offered as design thinking. No part of it is claimed to work. It is printed here because a blueprint that hid its hardest door would be a sales pitch, and this page is not one.

WHAT IT IS

The transmedium problem: one craft, two fluids 800× apart in density, and a boundary that punishes speed. The reported craft reportedly ignore it. No known craft does.

WHAT IT MEANS

The crossing is where this design will be proven or broken — not the deep, not the speed. A craft that masters depth and speed but pays full price at the surface is a very good submarine. The crossing is what would make it the thing the reports describe.

WHAT IT COULD MEAN FOR YOU

The crossing can be tested small, slow, and uncrewed — models through a tank surface, instrumented, hundreds of times. The Build Order puts cheap crossings early, because the most expensive door should be tried at the cheapest scale first.

04

Systems

The craft, system by system. Each one is labeled with what it honestly is today: proven submarine engineering, part of this design, or an open how still owed.

Pressure hull PROVEN — SUBMARINE ENGINEERING

The inner answer to the squeeze: a round, thick hull of high-strength material, shaped to spread pressure evenly. This is solved engineering — submarines and deep submersibles do it today. It is the design's floor: whatever the field adds, the hull must be honest on its own at the depths it is rated for.

Absorption field OPEN HOW — STEADY PRESSURE

The same field the Suit's chip and the Ship's hull carry, asked here to share the ocean's steady squeeze and to smooth the surface crossing. Impact is a spike; pressure is a siege. Whether the field can hold a siege — and for how long, at what power cost — is an open how, printed as one. The field itself runs Golden — the squeeze is taken across the whole covered hull, never borne at one point. (Standing Laws, above)

Buoyancy & ballast PROVEN — THE VERTICAL DRIVE

Flood to sink, blow to rise, balance to hover. Ballast is how this craft moves vertically in its home element — no wings, no thrust needed, just controlled heaviness. Trim tanks fine-tune the balance; emergency blow is the way home when everything else fails. Ballast runs Platinum — hover is exact equality, weight matched to displacement 1:1. (Standing Laws, above)

Propulsion in water OPEN HOW — QUIET SPEED

Specified by requirement, not by mechanism: quiet, no cavitation, at the speeds the reports describe. Propellers can't do it — cavitation forbids it. Whatever can — a field-driven push or something not yet imagined — is the biggest open door in the underwater design.

Crossing system OPEN HOW — THE HARDEST DOOR

The field, asked to smooth the water–air boundary at speed: parting water ahead of the craft, carrying it through the interface without the slam. Candidate thinking only, labeled as such. Until it is proven, this craft crosses the way submarines do — slowly, carefully, and on purpose.

Sensing & navigation OPEN HOW — DARK AND FAST

Below a few hundred metres the ocean is black, and at reported speeds a craft outruns its own sonar picture — sound is slow, echoes are old news. Seeing, knowing where you are, and not hitting anything in the dark at speed is an open how in its own right, not a detail.

Power OPEN HOW

Whatever drives propulsion, feeds the field, and runs the boat. Submarines answer this with diesel, batteries, or reactors; this design's budget also has to feed a field under siege-pressure, so its power question is bigger than a submarine's — and is not guessed at here.

Life support & crew PROVEN — LATER STAGES ONLY

Air, water, and endurance for people under water are solved submarine arts. They enter this design only at the crewed stages of the Build Order — the craft proves itself empty first, the way the Ship blueprint proves itself uncrewed.

05

Build Order — uncrewed, shallow, and slow first

The simplest form of the build: prove each layer before depth or crew. Every step answers one question at the cheapest, safest scale that can honestly answer it.

  1. Prove the water basics in a tank.

    An uncrewed model: ballast up, ballast down, hover at neutral buoyancy, hold depth. Buoyancy is the vertical drive — it gets proven first, in water you can stand next to.

  2. Prove the hull shallow.

    Pressure-test the hull in a chamber and in shallow water, past its rated depth, uncrewed. The squeeze is patient and total; meet it where a failure floods a test article, not a crew compartment.

  3. Put the field under pressure.

    The candidate question, asked directly: field on, in a pressure chamber, held — not struck. Measure whether it shares the steady load, how much, and at what power cost. A spike test proves nothing here; the siege is the test.

  4. Prove quiet propulsion, slow.

    Run the propulsion at harbour speeds and listen. Cavitation has a sound and a signature; the requirement is quiet first, speed later. Speed gets added only as the quiet survives it.

  5. Cross the surface small, slow, and often.

    Uncrewed models through a tank surface, instrumented, hundreds of crossings — with the field smoothing if it can, without it as the control. The hardest door gets tried at the cheapest scale, first and most.

  6. Go deeper and faster in steps.

    Each step down re-asks the pressure question at the new depth; each step up in speed re-asks the cavitation and sensing questions. When a step fails, that failure is the next version's first item.

  7. Crew it last.

    People go aboard only when hull, field, propulsion, and the crossing have each been proven at that depth and speed, uncrewed. The ocean does not grade on effort — so the order is the safety system.

06

Open Hows — printed at full size

These are the questions this blueprint does not answer. They are not fine print and they are not physics walls — an unknown is not a no. They are engineering specifications waiting for their engineers. Anyone who closes one improves the blueprint for everybody, under the same license.

Propulsion at reported underwater speeds OPEN HOW

The reports describe speeds no public submarine reaches, travelled quietly. Every known propulsor breaks that pairing: propellers cavitate — the water boils off the blades — becoming loud, erosive, and self-limiting; jets churn. What pushes water fast and silently? A field-driven push is this design's candidate direction; the mechanism itself is unwritten, on purpose.

The transmedium crossing OPEN HOW

The hardest door in the design. Water is ~800× denser than air; at the boundary, at speed, both media punish the crosser. The candidate — the field smoothing the crossing, parting the water, carrying the craft through — is design thinking, not fact. Nothing crosses at speed today. How anything could is an open how at full size.

Pressure capacity of the field OPEN HOW

The field's proven job in this family of designs is impact — a spike of energy, over in a moment. Depth asks something else: a steady squeeze, from all sides, forever. How much steady pressure can the field bear, how does that capacity scale with the craft, and what does holding it cost in power? Until those are measured, the hull alone carries the depth rating.

Sensing and navigation in the dark at speed OPEN HOW

The deep is black and sound is slow: a fast craft's sonar picture is always a picture of where things were. Seeing ahead, fixing position without surfacing, and missing the seafloor, the whales, and other traffic at reported speeds needs a sensing answer this blueprint does not have — and will not fake.
