The Ruler's Test — Cyril Ramaphosa, President of South Africa

Subject: Cyril Ramaphosa — President of South Africa since 15 February 2018, when he succeeded Jacob Zuma after Zuma's resignation; previously Deputy President (2014–2018) and, before that, a founding leader of the National Union of Mineworkers, a negotiator of the post-apartheid constitution, and a businessman who served as a non-executive director of the mining company Lonmin. He is also president of the African National Congress (ANC), first elected to that post in December 2017 and re-elected in December 2022. Re-elected President of the Republic by the National Assembly on 14 June 2024 after the ANC lost its parliamentary majority for the first time since 1994. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders; no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clean-up machinery he built or rebuilt after the Zuma era has reached figures from his own movement. The ANC's "step-aside" rule was applied to the party's own secretary-general, Ace Magashule, who was suspended in May 2021 after being charged in the Free State asbestos-removal corruption case; that case is among the state-capture matters the Presidency lists as enrolled for trial through 2025–2026 (ANC disciplinary record, 2021; Presidency progress report, 28 July 2025). Jacob Zuma himself — former president and Ramaphosa's predecessor — was expelled from the ANC in 2024 after founding a rival party (ANC disciplinary committee, 2024).
  • The National Prosecuting Authority's integrated task force is implementing 218 criminal-investigation recommendations from the Zondo (State Capture) Commission; as of March 2025, 21% were finalised or enrolled for trial and just over half were under active investigation, four state-capture cases had concluded with guilty verdicts, and asset recoveries had reached nearly R11 billion, up from R2.9 billion in October 2022 (Presidency progress report, 28 July 2025; Corruption Watch summary, 26 June 2025).
  • When his own Police Minister was accused, Ramaphosa acted against him — the July 2025 Mchunu matter is set out under Test 2 — placing a sitting ANC minister on leave and subjecting the allegations to a judicial commission rather than closing ranks (presidential address, 13 July 2025).
  • His appointment of the National Director of Public Prosecutions in 2018 was made through an independent advisory panel that interviewed candidates in public; Shamila Batohi took office in February 2019 as the first NDPP appointed by that route (Presidency, 2018–2019).

Evidence against:

  • Phala Phala is the central one-law file, and the findings point in opposite directions — each body stated here in its own terms. On 9 February 2020, cash in US dollars — the figure documented in subsequent proceedings is about $580,000, said by Ramaphosa to be proceeds of a buffalo sale to a Sudanese businessman on 25 December 2019 — was stolen from a sofa at his private Phala Phala game farm in Limpopo. The theft became public only on 1 June 2022, when former intelligence chief Arthur Fraser laid a criminal complaint alleging concealment, money laundering and corruption — ALLEGATIONs which Ramaphosa has denied throughout, maintaining the money was a legitimate commercial payment and that he reported the theft to his protection unit. Fraser's further claim that $4 million was stolen is not supported by the documented figure of $580,000 (Fraser complaint, June 2022; court and panel records, 2022–2026).
  • The clearances: the Acting Public Protector, Kholeka Gcaleka, reported on 30 June 2023 that the allegation that Ramaphosa violated the Executive Ethics Code — by exposing himself to a conflict between his duties and his private farming interests, or by undertaking paid work — was not substantiated; she found he had declared his interests and received no remuneration from the farm, while finding that the head of the Presidential Protection Service and others had acted improperly in investigating the burglary off the books, and directing the police commissioner to act against them (Public Protector report, 30 June 2023). The South African Reserve Bank reported on 21 August 2023 that, within its exchange-control mandate, it could not conclude there had been any contravention, because the buffalo transaction was subject to unfulfilled conditions precedent and so was not a "perfected transaction" creating a legal entitlement to the currency; the Bank expressly confined its finding to exchange control and kept its full report private (SARB media release, 21 August 2023). SARS likewise cleared Ramaphosa and his companies on the tax questions within its remit (SARS statement, 2023). On 10 October 2024 the Limpopo Director of Public Prosecutions decided not to prosecute anyone arising from Fraser's complaint, the NPA stating there was no reasonable prospect of a successful prosecution on the evidence in the Hawks' docket (NPA statement, 10 October 2024).
  • The finding the other way: the independent panel appointed by the National Assembly under Section 89 of the Constitution — chaired by retired Chief Justice Sandile Ngcobo, with retired Judge Thokozile Masipa and Advocate Mahlape Sello SC — reported on 30 November 2022 that there was prima facie evidence Ramaphosa may have committed serious violations of sections 96(2)(a) and 96(2)(b) of the Constitution and of the Prevention and Combating of Corrupt Activities Act, including in failing to report the theft to the police and in allegedly seeking discretion from Namibian authorities over a suspect (Section 89 Independent Panel report, 30 November 2022). Ramaphosa disputed the panel's reasoning and initially sought judicial review of it; the review was not pursued to judgment.
  • The party's vote: on 13 December 2022 the National Assembly voted 214–148 against referring the panel's report to an impeachment committee — 212 of the votes against coming from Ramaphosa's own ANC, which then held the majority (National Assembly record, 13 December 2022). On 8 May 2026 the Constitutional Court held that vote unconstitutional and invalid and set it aside, found the Assembly rule that had permitted it (Rule 129I) unconstitutional, and ordered the report referred to an impeachment committee; the Speaker confirmed Parliament would comply, and the process was restarted (Constitutional Court, EFF and Another v Speaker of the National Assembly and Others, 8 May 2026). The net documented position: every institution with a narrow mandate cleared him within that mandate; the one body charged with the constitutional question found a prima facie case; his party's majority stopped the inquiry; and it took the apex court three and a half years to restart it. (Separately, the burglary itself was prosecuted: on 2 November 2026 a Limpopo court convicted two of the three accused of the break-in and theft, acquitting the third — a verdict on the thieves, not on the President's conduct, which remains before Parliament's process.)
  • The one-law problem is also structural and older than Phala Phala: the ANC's cadre-deployment practice — party committees influencing appointments to the public service, state companies and, in the Zondo Commission's findings, the judiciary's support structures — continued under his presidency. The Zondo Commission found the practice unlawful and unconstitutional in its application; the ANC under Ramaphosa defended deployment as legitimate, while a 2024 court order compelled disclosure of the party's deployment-committee records (Zondo Commission report, 2022; Gauteng High Court order, 2024). A system in which the ruling party vets the referees is a standing exception to one law, whoever personally benefits.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the National Prosecuting Authority, the media, the Reserve Bank, and his own party and coalition partners keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts rule against him, personally, at the highest level, and stay standing. The Constitutional Court's 8 May 2026 Phala Phala judgment revived an impeachment process against the sitting president; Ramaphosa's public response the following Monday was that he accepted and respected the ruling and reaffirmed his commitment to the Constitution and judicial independence, while maintaining he had done no wrong (Presidency statement and address, 11 May 2026). No judge is documented to have been removed, transferred or sanctioned for ruling against him in eight years — including in the line of cases setting aside appointments and decisions of his administration.
  • He inherited and used the Constitutional Court's August 2018 judgment invalidating his predecessor's appointment of the NDPP, and filled the post through the public panel process described in Test 1 — an appointment method designed to limit presidential discretion (Constitutional Court, 13 August 2018; Presidency, 2018).
  • The Reserve Bank has operated without documented interference: under Governor Lesetja Kganyago it raised interest rates aggressively through 2022–2023 against the government's growth preferences and public criticism from ANC allies, and no retaliation against the Bank or its governor is documented (SARB Monetary Policy Committee record, 2022–2023).
  • The media environment remains free, adversarial and unpunished: South African outlets broke and sustained the Phala Phala story, the state-capture prosecutions and the police-corruption allegations against his own minister, without documented state retaliation against the outlets concerned (press record, 2018–2026).
  • His coalition partners keep their own spin in fact, not only in form: since June 2024 the Government of National Unity has seated the Democratic Alliance — the ANC's historic opposition — in real portfolios including Home Affairs and Agriculture, and DA ministers have publicly differed with the President inside the government (GNU Statement of Intent, 14 June 2024; ministerial record, 2024–2026).

Evidence against:

  • The prosecution service's weakness is the standing grip-adjacent fact: not interference proven against Ramaphosa personally, but an institution rebuilt so slowly that independence has had limited effect. Eight years after the "new dawn," the Zondo implementation report records only 21% of 218 criminal-investigation recommendations finalised or enrolled by March 2025, and former Chief Justice Raymond Zondo himself publicly expressed disappointment in the Hawks' handling of state-capture matters, particularly Transnet (Presidency progress report, 28 July 2025; Zondo remarks reported July 2025). The Investigating Directorate was made permanent only in August 2024, as the Investigating Directorate Against Corruption (IDAC), six years into his presidency (National Prosecuting Authority Amendment Act, 2024).
  • The Public Protector who cleared him in Phala Phala was appointed to the permanent post by Ramaphosa in October 2023, on a National Assembly recommendation carried by the ANC majority, over opposition objections that the process was rushed — the appointment was lawful and is recorded here as an optics-and-structure fact, CONTESTED between the ANC (which points to the Assembly vote and her acting tenure) and opposition parties (which dispute her independence on this file) (National Assembly and Presidency, October 2023).
  • Policing is where the grip allegation currently sits, and it sits against his minister, not yet against him. On 6 July 2025 the KwaZulu-Natal Police Commissioner, Lt Gen Nhlanhla Mkhwanazi, alleged publicly that Police Minister Senzo Mchunu had interfered with sensitive investigations, colluded with businesspeople including a murder accused, and disbanded the Political Killings Task Team at a criminal syndicate's behest — ALLEGATIONs which Mchunu has denied. On 13 July 2025 Ramaphosa placed Mchunu on leave of absence with immediate effect, appointed an acting minister, and established a judicial commission of inquiry chaired by Acting Deputy Chief Justice Mbuyiseli Madlanga to investigate the alleged infiltration of law-enforcement and intelligence structures by criminal syndicates (presidential address, 13 July 2025; AP/NPR reporting, 14 July 2025). Ramaphosa later told Parliament's ad hoc committee he had learned of Mchunu's 31 December 2024 directive disbanding the task team only on 1 February 2025, and expressed dissatisfaction with it; as of this grading he has deferred any final decision on Mchunu until the Madlanga Commission's final report (parliamentary submission and Presidency statements, March 2026). The commission is either the institution working or the decision deferred — the record currently supports both readings, and the dossier records the fact pattern rather than choosing.
  • Inside his own party, autonomy runs one way: the ANC's deployment practice (Test 1) and the step-aside rule have been applied unevenly in the eyes of internal critics — enforced against some charged officials while others retained posts — a dispute documented in ANC internal and press reporting and CONTESTED between party leadership and its critics (ANC and press record, 2021–2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, markets, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • The voters' wobble is the strongest documented answer in this dossier. In the election of 29 May 2024 the ANC won about 40.2% of the national vote — down from 57.5% in 2019 — losing the parliamentary majority it had held since 1994 (IEC results, 2024). Ramaphosa did not dispute the result, allege fraud, or reach for the populist parties demanding his removal as their price; within sixteen days the ANC had signed a Statement of Intent forming a Government of National Unity with the Democratic Alliance, the Inkatha Freedom Party and other parties, and on 14 June 2024 the National Assembly re-elected him president under that shared-power arrangement (GNU Statement of Intent, 14 June 2024; AP reporting, 14 June 2024). The excluded MK Party's claim that the election was rigged was made without proof and was rejected by the courts; the ANC's acceptance of its own demotion was not contested by any observer.
  • The court's wobble, answered the same way: after the Constitutional Court's 8 May 2026 judgment revived his own impeachment process, Ramaphosa publicly accepted and respected the ruling and the process restarted under a Speaker from his own party (Presidency, 11 May 2026). He had earlier complied with the August 2018 Constitutional Court judgment on the NDPP appointment rather than defending his predecessor's arrangement (2018 record).
  • The policy wobble on energy was a genuine reversal, openly made. Confronted with the worst power cuts in the country's history, Ramaphosa's July 2022 Energy Action Plan opened generation to private investment, lifted licensing thresholds his own party's doctrine had defended, created a National Energy Crisis Committee, and in 2023 appointed a dedicated Minister of Electricity and passed R254 billion in Eskom debt relief conditioned on reform (Energy Action Plan, July 2022; Eskom Debt Relief Act, 2023). The Electricity Regulation Amendment Act, effective January 2025, formally opened the sector to competition beyond Eskom (Act record, 2025).
  • External pressure was answered without capitulation or rupture: after the United States froze aid in February 2025 over the Expropriation Act and South Africa's ICJ case, and imposed 30% tariffs in April 2025, Ramaphosa stated publicly "we will not be bullied," then sought and attended a meeting with President Trump at the White House on 21 May 2025, where, confronted on camera with claims of a "white genocide," he denied the claim calmly and on the documented crime record — most murder victims in South Africa are Black South Africans — while keeping the trade negotiation open (presidential statements, February–May 2025; White House meeting record, 21 May 2025; SAPS statistics as analysed by Africa Check).

Evidence against:

  • Adjustment, in his record, is often real but late, and preceded by denial or delay. The energy reversal of July 2022 came after years in which load-shedding had already become a daily fact and his government had defended Eskom's existing model; the crisis peaked after the plan, in 2023, before it receded (Eskom and CSIR records, 2022–2024).
  • On Phala Phala his first institutional instinct was defensive: he disputed the Section 89 panel's findings and sought judicial review of its report in December 2022, and his party voted the inquiry down (Test 1). His later acceptance of the Constitutional Court's correction is on the for-shelf; the three and a half years the correction took are on this one.
  • His response to the Mkhwanazi allegations took a week — the provincial commissioner spoke on 6 July 2025; the presidential address came on 13 July — a delay noted across the coverage, after which the response itself (leave, acting minister, judicial commission) was substantive (press record, July 2025; Africa Risk Consulting briefing, 29 July 2025).
  • A pattern critics name and the record partly supports: commissions and dialogues as the first answer — Zondo implementation stretching across years, the Madlanga Commission's final report awaited before a decision on Mchunu, and the National Dialogue launched in August 2025 whose initial R700 million budget was cut to R485 million only after civil-society objection (Amnesty International country report, 2025). Whether this is due process or deferral is CONTESTED between the Presidency (due process) and opposition and civil-society critics (deferral); the timelines themselves are documented fact.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — the unemployed, the poor, foreign nationals, his opponents' voters, communities carrying crime and the years of load-shedding — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The poor are counted in, materially and at scale, by the state's own instruments: more than 26 million South Africans receive social assistance monthly, including some 9 million unemployed people receiving the Special Social Relief of Distress (SRD) grant introduced in May 2020 and repeatedly extended under Ramaphosa rather than allowed to lapse (Presidency, SONA-in-Numbers 2025). Stats SA's 2025 Poverty Trends Report records the share below the lower-bound poverty line falling from 57.5% in 2006 to 37.9% in 2023 — 23.2 million people still below it, a decline the report links in part to the grant system cushioning the COVID shock (Stats SA Poverty Trends Report, 2025; General Household Survey, 2025: grants reached 39.5% of individuals and 50.6% of households).
  • His opponents' voters were counted when they spoke: the 2024 result was accepted and the DA's 21.8% of the electorate was brought inside the government rather than governed against (Test 3). On land, the Expropriation Act signed on 23 January 2025 — replacing the 1975 apartheid-era statute and permitting nil compensation only in defined circumstances after negotiation, subject to court oversight — was defended by his government as a general law for equitable access, not a race-targeted instrument; the DA's constitutional challenge to it is before the courts, which is where the dispute now sits (Expropriation Act, January 2025; court filings, February 2025).
  • When xenophobic vigilantes of Operation Dudula blocked migrants from clinics and threatened schools through 2025, the state's courts were used against them: in November 2025 the Gauteng High Court interdicted Operation Dudula from harassing and intimidating migrants and from denying them access to health and education services (Gauteng High Court interdict, November 2025, as reported by Amnesty International).

Evidence against:

  • The unemployed are the largest group the record does not reach. Official unemployment was 26.9% when Ramaphosa took office in February 2018; it was 31.4% in the fourth quarter of 2025 (7.8 million people), 32.7% in the first quarter of 2026 (8.14 million), and 33.7% by the third quarter of 2026 (8.58 million), with the combined rate including the potential labour force at 42.1–43.7% and youth (15–24) unemployment around 58–61% (Stats SA Quarterly Labour Force Survey, Q4 2025, Q1 2026, Q3 2026; Reuters, 12 May 2026). His Presidential Employment Stimulus created millions of short-term opportunities — over 2.5 million by government count — but the flagship Basic Education Employment Initiative's lapse in November 2025 itself accounts for much of the 2026 public-sector job losses in the Stats SA figures: relief that expires is counted, on this record, as relief, not as work (Stats SA QLFS commentary, November 2026).
  • Communities carried the power crisis for years before they were counted out of it. Load-shedding began in 2007–2008 but peaked on his watch: 2023 was the worst year on record, with about 332 days of outages including 74 days at Stage 6, and the Reserve Bank estimated the cuts cost the economy between R204 million and R899 million per day depending on stage (Eskom/CSIR records, 2023; SARB estimates, 2023). The end of the crisis is equally documented: from the Generation Recovery Plan and the Energy Action Plan, the energy availability factor rose from about 55% in 2023 to about 65–66% by late 2025, load-shedding shrank to 26 hours in the whole 2025–26 financial year, and in May 2026 Eskom marked 365 consecutive days without it (Eskom statements, 2025–2026; Operation Vulindlela progress report, Q2 2026/27). A seam inside the success: "load reduction" — targeted cuts in overloaded areas, often poorer ones — still affected about 1.69 million customers in January 2026, with Eskom targeting its end by 2027 (Eskom, January 2026).
  • Violent crime remains a count that does not close: SAPS recorded 24,692 murders in 2024/25 — roughly 63–68 people a day — and 5,794 murders in July–September 2025 alone, with 12,787 sexual offences reported in that quarter (SAPS Annual Report 2024/25; SAPS quarterly statistics via Africa Check). Successive quarterly declines in murder numbers in 2025 are documented and were acknowledged even by the acting police minister as leaving levels "unacceptably high" (Police Ministry statements, 2025).
  • Foreign nationals were failed in fact before the courts were used: Amnesty International records that Operation Dudula's clinic blockades in 2025 included the case of a one-year-old baby who died after being unable to receive treatment at a Johannesburg clinic in July 2025, and that SAPS protection for migrants and for threatened communities — including members of the Abahlali baseMjondolo shack-dwellers' movement killed between 2018 and 2022 — was ineffective (Amnesty International, South Africa report 2025). The November 2025 interdict is on the for-shelf; the year of blockades preceding it is on this one.

The Marikana record, stated exactly as documented. It predates his presidency and belongs to this test because it concerns whose lives were counted, and to Test 5 because he pledged himself to its healing.

  • Documented facts: Ramaphosa was a non-executive director and shareholder of Lonmin when, on 16 August 2012, police shot dead 34 striking mineworkers at Lonmin's Marikana mine — the most lethal use of force by South African security forces against civilians since the apartheid era. In the days before the killings he exchanged a series of emails with Lonmin executives and with the ministers of police and of mineral resources; in one he called for "concomitant action" against the strikers, whom he characterised as engaged in criminal conduct, and he lobbied, on his own later account to the Farlam Commission, for a larger police presence and for the events to be treated as criminal rather than as an industrial-relations matter (emails entered in evidence before the Farlam Commission, 2012–2014; Ramaphosa's testimony to the Commission).
  • The Commission's finding on him: the Farlam Commission of Inquiry (report, 2015) made no finding that Ramaphosa caused the massacre: it found his intervention did not cause the increase in police on site, and that he did not know the operation of 16 August would take place. The Commission criticised the police operation, the strikers' and unions' conduct, and mine management in other respects (Farlam Commission report, 2015).
  • The contested remainder: victims' families and critics continue to hold that his lobbying contributed to the climate of the operation — an interpretation the Commission's findings do not support as causation and which Ramaphosa has rejected, while accepting a role in "healing and atonement": in Parliament on 20 February 2018, days after becoming president, he said he was "determined to play whatever role I can play in the process of healing and atonement," guided by the families' wishes, "notwithstanding the findings of the Farlam Commission on my responsibility" (Ramaphosa, National Assembly, 20 February 2018). Compensation processes for the families proceeded through the courts and the state in the years that followed; the families' representatives have continued to dispute their pace and completeness (court and press record, 2018–2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Signature promises vs. the record since February 2018, with dates: the "new dawn," the clean-up after state capture, economic reform and jobs.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The institutional clean-up was substantially built, if slowly. He promised in 2018 to end state capture and rebuild the institutions it had hollowed out. The documented delivery: the Zondo Commission's work completed and answered with a 60-action response plan (October 2022); eight new laws on corruption, procurement, intelligence and corporate accountability enacted by 2025; the State Security Agency split back into separate foreign and domestic services (General Intelligence Laws Amendment Act, March 2025); IDAC made permanent (August 2024); nearly R11 billion recovered and a further R10.6 billion under preservation or restraint orders; Bain & Company banned from state business for ten years (2022–2032) (Presidency progress report, 28 July 2025; Corruption Watch, June 2025).
  • The financial-integrity promise closed: South Africa was removed from the FATF grey list in October 2025, and received its first sovereign credit-rating upgrade in over sixteen years, with public debt stabilising after two consecutive primary budget surpluses — outcomes his administration had explicitly targeted (FATF, October 2025; Presidency newsletter, 2026; ratings actions, 2025).
  • The energy promise, kept late: he pledged to fix Eskom and end load-shedding; after the worst year on record in 2023, the documented result by May 2026 was a full year without load-shedding and Eskom's first profits since 2017 (Test 4).
  • Two signature statutes were signed as promised: the National Health Insurance Act in May 2024 and the Expropriation Act in January 2025 — both central ANC commitments he undertook to enact; their implementation and constitutionality remain contested in the courts and within his own unity government, which is recorded here as the state of the argument, not as a breach of the signing promise itself (Presidency, May 2024 and January 2025).
  • The investment drive met its own headline measure: his 2018 pledge to raise $100 billion in new investment over five years was pursued through annual investment conferences that his government reports secured R1.5 trillion in pledges over five years, with R634 billion already invested (Presidency statements, 2025–2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Jobs — the central economic promise — was not kept. He repeatedly pledged mass job creation and growth above 3%; GDP growth averaged below 1% a year across his tenure (0.6% in 2024), and unemployment rose from 26.9% on his arrival to 31.4% by late 2025 and 33.7% by late 2026 (Stats SA QLFS series, 2018–2026; Stats SA GDP releases). The National Development Plan's targets were missed by wide margins, a fact his own SONA fact-sheets no longer dispute (Africa Check SONA factsheet, February 2026).
  • Accountability for state capture remains mostly prospective. Of the Zondo Commission's 218 criminal-investigation recommendations, 21% were finalised or enrolled by March 2025 and four cases had produced guilty verdicts — against a commission that named scores of senior figures; several flagship trials (asbestos, Bosasa, Transnet, SA Express) were still scheduled rather than concluded in 2025–2026, and the Commission's own chair publicly criticised the investigative pace (Presidency progress report, 28 July 2025; Zondo remarks, July 2025). The promise was prosecutions; the record, eight years on, is process.
  • The "new dawn" against corruption sits beside Phala Phala: whatever the final parliamentary outcome, the president who promised a clean break was found by a Section 89 panel to have a prima facie case to answer over undeclared foreign currency in his sofa and an unreported theft, and his party closed the inquiry until the Constitutional Court reopened it (Tests 1–3). The gap between the 2018 promise and the 2022–2026 record is documented in the findings of the bodies themselves.
  • Marikana's atonement pledge is incomplete on the families' account: his February 2018 commitment to healing and atonement "guided by the needs and wishes of the families" has been followed by compensation processes the families' representatives continue to contest as slow and partial (Test 4; legal representatives' statements, 2018–2026).
  • Cadre deployment was promised away and kept: his renewal agenda pledged a professional, merit-based public service — a National Framework on Professionalisation was approved in October 2022 — while the ANC under his leadership continued and defended party deployment practices the Zondo Commission found unlawful in application (Cabinet framework, October 2022; Zondo report, 2022; ANC statements, 2022–2026). Both halves are on the record; they do not reconcile.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: HOLDS · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Cyril Ramaphosa's file is the inverse of most in this series: the grip is not the story. In eight years no judge, central banker, editor or prosecutor is documented to have been punished for ruling, deciding or publishing against him; the Constitutional Court could order his own impeachment process revived in May 2026 and be obeyed within the week; and when the voters withdrew his majority in 2024 he answered by sharing power with his opponents within sixteen days — the single clearest wobble answer any human subject of this Test has yet given. What runs the other way is delivery and symmetry. The law reached his movement's officials through process that took years and is still mostly prospective, while the one file with his own name on it was closed by his party's majority and had to be reopened by the apex court; the clean-up he promised produced institutions, recoveries and eight new laws, but four guilty verdicts against a mountain of findings. And the flock he counts in with the world's more generous grant systems — 26 million people a month — is the same flock among whom unemployment rose from 26.9% to over 33% on his watch, who carried the worst power cuts in the country's history before they ended, and among whom some 63 people a day are still murdered. He holds the seat more loosely than almost anyone graded here. The record asks whether holding loosely, without delivering, is its own kind of failure — and grades that question MIXED, because the evidence does.
