The old story of riddles — tap to open

Riddles are one of the oldest games in the world — a folk form, passed mouth to mouth for thousands of years, long before anyone wrote them down. Wherever people gathered, somebody would pose a puzzle in words and everybody else would try to crack it.

The old stories are full of riddle contests. In the Hebrew Bible, Samson poses a riddle at his wedding feast — “Out of the eater came something to eat” — and wagers garments on whether his guests can solve it in seven days. In Greek story, the Sphinx waits outside Thebes and asks every traveller the riddle of the creature that walks on four legs in the morning, two at noon, and three in the evening; Oedipus answers “a human being” and the city is freed. You will meet that same riddle in this deck, retold in plain words. Old English poets filled a whole book — the Exeter Book — with riddles about candles, rivers, keys and storms, and nobody signed their name to any of them.

Where these riddles come from: every riddle in this deck is either a traditional folk riddle — the kind passed down for centuries, like the echo, the shadow and the candle — retold here in our own plain words, or a new riddle written for this page in the same traditional style. No riddle is copied from a modern book, website or game.

How answering works, in plain words: the game tidies up what you type — capital letters, spaces and punctuation do not matter — and it accepts close answers where that is fair. Type “an echo”, “Echo!” or “echo” and all three count. Some riddles accept a near-synonym too, like “footprints” for “footsteps”.
