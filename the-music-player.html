      How streaming radio works — the full why

      Short answers, in plain words. Tap a question to open it.

      What is a stream, exactly? — Tap to open

      A music file sits still on a device until you play it. A stream is the opposite: the station's computer chops its broadcast into tiny pieces of sound and sends them to you one after another, a few seconds ahead of what you are hearing. Your player plays each piece as it arrives and throws it away. Nothing is saved, nothing piles up — it is a river, not a lake. That is also why this page has no download or record button for radio: a stream was never a file, and turning it into one would be copying music that is not ours to copy.

      What does bitrate mean — for sound, and for your data? — Tap to open

      Bitrate is how much sound-data travels per second, written as kbps (thousands of bits per second). More bits describe the sound more fully, so higher usually sounds better — 128 kbps is honest FM-radio quality, 192 kbps is a step finer, and past about 256 kbps most ears on most speakers cannot hear the difference. The trade is data: a 128 kbps stream uses about 58 megabytes per hour, and 192 kbps about 86 megabytes per hour. On home Wi-Fi that is nothing. On a capped phone plan it adds up — roughly a gigabyte every 12 to 17 hours of listening. Every station on the shelf prints its bitrate, so the choice is yours with the numbers in front of you.

      Why does a stream pause, stutter, or “buffer”? — Tap to open

      Your player keeps a small cushion — a buffer — of a few seconds of sound it has received but not yet played. If your connection slows down (a weak signal, a busy network, a train tunnel), the player spends the cushion while it waits for more. If the cushion runs dry before more sound arrives, the music stops until it refills. A stall is almost never the station's fault or your device's fault — it is the road between them narrowing for a moment. Moving closer to the router, or dropping from a 192 kbps station to a 128 kbps one, genuinely helps: fewer bits per second fit through a narrower road.

      Why do these stations let a page like this play them? — Tap to open

      Because they say so, in public. Stations like SomaFM, Radio Paradise, Radio France, and public broadcasters publish their stream links openly so that any player — a phone app, a smart speaker, a web page — can tune in. More listeners is the point of radio. The licences those stations hold cover their broadcast reaching you through a player; the player adds nothing and takes nothing. What this page will never do: record a stream, save it, re-host it, or strip out a station's own announcements. We link to the river; we do not bottle it.

      What happens when a station stops answering? — Tap to open

      Stations move servers, change links, and occasionally go off the air — sometimes for an hour, sometimes for good. When that happens, the player above tells you plainly that the station could not be reached, rather than spinning forever or quietly playing nothing. Try again later, or pick another station; the shelf was built so no single station is the only door to its kind of music.
