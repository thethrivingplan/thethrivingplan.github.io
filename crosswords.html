The bargain of a crossword

Every answer is crossed by other answers, so every letter you earn in one word is a free letter in another. That is the whole engine of solving: work the crossings, and the grid fills itself. Every clue below is one of a handful of kinds — learn to spot the kind, and you know how to attack it.

The clue kinds used on this page

  • Straight definition. The clue just describes the answer: “The organ that pumps blood around the body” (HEART). Attack it by thinking of synonyms and near-matches, then testing them against the crossing letters.
  • Fill-in-the-blank. A phrase with a gap: “'_____ of the matter'” (HEART). Say the phrase aloud — your ear usually knows the missing word before your eyes do. These are the friendliest clues in any grid and a good place to start.
  • Abbreviation. The answer is a shortened form, and the clue says so with “for short”: “Northeast, for short” (NE), “Ukulele, for short” (UKE), “A short life story, for short” (BIO). Compass points, shortened instruments and life stories are the regulars.
  • Anagram (wordplay). A definition plus letters to scramble. Words like broken, wild, mixed, stirred, scrambled or gone wrong signal the scrambling: “Warning wail, broken, becomes sticky tree sap” — SIREN, broken, becomes RESIN. Attack it by writing the letters in a circle and turning them until a word jumps out.
  • Container. One piece goes inside another. Signals: in, inside, holding, around, swallowing. “L put inside GOD makes this precious yellow metal” — L inside GOD builds GOLD.
  • Charade. Parts added in order, like a sum: “A tear after T” — T + REND (a tear) = TREND; “Ail after P” — P + AIL = PAIL. Signals: after, before, on, with, plus.
  • Sounds-like (homophone). The wordplay is in the ear: “Stitch cloth together — it sounds like 'so'” (SEW). Signals: sounds like, we hear, reportedly, spoken.
  • Hidden word. The answer hides inside a longer word in the clue: “Frozen water, found inside dice” — d-ICE. Signals: found in, inside, part of, some of. Attack it by reading the clue's longer words letter by letter.
  • Double definition. Two definitions of the same word, side by side: “Highest card in the pack — or a serve nobody touches” (ACE). If a clue seems to describe two unrelated things, suspect this kind.

Conventions worth knowing

  • “For short” always means the answer is an abbreviation or shortening.
  • Anagram signals are words of damage or disorder: broken, wild, mixed, stirred, scrambled, drunk, mad, wrong.
  • Container signals are words of holding: in, inside, holding, around, gripping, swallowing.
  • A question mark at the end of a clue means the setter is joking — read the clue sideways, not straight.
  • The definition in a wordplay clue sits at the very start or the very end of the clue — the middle is machinery.

Solving strategy, with the trade-offs stated

  • Crossings first. Before sweating over a hard clue, look at the words crossing it: one solved crossing can hand you two or three certain letters. Trade-off: it takes you away from the clue you wanted — but a certain letter beats a hopeful guess every time.
  • Start with fill-in-the-blanks and short answers. They fall fastest and seed the grid with letters. Trade-off: the easy corner is not always where the hard words are — sooner or later you must face the long ones.
  • Collect the regulars. Short answers repeat across all crosswords: ANI, OLIO, EKE, NE, UKE and their kin. Learning them is pure profit. Trade-off: a “usual suspect” that doesn't fit the crossings is still wrong — the grid always outranks memory.
  • Guess in pencil, not in pen. A likely letter, lightly held, lets crossings confirm or kill it. Trade-off: a wrong guess you forget you made will poison every word through it — which is what the Check tools are for.
  • Read the clue's grammar. The answer matches the clue's tense and number: a clue ending in “-ed” wants an answer ending in “-ed”; a plural clue wants a plural answer. Free letters, just for noticing.
