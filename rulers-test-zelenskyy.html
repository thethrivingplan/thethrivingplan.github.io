The Ruler's Test — Volodymyr Zelenskyy, President of Ukraine

Subject: Volodymyr Zelenskyy — President of Ukraine since 20 May 2019; elected in the April 2019 runoff with 73.2% of the vote, the largest landslide in Ukraine's presidential history, on promises to end the Donbas war and break the hold of corruption and the oligarchs. A former comedian and co-owner of the Kvartal 95 production company, and a native Russian speaker from Kryvyi Rih. Since 24 February 2022 he has governed under martial law through Russia's full-scale invasion; his five-year term formally ran out in May 2024 and continues under Article 108 of the Constitution until a successor is sworn in. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. Context, stated as context: Ukraine has fought a full-scale war of national survival since February 2022. Roughly a fifth of its territory is occupied, millions of its people are displaced, and its cities are struck from the air on a near-nightly basis. Emergency powers under invasion are the condition in which every act below occurred. The tests do not grade the war; they grade what was done with the powers the war conferred — and, where the record shows it, what was handed back.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The law reached his own former patron first. Ihor Kolomoisky, the oligarch whose television channel carried Zelenskyy's comedy career and whose support shadowed his 2019 campaign, was opposed by the May 2020 banking law — passed over Kolomoisky's interests and known in Ukraine as the "anti-Kolomoisky law" — then arrested by the Security Service of Ukraine (SBU) in September 2023 on fraud and money-laundering suspicions, held in custody, and sanctioned by the National Security and Defense Council (NSDC) in February 2025 (SBU statements; court record; Decree No. 81/2025). The suspicions are ALLEGATIONs awaiting trial; the state's willingness to move against the president's former backer is documented fact.
  • The largest corruption investigation of his presidency reached the center of his own circle and was not stopped. NABU and SAPO's "Operation Midas," announced 10 November 2025 after a 15-month investigation and more than 70 searches, alleges a network that extracted 10–15% kickbacks from contractors of the state nuclear operator Energoatom, laundering roughly $100 million. Its alleged organizer is Timur Mindich — Zelenskyy's friend and co-owner of Kvartal 95 — who fled Ukraine hours before the searches. Zelenskyy's documented response: he publicly backed the investigation, signed NSDC sanctions freezing Mindich's assets on 13 November 2025, called for and obtained the resignations of Justice Minister Herman Halushchenko and Energy Minister Svitlana Hrynchuk (dismissed by parliament 19 November), and told Bloomberg, "The president of a country at war cannot have any friends" (NABU/SAPO announcements, November 2025; AP and RFE/RL reporting, 12–19 November 2025). The underlying charges are ALLEGATIONs; Halushchenko denies wrongdoing and says he will defend himself in court; Hrynchuk is not accused of taking part in the scheme.
  • When the scandal reached his own chief of staff, the outcome was the same direction. On 28 November 2025 NABU and SAPO searched the home of Andriy Yermak, head of the President's Office and the most powerful official in Ukraine after Zelenskyy; Yermak resigned the same day, Zelenskyy accepted the resignation and announced a "reboot" of the Office. Yermak was not charged in the Midas case. In May 2026 anti-corruption agencies named him a suspect in a separate case alleging laundering of UAH 460 million through a housing development — an ALLEGATION, stated here at that strength (International IDEA Democracy Tracker, May 2026, citing the agencies' announcement).
  • The system also took down his prosecutor general over a scandal that favored insiders. In October 2024, reporting exposed dozens of prosecutors in Khmelnytskyi region holding disability status obtained through the medical-social commissions (MSEC) — status carrying pensions and exemption from service. Zelenskyy convened the NSDC, publicly demanded that "the Prosecutor General must take political responsibility," ordered the MSEC system abolished and disability decisions audited; Prosecutor General Andriy Kostin resigned on 22 October 2024 and was dismissed on 31 October; the SBU reported 64 commission officials under suspicion and 4,106 disability certificates cancelled (presidential address, 22 October 2024; Reuters, October 2024).
  • A sitting deputy prime minister was charged while in office — the first such case in Ukraine's history — and the courts, not the president's office, decided his immediate fate. NABU named Deputy Prime Minister Oleksiy Chernyshov a suspect on 23 June 2025 in an alleged land scheme from his earlier ministerial tenure said to have cost the state about UAH 1 billion; the High Anti-Corruption Court set bail of UAH 120 million and declined the prosecutors' motion to remove him (Kyiv Independent and court reporting, June–July 2025). Chernyshov denies the allegations. He left the government in the July 2025 reshuffle, and in November 2025 was detained in the Midas case, the court ordering him held with bail of UAH 51 million (OCCRP, November 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The NSDC sanctions regime is punishment without a court, and it has been applied to the leader of the opposition. On 12 February 2025 the NSDC imposed indefinite sanctions — asset freezes, loss of state awards, bans on trade, privatization and land purchase — on five Ukrainian citizens, enacted by Zelenskyy's Decree No. 81/2025: Kolomoisky, his former PrivatBank partner Hennadiy Boholyubov, businessman Kostyantyn Zhevago, the pro-Russian politician Viktor Medvedchuk, and Petro Poroshenko, the fifth president of Ukraine and leader of the European Solidarity opposition. The SBU stated that most of the five were already subjects of criminal investigations — Poroshenko had been notified of suspicion of treason in 2021, a case that has never come to trial — but sanctions are imposed by a political body on security-service proposals, with no judicial finding, no term, and no court that imposed them (SBU statement, 13 February 2025; decree text). Poroshenko calls the sanctions "politically motivated and unconstitutional" and holds Zelenskyy personally responsible; in February 2025 a joint statement of Ukrainian human-rights organizations — the Kharkiv Human Rights Protection Group, the Ukrainian Helsinki Human Rights Union, the Center for Civil Liberties among them — condemned sanctions against opposition politicians and businessmen as unconstitutional extrajudicial coercion. The government's position is that the targets threaten national security. The instrument is the documented fact; its use against the sitting opposition leader, indefinitely and without trial, is the one-law problem in its purest form in this file.
  • Protection of an insider is credibly reported in the Chernyshov case, and the report is contested rather than refuted. Ukrainska Pravda's investigation, sourced to law-enforcement and presidential-circle figures, reported that when Chernyshov remained abroad as the charges were unveiled in June 2025, his return was arranged with security-service involvement and assurances he would not be detained, and that a letter from the prime minister was submitted to the court arguing for keeping him in post (Ukrainska Pravda, June–July 2025). Chernyshov said he returned of his own accord at the end of a business trip and denied wrongdoing; the President's Office did not confirm the reported arrangement. Recorded here as a sourced ALLEGATION of favoritism, unresolved.
  • Prosecutors in the Midas case told the court of a recording in which Zelenskyy is heard speaking with Halushchenko while Halushchenko was on a group call with Mindich, and of a text exchange with Mindich shortly before; the contents are not public, Zelenskyy is not under investigation, and he said on 12 November 2025 that he had not spoken to Mindich since the investigation was announced (RFE/RL, 13 November 2025). The documented proximity is a fact of the record; what it means is CONTESTED.
  • The July 2025 attempt to subordinate NABU and SAPO (Tests 2 and 3) is also one-law evidence: a law signed in a day, whose practical effect would have placed the investigations then closing on his own circle — Midas had been running for a year, and the Chernyshov charges were a month old — under a prosecutor general he appoints. That it was motivated by those investigations is an ALLEGATION made by opposition figures and anti-corruption activists, and denied by implication by Zelenskyy, who said the law targeted Russian influence in the agencies. The timing is documented; the motive is contested.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the anti-corruption bodies, the media, local government, and the military keep their autonomy under martial law — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption institutions demonstrably bite the hand that governs. Within four months of their independence being restored (below), NABU and SAPO executed the Midas operation against the president's business partner, two sitting ministers, a former deputy prime minister, and — by search — his chief of staff. Whatever else is true of this file, by November 2025 the institutions were functioning against the innermost circle of power, in wartime, and the president did not stop them.
  • The courts rule against the government's preferences in public and are obeyed. The High Anti-Corruption Court refused prosecutors' motion to remove Chernyshov in July 2025 while setting his bail; the same court system processed the Midas detentions through open hearings. No judge is documented to have been removed for a ruling against the government during the full-scale war.
  • The opposition exists, organizes, and speaks. Opposition factions sit in the Verkhovna Rada, blocked its rostrum in protest at the Poroshenko sanctions in February 2025, and criticize the president daily in parliament and in a plural online and print press. Kyiv's mayor, Vitali Klitschko — Zelenskyy's most prominent elected critic — remains in office after years of open feud (below); he has been investigated by no agency and removed by no decree.
  • When the government overreached against an independent selection process, the process won. In June 2025 an independent commission with international members chose NABU detective Oleksandr Tsyvinskyy to head the Bureau of Economic Security; on 7 July the Cabinet refused to appoint him, citing an SBU letter about his father's Russian citizenship — a fact the commission had already reviewed. Legal experts and the commission itself stated the Cabinet had no lawful power to reject the winner. On 6 August 2025, after the SBU confirmed no grounds existed and Tsyvinskyy passed a polygraph, the government appointed him (OCCRP, July–August 2025; Interfax-Ukraine, 6 August 2025).
  • The military change of command, however contested, ran through lawful authority and ended in an embassy, not a cell. Zelenskyy dismissed Commander-in-Chief Valeriy Zaluzhny on 8 February 2024 — a presidential power — after documented disagreements over the failed 2023 counteroffensive, mobilization, and strategy, and amid reporting that the general's trust ratings exceeding the president's had become a political problem (Chatham House, February 2024; The Economist, February 2024). Zaluzhny was appointed ambassador to the United Kingdom. That the dismissal was within the president's authority is not disputed; whether its motives were military or political is CONTESTED between the two camps.

Evidence against:

  • On 22 July 2025 Zelenskyy signed Law No. 12414 the same evening parliament passed it, subordinating NABU and SAPO to the Prosecutor General — giving an official he appoints the power to issue binding instructions to NABU, override SAPO, and reassign or close active corruption cases. The day before, the SBU, the State Bureau of Investigation and the Prosecutor General's Office had conducted some 70 searches of NABU and SAPO staff, claiming to have uncovered Russian agent networks; NABU detectives were arrested, including, per subsequent reporting, detectives leading Operation Midas (Harvard Ukrainian Research Institute expert responses, 2025; ICDS analysis, December 2025). The independence was restored nine days later (Test 3) — but the signature, the speed, and the accompanying raids are the documented attempt, and the reversal law still left the agencies subject to SBU background checks and polygraph regimes.
  • In the same weeks, the state's instruments were turned on the anti-corruption movement's public faces. Vitaliy Shabunin, head of the Anti-Corruption Action Center and the most prominent anti-corruption activist in Ukraine, was charged by the State Bureau of Investigation in July 2025 with draft evasion and fraud — charges he denies and which Ukrainian and international anti-corruption organizations publicly described as selective prosecution of a critic (Kyiv Independent reporting, July 2025; the charges are ALLEGATIONs). Coming between the SBU searches of NABU and the signing of Law 12414, the sequence is documented; coordination is alleged by the activist community and not admitted by the authorities.
  • Television news — still the main information source for older Ukrainians — has been consolidated since the invasion's first days into the state-coordinated "United News" telethon, and the three channels associated with the opposition have been off the main digital network for over four years with no one accepting responsibility. Channel 5, Priamyi and Espreso were dropped from the DVB-T2 network on 4 April 2022 without a regulator's decision; Reporters Without Borders records that the media regulator, the network operator and the state infrastructure company each blame the others, that lawsuits have stalled, and that a year on it remained impossible to say who ordered the disconnection (RSF statement, April 2023). The channels broadcast by satellite, cable and YouTube. Polling cited by the Institute of Mass Information found over 60% of Ukrainian journalists consider the telethon a form of censorship, and its audience share fell from the start of the war to roughly a tenth of viewers by late 2023 (United News record; NYT reporting, January 2024). Successive U.S. State Department human-rights reports and Ukrainian media organizations have criticized the marathon's one-sidedness. The government defends it as wartime information policy. Its continuation, and the opposition channels' exclusion, are policy choices renewed every day of the war.
  • Power within the executive was centralized in an unelected office to a degree unusual even for wartime Ukraine. The ICDS analysis of Yermak's tenure records that the head of the President's Office, holding no electoral mandate, "effectively engineered a power vertical by staffing key offices across government and exerting informal influence over the legislative and judicial branches" (ICDS, December 2025). Opposition MP Yaroslav Zheleznyak has cited NABU recordings in which a figure he identifies as Yermak, under the code name "Ali Baba," tasks officials with undermining NABU detectives — an ALLEGATION attributed to an opposition deputy's reading of recordings not publicly released in full.
  • Local government has been pressed where it is oppositional. Kyiv has been governed since 2022 in parallel by the elected mayor and a presidentially appointed city military administration; Zelenskyy publicly blamed Klitschko for the capital's shelter failures in 2023 and, in November 2026, for Kyiv's power and water outages during Russia's energy campaign, saying decisions at city level had not been implemented, and disclosed he had approached former officials to head the Kyiv military administration. Klitschko answered that his requests for a meeting with the president had gone unanswered and that the center was failing the capital (Reuters, 6 November 2026). Klitschko remains mayor — the pressure is rhetorical and institutional, not removal — and he has used his own platform freely against the president throughout.
  • The pre-war record contains the template. In October 2020, after the Constitutional Court struck down parts of the anti-corruption framework, Zelenskyy asked parliament to dismiss the entire court and suspended its chairman, Oleksandr Tupytskyi, by decree — steps the Venice Commission criticized as lacking constitutional basis even while it also criticized the court's ruling. The crisis ended without the court's removal, but the episode established decree-first pressure on a checking institution before the invasion made such pressure routine.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, allies, protesters, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The July 2025 reversal is the fastest documented correction by any ruler in this series. Law 12414 was signed on the evening of 22 July; the first protests since the full-scale invasion began that night — the "Cardboard Revolution," spreading to at least 27 locations within two days, joined by EU warnings that accession and funding were at stake. On 24 July Zelenskyy submitted his own bill restoring independence; on 31 July parliament passed it 331–0 and he signed it within hours, saying: "It is very important that the state listens to public opinion… People asked for changes. We reacted." He acknowledged he had not held public discussion before the first signature, citing his focus on the war (RFE/RL, 31 July 2025; ZMINA, 31 July 2025; Harvard HURI, 2025). The correction was total, public, and attributed by the president himself to the protesters.
  • The Bureau of Economic Security appointment (Test 2) shows the same pattern at lower volume: refusal on 7 July, sustained legal and public pushback, appointment of the commission's winner on 6 August.
  • The October 2024 disability scandal drew a structural answer within days rather than a denial: NSDC convened, the prosecutor general gone within nine days, the MSEC system ordered abolished by year's end, thousands of certificates cancelled and officials' disability pensions ordered audited (presidential address, 22 October 2024; Reuters).
  • With his most important ally, he absorbed a public humiliation and renegotiated rather than rupture. The 28 February 2025 Oval Office meeting with President Trump and Vice President Vance collapsed on camera and the minerals agreement went unsigned; the United States paused military aid. Zelenskyy adjusted his diplomacy, expressed regret for the meeting's course, and on 30 April 2025 signed a Reconstruction Investment Fund agreement on terms materially better than the opening drafts: equal 50/50 management, Ukrainian ownership of resources retained, no repayment obligation for past aid (the opening U.S. draft had contemplated a $500 billion claim), and future U.S. military aid counted as fund contributions. Parliament ratified it on 8 May 2025 (CSIS analysis, May 2025; agreement text).
  • On elections, under direct American pressure in December 2025 — President Trump saying publicly "it's time" — Zelenskyy moved from "after the war" to a conditional yes: on 9 December 2025 he stated he was "ready for elections" within 60–90 days if the United States and European partners helped guarantee security, and that he would ask parliament to draft the necessary legislation (RFE/RL, 10 December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The July 2025 wobble existed because he created it: a law restructuring the anti-corruption architecture was drafted, passed and signed within a single day, without consultation with the agencies, civil society, or Ukraine's partners — the denial of the wobble's legitimacy came first ("cleansing Russian influence"), and the adjustment only after the largest protests of the war and explicit EU consequences.
  • On the telethon and the three excluded opposition channels, four and a half years of documented criticism — from RSF, the U.S. State Department, Ukrainian journalist organizations and his own parliament's media committee chair, who in 2026 publicly asked why Channel 5, Priamyi and Espreso are still off the digital network — has produced no adjustment at all. The exclusion's authorship has never even been officially acknowledged, which is a refusal to answer in its own right.
  • On the Poroshenko sanctions, protest by the opposition faction, condemnation by Ukraine's leading human-rights organizations, and the blocking of parliament's rostrum produced no review, no court referral, and no time limit. The measure stands as imposed.
  • On Yermak, the adjustment lagged the evidence by the standard of his own July precedent. Through November 2025, as Midas broke, MPs demanded the chief of staff's dismissal and Zelenskyy refused; only after NABU and SAPO searched Yermak's home on 28 November did the resignation come — accepted with public thanks for Yermak's negotiating work, not with any acknowledgment that the concentration of power in his office had itself been the problem (OCCRP, November 2025; presidential statement, 28 November 2025).
  • The Klitschko feud shows the doubling-down pattern in current form: as Kyiv's outages worsened in November 2026, the response to the mayor's criticism was escalation — public blame, and the disclosure that replacements for the capital's wartime administration had been sounded out — rather than the meeting Klitschko says he has requested (Reuters, 6 November 2026).
  • Mobilization's hardest correction was slow. Parliament passed the bill lowering the draft age from 27 to 25 in May 2023; Zelenskyy signed it only on 2 April 2024, after months of public argument about who would take responsibility for the war's most unpopular necessity — a delay he explained by the need to understand its consequences, and which commanders and allies criticized while the army's manpower shortage deepened (parliamentary and presidential records, 2023–24).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Russian-speaking citizens, men avoiding mobilization, displaced people, residents of occupied territories, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Russian-speaking Ukrainians are counted in by biography and by policy. Zelenskyy is himself a native Russian speaker who campaigned in both languages, used Russian in his 2019 inaugural address to speak directly to Donbas residents, and built his early presidency on unity between Ukraine's Ukrainian- and Russian-speaking parts. No law of his presidency bans or penalizes the Russian language in private life; his wartime addresses are in Ukrainian, with Russian used at chosen moments to address Russian soldiers and citizens. The millions of Russian-speaking Ukrainians serving in the armed forces are the standing refutation of the Kremlin's claim — and his government has not adopted the collective suspicion of them that the claim invites.
  • Displaced people were given an institution. In December 2024 his government created the Ministry of National Unity, charged with ties to the roughly six million Ukrainians abroad and their eventual return; Chernyshov, its first minister, held the post until the corruption charges described in Test 1 — the ministry's existence is the counting-in, its first head's file is graded where it belongs, under Test 1. Ukraine's stated position throughout the 2025 negotiations has been that people are not traded: no legal recognition of occupied territory as Russian, and the return of deported Ukrainian children named as a standing demand.
  • Residents of occupied territories remain, in his government's formal position, Ukrainian citizens whose land is temporarily occupied; his peace proposals through 2025–26 have refused to cede them in law even where the contact line is discussed as fact. Opposition voters' ballots remain live ammunition: opposition parties hold their Rada seats, their voters' representatives blocked the rostrum over the Poroshenko sanctions, and the February 2025 Rada resolution on elections — supported across factions — records a parliament, not a court of acclamation.

Evidence against:

  • The mobilization's burden falls on one slice of the flock, and its enforcement has been documented to abuse them. Since 24 February 2022 men aged 18–60 have been barred from leaving the country; since April 2024 the draft age is 25–60. The Ombudsman's office recorded complaints about recruitment-center (TCC) conduct rising from 18 in 2022 to 3,312 in 2024 and 6,127 in 2025 — forceful street detentions ("busification," chosen as Ukraine's word of the year for 2024), beatings, denial of access to lawyers, and mobilization of men holding lawful deferments or disabilities. Ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets has called the use of force and deaths inside recruitment centers a "shameful practice"; the Council of Europe's Commissioner for Human Rights has listed beatings, harsh detentions and denial of counsel among systemic violations; criminal cases have been brought against individual recruiters, including, in August 2026, ten Odesa TCC officers charged with kidnapping and torture (Ombudsman's reports as compiled in the Ukrainian Analytical Digest No. 019, May 2026, and SFG Media investigation, June 2026). The state needs the men; the documented record is that the men it takes by force are counted as a resource before they are counted as rights-holders, and presidential acknowledgment of the abuse pattern has been rare relative to its scale.
  • Men of draft age who left or stayed abroad were pressed as a class: in April 2024 Ukraine suspended consular services for military-age men abroad, a collective measure aimed at compelling return for registration, criticized by human-rights organizations as punishing a category rather than adjudicating individuals (Foreign Ministry announcement, April 2024).
  • The believers of Ukraine's historically largest church were made answerable for their institution's affiliation. Law No. 3894-IX, adopted 20 August 2024 and signed by Zelenskyy on 24 August, bans the Russian Orthodox Church and provides for terminating Ukrainian religious organizations "affiliated" with it — in practice the Ukrainian Orthodox Church (UOC), with more than 8,000 parishes. Affiliation can rest on signs in the Russian church's own charter, which the UOC cannot change; the UOC states it severed ties with Moscow in May 2022. The World Council of Churches warned of "unjustified collective punishment of an entire religious community"; Pope Francis expressed concern; the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights had criticized earlier drafts. The government's shelf is also documented: the SBU reported 68 criminal cases against individual UOC representatives, including treason, since the invasion (RFE/RL, August 2024; Forum 18 record of Law 3894-IX). Individual clergy prosecuted on evidence is one law; a whole communion terminated on affiliation criteria is counting a flock out by category, and the law is graded as the second while the cases are graded as the first.
  • People under occupation are counted in rhetoric and discounted in administration. Pension and social-payment delivery to occupied areas has been suspended since 2014 — a policy predating Zelenskyy and continued by him — leaving elderly residents dependent on travel through checkpoints that the full-scale war has made nearly impossible; and Ukrainians who lived and worked under occupation face a collaboration-law framework under which thousands of cases have been opened, with the UN and human-rights organizations documenting concern that ordinary survival activity — teaching, utility work, accepting a pension — can fall within prosecutorial reach. Zelenskyy's government has not narrowed the framework; its position is that collaboration is a real crime in a real occupation, which is true of the category's core and contested at its edges.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record, from 2019 to November 2026, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The institutional anti-corruption promises of 2019 were substantially legislated in his first year. He promised removal of parliamentary immunity, an impeachment law, and anti-corruption bills: constitutional amendments abolishing MPs' immunity were adopted on 3 September 2019 (effective 1 January 2020); the impeachment law he signed in September 2019; the land-market reform followed in 2020; and the "de-oligarchization" law of 23 September 2021 created the register he had promised. Whatever their later erosion (Tests 1–2), the paper promises of the campaign were, in the main, actually passed — a rarer sentence in this series than it should be.
  • The peace promise was attempted before it was destroyed. He campaigned on ending the Donbas war through the Minsk process: he met Putin at the Paris Normandy summit of 9 December 2019, agreed prisoner exchanges that were carried out, and accepted the July 2020 ceasefire that sharply reduced front-line deaths for a period. The process stalled on sequencing, and on 24 February 2022 Russia replaced the Donbas war with a full-scale invasion aimed at Kyiv. The promise — peace — was not kept; the record shows the attempt and the identity of the party that ended it.
  • On the war's central transaction of 2025, his stated terms and the signed terms match. Through the minerals negotiation he said publicly that Ukraine would sign a partnership, not a surrender of resources or a debt for past aid; the 30 April 2025 agreement is a 50/50 fund, resources remaining Ukraine's, past aid expressly not repayable (CSIS, May 2025). He also said Ukraine would not legally recognize occupied territory as Russian; through the 20-point-plan negotiations of late 2025 and into 2026, that line has held in every text his government has tabled.
  • European integration, promised as direction rather than deadline, overshot: EU candidate status in June 2022, accession negotiations opened in June 2024 — movement his 2019 program implied but no one scheduled.

Evidence against:

  • The one-term promise was walked back in stages, and the walk-back is documented at each step. In 2019 he said he would serve a single term. In May 2021 he said he had not yet made up his mind. The war then suspended the question legally — his continuation past May 2024 is constitutional, supported by a cross-party parliamentary consensus of November 2023 and by majority public opinion in polling against wartime elections — but his own statements have kept moving: on 25 September 2025 he told Axios he was "ready" not to run again after the war; on 15 December 2025 he told ZDF he would run if elections were held during the war and was unsure about peacetime (Next Ukrainian presidential election record, citing Axios and ZDF). A promise, a hedge, a constitutional extension he did not choose, and two different answers in one autumn: the honest grade is that the word was not kept as spoken in 2019, and the reason is partly history and partly his own revisions.
  • The 2019 debate pledge about his own circle has met its hardest test in 2025. Campaigning, he promised there would be no protected friends in his government and said he would leave himself if he broke the law — the standard his critics now quote against the Chernyshov protection reporting (Test 1), the July 2025 NABU law, and the Midas network's organizer being his own Kvartal 95 partner. His November 2025 answer — "the president of a country at war cannot have any friends" — is the pledge restated at the moment the record most strains it. Both are on the shelf; the strain is documented, the restatement is documented.
  • The anti-corruption promise, legislated early (above), was contradicted in his sixth year by the two largest entries in this dossier: a law subordinating the anti-corruption bodies, signed by him, and a $100 million kickback scheme alleged to have run for fifteen months inside the state nuclear operator under ministers he appointed, organized by his friend. That NABU exposed the second is to the system's credit and is graded under Test 2; that both happened is the word-keeping record.
  • His wartime assurance that Ukraine would not hold elections under fire — repeated from 2022 through 2025 as a matter of law and fairness to soldiers and the displaced — was qualified on 9 December 2025 into a conditional offer under American pressure (Test 3). The conditions (security guarantees, ceasefire, legislation) may never be met, and the constitutional position is unchanged; the shift from "impossible" to "60 to 90 days, if" is nonetheless a moved word, recorded as such.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Zelenskyy's file is the hardest in this series to grade flat, because the same presidency contains the July 2025 sequence whole: a president who signed away his anti-corruption bodies' independence in a day, and the same president, nine days later, signing it back at the demand of teenagers with cardboard signs — and saying, on the record, that the state must listen. The grip is real: a consolidated telethon with the opposition's channels off the air for four years and no author; punishment of citizens, including the opposition's leader, by council decree instead of court; an unelected chief of staff who built a power vertical; a church of millions made answerable for its charter. The limits on the grip are equally real: courts that rule against the government and are obeyed, a selection commission whose winner took office over the Cabinet's refusal, and anti-corruption detectives who, months after being raided, searched the chief of staff's home and ended his tenure. The count is generous to the displaced, the Russian-speaking, and the occupied in word and in formal position, and hardest on the two groups the war consumes: draft-age men, whose documented treatment by the recruitment system is the file's ugliest continuing record, and the UOC's believers, counted out by affiliation. His word was legislated early, strained late, and rescued more than once by the institutions and the street correcting him. A wartime ruler who has repeatedly reached for more than the law allows — and who governs a country that has, so far, repeatedly made him hand it back.
