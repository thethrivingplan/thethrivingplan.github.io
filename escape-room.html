The Locked Study — and two more doors

An escape room game in three original rooms: The Locked Study, The Greenhouse, and The Tide Boathouse. Search each drawn room by tapping, gather what you find, combine what belongs together, and work out every code from clues inside the room itself.

The fair-play law: every solution is findable inside the room you are standing in. No answer needs a guess, a search engine, or knowledge from outside the room. If you are stuck, the room is holding a clue you have not looked at yet — and the hint ladder below will walk you to it, rung by rung.

How to play

  1. Search. Tap places in the room — furniture, floor, walls. Some hold items; some hold clues; some are just a room being a room.
  2. Carry. What you find goes into your hands at the bottom of the room. Tap an item to select it (it glows), then tap a place to use it there.
  3. Combine. Some things are two halves of one tool. Tap one item, then tap a second item, and the game tries to put them together.
  4. Codes. A lock never wants a guessed number. Somewhere in the room, a note gives the order and the room itself gives the numbers — counts, colours, sequences. Find both halves before you touch the dials.
  5. Hints. The hint ladder has three rungs for wherever you are stuck: a nudge, a stronger hint, then the answer with its why. Every hint you take is written into your record, honestly — hints are a tool, not a cheat, and the record simply says you used them.
  6. Escape. The last key opens the door. Your time (on timed runs), your hints, and whether you escaped or gave up all go into your record below.
Tap to open: the three rooms, in plain words

The Locked Study. A scholar's room, locked from the outside. A key, a drawer, a dark corner, a safe, and a shelf of coloured books.

The Greenhouse. Glass, soil, and blooms. Tools come in pieces here, and the flowers themselves are keeping a number.

The Tide Boathouse. Nets, bells, and a chained door. One puzzle here is not a number at all — it is an order, and it rings.

Strategies — more than one way through a room

There is no single right way to search a room. These are the approaches experienced players actually use, with what each one costs and buys. Coach mode teaches them in the moment, on the room you are standing in.

1 · The grid sweep

Divide the room into quarters in your mind and search one quarter completely before moving on — top-left, top-right, bottom-left, bottom-right.

Buys: nothing gets skipped; your first pass is thorough. Costs: slower, and you will tap some scenery that holds nothing.

2 · Feature-first

Go straight for the things that look worked — drawers, locks, loose edges, anything slightly out of place — and sweep the plain scenery last.

Buys: fast progress when your eye is good. Costs: the one plain-looking place you skipped is, sooner or later, the one holding the key.

3 · Revisit after every find

Every new item changes what the old places mean. After each find, re-check the places you already searched — a locked drawer is a different place once you hold a key.

Buys: catches the chains other players stall on. Costs: some re-taps come back empty; it feels slower than it is.

4 · Inventory thinking

When stuck, stop searching the room and audit your hands: What haven't I used? What changed since I last looked? An unused item is an unanswered question.

Buys: breaks most stalls without a hint. Costs: none, except the pause itself.

5 · Code-hunting

Numbers hide in counts (how many books, blooms, bells), in colours (which order a note names them), and in sequences (what order things happen or hang). When you meet a lock, ask: what in this room can be counted, and what tells me the order?

Buys: turns a lock from a guess into a reading exercise. Costs: you must actually find the note — the order half — before the count means anything.

6 · The hint ladder, used well

Take one rung, then go back and work — don't climb all three at once. A nudge preserves the solve; the full answer teaches it.

Buys: you keep moving and still learn the room. Costs: every rung is counted in your record, so a clean-sheet run means climbing sparingly.

About the form

The escape room is a documented game form with two chapters. First came the browser games of the early 2000s: small point-and-click adventures, drawn in a web page, in which you woke in a single locked room and escaped by searching it — clicking drawers, pockets, and picture frames, combining what you found, and reading codes out of the room's own details. Then, from 2007 in Japan and spreading worldwide through the 2010s, the form left the screen: physical escape rooms locked real groups of people in themed rooms with the same grammar — search, combine, decode, unlock — against a clock.

This page is built in the first chapter's shape, on purpose: the drawn, tappable room, at your own pace. That is why the standard form here is untimed — the relaxed, documented form of the browser originals. The clock belongs to the physical rooms, so here it stands as a labelled variant you can switch on, never a pressure you must accept. All three rooms, their puzzles, their art, and their words are original to this page, written for it — the form is borrowed, nothing else is.

Tap to open: what "rules are settings" means here

Room choice, hints on or off, timer on or off — these are settings, not laws, and you may change them freely, even mid-room. When your settings differ from the standard form (untimed, hints allowed), the banner above the room says so and names the variant in play, and your record notes which form each finish was earned under. Nothing about a variant is hidden.
