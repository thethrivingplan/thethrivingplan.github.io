The Ruler's Test — Evika Siliņa, Prime Minister of Latvia (2023–2026)

Subject: Evika Siliņa — Prime Minister of Latvia from 15 September 2023 to 28 May 2026, at the head of a three-party coalition of her own New Unity (JV), the Union of Greens and Farmers (ZZS) and The Progressives (PRO), approved by the Saeima 52–39. Her tenure is COMPLETE and is graded as a completed tenure. On 7 May 2026 two stray Ukrainian drones, diverted by Russian electronic warfare, crashed near Rēzekne in Latgale, one hitting a fuel-storage facility. Siliņa announced on 10 May that Defence Minister Andris Sprūds (Progressives) had lost her trust and the public's; he resigned. The Progressives withdrew from the coalition, depriving her of her majority, and on 14 May 2026 — the same morning anti-corruption officers detained her agriculture minister — she resigned, saying "I am resigning, but I am not giving up." She served as caretaker until 28 May 2026, when the Saeima confirmed Andris Kulbergs (United List) as prime minister of a four-party coalition that included her own party. Her Saeima mandate was restored on 11 June 2026; she chairs the Saeima's European Affairs Committee. In the parliamentary election of 3 October 2026 New Unity fell from 26 seats to 7 (6.45% of the vote), and she was re-elected to the 15th Saeima from Vidzeme; Kulbergs' second government (United List + National Alliance) was confirmed on 17 November 2026, 57–34, with New Unity in opposition. She is graded on the documented record of her government, September 2023 – May 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The law reached her own party's most senior figure, and she helped it do so. In March 2024 the Prosecutor General's Office opened criminal proceedings over the use of chartered special flights by her predecessor and party colleague Krišjānis Kariņš (36 flights; about €613,830 from the state budget 2021–2023, roughly €1.36 million including EU funds). The State Audit Office found unlawful and uneconomical organisation of the flights had caused about €545,000 in unjustified expenditure. Siliņa set up an ad-hoc commission, suspended State Chancellery director Jānis Citskovskis and opened disciplinary proceedings against him; Citskovskis was charged in 2025 with failure to perform official duties causing serious consequences. Kariņš resigned as foreign minister in April 2024 after meeting Siliņa; he was not himself charged, and that limit of the case is stated exactly (Prosecutor General's Office and State Audit Office reporting, March–April 2024; LETA/BNS).
  • The law reached inside her own cabinet and her own office, on her last day. On 14 May 2026, acting on the prosecutor's instruction, the Corruption Prevention and Combating Bureau (KNAB) detained Agriculture Minister Armands Krauze (ZZS) and State Chancellery director Raivis Kronbergs — the head of the prime minister's own office — for procedural actions in the "wood industry" case: a criminal process, opened in March 2026, examining possible abuse of office and inaction in state support to wood-processing companies, centred on a late-2023 Cabinet decision that directed state forester Latvijas Valsts Meži to apply sharply lower prices to long-term sawlog contracts; the Prosecutor General's Office estimates possible damage to the state at about €50 million. Ten persons held procedural status by May 2026. The alleged offences are ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated; Krauze and Kronbergs were released the same evening and no preventive measures were applied. Siliņa suspended Krauze before signing her resignation, saying she could not tolerate any shadow of suspicion over ministers; Kronbergs resigned days later by mutual agreement with her (KNAB and Prosecutor General's Office reporting; LSM, May 2026).
  • She publicly demanded investigation of an allegation against her own party's past. When the head of the election-software firm SOAAR alleged on Latvian Television (2024) that Unity's former secretary-general Artis Kampars had asked him in 2014 to falsify Saeima election results to seat the party's then-chairwoman, Siliņa committed to requesting information from the security services and calling on the Prosecutor General to investigate: "Either it happened and one has to answer to the law, or it did not happen and the other has to answer to the law." The allegation is unproven and is carried here as an ALLEGATION; KNAB separately opened a departmental inquiry into Unity's free use of the firm's voting system (LTV, Kas Notiek Latvijā?; BNS, 2024).
  • The anti-corruption machinery ran without documented obstruction throughout her tenure: KNAB detained a sitting minister of her coalition; the prosecutor-selection process (Test 2) concluded with a consensus appointee; and the State Audit Office published findings damaging to her government (flights, Rail Baltica) without interference.

Evidence against:

  • Her majority was founded on a bargain with a convicted, internationally sanctioned figure's party. The coalition included ZZS, whose component party For Latvia and Ventspils is led by Aivars Lembergs: convicted of bribery, money laundering and abuse of office, with the Riga Regional Court's appeal judgment of 27 September 2023 upholding a four-year prison sentence; designated by the US Treasury under Global Magnitsky in December 2019 for corruption, and sanctioned by the United Kingdom in November 2024 as a "kleptocrat" (asset freeze and travel ban for him and his daughter). The risk was managed, not removed: the coalition agreement restricted decision-making to MPs and ministers and provided for a minister's resignation if contact with sanctioned persons were detected, and Siliņa stated the government "strictly dissociates itself" from cooperation with sanctioned persons. The documented structural fact remains that her government's survival depended on the party Lembergs leads, a link Freedom House and the opposition flagged from the coalition's first day (Freedom House, Nations in Transit 2024; US Treasury, 9 December 2019; UK Government, November 2024).
  • The wood-industry decision that prosecutors are now examining was her own government's collective act. The late-2023 price decision benefiting one industry's long-term contracts was taken by the Cabinet she chaired; Krauze's documented defence is precisely that — "if he is to be dismissed for the decision of the entire government… then the entire government should resign" (Latvian Television interview, February 2026). Siliņa publicly raised the question of Krauze's resignation in February 2026 as the scandal grew but did not remove him until KNAB detained him in May — three months in which a minister under a documented cloud kept his portfolio because his party's votes held up the coalition (inbox.lv/LETA reporting, February–May 2026). The underlying offences remain ALLEGATIONs; the delay is documented fact.
  • State support flowed repeatedly to the flag carrier on discretionary decisions: the government co-invested €14 million in airBaltic alongside Lufthansa (19 August 2025) and granted a €30 million short-term state loan in April 2026, for an airline that lost €118.2 million in 2024 and €44.3 million in 2025. The decisions were taken openly through the Cabinet and, for the loan, the Saeima budget committee — where her own partner ZZS initially blocked approval — but critics across the Baltic states questioned serial support for a loss-making state company while its promised IPO receded (Ministry of Transport and airBaltic reporting, 2025–2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the prosecutor's office, KNAB, the election commission, the media and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Constitutional Court decided the most sensitive cases of her tenure on the law, in both directions, and its rulings were obeyed. On 15 February 2024 it upheld the Immigration Law amendments requiring Russian citizens to certify Latvian at A2 level to keep residence — a ruling for the state's position. On 30 March 2026 it ruled the other way against the political majority's critics and in favour of narrowing minority-language obligations, holding provisions that required public media to produce a set share of minority-language content incompatible with the Constitution (invalidity postponed to 1 May 2027 to let parliament legislate). No retaliation against the Court, its judges or its budget is documented in either case (Constitutional Court judgments, 15 February 2024 and 30 March 2026).
  • The prosecution's leadership changed by the book, after open institutional friction that the executive did not resolve by force. Prosecutor General Juris Stukāns' term expired on 11 July 2025; a whistleblower-based inquiry into ALLEGATIONs against him (disclosure of confidential information; instructions against a supervising prosecutor's position — the disclosure-to-MPs allegation dismissed as a baseless assumption) was conducted by a Supreme Court justice on the Supreme Court president's instruction, and the first selection competition failed when no candidate won the Judicial Council's support. A second competition followed, and on 20 November 2025 the Saeima appointed Armīns Meisters prosecutor general by 88 votes to 0. The post stood in acting hands for over four months — drift, documented below — but the appointment was the Judicial Council's nominee, confirmed without dissent (Supreme Court and Judicial Council records; Saeima vote, 20 November 2025).
  • The central bank kept its governor. When coalition bargaining collapsed over Mārtiņš Kazāks' reappointment in December 2024, the outcome — after the opposition United List and National Alliance nominated him — was his re-election on 6 February 2025 by 67 votes to 18, with most of the coalition voting for the man its own bargaining had dropped. The Bank of Latvia's independence was never legislatively touched; the embarrassment of a temporarily vacant ECB Governing Council seat was political, not institutional capture (Saeima vote, 6 February 2025; Latvijas Banka).
  • Election administration failure was owned, not buried. The nationwide ballot-scanner system failed in the June 2025 municipal elections, forcing manual recounts; the responsible minister, Inga Bērziņa, resigned, as did the Central Election Commission's chair. On 26 March 2026 parliament amended the election law to require fully manual counting for the October 2026 election. ODIHR's Needs Assessment Mission (May 2026) recorded no major concerns about voter-list accuracy, and ODIHR deployed an Election Assessment Mission for the 3 October 2026 election, which transferred power away from her party without dispute (ODIHR NAM report, 24 July 2026).
  • Media scrutiny of her government was continuous and unpunished: LSM, TV3's Nekā personīga, Delfi and others broke and sustained the flights case, the wood affair and the SOAAR story; LSM's own journalists protested their management board's rebranding plan in June 2026 and won a compromise keeping the LTV and Latvian Radio brands — inside the state broadcaster, against its board, in the open (LSM reporting, June–July 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The state narrowed the public broadcaster's remit by language, by political decision. The National Security Concept approved by the Saeima on 28 September 2023 — days into her term, government-backed — provided that from 1 January 2026 public-media content be produced only in Latvian and languages "belonging to the European cultural space." Latvian Radio's Russian-language station LR4 closed on 1 January 2026, ending the RUS.LSM/LR4 services that had reached roughly 150,000–200,000 listeners and readers weekly. The International and European Federations of Journalists, the EBU and RSF protested that the move would prohibit public media from serving a quarter of the population in its language. The LTV/LR merger into a single state company (LSM), mandated by the Saeima on 18 January 2024 by 75–1, was likewise a majority-driven restructuring of the public media's governance. Private Russian-language media were untouched — the grip fell on the public institution's remit, executed by law (National Security Concept, 28 September 2023; SEPLP/LSM records; EFJ-IFJ statement, 2023).
  • The central-bank governorship was treated as coalition currency before it was treated as an institution. In December 2024 the coalition withdrew both its candidates on the eve of the vote — Kazāks (New Unity/Progressives) blocked by partner ZZS, which advanced its own candidate — leaving the post to an acting governor past the term's expiry on 20 December 2024; the coalition then agreed on Deputy Governor Santa Purgaile, whom ZZS refused to support days later, prompting her withdrawal. Siliņa conceded on the record that the process "did not go smoothly" and had been "rather politicized" (LETA reporting, December 2024 – February 2025). The institution's independence survived; the appointment process showed the governing parties reaching for the post first.
  • A key referee post stood vacant for a long stretch on her watch: the prosecutor generalship ran in acting hands for over four months in 2025 (above) because the first Judicial Council competition produced no nominee — institutional drift that left the office prosecuting her government's own cases without a confirmed head (Judicial Council competition records, 2025).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, farmers, partners or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • She adjusted to the farmers. When the Farmers' Parliament (ZSA) launched nationwide protests on 5 February 2024 — calling her government "particularly distinguished by its arrogance and indifference" — the government negotiated rather than confronted; the coalition agreed within the month to ban imports of Russian and Belarusian agricultural products, a central demand of the moment, and the protests wound down without force (LSM/BNS, February 2024).
  • She reset her own government in public. On 21 February 2025, amid falling ratings and open criticism of drift, she announced a "restart," dismissing one minister from each coalition party — Transport Minister Kaspars Briškens (Progressives), Education Minister Anda Čakša (her own New Unity) and Welfare Minister Uldis Augulis (ZZS) — and had replacements confirmed on 6 March 2025. The adjustment was owned as her decision and applied to her own party as well as her partners (Saeima confirmations, 6 March 2025).
  • On Rail Baltica she changed the model rather than defending the file. After the June 2024 joint review by the three Baltic state audit offices exposed the cost explosion (whole project €23.8 billion against €5.8 billion estimated in 2017; Latvian section €9.6 billion against €1.97 billion), she demanded a new management model and a 20% cost reduction, refused ad-hoc bill-paying ("the government is not an institution that pays bills"), and her government adopted a phased construction scenario in December 2024 — main line to both borders plus the Riga and airport stations first (State Audit Office review, June 2024; Cabinet decision, December 2024).
  • At the end, she left. Having lost her majority on 13–14 May 2026, she resigned on 14 May ahead of a no-confidence vote rather than clinging to a minority office, served as caretaker, and handed over constitutionally on 28 May; her party then joined the successor coalition as a partner. She had survived earlier no-confidence attempts, including the vote of 15 January 2026, through ordinary parliamentary arithmetic, not procedural evasion (Saeima records; Reuters, 13–14 May 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The defence wobble was answered by blaming downward, late. The 7 May 2026 Rēzekne explosion was not the first stray-drone episode of her tenure; reporting records repeated drone crashes on Latvian territory and months of pressure on Defence Minister Sprūds to explain why, despite a defence budget rising toward 4.9% of GDP, drones "continued to crash on Latvian territory before being intercepted." Her answer, three days after the explosion, was to declare the minister — a partner party's nominee — had lost her trust and the public's, forcing his resignation; the move detonated her coalition within four days. Whether the failure was the minister's or the government's collective underinvestment in air defence is CONTESTED between the parties: Sprūds said he resigned "to protect the Latvian army from politically divisive campaigns"; Siliņa said the incident "clearly demonstrated that the political leadership of the defence sector has failed to fulfil its promise of safe skies" (Le Monde, 15 May 2026; PISM Spotlight 26/2026).
  • The Rail Baltica adjustment was shallower than announced. By December 2025 — a year after the phased scenario — Latvian Television's analysis found the savings achievable by cutting noise barriers, bridges and tunnels amounted to only a few hundred million against a multi-billion gap, that the expected main-line reduction was about 15% before inflation rather than the 20% she had demanded, and that the government's own cost report had still not been considered (LTV, Kas Notiek Latvijā?, December 2025).
  • On the Istanbul Convention she could not hold her own coalition and the question was deferred, not decided. Her government existed partly to ratify the Convention (ratified November 2023, in force 2024). On 30 October 2025 the Saeima voted 56–32 to withdraw — opposition votes plus her own partner ZZS, voting against its earlier ratification position. Siliņa opposed withdrawal publicly, addressing protesters outside parliament; the law was stopped only by President Rinkēvičs returning it on 3 November 2025, and MPs then postponed the question until after the October 2026 elections. The protection remained in force at her exit, but by the president's hand, not her majority's (Saeima vote, 30 October 2025; Equality Now and FREE Policy Briefs records, November 2025).
  • Coalition pushback was routinely bought off rather than answered. In the autumn 2025 budget fight, ZZS issued a public ultimatum — find money for regional passenger transport or the government falls — pointing at Rail Baltica redesign funds; the documented expectation and outcome pattern was that the prime minister and finance minister would find the money from reserves. The coalition's public infighting (Istanbul, budgets, ministerial dismissals traded between partners) continued through the February 2025 restart and up to the collapse (inbox.lv/LETA coalition reporting, 2025–2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Russian-speaking minority, Latgale, the poor, women, her opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The social floor rose by general measures that did not test language or loyalty. The minimum wage rose from €700 (2024) to €740 (2025) to €780 (2026) under a new formula tying it to 46% of the average wage; the labour-tax reform effective 1 January 2025 raised the non-taxable minimum to €510 a month and simplified personal income tax to two rates (25.5% and 33%), benefiting low earners most; pensions were indexed; and a prescription-medicine pricing reform effective January 2025 reduced pharmacy mark-ups and prices for many medicines — an item on her own farewell list, though some public comment disputed the size of the effect (Labour Law and Cabinet regulations, 2024–2025; Ministry of Welfare; her resignation statement, 14 May 2026 — the medicine-price effect CONTESTED in public commentary).
  • The Russian-speaking population's private sphere was not touched: private Russian-language media (Delfi's and TVnet's Russian services, commercial radio) continued freely — a fact the Constitutional Court itself relied on in March 2026 — and no Russian-speaking party was banned or deregistered; Russian-speaking voters' parties (Latvia First, Sovereign Power, the Progressives' urban electorate) contested the October 2026 election and won 32 seats between the two Russian-speaker-oriented lists alone (Central Election Commission results, 3 October 2026).
  • The residence-permit regime, as implemented under her government, contained exemptions and second chances: the A2 language requirement did not apply to those aged 75+ or with health exemptions; failed candidates could retake the test; those who applied received temporary permits while learning. The Foreign Ministry's October 2025 accounting states that the majority of the roughly 30,000 affected Russian citizens completed the procedures and renewed their permits; the migration office had identified about 500 persons whose permits had expired or were expiring, and 10 residing illegally and issued departure orders (Ministry of Foreign Affairs implementation report, 17 October 2025).
  • Defence of the eastern border — the region where the Russian-speaking and poorest population is concentrated — was funded as protection of everyone in it: the 2026 defence budget (4.91% of GDP) earmarked €55 million for eastern-border anti-mobility infrastructure and continued the Selonia training area in Latgale's neighbouring region, and 0.25% of GDP for Ukraine support (Ministry of Defence, December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Across her tenure, the Russian-speaking minority — about 23% of residents are ethnic Russians and roughly 36–37% speak Russian at home — was governed primarily as a security category, in a cumulative narrowing each step of which was lawful and court-reviewed. The transition to Latvian as the sole language of instruction in public preschools and basic education, legislated in 2022, was implemented and completed under her government on 1 September 2025, with Russian to be dropped even as a second foreign language from the 2026/27 school year; her education minister defended completion while acknowledging teacher shortages in the east, where Rēzekne reported in 2024 that Latvian-medium vacancies could not be filled. UN experts expressed concern over the amendments' effect on minority-language education, and the UN Human Rights Committee received submissions documenting the elimination of minority-language schooling (Education Law implementation records; Ministry of Education and Science; UN expert statements).
  • The residence file's hard edge fell on elderly long-term residents. The 2022 Immigration Law amendments (implemented under her government; Constitutional Court upheld them 15 February 2024 as proportionate to national security) required former Latvian citizens/non-citizens who had taken Russian citizenship to prove A2 Latvian or lose permanent residence. By September 2024, migration authorities had issued 63 departure orders, nine people had been forcibly deported, and 632 had left voluntarily after failing or not taking the test (RFE/RL, 16 September 2024, citing the migration office). In June 2025 her Saeima majority extended the requirement to about 4,949 additional Russian citizens who had received permits before 2003 (vote 82–10), with loss of residence for non-compliance by November 2025. The government's rationale — integration and security in the context of Russia's war, with the same language standard asked of all third-country nationals — is documented and stated here at full strength; the documented effect is that a defined group of mostly elderly residents faced expulsion from the only home most had known, for a language test.
  • Public media stopped counting Russian speakers as its audience: LR4's closure (Test 2) removed the state's own Russian-language service — the channel through which the state had spoken to this population, including with counter-disinformation — while the state's security messaging continued to describe the same population as the primary target of hostile propaganda. Media-freedom organisations and community representatives documented the contradiction; the government answered that commercial media fills the space (EFJ/IFJ and EBU statements; Constitutional Court, 30 March 2026).
  • Latgale remained counted last in the economic ledger. The at-risk-of-poverty rate in Latgale was 35.8% (2024 incomes) against 15.6% in the Riga region; nationally, 404,000 people — 22.0% of the population, up from 21.6% in 2023 — were at risk of poverty, and Latgale had the highest share of minimum-wage earners (22.4%). Latgale is also the region that physically absorbed the war's spillover — the May 2026 explosion in Rēzekne district — while holding the country's weakest fiscal base (Central Statistical Bureau EU-SILC data, 2024–2025).
  • Non-citizens — roughly a tenth of residents, overwhelmingly Russian-speaking, without national voting rights — were not addressed at all: no naturalisation initiative, no local-franchise proposal, no change in their status across 32 months of a government that legislated extensively about the population they belong to (status quo, documented by omission from the government's declaration and legislative record).

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record, September 2023 – May 2026, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (word kept):

  • The central promise of her tenure was defence, and it was kept ahead of schedule. Her government committed Latvia to move toward 5% of GDP for defence; delivered figures were 3.3% of GDP in 2024 (€1.36 billion) and 3.8% in 2025 (€1.59 billion), and the 2026 budget adopted on 4 December 2025 set defence at €2.16 billion — 4.91% of GDP on the Finance Ministry's updated forecast — with 5% projected for 2027, years ahead of NATO's 2035 Hague horizon. In December 2025 she chaired the Baltic prime ministers' meeting that jointly confirmed the 5%-in-2026 goal (Ministry of Defence budget records, 2024–2026; Cabinet statement, 12 December 2025).
  • The coalition's founding social-liberal commitments were enacted in its first months: the Istanbul Convention was ratified in November 2023 (in force 2024) — a stated reason for forming this coalition rather than renewing her predecessor's — and partnership (civil-union) legislation passed in November 2023, effective July 2024.
  • The declared tax reform was delivered on schedule: labour-tax changes effective 1 January 2025 (non-taxable minimum €510; PIT rates of 25.5% and 33%), and the minimum-wage path was delivered year by year (€700 → €740 → €780).
  • Rail Baltica continuity: she promised the project would proceed under a new management model with a phased scope rather than be abandoned; the phased scenario was adopted in December 2024 and construction continued, with the first-phase target of 2030 maintained.

Evidence against (word not kept):

  • The Rail Baltica saving she demanded did not materialise: the 20% cost reduction announced in 2024 had, on the project's own numbers a year later, no documented basis — about 15% before inflation at best, against a Latvian-section estimate still around €9.6 billion (LTV analysis, December 2025). The word was a percentage; the record is a smaller one.
  • airBaltic's promised route out of state support reversed. The government's plan centred on an IPO that would bring in private capital and shrink the state's exposure; by the airline's 2025 annual report the IPO was suspended and "not currently viewed as a potential source of capital for 2026" — and the state instead put more money in: the €14 million co-investment of August 2025 and the €30 million loan of April 2026, weeks before her resignation. She did not rule out further injections ("this is never ruled out," Latvian Radio interview, 2026) (airBaltic annual report 2025; Baltic Times, 2026).
  • The "restart" promised renewal and delivered a reshuffle. The February 2025 restart was announced as a relaunch with newly defined tasks; within the year the coalition had fought publicly over the Istanbul Convention (her partner voting to undo the coalition's founding act), over the 2026 budget (a partner's public ultimatum), and over the wood-industry scandal, and it collapsed in May 2026, five months before the election, over her dismissal of a partner's minister. She had promised a government that would work as one team for the full term; it did not reach the term's end (coalition record, February 2025 – May 2026).
  • Government stability itself was part of the offer: she took office promising a broader, more stable majority than her predecessor's. The coalition began at 52 seats, governed effectively as a minority by its final year as seats and discipline eroded, survived repeated no-confidence attempts including that of 15 January 2026, and fell in May 2026. The electorate's verdict followed on 3 October 2026: New Unity fell from 19.2% and 26 seats to 6.45% and 7 seats, and her party ran its finance minister, Arvils Ašeradens, as its prime-minister candidate rather than the incumbent (Central Election Commission, October 2026).
  • Her farewell accounting claimed reduced bureaucracy for entrepreneurs and lower medicine prices among the tenure's achievements; the medicine-pricing reform is documented policy (January 2025) with its price effect CONTESTED in public commentary, and no independent measure in the record verifies a bureaucracy reduction of the size implied (resignation statement, 14 May 2026; public commentary, May 2026).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED

Evika Siliņa's completed tenure is the record of a law-governed government whose institutions worked — and repeatedly bit the hand that appointed them. The prosecutor's office and the audit office pursued her own party's former prime minister's flights until he resigned; KNAB detained her coalition's agriculture minister and her own chancellery director on the morning she fell, and she suspended the minister before she resigned. The Constitutional Court ruled for and against the state's positions and was obeyed both times; the central bank kept the governor her coalition's bargaining had dropped; the election that ended her party's decade in power was administered by a commission her government had reformed after its own failure, and she left office on the constitution's timetable, twice — in May 2026 by resignation when her majority vanished, and in October 2026 by the voters.

The seams are equally specific. Her majority was built with the party of a convicted and internationally sanctioned oligarch, fenced by a coalition clause rather than refused. The wood-industry decision now under criminal examination was her Cabinet's collective act, and the minister responsible kept his portfolio for three months of documented scandal because his party's votes were the majority. The Russian-speaking fifth of the country was counted, across education, public media and residence law, primarily as a security exposure — each measure lawful, exempted and court-upheld, and cumulative in effect — while Latgale, the poorest region and the one absorbing the war's physical spillover, closed none of its 20-point poverty gap with Riga. Her word was kept where it was largest — defence spending rose from 3.3% toward 5% of GDP on the path she announced — and not kept where it was most concrete: the Rail Baltica saving that never reached 20%, the airBaltic IPO replaced by fresh state money, and the stable full-term government that fell five months early over her own dismissal of a partner's minister. No test is failed outright, because no institution was captured and no law was bent into a personal instrument; no test holds outright, because at each point where holding would have cost her the majority — Lembergs' party, Krauze's portfolio, the coalition's comfort — the majority was chosen. The record is correctable: the wood-industry proceedings, when they conclude, will move Test 1 in one direction or the other, and they are graded here as what they are — allegations under examination, against a government that, to its credit, let the examination reach its own door.
