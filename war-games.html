The General's Table

This is an abstract strategy game; real war is not a game.

“Amateurs study tactics. Professionals study logistics.”

That saying is the heart of this game. Battles here are won less by brave charges than by supply lines — the path of food, fuel and ammunition back to your base. Cut that path, and even the strongest unit fades.

How it works — nothing hidden

The three units

Infantry (INF) — Strength 4, moves 2, shoots 1 hex, health 4. Steady everywhere, and the cheapest strength you own — the backbone that holds towns and bridges.
Armour (ARM) — Strength 5, moves 3, shoots 1 hex, health 5. Fast and hard-hitting on open ground; woods and hills cost it double movement to enter, so its speed is a plains weapon.
Artillery (ART) — Strength 4, moves 1, shoots exactly 2 hexes, health 3. Fragile up close, decisive at range. It cannot move and shoot in the same turn.

Terrain — and its real why

Plains — open ground. Fast to cross, nothing to hide behind.
Hill ▲ — high ground sees and shoots farther: a unit on a hill gets +1 attack, and its sight range grows by 1. Costs 2 movement to enter.
Woods ♣ — trees hide and slow: +1 defence for the unit inside, costs 2 movement to enter, and enemy sight into woods is shortened.
Town ▦ — buildings are strong to defend: +2 defence. Towns are also objectives — hold 3 to win.
River — an obstacle, not a road. No unit may enter a river hex. Cross only at a bridge hex (marked with planks). Whoever holds the bridges holds the map in two.

How supply works — the heart of the game

Every unit must be able to trace a path, hex by hex, back to its own base. The path may cross any passable ground except hexes held by the enemy or next to an enemy unit (their zone of control) — unless one of your own units stands there. Bridges matter: lose the bridge hexes and everything across the river is cut off.

At the end of each full turn, supply is checked and printed plainly on each unit:

Cut off, turn 1: movement −1, strength −1.
Cut off, turn 2: movement halved, strength −2.
Cut off, turn 3: the unit surrenders and is removed. No drama, no dice — an army that cannot eat cannot fight.

Reconnect the path — retake a hex, destroy the blocker, or move the unit back into contact — and supply is restored in full at the next check.

Combat — the whole formula, no hidden math

When a unit attacks, both sides roll one six-sided die, and the roll is printed in the battle log.

Attack total = attacker's strength + die roll + 1 if the attacker stands on a hill − supply penalty if the attacker is cut off.
Defence total = defender's strength + die roll + terrain bonus (town +2, woods +1, hill +1) − supply penalty if the defender is cut off.
The higher total wins the exchange. The loser takes 1 damage, or 2 damage if they lost by 4 or more. On a tie, both take 1 damage. A unit at 0 health is destroyed. Artillery takes no return roll damage when shooting from 2 hexes away — the defender still rolls, and if the defender's total is higher, nothing happens (the shots missed their mark); the formula and rolls are still shown.

Winning: move a unit onto the enemy base hex, hold any 3 objective towns at the end of your turn, or destroy every enemy unit. If turn 30 ends with no winner, the side holding more towns wins; if towns are level, the side with more total health left wins.

Is the bot honestly harder at higher levels? The self-play proof

The four commanders use the same rules and the same armies. They differ only in judgement: Learning mostly picks moves at random and often hesitates; Steady advances and takes clear shots but blunders about a quarter of the time; Sharp weighs attacks, terrain and supply, and blunders rarely; General does all of that, never blunders, focus-fires, and deliberately moves to cut enemy supply lines.

Strategy shelves — the Full Why

One tap down, nothing removed. Every principle is printed with its trade-off. Where more than one strategy can work, more than one is given — none of these is sold as the one answer.

Supply and logistics first — Tap to open

What it is: before any plan, ask “can my units eat?” Keep a clear path home, guard your bridges, and look for the move that cuts the enemy's path rather than the move that wins a single fight.

Why it works here: a cut-off unit loses strength every turn and surrenders on the third. Two of your units sitting on the enemy's supply path can defeat an army twice their size without firing.

The trade-off: units spent guarding lines are units not attacking. Spread too thin protecting everything and you are strong nowhere. The judgement call — which the game will not make for you — is how much safety is enough.

Concentration of force — Tap to open

What it is: mass your strength at one point instead of spreading it evenly. Three units attacking one enemy in a turn will usually destroy it; three units each duelling a separate enemy usually destroy nothing.

Why it works here: combat is an exchange of die rolls. Focused fire removes enemy guns from the board, and every gun removed stops rolling back at you.

The trade-off: concentrating leaves the rest of the map thin. A fast enemy armour unit can slip behind a concentrated push and sit on your supply path. Concentration wins fights; it can lose campaigns.

Interior lines — Tap to open

What it is: hold the middle. A force in the centre, with short paths to every part of the front, can shift to meet each threat in turn — while an enemy spread around the outside must travel the long way round to combine.

Why it works here: the central bridge and the middle towns are the interior position of this map. From there your units reach either flank in a turn or two; the enemy coming around the edges cannot say the same.

The trade-off: the centre is where you can be attacked from two sides, and holding it means giving up the far towns early. Interior lines buy time and flexibility, not ground.

Defence in depth — Tap to open

What it is: don't put everything on the front line. Hold a first line to slow and reveal the attack, a second line in towns and woods behind it, and keep one fast unit back as a reserve to plug gaps — or to counter-attack a unit that has pushed too far and cut its own supply.

Why it works here: an attacker who breaks through a single line often ends the turn beyond their own supply path. Depth turns their breakthrough into their trap.

The trade-off: ground given up early may include objective towns, and a passive defence surrenders the initiative — the enemy chooses where and when to fight. Depth must end in a counter-punch or it is only a slower defeat.

The inherent strength of the defence — Tap to open

What it is: in this game, as in the record of real war, the defender starts ahead. Terrain bonuses go to the unit being attacked, not the attacker: a town is worth +2 before a die is rolled. Attacking even odds into prepared positions is how armies bleed.

What follows: attack where you are locally stronger — two or three against one — or make the enemy come to you. Artillery is the attacker's answer: it strikes from 2 hexes, beyond reply.

The trade-off: someone must eventually take the base and the towns, and that someone must attack. Pure defence wins only if the enemy is obliging enough to waste themselves against it. Knowing when the balance has tipped — when their army is worn enough that your attack is no longer even odds — is the general's real skill.

Study cards — campaigns where supply and principle decided — Tap to open

The invasion that starved — Russia, 1812

Napoleon's Grande Armée entered Russia with roughly 600,000 men and the finest tactical reputation in Europe. It won its battles. What it could not do was feed itself: the supply system assumed living off the land, the land was stripped and burned, the distances outgrew the wagon trains, and horses died for want of fodder. Fewer than a tenth of the army came home in fighting order. The tactics were brilliant. The logistics were the campaign.

Saved by interior lines — the Marne, 1914

As German armies swung around Paris, the French and British held the interior position: short rail lines radiating from the capital let them shift troops to the threatened point faster than the Germans could march around the outside. The counterstroke at the Marne stopped the advance and saved France in 1914. Position and transport, not a single heroic charge, decided it.

An army that ate its supply line — North Africa, 1941–42

Rommel's Afrika Korps repeatedly out-fought its opponents tactically, and repeatedly outran its own fuel. Every mile advanced from Tripoli doubled the truck journey to reach the front, and trucks themselves burn fuel to deliver fuel. At El Alamein, Montgomery's army sat close to its depots while the Axis sat at the end of a 1,000-mile tether. The battle was lopsided before it began — on the supply map.

The defence, held in depth — Kursk, 1943

Warned of the German attack, the Soviet command built defences many miles deep — minefields, anti-tank strongpoints, and reserves held far back — and let the attack spend itself against layer after layer. The largest tank battle in history ended with the attackers exhausted inside the depth of the defence and the defenders counter-attacking. It is the textbook case for defence in depth — and for its cost: the ground, and the towns on it, were given up first on purpose.

Concentration at the point of decision — and its risk

Again and again in the record, the side that is weaker overall wins by being stronger at the one place that matters that day — and the same record shows the bill when the gamble fails: everything massed at one point is everything lost if that point collapses. Concentration is a wager, not a law. This page prints it as one strategy among several, with its trade-off, because that is what it honestly is.
