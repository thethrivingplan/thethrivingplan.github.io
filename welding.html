Safety — read this at full size

Real welding can burn you, blind you, and poison your lungs. Real soldering burns skin and involves lead. This page cannot protect you — it teaches knowledge and judgment only. Hands come from supervised, real practice.

  • Eyes: A welding arc is brighter than the sun. Looking at it unprotected — even a flash from across the room — causes arc eye, a painful burn to the cornea. Real welding means a proper helmet with the right shade, every time, and screens so bystanders are protected too.
  • Skin & burns: Arc UV burns exposed skin like severe sunburn in minutes. Sparks and spatter set clothing on fire. Welded metal stays hot enough to cause deep burns long after it stops glowing — treat every piece as hot.
  • Fumes: Welding fumes damage lungs. Galvanized metal gives off zinc fumes that cause metal fume fever; some coatings and metals are far worse. Real work needs ventilation, a respirator where required, and never welding on unknown coatings, tanks, or sealed containers — those can explode.
  • Electricity & fire: Stick and MIG welders can shock, especially in wet conditions. Clear flammables, keep an extinguisher in reach, and watch the area after you finish — smouldering fires start quietly.
  • Soldering: A soldering iron sits at 300–400 °C and looks exactly the same hot as cold. Solder often contains lead: never eat or touch your face while soldering, wash hands with soap every time you finish, keep lead solder away from children, and use ventilation — flux fumes irritate lungs.
  • Certifications are real: Structural, pressure-vessel, pipeline, and aerospace welding require tested certification (for example AWS qualifications in the US) because failed joints kill people. Passing this simulator is practice for your judgment — it is not a qualification to weld anything that carries load, pressure, or people.

The Welding Bench

Pick a joint and a process, set your heat, travel speed, and work angle, then run the bead. The puddle model grades what actually happens inside the metal — not how the bead looks on top.

Heat melts the base metal. Too little = the bead sits on top. Too much = you blow a hole through it.

How fast the torch moves. Too fast = the puddle can't form. Too slow = heat piles up and the bead sags wide.

Torch tilt from vertical. Wrong angle pushes the puddle to one side and digs a groove (undercut) along the edge.

Your grade will appear here. Try a first bead with the settings as they stand — a cold bead teaches as much as a good one.

Why the puddle decides everything

Welding is not gluing. A weld holds because both pieces of base metal melt and mix with the filler in one liquid puddle, then freeze as a single piece of metal. If the base metal never melts — a cold bead — the bead is a metal rope lying on the surface. It can look tidy and still peel off, because underneath, nothing ever joined. If there is too much heat, the puddle burns through the piece, leaving a hole and a weak, oxidised mess. In range, the puddle wets into both sides, and the frozen bead is as strong as the metal around it.

Full depth: Heat input is roughly amperage × voltage ÷ travel speed — which is why heat and speed trade against each other. A hotter setting can survive faster travel; a slow pass at high amps cooks the grain structure of the steel around the weld (the heat-affected zone), making it brittle even when the bead itself looks fine. That invisible zone is why procedure and certification exist for real work.

The Soldering Bench

A drawn practice board with six joints. Pick a joint, set the iron, heat, and solder feed — then make the joint. You are graded on the shape the solder freezes into, because the shape tells the truth about what happened.

Solder melts near 183–220 °C, but the joint itself must get hot enough to melt solder on contact — that takes a hotter iron.

How long the iron touches the pad and lead together. Too short = only the solder melted, not the joint. Too long = the pad lifts off the board.

How much solder you feed in. Enough to form a small volcano — no more. Extra solder hides bad joints and bridges to the next pad.

Your joint grade will appear here. Joints done: 0 / 6. A shiny volcano is the goal.

Why shape tells the truth: wetting, flux, surface tension

Molten solder behaves like water. On a hot, clean surface it wets — it spreads out and climbs, freezing into a smooth, shiny cone like a little volcano around the lead. On a cold or dirty surface, surface tension wins and the solder pulls itself into a dull ball that sits on the joint without bonding — a cold joint that will crackle, overheat, and fail. Flux is the helper inside the solder wire: it strips the invisible oxide skin off the metal as it heats, so the solder can actually touch clean metal and wet it. Rule of the bench: heat the joint, not the solder — solder should melt because the pad and lead are hot, never just because the iron touched the wire.

Full depth: A good joint is an intermetallic bond — tin in the solder alloys microscopically with the copper pad. A cold joint never forms that layer; it's solder resting on oxide. That's why it can pass a glance and fail in the field, and why rework means re-heating with fresh flux, not just adding more solder on top.

The Full Why of Joining Metals

Welding: fusion

Two pieces become one piece. An electric arc (or flame) melts the edges and a filler rod or wire; the shared puddle freezes as continuous metal. Strength comes from the melt mixing — which is why surface rust, paint, and oil matter: contamination in the puddle becomes holes (porosity) and cracks in the frozen weld.

Soldering: wetting, not melting together

The parts never melt. Solder — a lower-melting alloy — melts, wets both surfaces, and freezes as a metallic bridge that conducts electricity. It's a joint for signals and small loads, not structure: solder is soft and creeps under sustained stress.

Heat is a budget

Every joint needs enough heat to join and not a degree more than the part can take. Too little: no fusion, no wetting. Too much: burn-through, lifted pads, brittle grain, warped parts. Almost every defect on both benches is a heat-budget error — wrong amount, wrong place, or wrong time.

Why practice on a simulator

Judgment is pattern recognition: what a right puddle looks like, how a good joint flows. Building that eye here — where a mistake costs nothing and burns no one — is exactly what supervised real practice then turns into hands.

Butt joint — Level 1

Two edges meet face to face. The simplest test of fusion: the puddle must melt through both edges evenly. Used for plates, pipes, and frames.

Lap joint — Level 2

One piece overlaps the other. Easier to fit up, but the puddle must wet the top edge without melting it away — angle control starts to matter.

Fillet joint — Level 3

A corner: one piece at 90° to another. The torch works at about 45° so the puddle feeds both faces equally. The joint behind most brackets and frames — and the easiest to undercut.

Job Queue — The Difficulty Ramp

Work the queue in order. Each job narrows the window a little, like real bench time: plate first, brackets next, corners last. Pass a job (grade C or better) and the next one opens.
