The Ruler's Test — Samia Suluhu Hassan, President of Tanzania

Subject: Samia Suluhu Hassan — President of the United Republic of Tanzania since 19 March 2021. The seat graded is the presidency, because in Tanzania executive power sits there: the president is head of state, head of government, and commander-in-chief, appoints the electoral commission, the chief justice, the director of public prosecutions, the central bank governor, and the heads of the security services, and the prime minister serves as leader of government business in parliament, not as a rival executive. Hassan, Tanzania's first female head of state, was vice-president under John Pombe Magufuli and succeeded him constitutionally on his death in office in March 2021, completing his term. She then won a full term of her own at the general election of 29 October 2025, in which the Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC) declared her the winner with 97.66% of the vote (31,913,866 votes) on an officially claimed turnout of about 87% — an election held after the largest opposition party, CHADEMA, had been barred from participating and its chairman, Tundu Lissu, imprisoned on a treason charge, and after ACT-Wazalendo's presidential candidate, Luhaga Mpina, had been disqualified. The election was followed by the deadliest political violence in Tanzania's history. She was sworn in for the second term on 3 November 2025 at a closed ceremony at a military parade ground in Dodoma. She is graded on five years and eight months in office — the completed remainder of Magufuli's term (2021–2025) and the first year of her elected term — and the file is not thin: it contains both a documented opening (2021–2023) and a documented closing (2024–2026), and the grades turn on both. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Election-period death tolls are kept in their separate counts, each with its source, and are never merged into a single figure. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machinery has genuinely worked the administrative state, and the outcome measure has moved. The Prevention and Combating of Corruption Bureau (PCCB) reported monitoring 1,773 development projects worth TSh 11.48 trillion in 2023/24, investigating 92 projects found with discrepancies, and saving TSh 30.19 billion in a single year, including funds recovered from a state bank and a city council (PCCB Director General's report to the president, 2025). Tanzania's Corruption Perceptions Index score rose to 41 of 100 in the 2024 index (82nd of 180), from 40 in 2023 and 38 in 2020 (Transparency International, CPI 2024).
  • Enforcement reached senior office-holders inside the state from her first weeks. Receiving the Controller and Auditor General's report in late March 2021, she suspended the Director General of the Tanzania Ports Authority, Deusdedit Kakoko, on the spot to pave the way for a PCCB investigation into embezzlement the audit had recorded, and in the same first hundred days suspended a district commissioner, the management of the Kariakoo Market Corporation, and revoked a district commissioner's appointment over the handling of a land dispute (presidency statements and contemporaneous reporting, March–mid-2021).
  • The prosecution service has, at least once, visibly stood down against the opposition rather than press a weak case: CHADEMA chairman Freeman Mbowe, arrested in July 2021 and charged with terrorism financing under a case inherited from the transition, was freed in March 2022 when prosecutors dropped the charges, and met Hassan hours after his release (Reuters, January 2023).

Evidence against:

  • The state's heaviest charge — treason, which carries the death penalty — has in this tenure been laid only in one political direction. CHADEMA chairman Tundu Lissu was arrested on 9 April 2025 and charged with treason over remarks calling for electoral reform; CHADEMA's elections director John Pambalu was charged with treason on 21 October 2025 for allegedly intending to "persuade the public to cause obstruction" of the election through protests; scores of protesters were charged with treason after the October 2025 election, among more than 2,000 people arrested in the crackdown period (Human Rights Watch, October 2026; contemporaneous reporting, April–November 2025). In July 2026 CHADEMA reported that 51 of its officials had been charged with terrorism offences (ICNL Civic Freedom Monitor, updated 16 November 2026). No comparable capital or terrorism process has touched any figure of the ruling party or the security services.
  • The law has not reached the security forces for the largest killing in the country's history. One year after the election violence, Human Rights Watch recorded that "authorities have yet to make any arrests for human rights violations committed before and during the vote" — while the arrests of protesters numbered in the thousands (HRW, 28 October 2026). The president's own Commission of Inquiry concluded at least 518 people were killed (April 2026); the prosecutions that followed were of the governed, not of the governors.
  • A parallel, unaccountable track ran beside the courts. The Tanganyika Law Society documented 83 cases of politically motivated disappearances as of August 2024 (cited in ARIES Consult strategic reporting, 2026). CHADEMA strategist Ali Kibao was abducted in September 2024 and found murdered; former ambassador to Cuba Humphrey Polepole, a ruling-party insider turned critic, was reported missing weeks before the 2025 election (HRW, October 2026). Hassan said in 2024 that she had ordered an investigation into reports of abductions; no official findings have been made public (Reuters, October 2025). Disappearance is the negation of one law: it is punishment with no charge, no court, and no record — and on the documented record it has fallen on critics, and its files stay closed.
  • The anti-corruption shelf, real as it is, stops at the political line. The documented suspensions and PCCB cases run against administrators and agency heads; no senior ruling-party figure has faced prosecution in this tenure, and the bureau that investigates corruption reports to the president it would have to investigate around. One law operates inside the civil service. A different law — treason, terrorism, disappearance, and impunity for the forces of order — operates on the opposition and the street.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts have twice ruled against the state's immediate interest in the highest-stakes cases. In September 2025 the High Court at Dodoma overturned INEC's disqualification of ACT-Wazalendo candidate Luhaga Mpina, and INEC initially complied and accepted his nomination papers (Business Daily, September 2025). And on 6 November 2026 the High Court in Dar es Salaam acquitted Tundu Lissu of treason — the prosecution's central charge, on which it had called 17 of 30 listed witnesses — though it convicted him of incitement under Section 390 of the Penal Code and sentenced him to four years (AP, The Citizen, The Chanzo, 6 November 2026). A wholly captured bench does not acquit the opposition leader on the capital count.
  • The central bank has run monetary policy on its own stated framework: inflation averaged 3.3% in 2025, inside the Bank of Tanzania's 3–5% target band, and the World Bank's October 2026 Economic Update describes inflation within target, sustainable public debt, and strong private-sector credit growth — the bank speaking and acting as a technocratic institution, not a treasury window (World Bank, October 2026; TICGL analysis, February 2026).
  • The commissions she appointed after the election were not staffed as ornaments: the inquiry into the election violence was chaired by a retired chief justice, Mohamed Chande Othman, and reported a death toll and recommendations — accountability, institutional reform, national reconciliation, a national day of mourning — that the government has found uncomfortable enough to leave partly unimplemented (ICNL, 2026; HRW, October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The electoral referee is the president's own appointment, and it decided who she would face. The African Union observer mission's preliminary statement recorded as a structural defect that all members of the electoral commission are appointed by the president (AU Election Observation Mission, 5 November 2025). INEC disqualified CHADEMA — the largest opposition party — from the 2025 election and all by-elections until 2030 because the party declined to sign the electoral code of conduct, a ground CHADEMA contests as beyond INEC's authority (INEC decision, April 2025; ICNL, 2026). In the November 2024 local elections, mass disqualifications had already left opposition parties fielding candidates for barely a third of posts — 30,977 candidates for 80,430 positions (ICNL, 2026).
  • When the court corrected the referee, the referee overruled the court. After the High Court overturned Mpina's disqualification and INEC accepted his papers, INEC disqualified him a second time within days, on an objection originating with the Attorney General's office and a registrar's ruling on his party's internal primaries — leaving the president facing only minor-party candidates (Reuters, 27 August 2025; Business Daily, September 2025). The sequence is the test in miniature: judicial autonomy existed, spoke, and was routed around.
  • The verdicts of the referees' referees were against the process itself. The AU mission concluded the 2025 elections "did not comply with AU principles, normative frameworks, and other international obligations and standards for democratic elections," documenting ballot stuffing, multiple ballots issued to single voters, observers expelled from counting, and a total internet shutdown on election day (AU EOM preliminary statement, 5 November 2025). The SADC mission concluded the election "fell short of the requirements of the SADC Principles and Guidelines Governing Democratic Elections" and that "in most areas, voters could not express their democratic will" (SADC SEOM, 3 November 2025). Hassan publicly rejected the substance, insisting at her swearing-in that the polls were credible and conducted with "efficiency and transparency" (swearing-in address, 3 November 2025).
  • The media regulator answers upward, and the record is dated. On 2 October 2024 the Tanzania Communications Regulatory Authority suspended the online operations of Mwananchi Communications — The Citizen, Mwananchi, and Mwanaspoti — for 30 days after The Citizen published an animated video depicting a figure resembling the president watching reports of abductions and killings (CPJ, October 2024). In September 2025, weeks before the election, authorities suspended JamiiForums, the country's main online discussion platform, for 90 days; a roughly five-to-six-day nationwide internet shutdown followed on election day; journalists were arrested in the post-election crackdown (CPJ UPR submission, April 2026; RSF reporting, 2026). Tanzania fell 22 places in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index, to 117th — the steepest decline in the region (RSF, April 2026).
  • The assembly right she restored was withdrawn again by her own government: in 2026 the Minister of Home Affairs announced an indefinite nationwide ban on public political rallies, and in July 2026 police arrested 130 people following calls for nationwide demonstrations (ICNL, updated 16 November 2026; Tanzanian reporting to the UPR, November 2026). The 2023 lifting of the rally ban (Test 5) had been done by presidential word rather than by law — and what one word gave, another took back. That is the definition of grip: the institutions hold their freedoms at the seat's pleasure.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The early tenure was a documented series of adjustments toward the pushback. She inherited the Mbowe terrorism case and let it die (March 2022); she negotiated with CHADEMA over months; on 3 January 2023, meeting leaders of 19 registered parties, she lifted the six-year ban on political rallies her predecessor had imposed — "It is the right of political parties to hold rallies" — and the exiled Lissu returned within the month saying the climate had improved (Reuters, 3 January 2023; International IDEA, January 2023). The 4Rs doctrine — Reconciliation, Resilience, Reforms, Rebuilding — was the stated frame, and for two years the record substantially matched it.
  • After the election violence, the adjustment came — late, partial, but on the record and in her own voice. In her first parliamentary address after the election (14 November 2025), she announced the commission of inquiry, acknowledged the opposition's position that constitutional reform was the precondition of reconciliation, pledged a constitutional reform process within her government's first 100 days, and ordered the release of arrested youths who had "just followed the wave": "For those who were just following the wave, let them be counselled and released… as a mother, I have forgiven those who may have joined the protests out of peer pressure" (AP, 14 November 2025). Some of the more than 2,000 arrested were in fact released (HRW, October 2026).
  • The inquiry she appointed reported against her government's comfort: at least 518 dead, more than 2,300 injured, root causes assigned to longstanding political, socioeconomic, and governance grievances, and recommendations for accountability, institutional reform, reconciliation, and a national day of mourning (Chande Commission report to the president, April 2026). She then established a second commission, chaired by retired Court of Appeal Justice Shaban Ally Lila, mandated to identify individuals who planned, financed, coordinated, or participated in the violence and to examine excessive and unlawful use of force; it began field investigations in August 2026 on a 150-day mandate (ICNL, 2026).
  • The adjustment continued into the grading month. On 16 November 2026 she convened the Council of Political Parties at State House on the proposed Commission for Reconciliation and Mediation, and on 20 November 2026, addressing senior police officers in Moshi, she set a public timetable — a new or revised constitution by 2028 or 2029 — with the reconciliation commission's nominations being gathered from religious bodies, parties, and civil society (The Guardian/IPP Media, 21 November 2026; Africa Briefing, November 2026). Tanzania also presented itself to its fourth Universal Periodic Review in Geneva on 5 November 2026, which she framed as partnership rather than trial (UPR reporting, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The first answer to the wobble was denial, at the moment it mattered most. Sworn in on 3 November 2025 behind closed doors at a military parade ground — no public, no independent media — she attributed the unrest to foreigners ("It did not surprise us to see that some of those arrested for causing disturbances came from outside the country"), vowed to safeguard the nation "through all means and at all cost," and dismissed the observer missions' findings while insisting the election had been credible and transparent (swearing-in address, 3 November 2025; AP and Capital FM, 3 November 2025). The five-day nationwide curfew and the internet shutdown were the same answer in operational form.
  • The adjustment stopped exactly where accountability begins. A year on: no arrests of any security-force member for the killings (HRW, 28 October 2026); the Chande Commission's full report unpublished, with UN experts on 29 October 2026 calling for its publication (ICNL, 2026); the recommended national day of mourning not declared by the first anniversary, when victims' families, churches, and civil society renewed their calls (ICNL, 2026). The second commission's mandate — to identify who "planned, financed, coordinated, or participated in" the violence — is framed around the protesters' side of the street; whether it extends to prosecuting security-force abuses "remains unclear" (HRW, October 2026).
  • On the street's continuing pushback, the government doubled down in 2026: the indefinite rally ban, 130 arrests in July 2026 after calls for demonstrations, terrorism charges against 51 CHADEMA officials, and the Lissu prosecution carried through to a four-year incitement sentence (ICNL, 2026). The pattern across the tenure is consistent: the wobble is answered with words, commissions, and timetables — and simultaneously with the instruments that caused it. Adjustment and doubling down run in parallel, which is why this test, alone among the five, is genuinely two-sided.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the minorities, the opposition's voters, Zanzibaris, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The broad economy counts more people than it did. Growth reached 5.5% in 2024 and an estimated 5.9–6.0% in 2025 with inflation held near 3%, and the World Bank's indicator table shows the international poverty rate ($3.00, 2021 PPP) edging down from 51.3% (2018) to an estimated 48.3% (2025) (World Bank indicators, 2026; IMF estimates, 2025). The Bank projects 6.1% growth for 2026 while noting the unfinished work is job quality (World Bank Economic Update, 2 October 2026).
  • A concrete, delivered transfer to the counted: on 1 May 2025 she announced a 35.1% increase in the public-service minimum wage, from TSh 370,000 to TSh 500,000 a month, effective July 2025 — announced, dated, and paid (The Citizen, May 2025).
  • Representation widened on paper: women hold 153 of 387 National Assembly seats after the 2025 election (election reporting, 2025) — though the bulk arrive through reserved special seats rather than constituency victories, a qualification the AU mission's inclusion findings share.
  • For the Maasai who did relocate from Ngorongoro, the state built the receiving end: houses, two-to-five-acre plots, roads, a school, a dispensary, water, electricity, and a police post at Msomera (HRW, July 2024, recording the government's provision alongside its findings). The government's position, stated at the November 2026 UPR, is that the law treats all citizens equally and recognizes no separate category of ancestral land (UPR reporting, 5 November 2026) — a universalist claim, recorded here as the government's own framing.

Evidence against:

  • The election dead were, for months, not counted by their own state at all — and the counts that exist are kept separate here because they do not agree. The government's initial position played down the scale; the UN human rights office's first credible reports put the dead at at least 10 in three cities (31 October 2025); Amnesty International had information of at least 100 dead; CHADEMA said about 700 within days, later claiming to have verified 1,000–2,000 deaths; the president's own Commission of Inquiry concluded at least 518 killed and more than 2,300 injured (April 2026), a figure the opposition rejects; later reporting by legal, human-rights, and medical organizations estimates 5,000–10,000 deaths with thousands missing (OHCHR, October–November 2025; CHADEMA statements; Chande Commission, April 2026; MEWC election review, June 2026). CNN documented evidence of police firing on protesters and signs of mass graves, which the government denies (CNN investigation, 2025). The Chande figure includes 21 children. A state that counts its dead only under pressure, publishes no full report, and declares no day of mourning has not counted them in the sense this test measures — and families of the killed and missing were still waiting for answers at the first anniversary (HRW, October 2026).
  • The Maasai of Ngorongoro are being counted out of their land by plan. The government's relocation program, devised by 2021, targets about 82,000 residents of the Ngorongoro Conservation Area for removal to Msomera, some 600 km away, by 2027 (OHCHR communications; HRW, July 2024). HRW's investigation — nearly 100 interviews — found services deliberately defunded from 2022, grazing restricted, and consent manufactured by pressure: "constructive forced eviction" in violation of international standards; the government maintains relocation is voluntary. Two presidential commissions Hassan appointed in December 2024 reported in March 2026: they identified real failures — inadequate consultation, poor planning, inconsistent incentives — and still endorsed continuing the relocations; the reports have not been published, only 1,678 of a targeted 23,000 households had relocated on The Chanzo's count, and Maasai leaders state the communities overwhelmingly reject removal (The Chanzo, 2026; commission reporting, March 2026). UN CERD has called for an end to forced evictions; UNESCO stated in April 2026 that forced evictions are incompatible with Tanzania's obligations. The count proceeds anyway.
  • The opposition's voters were counted out before a ballot was cast: their largest party barred until 2030, their second party's candidate removed twice, their chairman in prison — and the AU and SADC missions then recorded low turnout and voters unable to express their will (AU EOM; SADC SEOM, November 2025). In Zanzibar, CCM's Hussein Mwinyi was declared re-elected with nearly 80% amid opposition fraud claims — an ALLEGATION recorded as contested, in an archipelago whose elections have been disputed for three decades.
  • The growth does not reach the poor at the rate the headline claims. The World Bank estimates poverty at about 43% on its national-comparable measure, only slightly below 2018's 45%, and independent analysis of her first hundred days of the new term found urban wages up only 5.3% and rural wages 4.9% in nominal terms across 2020–2025 against 37.5% nominal GDP growth — an effective inflation rate for the poorest half estimated at 5.5–6.5%, well above the 3.4% headline (World Bank, 2026; TICGL, February 2026). The young protesters of October 2025 were, in large part, this arithmetic in the street; the inquiry she appointed named socioeconomic grievance among the violence's root causes (Chande Commission, April 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 19 March 2021, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The rally ban — kept, on a date. Her reconciliation program promised political space; on 3 January 2023 she lifted the rally ban in person, before the leaders of 19 parties, and opposition rallies resumed within weeks (Reuters, 3 January 2023). The exiles came home under the same opening: Lissu returned on 25 January 2023, and four newspapers banned under her predecessor had their bans lifted in her first two years (Reuters; Africa Report, 2023). (The 2026 reimposition of a rally ban is scored where it belongs — against the durability of this word, below and in Test 2.)
  • The minimum wage — kept to the shilling. Announced 1 May 2025 at 35.1%, TSh 370,000 → TSh 500,000, effective July 2025, and implemented (The Citizen, May 2025).
  • The big builds — delivered. The CCM program she inherited and renewed promised completion of the flagship projects; the Julius Nyerere Hydropower Project — East Africa's largest dam — was completed in 2025 and anchors the power supply her growth figures run on (TICGL, February 2026). The 2020 manifesto's 8-million-jobs target was reported by CCM as met — 8,084,203 jobs — though the party's own breakdown shows 7,037,024 of them informal against 1,047,179 formal, an accounting critics contest (CCM manifesto reporting, 2025; recorded here as the party's claim, contested).
  • The inquiry — promised and produced. Promised 14 November 2025; the Chande Commission reported in April 2026 with a death toll her government had every incentive to suppress and recommendations it has only partly followed. A promised inquiry that reports 518 dead is not nothing; it is recorded here at full strength, and its unfinished implementation is scored below.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The constitution — the longest-running word, repeatedly re-dated. The journey was promised from 2021: her task force collected views in 2022 and the process was publicly pledged as ready to start — Lissu said in January 2023 he was returning home precisely for "that trip." No process began before the 2025 election. On 14 November 2025 she pledged again, precisely: a constitutional reform process within the first 100 days. One hundred days passed; by July 2026 critics counted 268 days with no process begun, and the ruling party's own former running mate was publicly urging citizens to fight for what the manifesto had pledged (public criticism, July 2026). Consultations on a reconciliation commission began only in November 2026, and on 20 November 2026 the promise acquired its newest date: a new or revised constitution by 2028 or 2029 (IPP Media, 21 November 2026). The word has not been abandoned; it has been refinanced four times, and each refinancing moved the date further from the election that made reform urgent.
  • The opening — reversed. The 4Rs settlement of 2023 rested on her word that parties could rally, newspapers could publish, and critics could organize. By the 2025 election every element had been withdrawn in practice — rallies' restoration undone by the 2026 indefinite ban, the largest newspapers suspended online in October 2024, the main discussion platform suspended in September 2025 — and in April 2026 she herself redrew the doctrine, warning at the Union anniversary that the 4Rs must never serve as a pretext for breaking the law or threatening stability (UPR reporting, November 2026). A promise kept for three years and then revoked by the promiser is scored as it stands at grading: not kept.
  • Electoral reform — the precondition that never arrived. CHADEMA's stated price for signing the electoral code was reform of the electoral laws; the 2024 amendments left every INEC member a presidential appointee, as the AU mission noted in November 2025. The 2025 election was therefore run under the referee structure she had spent four years saying she would fix — and she then declared its output credible and transparent.
  • The inquiry's own recommendations — the word within the word. The Chande Commission recommended a national day of mourning and accountability; by the first anniversary neither had happened, and the full report remains unpublished despite UN experts' public call on 29 October 2026 (ICNL; HRW, October 2026). The 2025–2030 manifesto's forward promises — at least 7% annual growth, 8 million further jobs with at least half formal, tax revenue at 15.6% of GDP — are recorded here as stated; growth at 5.9–6.0% in 2025 runs below the 7% line in the manifesto's first measured year, and the rest cannot yet be graded.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · Her word: MIXED.

Samia Suluhu Hassan's file is two tenures in one seat, and the grades are what happens when they are read together rather than separately. The first tenure (2021–2023) genuinely opened the country her predecessor had closed: rallies restored, exiles home, newspapers unbanned, a terrorism case against the opposition chairman allowed to die, anti-corruption enforcement that suspended senior officials in her first hundred days and has since lifted the country's CPI score from 38 to 41. The second movement (2024–2026) closed it again by instruments rather than by announcement: a regulator's suspensions, a registrar's objection, an electoral commission that barred the largest party and re-barred a candidate a court had reinstated, a treason charge for a speech about electoral reform, 83 documented disappearances with no published investigation, and — when the excluded country voted with its feet in October 2025 — a crackdown whose dead her own commission counts at no fewer than 518, including 21 children, a figure the opposition and independent estimators place far higher and which this dossier keeps in its separate counts. No member of the security forces had been arrested for any of it a year later. Her word divides cleanly down the same line: where a promise runs through the administrative state — a wage, a dam, an inquiry's existence — it is kept to the shilling and the date; where a promise would constrain the seat itself — a constitution, an independent referee, the rally right — it is deferred, re-dated (2028/29 is the fourth refinancing of the constitutional word), or revoked. The wobble is the exception that proves the shape: she does adjust — inquiries appointed, youths released, commissions multiplied, a constitution timetable finally spoken aloud in November 2026 — but the adjustment is always of language and process, never yet of accountability, and it has so far arrived paired with the rally ban, the terrorism charges, and an unpublished report. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the Lila commission's work reaches the security forces or only the street; whether the Chande report is published and its day of mourning declared; whether the reconciliation commission includes the party that was barred from the election; and whether the 2028/29 constitution, if it comes, removes the president's hand from the electoral commission — the single appointment from which, on this record, the rest of the grip descends.
