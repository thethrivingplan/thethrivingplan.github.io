The Ruler's Test — Sitiveni Rabuka, Prime Minister of Fiji

Subject: Sitiveni Ligamamada Rabuka — Prime Minister of Fiji since 24 December 2022. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Fiji's presidency is a ceremonial head-of-state office elected by Parliament, while executive power — the government, the budget, and ministerial direction of the state — sits with the prime minister. The president for most of this tenure has been Ratu Naiqama Lalabalavu (in office since 12 November 2024, succeeding Ratu Wiliame Katonivere). Rabuka, born 13 September 1948, is graded across a record no other sitting leader in this series carries: he led the two military coups of 1987 that overthrew an elected government to reassert ethnic Fijian supremacy, then served as an elected prime minister from 1992 to 1999, chaired the Great Council of Chiefs (1999–2001), led SODELPA in opposition (2016–2020), and founded the People's Alliance in 2021. In the general election of 14 December 2022, the incumbent FijiFirst won the plurality (26 of 55 seats, 42.55% of the vote) but lost its majority; Rabuka's People's Alliance won 21 seats, the National Federation Party 5, and SODELPA 3, and the three formed a 29-seat coalition. During the count, when a glitch in the Elections Office results app reversed the displayed lead, Rabuka called for a recount and an independent audit and asked the military to intervene; the Republic of Fiji Military Forces commander, Major General Jone Kalouniwai, refused, and international observers reported no significant irregularities. Parliament then elected Rabuka prime minister by secret ballot, 28 votes to 27, on 24 December 2022 — one member of his own coalition voted for his opponent. He is graded on the current tenure, December 2022 to November 2026, with his 1987 and 1992–1999 record admitted as context where it bears directly on a test, as this series grades other returning rulers across their tenures. The tenure is still running at grading, and its largest files are open: the third reading of the Constitution (Amendment) Bill is scheduled for 30 November–4 December 2026, a constitutional referendum has been announced for 9 December 2026, the writ for the next general election falls due by 24 December 2026, and the election itself must be held by 6 February 2027. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The law reached the top of his own government, in office, twice in one month. In late September 2025 the Fiji Independent Commission Against Corruption (FICAC) charged Deputy Prime Minister Manoa Kamikamica — a founding member of Rabuka's own party — with perjury and, in the alternative, giving false information to a public servant, over evidence he had given to a commission of inquiry; Kamikamica resigned from Cabinet effective 22 October 2025 to clear his name, and Rabuka accepted the resignation the day it was offered (Office of the Prime Minister statement, 21 October 2025; RNZ Pacific, October 2025). Days later FICAC charged the second deputy prime minister, Finance Minister Biman Prasad, over a 2015 asset declaration; Prasad likewise resigned (RNZ Pacific, 29 October 2025). Both men were then prosecuted through the ordinary courts, and both were acquitted on "no case to answer" rulings — Kamikamica on 3 August 2026, Prasad on 11 November 2026 — after which Rabuka restored each to Cabinet within days (MaiTV Fiji, August and November 2026). The charges against his own deputies were ALLEGATIONs, are recorded here as such, and ended in acquittals; what Test 1 grades is that the process ran against the prime minister's own camp at full strength and was allowed to finish in court.
  • The same courts convicted his predecessor and acquitted him, in sequence, on the evidence. Frank Bainimarama was acquitted at first instance in October 2023, convicted on appeal by the High Court on 14 March 2024 of attempting to pervert the course of justice, and jailed for a year on 9 May 2024; convicted again on 2 October 2025 of making an unwarranted demand with menace — the first conviction under that provision — and given a 12-month suspended sentence; his co-accused, former police commissioner Sitiveni Qiliho, was acquitted in the second case (High Court of Fiji; ODPP statements, 2024–2025). In June 2026 the Chief Justice acquitted former Attorney-General Aiyaz Sayed-Khaiyum and former Supervisor of Elections Mohammed Saneem, finding the prosecution rested on a fundamental misreading of an employment contract; the state has appealed (ODPP media update, 30 June 2026). A system that convicts the former prime minister twice, acquits his police chief once, and acquits his attorney-general outright is not running one law for the government's friends — the verdicts point in every direction, which is the outcome pattern of a single standard.
  • Discipline inside his own camp was applied on documented process, not only by prosecutors. Education Minister Aseri Radrodro (SODELPA) was dismissed in January 2024 after three written directives from Rabuka and Solicitor-General advice to reverse an unlawful removal of Fiji National University council members went unheeded; the dismissal letters and the legal advice were published with the decision (Prime Minister's statements, 19–22 January 2024). Minister for Women Lynda Tabuya was removed from Cabinet on 26 December 2024 over a leaked private video — a matter police treated as an invasion of her privacy — on stated conduct grounds, and returned to Cabinet as Minister for Information in August 2026 after police identified suspects in the leak (RNZ Pacific, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The tenure's central legal episode began with a compromised appointment and ended with the government's own instruments declared unlawful. Barbara Malimali was appointed FICAC Commissioner on 4 September 2024 on the recommendation of the Judicial Services Commission, at a time when FICAC itself was investigating a complaint against her and when an earlier professional incident in Tuvalu bore on her fitness — circumstances the High Court later held were relevant to her suitability and should have been disclosed to the Commission (High Court of Fiji, judgment of August 2026, as reported by MaiTV Fiji). She was arrested by FICAC officers on her first day in the job. Rabuka's government established a Commission of Inquiry (COI) under retired Australian judge David Ashton-Lewis in October 2024; the COI's report of May 2025 made grave findings against nine named people, including that some had lied under oath or obstructed justice — findings those named always disputed. In August 2026 the High Court declared the report null and void in its entirety: the inquiry had legally ceased to exist on 31 January 2025 when its authorised term expired, the President had no power to extend it, persons facing adverse findings were denied natural justice, and the inquiry was affected by apparent bias (High Court of Fiji, Justice Tuiqereqere, August 2026). The adverse findings that cost an attorney-general his office and shadowed the Chief Justice, two former law society presidents, and a deputy prime minister were made, in law, by a body that did not exist when it made them.
  • The removal of the commissioner was itself unlawful. Malimali was suspended by the President on 29 May 2025 on the COI's recommendation and her appointment was revoked on 2 June 2025 on Rabuka's advice; Attorney-General Graham Leung was dismissed on 30 May 2025. On 2 February 2026 the High Court declared the revocation "unlawful and ultra vires": under the FICAC Act only the Judicial Services Commission may advise the President on the commissioner's appointment or removal, the Prime Minister had no such power, and the State's argument that the Commission was "constitutionally paralysed" failed — it has five members and needs three for a quorum (High Court of Fiji, Justice Tuiqereqere, 2 February 2026). The court declined to order reinstatement and referred her status to the Commission. Police files referred from the COI were subsequently closed by prosecutors for insufficient evidence, and the government entered a confidential settlement with the Chief Justice — one of the officials the voided report had accused — rather than pursue its findings (Fiji media reporting, 2026). The law at the source ran one way for the government's appointee when she became inconvenient, and the courts had to say so twice.
  • The pattern of cases that collapse is itself evidence, and it is contested in both directions. The Prasad prosecution concerned a declaration filed in 2015; FICAC under Malimali had closed the file, and FICAC under her successor reopened it and charged the sitting finance minister a decade after the fact, only for the magistrate to hold that a party leader is not an "office holder" under the Act and there was no case to answer (Suva Magistrates Court, 11 November 2026). The DPP file shows the same instability from the other side: Christopher Pryde, suspended in April 2023, was exonerated by a tribunal and reinstated in January 2025, then charged by FICAC within days on payment allegations overlapping matters the tribunal had dismissed; he never resumed duties, the President suspended his salary in July 2025 after Rabuka publicly criticised his absence, and in July 2026 FICAC dropped the charges by nolle prosequi because it could not serve him in New Zealand — the allegations undetermined, neither proven nor cleared (ODPP press release, 15 January 2025; RNZ Pacific, July 2026). Pryde's claim of a government conspiracy to remove him is recorded here as an ALLEGATION, which he maintains and the government side disputes. What is documented, and undisputed, is the sequence: tribunal clearance, immediate re-charge, two years of an acting DPP, and a prosecution abandoned unserved.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The media were ungripped by statute and stayed ungripped in practice. Parliament repealed the Media Industry Development Act 2010 — the Bainimarama-era law under which journalists faced imprisonment — on 6 April 2023, honouring a coalition commitment Rabuka described in terms of media freedom as the "oxygen of democracy" (Parliament of Fiji, April 2023; CIVICUS Monitor, 2023). Reporters Without Borders records the result across the whole tenure: Fiji rose from 102nd of 180 in 2022 to 89th in 2023, 44th in 2024, 40th in 2025, and 24th in the 2026 World Press Freedom Index — its highest placing ever, with a 10th-place ranking worldwide for journalist safety — and attributes the climb to the easing of pressure from civil and military authorities since Rabuka took office (RSF World Press Freedom Index, 2023–2026). In his first weeks he also reversed the previous government's politically motivated travel bans on the University of the South Pacific's vice-chancellor and on an academic critic (CIVICUS Monitor, 2023).
  • The courts ruled against him personally, repeatedly, and he answered through the courts rather than around them. The Supreme Court's advisory opinion of 29 August 2025 rejected the State's argument that the 2013 Constitution could be amended by simple majority, read down the amendment thresholds to two-thirds of Parliament plus a majority of votes cast in a referendum, and held the Chapter 10 coup immunities unamendable — and the government proceeded on the Court's terms, not its own (Supreme Court of Fiji, advisory opinion, 29 August 2025; International IDEA explainer, October 2025). The High Court then held his advice to remove the FICAC commissioner unlawful (February 2026) and voided his commission of inquiry (August 2026); no judge was removed, transferred, or publicly threatened in response — the government's instruments were appeals and a stay application, filed and argued in open court (MaiTV Fiji, September–November 2026). The Chief Justice whose institution the voided report had accused was dealt with by negotiated settlement, not by removal proceedings (Fiji media reporting, 2026).
  • The constitutional referees were left to run their own processes even when the outcomes cut against his camp's convenience. The Registrar of Political Parties deregistered FijiFirst on 1 July 2024 under section 12(4) of the Political Parties Act after the party failed to amend its constitution by the 28 June deadline — a decision taken against the largest party in Parliament on the statute's letter, after the party's founders had resigned and its internal dispute made compliance impossible; an appeal was withdrawn in early 2025 and the High Court later wound the party up (Registrar of Political Parties statement, 1 July 2024; MaiTV Fiji, 2025). The same Registrar later approved a successor party formed by former FijiFirst figures and rejected a rival application ("FijiansFirst") on name-similarity grounds after objections from the governing parties — the referee ruling for and against government interests in different files, on stated statutory tests (Fiji media reporting, 2025–2026). The tribunal process for the DPP was likewise honoured in form: when the tribunal exonerated Pryde, the President reinstated him on its recommendation, and Rabuka confirmed the return publicly even while noting the fresh complaint against him (Office of the President; Prime Minister's statements, January 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The hand on the judiciary's own house is heavier than the standard allows. On 24 December 2024 — with the Commission of Inquiry into the Malimali appointment still sitting, and one day after the Pryde tribunal's exoneration of the DPP was delivered — Rabuka advised the President to confirm Salesi Temo as substantive Chief Justice. Temo, acting chief justice since January 2023, chairs the Judicial Services Commission that had recommended Malimali, had been a central figure in the pursuit of Pryde, and would within a year be named adversely in the COI report and acquit the former attorney-general in the High Court. The appointment was constitutionally regular and Temo's judicial experience runs to three decades; the timing and the accumulation of roles — chief justice, commission chair, and recurring actor in the tenure's contested files — were criticised at the time and are recorded here as the documented criticism (Fiji Government, December 2024; Fiji media criticism, December 2024). The February 2026 judgment then recorded the institutional cost of the arrangement: the executive had tried to bypass the Commission altogether on the ground that it was paralysed, a claim the court examined and rejected.
  • The executive reached for the electoral machinery at the tenure's most sensitive point. On 5 October 2026, announcing the constitutional referendum for 9 December 2026, Rabuka stated that he had written to the Electoral Commission "to suspend all general election campaigning until after the return of the Writ for the Referendum" (Prime Minister's national address, 5 October 2026). The referendum is his government's project; the general election writ falls due by 24 December 2026 and the election by 6 February 2027. Whatever its administrative rationale, an incumbent prime minister asking the electoral referee to pause his opponents' campaigning during his own referendum is a grip on the board, and it was made by letter, by him, on the record. In the same period his government postponed the local-government elections — not held since 2005 — until after the general election, citing fiscal constraints (Parliament of Fiji reporting, 2026).
  • Power also concentrated in his own hands in the ordinary way. Cabinet listings through the tenure show Rabuka personally holding, alongside the premiership, the portfolios for Foreign Affairs, Climate Change and Environment, Civil Service, and Public Enterprises, holding Information as well until August 2026, and absorbing his resigned deputies' portfolios (Trade in October 2025) rather than redistributing them (Cabinet of Fiji listings, 2025–2026). The prosecution service, meanwhile, has been led by an acting Director of Public Prosecutions since April 2023 — the substantive holder reinstated but never resumed, his salary suspended by the President in July 2025 — an arrangement that leaves one of the state's referees in a acting, and therefore weaker, posture for effectively the whole term (ODPP; RNZ Pacific, July 2025). No documented interference with the Reserve Bank appears in the record assembled here; monetary settings were continuous across the tenure (Reserve Bank of Fiji policy rate unchanged at 0.25% since 2020, per its October 2026 release) — recorded as an absence of grip evidence, not as a positive act.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His largest parliamentary defeat was owned in public, in terms. When the Constitution (Amendment) Bill failed at second reading in March 2025 — 40 votes in favour, 14 against, one abstention, a single vote short of the 41 then required — Rabuka admitted the government had miscalculated its support, and rather than force the question he took it to the Supreme Court by cabinet reference and accepted the answer the Court gave in August 2025, including the parts that rejected his government's own argument (Parliament of Fiji, March 2025; Supreme Court advisory opinion, 29 August 2025). The process that followed — a Constitution Review Commission (established March 2026), nationwide consultation, a report tabled in Parliament on 22 September 2026 — was the adjustment, conducted in the open (Constitution Review Commission report, September 2026).
  • The adjustment continued under pressure in the tenure's final months. The third reading of the Constitution (Amendment) Bill 2026 was initially scheduled for 2 November 2026; Parliament postponed it to 30 November–4 December to allow three further weeks of public consultation after coalition partners and civil society objected to the pace (Parliament of Fiji, November 2026). When his own acquitted deputy Biman Prasad publicly conditioned his return to Cabinet on deferring the constitutional bill until after the election, the condition was aired openly rather than suppressed (Fiji media reporting, November 2026). And when the High Court voided the COI report in August 2026, Rabuka's public position was that the report's findings "would ultimately be tested by the courts whenever criminal prosecutions were brought forward" and that FICAC "continued to operate under the Constitution" — a defence of the institution, delivered as his two deputies were being cleared by it (MaiTV Fiji, August 2026).
  • The founding wobble of the tenure was answered constitutionally. In December 2022, facing a results-app glitch and a count he distrusted, Rabuka asked the military to intervene; the RFMF commander refused, and Rabuka accepted the refusal, took his majority of one in Parliament, and has governed for four years without the military leaving its barracks — including through the February 2026 arrest of Bainimarama and Qiliho on incitement-to-mutiny charges arising from a 2023 meeting with senior officers, a prosecution conducted by the ODPP in court, to which both men pleaded not guilty on 19 March 2026 (ODPP; MaiTV Fiji, March 2026). The charges are ALLEGATIONs; what Test 3 records is that the state's answer to an alleged military conspiracy ran through a courtroom.
  • On the street's clearest economic pushback he eventually moved, twice. The May 2024 parliamentary pay determination (Test 5) drew sustained public anger; Rabuka and some Cabinet colleagues declined the increase personally, and in April 2026, amid the fuel crisis, he himself moved the motion by which Parliament unanimously cut all MPs' salaries by 20% for the duration of the crisis — his own pay from $320,000 to $256,000 (Parliament of Fiji, 30 April 2026; PINA, June 2026). Explanation first, partial adjustment later: the shelf records both halves.

Evidence against:

  • The Malimali file shows the limit of the adjustment: he complies, then litigates the compliance away. The February 2026 ruling that his removal advice was unlawful was initially accepted — the government announced it would not appeal, and the Judicial Services Commission was to decide Malimali's status by 31 March (Fiji Council of Social Services statement and government announcements, February–March 2026). By September 2026 the Attorney-General's office had joined Rabuka's application to stay that same ruling pending his substantive appeal, with the matter adjourned to 7 December 2026 for mention (MaiTV Fiji, September–November 2026). His lawyers likewise appealed parts of the August 2026 COI judgment, disputing the finding that the Executive owed procedural fairness before publishing a report the court held was made without natural justice — while publicly maintaining the inquiry had "served a useful purpose" (MaiTV Fiji, 2026). Accepting a judgment in February and appealing it by September is not defiance; it is also not the open adjustment the first response promised, and both halves are on the record.
  • On the referendum timetable he has extended the consultation without yielding the substance. Six civil society organisations criticised the government for running a constitutional replacement into the election deadline and warned it would complicate holding the election on time; the NFP — his own coalition partner — says the process is being rushed and should be deferred to the next government; SODELPA has set conditions including complete removal of the immunity clause (MaiTV Fiji, November 2026; Wikipedia summary of the referendum process, November 2026). The government's answer, through Justice Minister Filimoni Vosarogo, is that it still needs 35 votes for the two-thirds threshold at the third reading and remains "positive about securing the numbers," while floating a combined referendum-and-election to save money. The third reading and the 9 December referendum date stand as announced. A prime minister is entitled to press his program; the test asks whether pushback changed the course, and on this file, at grading, it has changed the calendar by three weeks and nothing else.
  • The campaigning suspension request (Test 2) belongs on this shelf too: faced with the wobble of a referendum and an election colliding, the adjustment he chose was to ask that election campaigning pause — the board adjusted to the plan, not the plan to the board (Prime Minister's national address, 5 October 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the iTaukei, the Indo-Fijian community, the smaller minorities, the opposition's voters, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The coalition itself is the count, made structural. The man who led the 1987 coups against an Indo-Fijian-backed government has governed for four years in coalition with the Indo-Fijian-led National Federation Party, whose leader held the finance portfolio and a deputy premiership for most of the term — fiscal policy for the whole country run by the community his younger self counted out (Cabinet of Fiji, 2022–2026). The reconciliation machinery was built, not just announced: the Great Council of Chiefs, abolished by his predecessor in 2012, was reconvened in May 2023 — restored as an institution without its former constitutional veto; a national Girmit Day holiday commemorating the indentured Indian labourers was instituted in 2023; and a Truth and Reconciliation Commission was legislated in December 2024 and has taken public testimony through 2025–2026, including from chiefs, priests, and soldiers critical of the institutions that backed the coups (Parliament of Fiji; Fiji Truth and Reconciliation Commission hearings, 2025–2026).
  • He submitted himself to that accounting personally. At a Methodist Church reconciliation service in May 2023, Rabuka apologised to the Indo-Fijian community; at the military's Reconciliation and Restoration ceremony he told officers, "I bear the blame and I seek forgiveness from you all"; and appearing before his own Truth and Reconciliation Commission he testified of 1987: "They can all say that I opened the door. For which I accept responsibility" — while also naming the chiefs' council and church leaders who encouraged the takeover, a distribution of blame the Commission heard contested in other testimony (IDN reporting, May 2023; PINA, 1 December 2025). The Constitution Review Commission his government appointed recommended in September 2026 the repeal of Chapter 10 of the Constitution — the absolute immunity that covers the coup participants of 1987, 2000, and 2006, including Rabuka himself — and he has let that recommendation sit in Parliament in his own name's shadow rather than suppress it; the Supreme Court has held the immunity unamendable by ordinary amendment, so the recommendation's path runs through the referendum his government has called (Constitution Review Commission report, 22 September 2026; Supreme Court advisory opinion, August 2025).
  • The material count moved toward the bottom of the table. The national minimum wage rose in steps from $3.67 to $5.00 an hour across 2024–2025 (Employment Relations (National Minimum Wage) Regulations). The 2025–2026 budget cut VAT from 15% to 12.5% from 1 August 2025 while keeping zero-rating on 22 essential items, raised social welfare allowances by 5%, funded back-to-school assistance reaching more than 200,000 students, and subsidised bus fares and utilities for low-income households (Ministry of Finance, Citizens' Budget Guide 2025–2026; Value Added Tax (Budget Amendment) Act 2025). The constitutional question itself is being put to all registered voters in a referendum rather than settled by Parliament alone — the largest act of counting in the tenure, if it proceeds (National Referendum Act 2026, assented 2 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The fiscal adjustment was distributed downward first. His government's first budget, in July 2023, raised VAT from 9% to 15% — a consumption tax whose burden falls hardest on the poor — in the teeth of a campaign fought on the cost of living; the rate stood at 15% for two years before the 2025 cut to 12.5%, which remains above the 9% he inherited. The World Bank's October 2026 assessment records that the cut "provided broad-based relief but, because higher-income households account for a larger share of consumption, delivered a disproportionate share of its benefit to better off households at a significant fiscal cost," and that the 2025 measures widened the deficit and put public debt — reduced from about 95% of GDP in 2021 to 77% in 2025 — back on an upward path (World Bank, Fiji Economic Update, October 2026). The poor were counted in the rhetoric of every budget and paid first in the arithmetic of the first one.
  • The largest bloc of voters in the country has had no party for two years. FijiFirst won 42.55% of the vote in 2022; it was deregistered in July 2024 after its imploding leadership failed a compliance deadline, and when its supporters sought to register a successor under a similar name the application was refused. The deregistration was the Registrar's act on the statute's letter, and the party's collapse was substantially self-inflicted — both stated on the for-shelf of Test 2. What Test 4 counts is the effect: the voters of the largest party have been represented by independents and fragments (a bloc of former FijiFirst MPs now supports the government as the "G11"; others formed successor parties in 2025), and where their 2022 votes go has not been tested at a national poll (Fijian Elections Office results, 2022; Fiji media reporting, 2024–2026). A count in which the plurality's vehicle disappears between elections is a count with a hole in it, whoever dug it.
  • Two electorates remain uncounted entirely. Municipal elections, last held in 2005, were postponed by his government until after the general election — town and city voters will have gone more than two decades without a local vote, under a government that came to office promising democratic restoration (Parliament of Fiji reporting, 2026). And the founding exclusion stands in the record as context that no apology removes: the 1987 coups he led drove an exodus of Indo-Fijian professionals and reduced that community's share of the population to roughly a third; the repair documented on the for-shelf is real, and so is the original subtraction it answers. The dossier grades the repair; it does not grade the subtraction away.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign and program pledges vs. the record since 24 December 2022, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Media freedom — kept, early and completely. The coalition promised to repeal the repressive media law; Parliament repealed the Media Industry Development Act on 6 April 2023, inside the government's first four months, and the RSF index climbed for four consecutive years thereafter (Parliament of Fiji, April 2023; RSF, 2023–2026). The companion promise to reverse the previous government's travel bans on its academic critics was honoured in his first weeks in office (CIVICUS Monitor, 2023).
  • The chiefs and the truth commission — kept. The Great Council of Chiefs, whose restoration he promised, reconvened in May 2023. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission he promised was legislated in December 2024 and has sat through 2026, hearing testimony against the coup-era institutions — and against himself, in person (Parliament of Fiji; FTRC hearings, 2025–2026).
  • The constitution — the promise was a process, and the process has run. The 2022 coalition presented review of the imposed 2013 Constitution as a key promise. The documented sequence: amendment bill introduced and lost, March 2025; Supreme Court reference answered, 29 August 2025; Constitution Review Commission established March 2026, reporting 22 September 2026; a special parliamentary committee consulted nationwide; the amendment bill passed its second reading on 5 October 2026; the National Referendum Act assented 2 October 2026 and a referendum announced for 9 December 2026 (Parliament of Fiji; Fiji Government national address, 5 October 2026). Four years is slower than the promise sounded, and the outcome is not yet delivered — the third reading (30 November–4 December 2026) and the referendum both fall after this grading. The word is graded as credibly in motion, not kept.
  • No more coups — kept. He promised democratic stability after 2022; the military refused his own request for intervention in December 2022, stayed in barracks through two changes of president, a deregistered governing-party-turned-opposition, and the arrest of a former prime minister on mutiny-incitement charges, and the state's disputes ran through courts and Parliament (record assembled across Tests 2–3).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The cost of living — the first budget broke the campaign's central economic word. The coalition campaigned against FijiFirst on the cost of living; its first budget raised VAT from 9% to 15% in July 2023. The partial reversal to 12.5% came two years later, in August 2025, and the rate at grading remains 3.5 points above the one he inherited (National Budgets 2023–2024 and 2025–2026; VAT (Budget Amendment) Act 2025). A promise of relief delivered as a tax rise, then half-undone, is the clearest broken word in the file.
  • Parliamentarians' pay — the institution's word broken, his own kept, the repair partial. His party campaigned against the previous era's perks, including a pledge to cut the $3,000-a-day overseas travel allowance. On 24 May 2024 Parliament — his party in favour, his NFP partner opposed, himself reportedly threatening action against the NFP for breaking ranks — approved the Emoluments Committee's determination 40 votes to 7 with 5 abstentions: MPs' base pay rose from $50,000 to $95,000, ministers' to $200,000, the prime minister's to $320,000, with tax-free vehicle and allowance benefits restored (Parliament of Fiji, 24 May 2024; Lowy Institute analysis, 31 May 2024). Rabuka personally declined the increase, as did some Cabinet colleagues — his own word kept in his own case. The 20% crisis cut of April 2026 (Test 3) still leaves MPs paid well above the pre-2024 level the campaign had implicitly promised to hold down.
  • The election timetable — the word is now the calendar's, not his. The coalition's democratic-restoration program included holding the local elections Fiji has awaited since 2005; in 2026 his government postponed them until after the general election (Parliament of Fiji reporting, 2026). The general election itself has not been called at grading: the writ falls due by 24 December 2026 — the fourth anniversary of his taking office — and the poll by 6 February 2027. Not yet broken; entirely unredeemed, and compressed by his own decision to run a constitutional referendum into the same window (Prime Minister's national address, 5 October 2026).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Sitiveni Rabuka is the only leader in this series who is graded, in part, on whether a coup-maker can become a counter of everyone — and the file is genuinely two-sided at a scale few dossiers match. The for-shelves are structural, not cosmetic: a media law repealed and a press-freedom ranking that climbed 78 places in four years; a coalition in which the community his 1987 coups targeted held the finance ministry; a truth commission before which he testified that he "opened the door" and accepted responsibility; courts that convicted his predecessor twice, acquitted his officials and his enemies in turn, ruled against him personally twice in seven months, and were answered with appeals rather than defiance; and a constitutional question that — after he lost the vote, owned the loss, and asked the Supreme Court — is being put to the whole electorate. The against-shelves concentrate in one vortex and one habit. The vortex is Malimali: an anti-corruption commissioner appointed over an undisclosed fitness file, arrested on her first day, investigated by a commission that outlived its own legal existence, removed on prime-ministerial advice the High Court held unlawful and ultra vires, her case generating the charges that felled both his deputies and then collapsed in court — a two-year demonstration that bending the instrument at the source poisons everything the instrument touches, for his camp and against it alike. The habit is the reversal: VAT raised from 9% to 15% and then cut to 12.5%, MPs' pay nearly doubled and then cut by a fifth, a court ruling accepted in February and appealed by September — he adjusts, but characteristically only after the institution he pushed against pushes back through a court or a ballot, and the adjustment rarely reaches all the way back.
