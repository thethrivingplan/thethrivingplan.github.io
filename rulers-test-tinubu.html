The Ruler's Test — Bola Tinubu, President of Nigeria

Subject: Bola Ahmed Tinubu — President of Nigeria since 29 May 2023; previously Governor of Lagos State (1999–2007) and national leader of the All Progressives Congress (APC). He won the 25 February 2023 presidential election with 8.79 million votes (36.6%) in a three-way race, and his victory was affirmed by the Presidential Election Petition Court (6 September 2023) and the Supreme Court (26 October 2023). At grading he remains in office and is an active candidate for a second term: he formally launched his re-election campaign in Kaduna on 24 October 2026, ahead of the presidential election scheduled for 16 January 2027. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The EFCC under his appointee Ola Olukoyede prosecuted a senior figure of his own party and his own 2023 campaign organisation. Former Kogi State governor Yahaya Bello (APC) was charged with money laundering alleged at ₦80.2 billion, later amended to ₦110.4 billion; he was declared wanted, placed on an immigration watchlist, and put on trial, and in June 2026 an FCT High Court dismissed his application to halt the proceedings. Presidency sources stated on the record that Tinubu declined to intervene to stop the case (EFCC filings, 2024; Western Post reporting, October 2024). The alleged fraud is an ALLEGATION; Bello denies wrongdoing and no verdict has been reached.
  • He suspended a minister from his own cabinet and ordered her investigated. On 8 January 2024 Tinubu suspended the Minister of Humanitarian Affairs and Poverty Alleviation, Betta Edu — a prominent figure in his own campaign — after a leaked memo showed ₦585 million of public funds directed to a private bank account, and directed the EFCC to investigate "all aspects of the financial transactions" of her ministry. The EFCC questioned her and reported recovering over ₦30 billion in the wider ministry investigation (Presidency statement, 8 January 2024; EFCC statements, 2024). Edu denied wrongdoing; the ₦585 million transfer is documented, its criminality an ALLEGATION she disputes.
  • The EFCC also arrested a leading figure of the opposition: former Delta State governor Ifeanyi Okowa, the PDP's 2023 vice-presidential candidate, was taken into custody on 4 November 2024 over the alleged diversion of ₦1.3 trillion in derivation funds (EFCC statements, November 2024). The allegation is unproven; Okowa denies it, noting the sum would imply taking ₦16–20 billion monthly for eight years.
  • His own election was tested in court and he submitted to the process: the tribunal (6 September 2023) and a unanimous seven-justice Supreme Court panel (26 October 2023) dismissed the petitions of Atiku Abubakar (PDP) and Peter Obi (Labour Party) — on qualification, on the 25%-of-FCT question (the Court held the FCT counts as a 37th state), and on INEC's failure to transmit results to the IReV portal in real time, which the Court held did not affect the outcome. The Court refused Atiku's bid to introduce Chicago State University records as fresh evidence on forgery, holding the issue had not been pleaded at the tribunal. The opposition's position — that the election was flawed and the evidence was excluded on technicality — is printed here as their position; the judgments are the documented outcome.

Evidence against:

  • The two flagship cases above each stop short in a way that tracks political alignment. Edu has never been charged: more than two years after her suspension, the EFCC investigation has produced no public conclusion, and in September 2026 she returned to the front line as a director of women mobilisation in Tinubu's own 2027 campaign council — with civil-society groups (CISLAC, September 2026) publicly asking the EFCC and the Presidency to state what became of the probe. Okowa's case likewise produced no charges after his April 2025 defection to the APC (see Test 2); the EFCC's legal director stated in October 2025 that the case "is still undergoing investigation" and denied shielding — the denial is on the record, and so is the sequence.
  • His administration's largest infrastructure contract was awarded without competitive bidding to a company owned by a documented longtime associate. The Lagos–Calabar Coastal Highway (700 km) was awarded to Hitech Construction by direct procurement; Works Minister David Umahi confirmed on the record that the project "was not subjected to public advertisement or open competitive bidding," defending the method as lawful under procurement rules given Hitech's coastal-engineering record at Eko Atlantic, with a Bureau of Public Procurement certificate of no objection and Federal Executive Council approval (Umahi, ARISE News interview; BusinessDay). Hitech's founder, Gilbert Chagoury, is a Lebanese-Nigerian businessman whose association with Tinubu dates to Tinubu's Lagos governorship — a fact the government does not dispute. The opposition's charge — that the award was a favor to an ally, at a cost they put at up to ₦8 billion per kilometre against the minister's ₦4 billion — is an ALLEGATION of favoritism; the documented facts are the direct award, the association, and the government's legal defense. Umahi also acknowledged no complete design for the whole highway existed before procurement.
  • Criminal process reached downward against protesters in terms it did not reach upward. After the August 2024 #EndBadGovernance protests, police arraigned over 146 people, including minors, on charges including treasonable felony and incitement to mutiny — a capital framing applied to street protesters while no minister or former governor has been convicted of corruption in his term (police charges, August–November 2024; see Tests 3 and 4 for the full record, including his later order releasing the minors).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the anti-corruption commission, media, state governors, local governments, and the electoral commission keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His government sued the state governors — including governors of his own party — to free the tier of government below them, and won. In May 2024 his Attorney General filed suit SC/CV/343/2024 against all 36 states; on 11 July 2024 the Supreme Court held (6–1) that governors may not dissolve elected local councils, may not run them through caretaker committees, and may not retain local-government funds, ordering allocations for the 774 local governments paid directly to elected councils (Supreme Court judgment, 11 July 2024). It is the largest single loosening of executive grip in his term, and it was his own administration that sought it.
  • The Chief Justice succession followed the institutional track: on Olukayode Ariwoola's retirement at the mandatory age of 70 (22 August 2024), the National Judicial Council recommended the most senior justice, Kudirat Kekere-Ekun; Tinubu appointed her, the Senate confirmed her, and she was sworn in as substantive CJN in September 2024. No seniority bypass is documented.
  • The electoral commission changed hands at the end of a tenure, not by removal: Mahmood Yakubu completed his two terms as INEC chairman (2015–2025); Tinubu's nominee, law professor Joash Amupitan, was endorsed by the National Council of State, confirmed by the Senate on 16 October 2025, and sworn in on 23 October 2025 with a public presidential charge to protect the process's integrity. The 2027 election will be the test of the appointment; at grading, the appointment process itself followed the constitutional path.
  • The Rivers emergency (below) ended on the schedule he had announced: declared for six months on 18 March 2025, it was lifted effective 17–18 September 2025 and Governor Fubara, his deputy, and the Assembly resumed office — and in December 2025 the Supreme Court upheld the President's power to declare the emergency and suspend officials by 6–1 (see against-shelf for the dissent and the criticism).

Evidence against:

  • The Rivers State emergency is the strongest grip fact of his presidency. On 18 March 2025, invoking Section 305 of the Constitution amid the feud between Governor Siminalayi Fubara (PDP) and a pro-Wike Assembly faction, Tinubu suspended an elected governor, his deputy, and an entire elected state legislature for six months and installed a retired naval chief, Ibok-Ete Ibas, as sole administrator of an oil-producing state. The feud's other principal, Nyesom Wike — Fubara's predecessor and the Assembly faction's patron — sits in Tinubu's cabinet as FCT Minister and was not touched. The Supreme Court's December 2025 ruling upheld the suspension power 6–1; the dissent of Justice Obande Ogbuinya states the opposing documented position: the President "cannot use such power as a tool to suspend elected state officials, including governors." Over 40 lawsuits were filed against the proclamation. The restoration happened on time (for-shelf), but the precedent — an elected state government removed by proclamation during a political dispute in which the President's own minister was a party — now stands affirmed.
  • A documented pattern of security-agency action against journalists runs through his term. The Committee to Protect Journalists documented at least 91 journalists arrested, attacked, or harassed between May 2023 and May 2026, and the CJID Press Attack Tracker verified at least 231 attacks. Named cases: FIJ reporter Daniel Ojukwu, taken by police on 1 May 2024 and held ten days over an investigation into ₦147.1 million paid by the President's SDG office to a restaurant for classroom construction — and in November 2026 arraigned on 12 counts including defaming the same presidential aide and remanded to Kuje prison pending a bail hearing; FirstNews editor Segun Olatunji, taken from his home in March 2024 and held two weeks by the Defence Intelligence Agency over reporting on the Chief of Defence Intelligence; four journalists charged under the Cybercrimes Act in September 2024 over reporting on a bank CEO, and made to take the reports down; at least 56 journalists assaulted or harassed while covering the August 2024 protests (CPJ; FIJ; IPI). The government's documented position is denial at the top: in February 2026 Vice President Shettima told the Nigerian Press Council, "For the past three years, have you heard of any harassment of journalists?" — a claim CPJ and Nigerian press bodies answered with the documented counts, and a coalition letter to Tinubu in April 2026 went unanswered (CPJ, May 2026). No outlet has been formally shuttered by decree; the pressure documented is by arrest, charge, and raid rather than by closure.
  • The opposition's institutional space contracted sharply through defections he welcomed and his party organised. Between 2025 and early 2026, at least five sitting opposition governors — Umo Eno (Akwa Ibom), Sheriff Oborevwori (Delta), Peter Mbah (Enugu), Douye Diri (Bayelsa), Abba Yusuf (Kano) — defected to the APC, alongside Fubara after his restoration; more than 140 elected officials have crossed since May 2023, bringing APC control to roughly 24–26 of 36 states. The opposition's characterization — that governors are "persuaded" by inducement and fear, including fear of EFCC prosecution (former governor Nasir El-Rufai, January 2026; PDP and ADC statements) — is an ALLEGATION as to motive, contested by the APC, which states the defections are voluntary and reflect confidence in Tinubu's leadership; defecting governors cite their own parties' internal crises, which are also documented. What is not contested is the outcome: the legislature and the state map are more concentrated in the President's party than at any point in his term, and the APC leadership publicly anticipated further governors joining.
  • Central bank: he replaced the central bank governor early in his term (Godwin Emefiele was suspended in June 2023 and prosecuted — charges documented, contested by Emefiele, cases ongoing) and appointed Yemi Cardoso, under whom the bank raised the policy rate from 18.75% to 27.5% by February 2025 in an orthodox tightening the government publicly backed rather than overruled. The appointment is graded here as an early removal of a predecessor's man — a standard transition act with a prosecution attached — not as documented interference in rate decisions, of which none is on the record reviewed.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, governors, unions, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He reversed the protest prosecutions' worst element personally. After malnourished minors were arraigned for treason in Abuja on 1 November 2024 — three defendants collapsed in court — public outcry followed, and on 4–5 November 2024 Tinubu ordered the immediate release of all detained minors, directed the Attorney General to discontinue the charges, ordered the humanitarian ministry to reunite them with their families, and announced a committee to investigate the officials involved in the arrests and prosecutions. The Federal High Court discharged the group, and over 114 detainees were handed to the Kano and Kaduna state governments (Presidency statements, November 2024; Premium Times).
  • He settled with organised labour rather than legislating over it: after months of negotiation and strike threats, he agreed a new national minimum wage of ₦70,000 with the NLC and TUC on 18 July 2024 — more than double the ₦30,000 rate — signed it into law on 29 July 2024, and shortened the review cycle from five years to three.
  • He accepted adverse arithmetic from his own reform program in public: his government rebased and published inflation figures showing the December 2024 peak of 34.8%, and the IMF's August 2026 Article IV review — welcomed publicly by his finance ministry — carried the Fund's own sentence that "conditions for many Nigerians remain difficult, with poverty sitting at 63 percent on the national line." The figure was not suppressed.
  • On Rivers, having taken the maximal step, he took the off-ramp he had announced: mediation produced a June 2025 settlement between Fubara and Wike, and the emergency was lifted on its six-month schedule rather than extended (17 September 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The first answer to the August 2024 protests was force and treason charges. Amnesty International documented at least 24 people killed — including two children — and over 1,200 detained during the 1–10 August 2024 #EndBadGovernance protests, with live ammunition used in Kano, Jigawa, Katsina, Kaduna, Maiduguri, and Niger states; the Nigeria Police Force denied excessive force and resisted calls for investigation (Amnesty International, "Bloody August," November 2024). In his 4 August 2024 broadcast Tinubu offered dialogue but framed the protests as politically motivated and warned against them; he did not concede any policy change. The minors' release (for-shelf) came three months later, after children had been held some 68–90 days and collapsed in his own capital's courtroom.
  • On the two policies that caused the wobble — subsidy removal and the naira float — he has never adjusted the policy, only the cushioning, and the cushioning lagged the harm (Test 4). His public framing attributes the hardship substantially to inherited conditions and, at his October 2026 campaign launch, to the opposition: AP reported him blaming opposition parties for the economic anger at the rally opening his re-election bid.
  • The Rivers wobble was answered by removing the wobblers: a dispute between a governor and an assembly — of a kind Nigeria's courts were actively adjudicating, with the Supreme Court ruling on the Assembly's composition in February 2025 — was settled not by the political process but by suspending both elected arms (Test 2). Adjustment came only after the removal had done its work.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the poor hit by subsidy removal, the southeast, opposition voters, protesters, and journalists — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The flagship compensation was real money, broadly aimed. The Renewed Hope conditional cash transfer (₦25,000 monthly for three months) was targeted at 15 million poor households from the National Social Register; the government's own January 2026 factsheet records 9,178,837 households receiving at least one tranche, 7.2 million a second, and 6.5 million a third; the Finance Minister put total disbursement above ₦330 billion by September 2025 and the Humanitarian Minister at over ₦600 billion by August 2026. State allocations from the federation account rose sharply after subsidy removal — FAAC distributions roughly tripled in nominal terms by 2026 — flowing to all states including opposition-governed ones.
  • Students were counted in at scale: the NELFUND student-loan scheme he signed into law disbursed over ₦140 billion to nearly 789,000 students by late 2025 (NELFUND figures), with no partisan or regional test documented.
  • The southeast received institutional recognition: in July 2024 he signed the South East Development Commission into law, and in November 2026 his government withdrew its cross-appeal seeking the death penalty for Nnamdi Kanu (below), with southeast governors reporting after an October 2025 State House meeting that the President was "favourably disposed" to a political resolution of Kanu's case.
  • Local communities were counted in against the governors: the July 2024 local-government autonomy judgment (Test 2) directs federal allocations to 774 elected councils rather than through state governments — a transfer of resources toward the most local tier, benefiting citizens of opposition states equally.

Evidence against:

  • The poor paid for the reforms first and were compensated last, partially, and thinly. Petrol rose from ~₦190 to over ₦1,000 per litre within his term; the naira lost roughly two-thirds of its value; inflation peaked at 34.8% in December 2024. The World Bank warned in 2023 that subsidy removal without adequate compensation could push 7.1 million more Nigerians into poverty; by its October 2025 estimate some 139 million Nigerians were poor, and the IMF's August 2026 review put poverty at 63% on the national line. Against that: the cash transfer reached roughly 9–10 million of its 15 million target households (IMF, May 2026 Article IV: 9.2 million enrolled, each receiving at most three ₦25,000 transfers since 2023), and the World Bank's November 2025 safety-nets report found only 44% of total benefits from government safety-net programmes actually reach poor Nigerians, with all social protection combined reducing the poverty headcount by just 0.4 percentage points. The President's own public claims of "15 million households reached" (July 2026) exceed his own humanitarian minister's figure of slightly over 10 million (August 2026); the discrepancy is on the record.
  • Protesters were counted as enemies of the state. At least 24 killed and over 1,200 detained in August 2024 (Amnesty International); over 146 arraigned including minors on treasonable-felony charges carrying a mutiny-incitement count alleging they urged the military to remove the President — for a cost-of-living protest. The minors were released on his order after three months (Test 3); the adult cases and the killings have produced no documented prosecution of any security officer, and the police denied the force used.
  • Journalists are counted out in practice (Test 2): at least 91 arrested, attacked, or harassed in three years by CPJ's count — including a reporter imprisoned in November 2026 over an investigation into the President's own SDG office — while the Vice President denied on the record that any harassment had occurred at all.
  • The southeast's central case ended in a life sentence its leaders call a double standard. On 20 November 2025 a Federal High Court convicted IPOB leader Nnamdi Kanu on all seven terrorism counts and sentenced him to life imprisonment, the judge holding his broadcasts incited deadly attacks; Kanu rejected the court's authority and his conviction is on appeal. Southeast leaders — including a former spokesman for Tinubu himself — publicly contrasted Kanu's treatment with federal rehabilitation programmes for repentant Boko Haram fighters in the north; the government's withdrawal of its death-penalty cross-appeal in 2026 (for-shelf) left the life sentence intact and no political release followed, despite the reported presidential disposition toward one. The grievance is documented; so is the court's judgment; the count of the region in the federation's councils remains, on its leaders' documented account, unmade.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises and stated positions vs. the record since May 2023, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Subsidy removal — kept, on day one, exactly as campaigned. He promised in the 2023 campaign to remove the petrol subsidy; his inaugural address on 29 May 2023 declared "subsidy is gone," and the removal was never reversed — including through the October 2026 NNPC measure in which the state oil company sold petrol at cost for 30 days, which the government expressly stated was not a subsidy restoration.
  • Exchange-rate unification — kept. The naira was floated and the multiple windows unified in June 2023, as pledged.
  • Minimum wage — kept. ₦30,000 → ₦70,000, agreed 18 July 2024 and signed 29 July 2024, with the review cycle shortened to three years.
  • Student loans — kept. NELFUND established and disbursing (₦140 billion+ to ~789,000 students by late 2025).
  • Tax reform — kept in substance. The four tax reform bills were signed on 26 June 2025, consolidating the tax code, exempting minimum-wage earners, and cutting company income tax — a manifesto commitment carried through the legislature against state-level resistance.
  • Local government autonomy — kept. A stated Tinubu position delivered through the July 2024 Supreme Court judgment his Attorney General sought (Test 2).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The palliative promise was scaled to the announcement, not to the harm. He promised the subsidy savings would be rechannelled to cushion the vulnerable — in his inaugural words, into "public infrastructure, education, health care and jobs." The compensation that followed reached roughly two-thirds of its own 15-million-household target with at most ₦75,000 per household across three years (Test 4), while poverty rose by every documented measure (World Bank: ~139 million poor, October 2025; IMF: 63% national poverty line, August 2026). The promise's direction was honored; its proportion was not.
  • Inflation and growth targets — missed. His economic programme targeted inflation trending to ~21% and growth rising toward 6%; the record is a 34.8% inflation peak (December 2024), easing to roughly 15% by late 2026 on the rebased series, and growth of roughly 3.4–4% — respectable, and well short of the stated trajectory.
  • Security — the promise of restored safety is unmet on the documented record. Mass kidnappings and insurgent attacks continued through his term, including the November 2025 abduction of schoolchildren in Niger State; at his October 2026 campaign launch his new manifesto again promised restoration of security — the opposition's documented rejoinder (Premium Times, November 2026) being that three and a half years in, the question is why it still lies ahead.
  • "Judge me by my record, not promises" — his own late-term standard. On 11 November 2026, speaking through the Senate President, Tinubu urged Nigerians to judge him by his record rather than campaign promises. Graded by that standard, the record above is what it is: the structural promises (subsidy, float, tax, wage, loans, local autonomy) were kept with unusual literalness; the welfare promises attached to them were not kept at the scale at which they were made.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Bola Tinubu has governed as the most programmatic president of Nigeria's Fourth Republic — the rare ruler whose manifesto items were executed on schedule and almost to the letter: the subsidy ended on his first day, the currency floated in his first month, the tax code rewritten, the minimum wage more than doubled, student loans flowing, and local governments freed from the governors by a lawsuit his own government brought. The same record shows where the law bends: the two corruption cases that touched his own circle — a suspended minister never charged and now campaigning for him, an arrested opposition governor never charged after defecting to his party — sit unresolved in a pattern the EFCC denies and the sequence documents; his signature highway went without competitive bidding to a company owned by his longtime associate; an elected state government was suspended by proclamation while his minister stood on the other side of its feud; and the compensation for the hardest economic shock in a generation reached a fraction of the poor it was promised to, while protesters who named the hardship were met with live ammunition and treason charges, and journalists who documented it were arrested and, at the top of his government, denied to exist. The front-lines question his file answers is precise: the institutions around him are alive — courts that ruled against governors at his asking, a commission that took his own party's governor to trial, a press that publishes all of this — but the accounting of who bears the cost, and who is counted when they object, is where his seat fails its test.
