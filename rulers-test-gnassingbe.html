The Ruler's Test — Faure Gnassingbé, President of the Council of Ministers of Togo

Subject: Faure Essozimna Gnassingbé — President of the Council of Ministers of Togo since 3 May 2025, and the holder of Togo's executive seat, under successive titles, since February 2005. The seat graded is the executive seat, and the reason is stated plainly: under the constitution promulgated on 6 May 2024 (the Fifth Republic), the presidency of the Republic became a largely ceremonial office, elected by Parliament for a four-year term renewable once, while executive power — the government, the administration, and command of the armed forces — moved to the President of the Council of Ministers, an office filled by the leader of the majority party in the National Assembly, serving six-year terms with no term limit, removable only by a three-quarters majority of the Assembly. Gnassingbé, whose fourth presidential term would have expired in 2025 under the previous constitution, took the new office on 3 May 2025; the ceremonial presidency passed to Jean-Lucien Savi de Tové, a veteran opposition figure from his father's era, elected unanimously by Parliament in Congress the same day. Gnassingbé is therefore graded on the whole holding, 2005–2026: army-installed successor to his father on 5 February 2005, stepped down on 25 February 2005 under regional sanctions, elected on 24 April 2005, re-elected in 2010, 2015, and 2020, and translated in May 2025 into the new unlimited office. His father, Gnassingbé Eyadéma, seized power in the coup of January 1967 and ruled 38 years until his death in office; the family's hold on the seat is in its 59th year. The father's record is context, not the grade — but the structures the son inherited, kept, and rebuilt are the son's record. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic printed on the Test card governs the grading: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one; the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier names the options that actually existed, records the constraint rather than a demerit, and grades only what remains his: whether he built the trap, whether he was honest inside it, and whether he took the least-harm door actually open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The state's social machinery, where it runs, runs by published rule rather than by party card. The Novissi programme — a fully digital cash-transfer scheme built during COVID-19 that paid hundreds of thousands of informal workers 30–35% of the minimum wage by mobile money, targeted by data rather than by affiliation — was documented and praised internationally as a model of rule-based delivery (UN DESA compendium of digital government practices). Free public primary schooling dates from 2008, and universal health insurance (AMU) took effect on 1 January 2024 under published eligibility rules (Togolese government announcements, January 2024).
  • The law has, at least once, reached into his own house. His half-brother Kpatcha Gnassingbé, a former defence minister, was arrested in 2009 and sentenced in 2011 to 20 years for an alleged coup plot — the charges are recorded here as ALLEGATIONs that Kpatcha Gnassingbé has always denied, and rights groups criticized the trial; he served some 14 years before a presidential pardon on health grounds freed him in March 2023 (contemporaneous reporting, 2009–2023). A seat that lets its own brother sit in prison for over a decade is not, on that file, running two laws.
  • Clemency, when it came, crossed the line too. The measure announced on 3 December 2025 and decreed on 30 December 2025 covered 1,511 detainees and included at least nine known activists and dissenting voices — members of the Tournons La Page movement arrested in August 2025 among them — alongside ordinary prisoners (MFWA Togo Watch, December 2025; Front Line Defenders, January 2026).
  • The long trend line on perceived corruption has moved slowly in the right direction under his governments: Togo's Corruption Perceptions Index score stands at 32 of 100 (CPI 2024 and CPI 2025), up from the high 20s a decade earlier (Transparency International).

Evidence against:

  • The deepest second law in this file is not an exemption from the law; it is the law itself, re-cut twice to fit one man. In May 2019 his parliament restored the two-term presidential limit — expressly without retroactive effect, resetting his count to zero after three terms already served (National Assembly vote, 8 May 2019). In March–April 2024, when the restored limit would finally have bound him, the same parliamentary majority abolished the directly elected presidency altogether and created an executive office with no term limit, which he took the following year. A rule that is rewritten each time it is about to apply to the ruler is not one law for all; it is a law with one person's name silently appended.
  • No completed accounting exists for the founding violence of his tenure. Between 400 and 500 people were killed in the February–April 2005 succession crisis on the UN fact-finding mission's count, which placed principal responsibility on the state security apparatus (OHCHR report, September 2005); Togo's own National Commission of Inquiry counted 154 dead and 654 injured; the Interior Ministry's early figure was 69; the Togolese League of Human Rights put the toll above 800 — the counts are printed separately, as the record gives them, and were never reconciled. The 2006 Global Political Agreement promised an end to impunity, and the Truth, Justice and Reconciliation Commission reported in 2012; two decades on, no senior figure has been prosecuted for the 2005 killings, and contemporaneous reporting already noted by 2006 that justice measures had produced "not a single concrete measure" (IRIN, April 2006).
  • The criminal law's documented weight falls overwhelmingly on one side of the map. The Irish-Togolese businessman Abdoul Aziz Goma was arrested on 21 December 2018 after sheltering student protesters, held incommunicado for nearly two years, found arbitrarily detained by the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention in September 2023, convicted in a one-day trial on 3 February 2025, and sentenced to ten years — a conviction the UN Special Rapporteur on human rights defenders described as based on humanitarian action, not crime (European Parliament resolution B10-0377/2025; OMCT, 2026). The singer-activist Aamron was arrested in the night of 26–27 May 2025 without summons or warrant after calling for mobilization, released on 21 June saying he had been forcibly admitted to a psychiatric hospital, and re-arrested on 19 September 2025 on charges including incitement to revolt — charges recorded here as ALLEGATIONs, untried at grading (Amnesty International, Togo 2025 report; International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, September 2025). A poet was arrested on 12 January 2025 after publishing a poem denouncing the government (Amnesty International, 2025). His 2020 challenger Agbéyomé Kodjo, who claimed victory, had his home surrounded, his parliamentary immunity lifted, and fled into exile, where he died in March 2024 (Reuters and AFP reporting, 2020–2024). No comparable file — a prosecution of a senior ruling-party figure for corruption or violence — appears in the record assembled here.
  • The June 2025 protest prosecutions ran the same direction: nineteen protesters were sentenced to a year in prison on 6 July 2025, and on 10 July the government announced international arrest warrants for exiled leaders of the June 6 Movement (M66), while no security-force member is documented as charged over the seven deaths the same weeks produced (International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, July 2025).

Why the grade lands here: One law is tested at the source — whether the rules bind the ruler and his side as they bind everyone else. Gnassingbé's Togo runs real rule-based machinery for ordinary life, and the clemency and the Kpatcha file are genuine. But the constitution itself has been re-cut twice at the exact moment it was about to bind him, the founding killings of his tenure have never met a courtroom, and the criminal law's senior targets are, on the documented record, critics, challengers, poets, and protesters. That is a second law, written into the first one.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — informal workers and the rural poor reached by rule-based transfers (Novissi); prisoners and activists freed by the December 2025 clemency, including Abdoul Aziz Goma (released 1 January 2026). Hurt — the families of the 2005 dead, still without a completed accounting; prosecuted and exiled opponents (Kodjo, Atchadam, Goma, Aamron, Gnakadé); every Togolese voter whose constitutional limit was reset or abolished before it could apply.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The referees are not wholly silent, and some of their sounds are on the record. Opposition parties hold five of 113 National Assembly seats and won communes in the municipal elections of 17 July 2025, which were held on schedule under the new system and passed peacefully (International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, July 2025). Togo hosted the ECOWAS Court of Justice's own stakeholder mission in November 2025, with the Court's president thanking the Togolese authorities for facilitating it (ECOWAS Court communiqué, 21 November 2025). Critical outlets continue to publish inside Togo, and the media regulator's suspensions documented below were time-limited rather than closures.
  • The February 2024–May 2025 institutional build-out was executed through the forms: a Senate was elected in February 2025 (two-thirds indirectly elected by local authorities), completing a second chamber that had existed on paper since 2002 (Britannica, Togo country entry, 2026).
  • In 2005, facing ECOWAS and African Union sanctions, he did step down from the presidency the army had handed him and submit to an election within the constitutional sixty days — the referees of that moment, regional rather than domestic, pushed, and he yielded (OHCHR report, September 2005; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2006).

Evidence against:

  • The electoral referee operates inside a frame his side built and his side staffs. The constitution that created his current office was adopted on 25 March 2024 by a National Assembly whose mandate had expired on 31 December 2023, with the text not made public before the vote, and adopted again on 19 April 2024 after consultations he ordered — without the referendum the opposition and civil society demanded (ISS Africa, 2024; International IDEA election note, 2024). In the 29 April 2024 legislative elections that seated the parliament of the new order, foreign journalists were barred from accreditation and the Catholic Church was prevented from deploying election observers; his UNIR party took 108 of 113 seats; the Constitutional Court rejected every opposition appeal (ISS Africa, 2024; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024; ARIES Togo Strategic Intelligence Report, 2026).
  • The new office is structurally beyond the referees' reach. The President of the Council of Ministers serves six-year terms without limit and can be removed only by a three-quarters majority of an Assembly his party holds 108 of 113 seats in; he also appoints one-third of the Senate (Constitution of 2024; Britannica, 2026; Wikipedia, Prime Minister of Togo, summarizing the 2024 constitution). A referee who can only act by a margin the office-holder's party structurally cannot lose is not a check; it is a decoration.
  • The media referee is used against the coverage, on his watch and by his government's appointees. On 16 June 2025 the media regulator (HAAC) suspended Radio France Internationale and France 24 for three months over "inaccurate and biased" coverage, in the middle of the June protest crisis (International IDEA Democracy Tracker, June 2025). On 6 June 2025 the gendarmerie arrested a TV5 Monde correspondent covering a protest and forced her to delete her footage (Amnesty International, 2025). Between late June and September 2025 internet access was disrupted, with social media particularly targeted (Amnesty International, 2025) — a repetition of the internet shutdowns used against the 2017–2018 protests.
  • The prosecution's independence fails its clearest documented test. The Goma file (Test 1) ran six years of pre-trial detention, a UN finding of arbitrariness, and a one-day trial producing a ten-year sentence — and when the European Parliament called for his release in September 2025, the foreign ministry summoned the EU ambassador and asserted that Togolese justice operates independently, a claim the documented file does not carry (European Parliament resolution, September 2025; International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, September 2025).
  • The regional referee ruled against the seat's founding act, and the seat had not even appeared to argue. In case ECW/CCJ/APP/15/24 (Ligue Togolaise des Droits de l'Homme and 12 others), the ECOWAS Court of Justice heard the matter in default — Togo did not appear — and by judgment of 29 January 2026 (ECW/CCJ/JUD/01/26, made public in June 2026) held that the constitutional amendment of 25 March 2024, passed by an assembly whose mandate had expired and abolishing direct presidential elections, "amounted to an unconstitutional change of government" contrary to Article 23 of the African Charter on Democracy, Elections and Governance, ordering Togo to ensure future reforms comply with regional democratic standards. The Court also dismissed the applicants' participation-rights claims for lack of proof and awarded no compensation (ECOWAS Court of Justice, judgment summary, 2026). As of this grading, the Court's follow-up letter of 23 October 2026 asking the Minister of Justice to detail implementation steps had produced no documented compliance measure (ECOWAS Court follow-up reporting, October 2026).

Why the grade lands here: A holder can grip referees by dismissing them, or — more durably — by writing the rules so their independence cannot reach him. Gnassingbé's method is the second: an expired-mandate assembly rewrote the constitution unpublished and unreferended; the electoral process ran with foreign press barred and church observers blocked; the media regulator suspends foreign broadcasters mid-crisis; and his office can be vacated only by a three-quarters vote of his own party's chamber. The referees exist, sit, and sometimes speak. They cannot blow a whistle that costs him anything.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — UNIR's parliamentary bloc, whose dominance is now load-bearing constitutional structure; the appointees of the new Senate's presidential third. Hurt — voters, who lost the direct election of the executive entirely; journalists and broadcasters (RFI, France 24, TV5 Monde's correspondent); the Catholic Church's observer mission; opposition parties whose appeals were rejected wholesale; the ECOWAS Court, whose judgment stands unimplemented.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The first wobble of his life was answered by yielding. Installed by the army on 5 February 2005 in defiance of the constitution's succession rule, he stepped down twenty days later under ECOWAS and AU sanctions and condemnation, and submitted to the April election (OHCHR, September 2005). Whatever else it was, it was an open adjustment under pressure.
  • In April 2024, when the first adoption of the new constitution (25 March) triggered immediate protest, he suspended promulgation, ordered a second reading, and held consultations with "the nation's vital forces" from 8 to 12 April before the text was adopted again on 19 April and promulgated on 6 May (presidency statement via Global South World, May 2024; ISS Africa, 2024). The outcome did not change; the pause, the second vote, and the consultations were real procedural adjustments, openly made.
  • The December 2025 clemency was an adjustment after the June crisis: announced by him personally in the State of the Nation address of 3 December 2025, decreed on 30 December, it freed 1,511 detainees including nine known activists, and Abdoul Aziz Goma walked free on 1 January 2026 after seven years (MFWA, December 2025; OMCT, January 2026).
  • Regionally, when another option became available, he took it. As ECOWAS relations soured, Togo kept the Lomé corridor open to the Alliance of Sahel States, his foreign minister publicly floated AES membership in January 2025, and Lomé unveiled a cooperation strategy with the Sahel states in April 2026 — a pragmatic pivot, openly pursued, that also protected the port revenues his economy runs on (regional reporting, January 2025–April 2026). Togo also detained the exiled former Burkinabè president Paul-Henri Sandaogo Damiba in Lomé and handed him to Burkina Faso's authorities in January 2026, as reported by regional outlets — an adjustment to the new Sahel order, recorded as reported (Africa Press / Jeune Afrique reporting, January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The largest street wobble of his tenure before 2025 — the 2017–2018 protest movement, which demanded restoration of the 1992 constitution's two-term limit, a two-round ballot, electoral-commission reform, and diaspora voting, and at its height drew crowds estimated in the hundreds of thousands — was answered with internet shutdowns, arrests, and at least 16 deaths including two soldiers (contemporaneous reporting), and then with the one concession that cost him nothing: the 2019 term limit that did not apply to him. The movement's leader, Tikpi Atchadam, has lived in exile since 2017 (2017–2018 Togolese protests, documented record; SSRC working paper on the ECOWAS mediation, March 2020). The core demand was not adjusted to; it was waited out and then drafted around.
  • The June 2025 wobble was answered first with force and then with denial. Protests on 5–6 June against the constitutional change, electricity prices, and Aamron's arrest were dispersed with at least 81 arrests (Observatory for the Protection of Human Rights Defenders, via Amnesty International, 2025). When protests resumed 26–28 June, Togolese civil society organizations reported seven people dead, their bodies recovered from Lomé's lagoons — two brothers aged 22 and 25 in the Fourth Lake, three bodies in the Bè Lagoon including a 16-year-old, two more in the Nyekonakpoé Lagoon — with witnesses reporting injuries consistent with live ammunition (MFWA/IFEX statement, 1 July 2025). The government denied responsibility and stated that forensic evidence showed the seven had drowned (government statement, 29 June 2025, via CIVICUS Monitor). Amnesty International's witnesses documented unlawful force, torture, and ill-treatment, and the enforced disappearance of one man and one woman (Amnesty International, 2025). The counts are printed as their sources give them; the denial is printed as the government's. What is not in the record is an independent investigation, an acknowledged death, or a charged officer.
  • The pushback from inside his own family was answered with an arrest. Former armed forces minister Marguerite Gnakadé — his sister-in-law — spent 2025 publishing videos denouncing his extended hold and calling on the military to side with the people; she was arrested on 17 September 2025 on charges including incitement to revolt, ALLEGATIONs untried at grading, and remained detained through the UN CEDAW committee's October 2026 review, which called for the release of women detained solely for exercising freedom of expression (International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, September 2025; CEDAW review of Togo, October 2026, as reported in the Togolese press).
  • The regional court's wobble was not answered at all. Togo did not appear in the ECOWAS Court case on its own constitutional reform; the judgment of 29 January 2026 went against it by default; and the Court's implementation inquiry of 23 October 2026 stands, at grading, without a documented Togolese compliance step (ECOWAS Court, 2026). Refusing to look is the precise failure this test names: when another option became available — appear, argue, negotiate a compliant reform path — the seat declined to open the compare screen.
  • One wobble is recorded under the no-good-choice clause, with its boundary stated. The electricity price increases that helped spark June 2025 were taken inside a genuine fiscal trap: public debt around 66% of GDP, an IMF Extended Credit Facility programme requiring consolidation, and a deficit that had to fall (it did, from 6.8% of GDP in 2024 to 3.5% in 2025 on World Bank figures). The options that actually existed — hold tariffs and breach the consolidation path that keeps credit and the port economy financed, or raise them and take the street's anger — each carried real harm, and the price decision itself is graded as the best available in the moment, not held against him. The trap's origin is partly his own two decades of borrowing, which is recorded; and the clause covers the price decision only — not the batons, the lagoon denial, or the warrants that answered the anger, which are graded above on their own record.

Why the grade lands here: Gnassingbé adjusts — on presentation, on procedure, on periphery: a second reading here, a clemency there, a regional pivot when the map changed. On the one question every major wobble of his tenure has actually asked — how long he holds the seat — the answer has never once been adjustment. It has been a reset (2019), a new office (2024), denial of documented deaths (2025), and silence before the regional court (2026). The pattern over twenty-one years is not a man who cannot adjust; it is a man who adjusts everything except the thing being asked.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the 1,511 clemency beneficiaries and their families; Sahel-state traders and governments whose corridor stayed open; his government's regional room for manoeuvre. Hurt — the June 2025 dead and their families, met with a drowning finding they dispute; detained critics (Gnakadé, Aamron); the 2017–2018 movement, whose demands were drafted around; Togolese litigants whose regional judgment stands unimplemented.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition, the protesters, the diaspora, the north, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The economic record his governments run on is real, broadly shared in design, and independently documented. Growth ran at about 6.5% in 2024 and roughly 6% in 2025 — the IMF estimates 6.3% for both years; the World Bank's Macro Poverty Outlook puts 2025 at 5.9% amid fiscal consolidation; the figures are printed as their sources give them (African Development Bank country outlook, June 2026; World Bank MPO, October 2026; IMF country data, 2026). The Port of Lomé — West Africa's only deep-water container port able to receive the largest vessels on the route — handled 2.06 million TEU in 2024 and about 2.4 million in 2025, ranking inside the world's top 100 and serving as the sea door for Mali, Niger, and Burkina Faso; a €120 million expansion programme runs through 2027 (Lloyd's List rankings via trade press, 2025–2026).
  • The social floor is built as universal, not as patronage. Novissi paid informal workers by mobile money on data-driven eligibility (UN DESA). Universal health insurance (AMU) took effect in January 2024. Free primary schooling dates from 2008, with a national school-canteens programme. The fiscal consolidation of 2025 — deficit cut from 6.8% to 3.5% of GDP, public debt easing from about 66% of GDP — was executed without dismantling those floors (World Bank MPO, October 2026).
  • The north is counted in the security ledger at real cost. The Savanes region has absorbed the Sahel conflict's spillover: around 60 people were killed in armed-group attacks between January and August 2025, the state of security emergency was extended in March 2025, thousands of Togolese were displaced, and northern Togo simultaneously hosted a large influx of refugees from Burkina Faso (Amnesty International, Togo 2025 report). The state held the territory and sheltered the inflow rather than sealing the border.
  • Local democracy, at the lowest rung, was allowed to count: municipal elections on 17 July 2025 were held across the country and passed peacefully, the first under the new constitution (International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, July 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The growth has not reached the bottom of the map at the same speed. Some 3.1 million of Togo's 8.6 million people live on less than $3.00 a day (World Bank MPO, 2025 figures); more than half the rural population lives under the national poverty line, poverty is deepest in the northern regions, and the World Bank's own assessment is that poverty reduction has been slow and job creation barely matches labour supply, with some 60% of working-age Togolese underemployed (World Bank Economic Update, 2023; MPO, October 2026). Out-of-pocket payments still carried 64.3% of health spending in 2023, the year before AMU began (WHO data) — the universal floor exists; the counting is not yet felt at the clinic window.
  • The young are counted as a threat when they assemble. The June 2025 movement was led by the demographic his growth story most needs — urban youth facing electricity prices and unemployment — and it was answered with at least 81 arrests on 5–6 June, more than 100 detentions across June including minors, women, journalists, and health workers (OMCT, 2026), nineteen one-year prison sentences, and seven deaths the government attributes to drowning (sources as printed in Test 3). A generation asking to be counted was entered in the ledger as a public-order problem.
  • The diaspora remains counted out of the vote. Diaspora voting was a headline demand of 2017–2018; the 2019 reform gestured at it; under the 2024 system, with no direct presidential election at all, Togolese abroad — whose remittances are a pillar of household income — have no ballot for the executive that governs them.
  • The old regional skew survives inside the state's coercive arm. The UN's 2005 fact-finding already noted the army as the long-standing preserve of the ruling family's Kabiyè group, and the 2005 repression drew reinforcements bussed in from the north against opposition neighbourhoods in the south (OHCHR, September 2005; IRIN, September 2005). No documented restructuring of that composition appears in the record since; it is recorded as an inherited structure he has kept, not reformed.

Why the grade lands here: This is the one test where the two shelves genuinely contend. The port, the growth, the transfers, the insurance scheme, the held north, and the peaceful local elections count millions of Togolese in, by rule and by design. Against that stand the stuck rural poverty map, a voteless diaspora, an unreformed army composition, and a young generation whose one attempt to enter the count in 2025 was met with batons and prison terms. The counting is real for services and absent for power — MIXED, at the failing edge.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — port and corridor workers and the Sahel hinterland's traders; informal workers paid through Novissi; patients and pupils inside the universal schemes; northern communities held inside the state's protection and refugees sheltered there. Hurt — the rural poor, whose poverty rate barely moves at 6% growth; the diaspora, still voteless; the June 2025 protesters and detainees, counted as a security problem; the families of the seven dead of June 2025, whose count the state does not accept.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since February 2005, with dates; the word×outcome crossings named.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The 2005 submission — kept. Pressed by ECOWAS and the AU in February 2005, he stepped down on 25 February and an election was held within sixty days, on 24 April 2005, as the constitution he had just been installed under required. The election's fairness was disputed and its aftermath was bloody (Test 1); the step-down itself happened, on schedule, as demanded (OHCHR, September 2005).
  • The development programme — substantially delivered. His governments promised a logistics-and-growth state: port expansion, corridor infrastructure, industrial platforms, revenue mobilization. The record shows the Port of Lomé in the world's top 100 container ports, growth around 6% sustained across a decade, the Adétikopé industrial platform built with private partners, tax effort rising, and an IMF Extended Credit Facility programme (approved 2024) being implemented with the deficit cut from 6.8% to 3.5% of GDP in a year (AfDB, 2026; World Bank MPO, October 2026; IMF, 2026). On the economic shelf, the word and the record mostly match.
  • The December 2025 clemency — announced and delivered, to the day. Announced in his State of the Nation address on 3 December 2025; decreed 30 December 2025; releases followed, including nine known activists and, on 1 January 2026, Abdoul Aziz Goma (MFWA, December 2025; OMCT, January 2026). A promise made in public, kept in public, within the month.
  • The 2024 process word — kept in form. When the first adoption of the constitution provoked protest, he said the text would be re-read and the nation's forces consulted; it was, 8–12 April 2024, and adopted again on 19 April (presidency statement, May 2024). The substance is graded below; the procedural promise was performed as stated.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or kept only in the letter that was written to exclude him):

  • The term-limit word — the centre of the file, and it fails twice. The 1992 constitution's limit — no one serves more than two presidential terms — was the word the 2017–2018 movement marched to restore. In 2019 his parliament restored it in form, with the count reset so that it bound everyone except the man who had already served three terms; he took a fourth in 2020. In 2024, before the restored limit could ever apply to him, the office itself was abolished and replaced by an unlimited one, which he assumed in May 2025. At no point in twenty-one years has a term limit ever applied to Faure Gnassingbé. The word "limit" was kept on paper; its substance — that he, too, would one day be limited — was not. The ECOWAS Court's judgment of 29 January 2026 found the 2024 amendment's "circumstances, content, and anticipated outcomes" indicated an intention to circumvent the term limits of the previous constitution (ECOWAS Court, 2026).
  • The 2006 Global Political Agreement — the reform word, undelivered across nineteen years. The APG of August 2006 committed the state to electoral-framework reform, security-sector reform, and an end to impunity. The electoral framework remained executive-shaped through five further elections; the army's composition went unreformed; the 2005 killings met no prosecutions (Test 1); the Truth Commission's 2012 recommendations were largely unimplemented. A word given to the whole political class in 2006 was still outstanding when the class was dissolved into a 108-to-5 parliament in 2024.
  • The regional word — refused at the source. In May 2015, ECOWAS sought a regional protocol limiting presidents to two terms; Togo under Gnassingbé, with Gambia under Jammeh, blocked it (contemporaneous reporting, May 2015). His stated position — term limits are each state's internal affair — was honestly held and consistently applied, and is recorded as kept+hurt below rather than as a broken promise; but it means the one external word that could have bound him was a word he personally prevented from existing.
  • The aspirations word — contested by the street and the court. His government presented the 2024 reform as answering the nation's aspirations for a parliamentary system. Within thirteen months, the largest protests in years were in the streets against it, seven people were dead, and the regional court had found the reform an unconstitutional change of government. Whether the claim was a promise or a description, the record does not sustain it.

The four crossings, named: (1) Kept his word and it helped — the December 2025 clemency, announced and delivered, freeing 1,511 including Goma; the economic programme, delivered broadly as promised. (2) Kept his word and it still hurt — the 2019 term-limit restoration, kept to the letter he had written, the letter being that it never bound him; and his consistent, openly stated refusal of a regional term-limit norm (2015), faithfully maintained, which hurt the region's democratic standard and every Togolese who relied on the limit's substance. (3) Went against his word and it helped — not documented: no file in the record shows a broken commitment of his producing a documented public benefit; the claim that the 2019 reset bought stability is his government's framing, and the security metrics that would test it are not published by an independent source. (4) Broke his word and it hurt — the substance of the term-limit word and the 2006 APG reform commitments, broken across two decades, with the hurt recorded in Tests 1, 2, and 4: unprosecuted killings, a gripped electoral frame, and a parliament of 108 to 5.

Why the grade lands here: Gnassingbé keeps the words that cost him nothing — clemency, consultations, development targets — and keeps them punctually; the file credits every one. But the Test weighs a ruler's word over time, and the heaviest words of his tenure were the ones about his own limits. Those he answered by rewriting them — in 2019 by resetting the count, in 2024 by replacing the office — so that the keeping and the breaking are the same act viewed from two sides. A word that is renegotiated every time it comes due is not being kept; it is being managed. FAILS.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — clemency beneficiaries and their families; businesses and workers carried by two decades of delivered growth policy; foreign partners who got a predictable counterparty. Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: FAILS.

Faure Gnassingbé is the series' clearest case of the seat that changes shape around the man. Graded on the executive office he has held without interruption since February 2005 — as president for twenty years, and since 3 May 2025 as President of the Council of Ministers under a constitution his expired-mandate parliament adopted unpublished and unreferended — the record divides cleanly in two. On the delivery side stands a genuinely functioning development state: a top-100 world port, a decade of ~6% growth, rule-based digital transfers, universal health insurance begun, a northern border held against the Sahel war, and a clemency in December 2025 that was announced, decreed, and executed within the month — which is why Test 4 lands MIXED and why the FOR shelves of this dossier are long. On the holding side stands the pattern that decides the other four tests. One law fails because the law itself was re-cut in 2019 and again in 2024 at the moment it was about to bind him, because the 400–500 dead of his founding election (UN count; 154 on the state's own inquiry; the counts never reconciled) have never met a prosecution, and because the criminal law's senior targets are critics — a businessman who sheltered students, a rapper, a poet, his own sister-in-law. The grip test fails because the referees were not seized but redesigned: an electoral frame run with foreign press barred and church observers blocked, a media regulator suspending foreign broadcasters mid-crisis, and an office removable only by three-quarters of his own party's chamber. The wobble fails because every adjustment he has ever made — the 2005 step-down, the 2019 concession, the 2024 second reading, the 2025 clemency — was made on the periphery, while the centre, his tenure, was answered in June 2025 with force and a drowning finding, and in 2026 with non-appearance before the regional court that then found his founding act an unconstitutional change of government. His word fails on the heaviest promise of all: across twenty-one years, no term limit has ever applied to him, because each was rewritten before it could — the crossings are named in Test 5, including the honest entry that a word broken to the public's benefit is not documented in his file. The open files that could move these grades are equally plain: a documented compliance step on the ECOWAS Court judgment, verdicts or releases in the Gnakadé and Aamron cases, an independent investigation of the June 2025 deaths, and a constitutional path by which the seat he holds could one day be lost. Until the record shows them, the grades stand as graded.
