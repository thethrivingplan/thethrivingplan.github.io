The Ruler's Test — Philip Davis, Prime Minister of The Bahamas

Subject: Philip "Brave" Davis — Prime Minister of The Bahamas since 17 September 2021, and the holder was verified before grading: he still holds the seat in November 2026. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: The Bahamas is a constitutional monarchy whose Governor-General is a ceremonial head of state, while executive power — the cabinet, the budget, the police and defence forces' political direction, and appointments — sits with the prime minister. Davis, leader of the Progressive Liberal Party (PLP), first took the seat after the PLP won the 16 September 2021 general election. He then did something no Bahamian prime minister had done since Hubert Ingraham in 1997: he won a second consecutive term. Parliament was dissolved on 8 April 2026, ahead of the October 2026 deadline, and at the early general election of 12 May 2026 the PLP took 33 of 41 seats on 51.21% of the popular vote, against 8 seats and 35.28% for the Free National Movement (FNM) under Michael Pintard, who conceded by telephone roughly four hours after polls closed; Davis was sworn in for the second term on 14 May 2026 (Reuters and Bahamian press reporting, May 2026). He is graded on five years and two months in office — a full first term and the opening of a second — with the post-election cabinet (28 members of the executive: 21 ministers and 7 ministers of state, among the largest in Bahamian history, per the Caribbean Council) part of the current record. Where a promise belongs to the 2021 "Blueprint for Change" platform or to his own dated public statements, the record is checked against those words. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The defining file is his government's answer to the United States indictment unsealed in the Southern District of New York in late November 2024, which alleged a massive cocaine-importation conspiracy operating since May 2021 "enabled by corrupt Bahamian government officials" — officials allegedly controlling the main airport, passing US Coast Guard movements to traffickers, and taking bribes of $10,000 to $2 million, including a shipment of some 1,320 pounds of cocaine through the airport. Those indicted were Bahamians at the working top of his own security state: the Royal Bahamas Police Force's chief superintendent in charge of the airport division, a police sergeant, and a Royal Bahamas Defence Force chief petty officer, alongside eight other Bahamians and two Colombians (US Department of Justice, indictment unsealed November 2024; all charges are ALLEGATIONs and no verdicts are recorded in the record assembled here). Davis did not bury it. He addressed Parliament calling it a "betrayal" and stating that those responsible, "regardless of rank or position — will be held accountable" (Nassau Guardian, 28 November 2024); he accepted the resignation of Commissioner of Police Clayton Fernander on 4 December 2024, within a week — Fernander faced no charges, and that is stated; he committed his government to cooperating with US arrest requests; and when the indictment referenced a $2 million bribe requested on behalf of an unnamed "high-ranking Bahamian politician," Davis sent a formal diplomatic note to Washington demanding the name be disclosed, a request confirmed through the US Chargé d'Affaires. The name has never been publicly identified; it is recorded here as unidentified, and no identity is guessed at.
  • The prosecution service reached the state's own top detective. After leaked voice notes circulated in July 2024 purporting to capture the head of the Criminal Investigations Department, Chief Superintendent Michael Johnson, negotiating with a suspected gang figure over clearing charges in a $1.475 million airport armored-truck robbery for payment, Johnson was placed on garden leave, foreign (UK and US) investigative assistance was invited to keep the probe independent, and in January 2025 prosecutors charged the former CID chief with conspiracy to commit bribery, stealing, receiving, and accessory after the fact, alongside a prominent attorney and a police sergeant (Nassau Guardian, July 2024 and 8 January 2025). The charges are ALLEGATIONs; no verdict appears in the record assembled here. What is documented is that the case was brought at all, against the man who had run criminal investigations for the force.
  • The same prosecution service has run, for over four years, the largest corruption case against the other camp: Adrian Gibson, an FNM MP and former executive chairman of the Water and Sewerage Corporation under the previous government, was arraigned in June 2022 on what became a 98-to-101-count case alleging roughly $1.1–1.28 million in kickbacks from corporation contracts, alongside the corporation's former general manager and others (Bahamian court reporting, 2022–2026). The case survived every constitutional motion thrown at it — all rejected, with the Court of Appeal lifting a stay in June 2023 — and on 30 September 2026 prosecutors themselves dropped 18 counts and one co-accused entirely, leaving Gibson facing 41 charges as the case moved to the defence (Bahamian press reporting, September–October 2026). No verdict has been reached; Gibson's guilt is an ALLEGATION, and his and the FNM's claim that the prosecution is political is likewise recorded as an ALLEGATION-level claim, contested by the prosecution's conduct in narrowing its own case. The shelf records the shape: the law's heaviest corruption files under Davis run against an opposition MP and against his own police command at the same time.
  • In the FTX collapse, the regulator under his government moved against the largest financial failure ever seated in the country: the Securities Commission froze FTX Digital Markets' assets on 10 November 2022, took some $3.5 billion in digital assets into custody under Supreme Court order days later, and Bahamian police arrested Sam Bankman-Fried in Nassau on 12 December 2022; he was extradited to the United States on 21 December 2022 and later convicted there. Davis's public statement committed The Bahamas to "its own regulatory and criminal investigations into the collapse of FTX" alongside the US case (Office of the Prime Minister statement, December 2022; Securities Commission statements, November–December 2022). FTX's US bankruptcy counsel alleged in litigation that Bahamian authorities had worked with Bankman-Fried to move assets out of the estate's reach — an ALLEGATION the Commission answered by pointing to the court order under which the transfers were made; it is recorded as contested, not as a finding.

Evidence against:

  • The accountability machinery Davis promised for exactly these cases does not exist. In December 2024, answering the indictment, he promised an Independent Commission of Investigations rather than the opposition's proposed commission of inquiry; the bill was debated in February 2025 and the legislation passed. Eighteen months later the commission has never been established: "more than a year and a half after the Davis administration passed legislation to establish an independent commission of investigations, it still doesn't exist" (Our News, 18 November 2026). His own party's 2026 election platform promises to "fully fund and operationalise" it — an admission, in his own camp's words, that the law on paper is not a body in fact. A one-law state needs a referee that sits whether or not the case is comfortable; on the documented record, this one has never sat.
  • Inside his own cabinet, the law bent. As Minister of Labour and Immigration, Keith Bell swore in a Haitian-born family as citizens at a funeral in June 2023 — an act captured in viral audio — and, according to leaked internal departmental correspondence recording his own acting director's minutes, ordered the release of undocumented Chinese workers found working at a major hotel construction site in January 2023; internal correspondence also flagged citizenship swearing-ins conducted without the required renunciations in 2022. Davis's answer, in the September 2023 reshuffle, was to move Bell out of Immigration — and keep him in Cabinet, at Housing and Urban Renewal. Opposition figures called the move cowardice; that characterization is contested and recorded as such. What is not contested is the outcome: the documented consequence for a minister's documented interventions in individual immigration cases was a lateral move, not an exit — a different rule than the one applied to the police officers in the shelves above.
  • The outcome measure has not moved. The Bahamas scored 64 of 100 on Transparency International's 2021 Corruption Perceptions Index, 64 in 2022, 64 in 2023, 65 in 2024, and 64 in 2025 (29th of 182) — five years of a reform-branded government, and perceived public-sector corruption stands exactly where it stood when he arrived (Transparency International, CPI 2021–2025). Perception is not proof, and the index is recorded as an outcome measure, not a verdict; but a seat that grades by evidence notes when the needle it promised to move has not moved.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts rule against his government and are obeyed, including at the highest level. On 4 May 2023 the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council dismissed his own Attorney General's appeal and held that children born in The Bahamas to unmarried non-citizen mothers and Bahamian fathers are citizens at birth ([2023] UKPC 13, Attorney General v Rolle). Davis publicly welcomed the ruling the same day as "an important step towards ensuring equal citizenship rights" and committed his government to implementing it — his government's litigating position lost, and the loss stood. In the shantytown litigation, after a February 2023 judgment discharged the 2018 injunction and held his government's eradication policy lawful, the government still had to seek court orders structure by structure; Chief Justice Sir Ian Winder ordered only two of more than 300 targeted structures demolished — the two proven to be wilful breaches — and the government complied, proceeding otherwise through the statutory Buildings Regulation Act process (Bahamian court reporting, 2023). The referee narrowed the government's win, and the government played on.
  • The prosecution's independence was given a substantive holder. Cordell Frazier served as Acting Director of Public Prosecutions from May 2023 — through the Gibson trial's most sensitive years, a prolonged acting tenure the opposition itself criticized as a risk to the office — and on 28 November 2024 she was appointed substantively, the first woman to hold the post, with the department's prosecutor complement grown from under 20 to 45 (Attorney General's budget-debate contribution, June 2025; Government House ceremony reporting, November–December 2024). The office that prosecutes his own police commanders and the opposition's MPs alike answers to a confirmed constitutional officeholder, not a caretaker.
  • The Central Bank speaks in its own voice and is not documented to have been pressed: Governor John Rolle's bank publishes its own assessments, including unwelcome ones, and no documented instance of executive interference with monetary or supervisory decisions appears in the record assembled here. The fiscal framework his government legislated operates with the bank's independence intact on the documented record.
  • The media environment is free in fact, not only in law: the Nassau Guardian and The Tribune have covered the indictment, the voice-notes scandal, the energy crisis, and the government's failures at full strength throughout his tenure, and opposition voices — including sustained attacks on Davis personally — circulate unimpeded. No documented closure, prosecution, or licensing retaliation against a critical outlet appears in the record assembled here.

Evidence against:

  • The electoral machinery is not independent, and the referees of his own re-election said so in writing. The Commonwealth Observer Group's interim statement on the 12 May 2026 election noted that "the composition of the Constituencies Commission, responsible for this delimitation, is tilted in favour of the ruling party" and that delimitation under that arrangement "has the potential to confer an unfair advantage in election outcomes"; the same commission's December 2025 report added two seats (St James; Bimini and Berry Islands), both won by the PLP (Commonwealth Observer Group interim statement, 14 May 2026). The CARICOM Election Observation Mission's final report likewise recorded stakeholder calls "for greater independence of the PRD [Parliamentary Registration Department] and the Boundaries Commission to avoid perceptions of political bias," alongside concerns over the voters' register's integrity, campaign-finance gaps, and limited media impartiality (CARICOM CEOM report, 6 August 2026). Both missions also recorded what belongs on the other shelf of Test 3: the election itself was assessed as peaceful and credible, and its result peacefully accepted. The grip finding here is structural, not episodic: the body that draws the map and the department that keeps the roll sit inside arrangements the ruling party of the day controls, and Davis — twice the beneficiary — has not moved them out.
  • Two of the accountability offices his government legislated remain unbuilt (Test 1): the Independent Commission of Investigations does not exist eighteen months after its law passed, and the Ombudsman — created by the Ombudsman Act assented to on 12 March 2024, with appointment by a committee including the Prime Minister and the Leader of the Opposition, and a selection committee announced in February 2025 — had still not been appointed at grading; his own party's 2026 platform promises to "appoint fully independent Ombudsman," in the future tense (Our News, November 2026; PLP 2026 platform). Institutions that exist only as statutes do not hold anything.
  • Power at the center has widened rather than narrowed. The executive sworn in after the May 2026 election numbers 28 — 21 cabinet ministers and 7 ministers of state — among the largest in Bahamian history, a documented fact criticized on cost and patronage grounds against a government elected on fiscal discipline (Caribbean Council, May 2026; Bahamian press commentary, 2026). And the police commissioner's chair turned over in the indictment's immediate aftermath by resignation accepted at the top (Test 1): lawful, arguably necessary, and recorded here as what it also is — the security state's leadership changed by the prime minister's hand within a week of a foreign indictment, with the force's next commissioner, Shanta Knowles, the first woman to hold the post, in place by early 2025. No documented punishment of a judge, banker, prosecutor, or editor for an unwelcome decision appears in the record; the grip in this file is the grip of unbuilt referees and a tilted map, not of retaliation.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The hardest wobble of his tenure — a US federal indictment alleging his own security officials were on traffickers' payrolls — was answered in days, in the open, and against his own house: a national parliamentary address naming it a betrayal, the police commissioner's resignation accepted within the week, cooperation with US arrest requests committed publicly, legislation promised before Christmas, and a diplomatic note demanding Washington name the unnamed "high-ranking politician" the indictment said a $2 million bribe was solicited for (Nassau Guardian, November–December 2024; US Chargé d'Affaires confirmation of the note). He did not shoot the messenger, dispute the indictment's right to exist, or wrap the accused in the flag.
  • The summer 2026 electricity crisis — rolling blackouts across the islands under a reform that had promised lower bills — was owned in person. In a national address Davis stated he was not there to manage the failure but to fix it, declined to promise that every light would stay on the next day, and published dated capacity returns rather than a slogan (Prime Minister's national address, summer 2026, as reported in Bahamian press). The ownership is documented; the underlying failure is graded on Tests 4 and 5.
  • On food prices, the pushback eventually moved the policy all the way. His government had applied the 10% VAT to previously zero-rated breadbasket items in 2021 — a documented grievance throughout the cost-of-living years — and after years of resisting full removal it cut VAT on unprepared food from 10% to 5% on 1 April 2025 (some $30 million in revenue forgone) and then removed VAT from qualifying unprepared food entirely on 1 April 2026 (Ministry of Finance announcements; Bahamian press reporting, 2025–2026). Slow, and argued with at every step; but the street's central complaint was finally answered with the policy itself, not with messaging.
  • Court losses were absorbed without retaliation or evasion: the Privy Council citizenship ruling (Test 2) was welcomed publicly the day it went against his Attorney General, and the shantytown demolitions proceeded only within the orders the Chief Justice actually granted. When the 2026 election's official referees criticized the electoral machinery (Test 2), the documented response was acceptance of the missions and of the result process — and his defeated opponent's concession was met without triumphalist purges; the transfer of power machinery ran as written.

Evidence against:

  • The first instinct, when the wobble is reputational, is defense. When the US Embassy issued a crime alert in January 2024 after 18 murders in the year's first 24 days, Davis's initial public response was that the incidents did not reflect the general safety of The Bahamas — true of resort areas, and beside the point the alert was making about the communities where the killings were concentrated (US Embassy alert and Prime Minister's response, January 2024). The pattern recurs in the energy file: through 2024 he dismissed the opposition's "secret deal" characterization of the Bahamas Power and Light transmission-and-distribution transfer and declined its demand for a dedicated parliamentary debate on the arrangement, while assuring the public that bill reductions would arrive "within two to three months" — reductions that did not arrive on that clock (Bahamian press reporting, March 2024 onward).
  • On marital rape, consultation was the form the doubling-down took. A Sexual Offences (Amendment) Bill that would have deleted the spousal exemption was drafted and released for consultation in September 2022; consultations were promised complete by early 2023; the bill was never tabled, and in February 2025 the Attorney General conceded it was unlikely to come that term, after sustained church and social pushback. The Senate's own President had publicly demanded the change in November 2022. Women's-rights advocates at Equality Bahamas called the government "cowardly" and "spineless" — activist characterizations, recorded as such (Bahamian press reporting, 2022–2025). The documented sequence is: a drafted reform, a consultation that never closed, and a quiet shelving when the pushback came from pulpits rather than courts.
  • The diplomatic-note episode cuts both ways and is recorded on both shelves deliberately: demanding the unnamed politician's identity was the open answer (above); but the note also asked a foreign government to resolve the single most dangerous unknown in the file, and two years on the name remains undisclosed, the promised independent commission unbuilt (Test 1), and no domestic mechanism of his government's making has answered the question instead. Adjustment that depends on someone else's disclosure is adjustment deferred.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the minorities, the opposition's voters, the poor, the Haitian community that shares the streets — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • For citizens inside the circle, the counting is documented and broad. The minimum wage rose from $210 to $260 per week — public sector July 2022, private sector from 1 January 2023 — as pledged. The National School Breakfast Programme reports more than two million breakfasts served to roughly 20,000 students (government-cited figures, recorded as the government's claim). National Health Insurance prescription coverage expanded from 72 to 142 medications (government-cited, same caution). The murder count — the heaviest burden on the poorest neighborhoods — fell from roughly 120 in 2024 to 83 in 2025 on the police force's preliminary figures, a decline of some 31% (RBPF figures via Bahamian press, February 2025 onward). The VAT removal on unprepared food (Test 3) is a counting measure aimed squarely at the grocery bills of the poor, and the fiscal consolidation behind it (Test 5) was achieved without new household taxes.
  • The citizenship ruling was implemented, not merely accepted: after the Privy Council held in May 2023 that children born in The Bahamas to Bahamian fathers and unmarried non-citizen mothers are citizens at birth, Davis welcomed it the same day and committed to implementation and a legislative review — a ruling whose beneficiaries are precisely the children of mixed-status families, including Haitian mothers (Prime Minister's statement, 4 May 2023).
  • Women hold visible firsts under his government — the first female Commissioner of Police and the first female substantive Director of Public Prosecutions, a woman Speaker of the House and a woman President of the Senate — and the Protection Against Violence Act passed in 2023. The 2026 election itself was assessed by CARICOM observers as peaceful and credible, with results peacefully accepted: the opposition's voters' ballots counted, in the plain mechanical sense, exactly as his own did (CARICOM CEOM report, 6 August 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Haitian community — the population that shares the streets without sharing the franchise — is counted out as policy. From 2023 his government prosecuted an "unregulated communities" programme demolishing shantytown structures, concentrated in Abaco (The Farm and Treasure Cay areas, hundreds of structures) and New Providence, after the courts cleared the legal path (Test 2). Davis's stated process — documented Haitians' employers obliged to house them, the undocumented repatriated, Bahamians asked to move — is recorded as his position. Against it: in April 2023 the UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination called on states to suspend deportations to Haiti amid its collapse and raised expulsions carried out without individual protection assessment; his Immigration Minister answered on the record, "Repatriations will not cease." Officials' own figures record roughly 2,500 people deported by March 2023 alone, at roughly $1 million a year in repatriation spending (UN CERD, April 2023; Bahamian officials' statements, 2023). UN special rapporteurs had already communicated concerns in 2021 about shantytown eradication without consultation or alternative housing — a file that predates Davis and that his government continued rather than closed. And in 2026 the CARICOM observer mission recorded "instances of xenophobic rhetoric regarding the Haitian community" in the election environment itself (CARICOM CEOM report, 6 August 2026). Demolition and deportation proceeded under court supervision and stated process; they also proceeded against a named UN treaty body's suspension call, without the individual assessments it asked for. That is the documented shape of the counting: everyone inside the citizenship line is counted with increasing care; the community outside it is counted as a problem to be removed.
  • Women are counted short on the one protection his own government drafted. Marital rape remains lawful in The Bahamas: the amendment that would have criminalized it was written, consulted on from September 2022, and shelved (Test 3), leaving wives outside a protection every other adult holds. The wider citizenship inequality the Privy Council did not reach — Bahamian women's unequal power to pass citizenship to their children born abroad, the mirror image of the Rolle ruling — was pledged as legislative work by his Attorney General and remains undelivered; no referendum has been held. The count of firsts on the shelf above is real; so is the statute book's remaining answer.
  • The violence burden falls where it has always fallen, and the worst year came on his watch: 128 murders in 2022, 110 in 2023, roughly 120 in 2024 — a homicide rate of about 29.9 per 100,000 (UNODC, 2024) — with 105 of the 2024 killings in New Providence, 72% attributed by police to retaliation, gang, and conflict motives, 78% of victims aged 18 to 45, and 18 victims killed while on bail. The legislative answers (Bail (Amendment) Act 2024; Anti-Gang Act 2024) and the 2025 decline to 83 are on the shelf above; the grade rests on the whole run: for the young men of the capital's poorest districts, the state's counting — protection from being killed — failed at scale for three consecutive years before it improved. Electricity tells the same story in milder form: the cost and the blackouts of the failed energy transition (Test 5) fell hardest on households least able to buy their way around them, through the summer of 2026.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

The 2021 Blueprint pledges and his dated public statements vs. the record since 17 September 2021, through the opening of the second term.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The headline tax word — kept, to the number. The PLP pledged to cut VAT from 12% to 10%; it took effect in January 2022, on the early schedule his platform set. The minimum-wage pledge ($210 to $260 per week) was likewise delivered — public sector July 2022, private sector 1 January 2023.
  • The fiscal trajectory — delivered, with a documented caveat. He inherited a deficit above $1 billion (some 12.5% of GDP in FY2021/22) and debt above 100% of GDP, and pledged consolidation without new household taxes. In October 2026 his government announced a preliminary surplus of $39.3 million for FY2025/26 (0.2% of GDP) — the first surplus of the independence era — with debt-to-GDP down to roughly 64.6% by his June 2026 budget communication, a positive outlook move from Moody's (the first in 17 years) and an inaugural BB− from Fitch. The caveat is documented and belongs on this shelf's edge: the surplus figure was successively re-forecast — $448.2 million projected, then $75 million, then $39.3 million preliminary — and the FNM calls the announced surplus "fiction," alleging omitted borrowing; that dispute is recorded as CONTESTED, the official figure being the government's preliminary number, substantially built on June 2026 revenue including $68.6 million from the new Domestic Minimum Top-Up Tax (Bahamian press reporting, October 2026; Tribune budget coverage, 2025–2026).
  • Named statutes and deals — closed. The Cannabis Bill passed in 2024 (medical and religious use), the Protection Against Violence Act in 2023, and the Ombudsman Act in 2024 — the last with the institution's non-delivery graded on Tests 1 and 2, the statute itself being the kept half of the word. The Grand Lucayan resort sale, promised and re-promised through missed deadlines, closed in May 2025 at $120 million to Concord Wilshire. Upskill Bahamas launched in November 2025 with 8,000 free course places, its first 1,000 graduates reported in April 2026 (government-cited figures). And the democratic word underneath all of it was kept twice: elections held, results accepted, and — in May 2026 — a second mandate won at an election international observers assessed as peaceful and credible.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Freedom of information — the longest-running broken word in the file. Full implementation of the Freedom of Information Act was pledged from 2021 and re-pledged ahead of the 2026 election. The record at grading: the Information Commissioner's office funded at $140,000 in both the 2025/26 and 2026/27 budgets against the Commissioner's own estimate of roughly $1 million needed; a commissioner vacancy reported in July 2026 with no timeline from the Attorney General's office; the FOI (General) Regulations existing, as of about August 2026, only as a consultation draft on the government's own laws site; and the Act's access regime still not in force five years into his government. In June 2025 Davis himself said FOIA "wouldn't significantly improve access" to government information — a statement Pintard answered as "undemocratic… blatant disregard" (contested characterizations, both recorded). The pledge was not merely unmet; its author publicly discounted it.
  • Marital rape — consulted, drafted, shelved. The September 2022 consultation draft would have removed the spousal exemption; consultations were to conclude by early 2023; the bill was never tabled, and the Attorney General conceded in February 2025 it would not come that term (Test 3). A dated promise, a drafted bill, and a documented abandonment.
  • The accountability institutions — legislated, not built. The Independent Commission of Investigations: promised in December 2024 in direct answer to the US indictment, legislated by early 2025, nonexistent in November 2026 (Our News, 18 November 2026). The Ombudsman: Act assented March 2024, office unstaffed at grading, his own 2026 platform re-promising both. The pattern is the finding: where a promise's fulfillment would create a referee over his own government, the statute arrives and the institution does not.
  • Energy — the core promise missed on its own terms. The pledge was lower electricity bills. What the record shows: Bahamas Power and Light's New Providence transmission and distribution transferred to Bahamas Grid Company, a Pike Corporation subsidiary part-owned by BPL, at reported management payments of some $8 million a month; the opposition alleged procurement law was disregarded (June 2024 — an ALLEGATION, contested by the government); promised bill reductions "within two to three months" did not arrive; Pike exited the arrangement in 2026, leaving the ownership of the 60% private stake (Island Grid Solutions) undisclosed in the public record (CSJ Report, November 2026); and the summer of 2026 brought rolling blackouts across the islands despite "summer ready" assurances, with LNG and combined-cycle relief now promised for 2027–2028. The promise was not re-dated once; it was re-dated continuously, and the relief now sits in the next term.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Philip Davis is graded on the longest file of any Caribbean seat in this series: a full term, a re-election no Bahamian prime minister had managed in a generation, and a second term six months old. The record's center of gravity is a genuine one-law moment — when a US indictment alleged his own security state was rented to cocaine traffickers, he named it betrayal in Parliament within days, took his police commissioner's resignation within the week, demanded Washington identify the unnamed politician a $2 million bribe was meant for, and let his prosecutors charge his own CID chief and the opposition's most prominent defendant in parallel. No judge, banker, prosecutor, or editor in this file was punished for ruling or reporting against him; the Privy Council took a citizenship case away from his Attorney General and he implemented the loss the same day. Against that stands the quieter pattern the grades rest on: the referees he promised for exactly these dangers — an independent investigations commission, an ombudsman, a working freedom-of-information regime — were legislated and never built, and the electoral map is still drawn by a commission the Commonwealth's observers call tilted toward whoever holds power, which has now been him, twice. His word is kept where it costs money — the VAT cut, the wage floor, the deficit closed to a contested first surplus — and broken where it would cost control: information, investigation, and the marital-rape bill his own government drafted and shelved. And the count fails on the population the whole system is built to not see: the Haitian community, demolished and deported under stated process against a UN treaty body's suspension call, while wives remain outside a protection his government wrote and would not pass. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the Independent Commission of Investigations ever sits, whether the unnamed $2 million politician is ever identified, whether the Gibson and Johnson prosecutions reach verdicts, whether the FOIA regime ever comes into force, and whether the 2025 murder decline holds through the second term.
