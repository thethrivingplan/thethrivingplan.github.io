Westward

Outfit a wagon, name your people, and walk them roughly 2,000 miles from Independence, Missouri, to Oregon's Willamette Valley — at the speed of oxen, through the country and the calendar that decided who arrived. There is no score here that matters more than who arrives.

A note of homage: Generations of schoolchildren learned this journey from an early educational trail game, and this page borrows that shape — outfit, travel in legs, meet the trail's events — because it works. Everything here is our own: our name, our words, our drawn trail, our events. The history underneath is the documented record, and it is harsher and more interesting than the legend: the trail's great killer was disease, not drama.

1 · Your party — named people, not points

Five people ride and walk with you. Their health is the game. When someone sickens, the wagon slows for them or it doesn't — that choice is yours, and the trail will remember it in the log. You may rename anyone (alias freedom: use your family's names, your friends', or invented ones — the trail treats them the same).

Why names? Emigrant companies were neighbours, kin, and hired hands travelling together for four to six months. Diaries record deaths by name, often with a verse or a psalm. A game that turns people into a single “health” number loses the thing the diaries insist on: it was always someone.

2 · Outfit the wagon — every pound is a decision

You start with $420 in game money of the era (1840s–50s dollars — roughly a farm family's stake; a real outfit commonly cost $400–$600 of the time). A loaded wagon could carry about 2,000 lb of cargo before the load itself began killing oxen and snapping axles. Buy with the trade-off in mind, not the price tag alone.

The real arithmetic: Guidebooks of the era advised roughly 200 lb of flour, 30 lb of pilot bread, 75 lb of bacon, 10 lb of coffee, 25 lb of sugar, 15 lb of dried fruit, plus salt and cornmeal per adult — near half a ton of food for a family, before a single tool. Wagons were chronically overloaded at the start and the trail east of Fort Laramie was littered with abandoned goods: stoves, trunks, even pianos, jettisoned as oxen tired.

4 · Camp — pace, rations, and the evening choices

Pace

Why pace ate the oxen: An ox walks about two miles an hour and needs hours each day to graze and chew cud, or it loses condition no grain can replace on the trail. Pushing miles borrowed against the team's bodies. Companies that raced early often walked into the mountains in autumn with starved teams — the calendar, not the distance, was the real enemy: snow closed the passes, so the race was real too. That tension — hurry and starve, dawdle and freeze — is the trail's central problem, and no pace solves it cleanly.

Rations

Half rations are not half as safe: Trail work — walking 15 miles, herding, hauling water — burns enormous energy. Chronic short rations show up first as weakness and slow healing, then as scurvy when fresh food runs out: old wounds reopening, gums bleeding, men carried in the wagon who had walked out of Missouri. Diaries treat “the scurvy” as a season, arriving late in the journey like weather.

Evening actions

Hunting, honestly: Buffalo were plentiful on the central plains early in the migration years and emigrants did shoot them — sometimes wastefully, taking tongues and leaving carcasses, a fact Indigenous nations and later travellers both recorded with anger. By the later years the herds near the trail were thinned and a hunt was a gamble, not a grocery. Antelope and small game fed more families than the famous buffalo ever did.

5 · History shelves — the documented record, one tap down

Each shelf states what is documented, and labels the character of its sources — whose words, gathered how, and where the numbers are estimates rather than counts.

What actually killed people on the trail — in honest order (Tap to open)

Disease first, by a wide margin. Cholera was the trail's great killer, especially 1849–1852, spread through contaminated water at crowded crossings and campsites; it could take a healthy adult from first cramps to death in a day. Dysentery and “mountain fever” (likely tick-borne and typhoid-like illnesses) ground people down more slowly. Scurvy weakened the late legs of the journey.

Then accidents. Drownings at river crossings; firearm accidents — often while pulling a loaded rifle from a wagon muzzle-first; children falling under wagon wheels; wagon and animal accidents on descents.

Violence was real but far rarer than the movies taught. Emigrant-on-emigrant violence and attacks by Native nations both occurred, but historians' counts put killings by Native people well below deaths from disease — and emigrants killed Native people too, a part of the record older tellings left out. The trail crossed Indigenous homelands; Native people also traded with, guided, and rescued emigrant parties, including at river crossings where their ferries and knowledge saved lives.

Overall mortality is commonly estimated around 4–6% of the roughly 350,000–400,000 emigrants who travelled the overland trails in the peak decades — on the order of 20,000 deaths, with graves so frequent along some stretches that diarists wrote of passing several a day.

Source character: emigrant diaries and letters (primary, firsthand, uneven); grave counts and trail registers (partial counts); U.S. National Park Service trail histories (agency synthesis); modern historians' demographic estimates (secondary — figures are ranges, not censuses, and are labelled as estimates here accordingly).

What a river ford really risked (Tap to open)

A loaded emigrant wagon weighed over a ton. Fording meant driving oxen into current over an unseen bottom: a wheel in a hole could pitch the wagon, a soft bank could mire the team mid-stream, and a wagon that tipped spilled a family's entire food supply into the river — a loss that could kill more surely in October than the drowning risk did in June. Drowning itself was common enough that crossings were among the trail's most feared places, and the victims were often strong swimmers caught by current, cold, and heavy clothing.

The alternatives each had a price: caulking the wagon box with tar or clay and floating it across (slow, and everything got wet if the seal failed), ferries run by traders or Native operators (safer, but queues of days and fees that rose with the water), or waiting for the river to fall (safe, and the calendar charged interest). At the Green River and the Snake, ferries and toll bridges became businesses — the trail's danger, monetised.

Source character: emigrant diaries describing specific crossings and losses (primary); ferry and toll records (commercial records, partial); National Park Service and state historic-site interpretation at preserved crossings (agency synthesis of the above).

Why the oxen decided everything (Tap to open)

Oxen were preferred over horses and mules for plain reasons: cheaper to buy, calmer, less likely to be stolen, able to live on prairie grass, and — at the end of everything — edible. But an ox is a ruminant: it must spend hours grazing and chewing cud daily, and a team driven long hours on short grass loses flesh steadily. Emigrants watched their teams' ribs the way you watch a fuel gauge. Dead oxen along the trail were a landmark diarists recorded by the mile in bad stretches, particularly the forty-mile desert crossings of Nevada and the dry reaches before the Cascades, where water and grass, not distance, set the day's march.

Source character: emigrant diaries and guidebooks of the era (primary — guidebooks sold advice, so their claims are read with that in mind); later veterinary and agricultural knowledge applied retrospectively (modern, labelled as such); National Park Service synthesis.

Who was actually on the trail (Tap to open)

Mostly families — the Oregon migration was famously a family migration, with women and children a large share of many companies, which is one reason its diaries are so rich: literate women wrote a great deal of the record we have. Alongside them: single men bound for California gold after 1849, hired teamsters, Black emigrants both free and enslaved (Oregon's exclusion laws awaited some of them — the valley's welcome was not equal), Mormon companies on their own road to Utah, missionaries, and the Indigenous nations whose lands every mile crossed, whose trade, ferries, food, and diplomacy the emigrants depended on far more than the legend admits.

Source character: census and company rosters (partial records); women's diaries and letters, many published by historical societies (primary); tribal histories and oral traditions (primary for Indigenous experience of the migration); modern demographic scholarship (secondary synthesis).
