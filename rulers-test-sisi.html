The Ruler's Test — Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, President of Egypt

Subject: Abdel Fattah el-Sisi — President of Egypt since 8 June 2014; previously Minister of Defense and commander of the armed forces (2012–2014), and director of Military Intelligence before that. As defense minister he announced the removal of President Mohamed Morsi on 3 July 2013, following mass protests against Morsi's rule, and served as deputy prime minister for security affairs in the interim government that followed. He won the presidency in 2014 with 96.9% of the vote, in 2018 with 97.1%, and in December 2023 with 89.65% on an officially reported 66.8% turnout. Under constitutional amendments adopted in 2019, his current term runs to 2030. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The pardon power has been used, at documented scale, in the direction of release. After el-Sisi reactivated the Presidential Pardon Committee on 26 April 2022 and launched a National Dialogue, more than 1,000 pretrial detainees were released in batches in the dialogue's first months, and the dialogue's general coordinator later stated that judicial authorities had released over 1,500 pretrial detainees in total; named activists with final sentences — including Hossam Mones, Yehia Abdel-Hady and Hisham Fouad — were pardoned (Presidential Pardon Committee statements, 2022; State Information Service reporting; Africa News 360, November 2022).
  • The single most prominent prisoner-of-conscience case ended in release on his signature: on 22 September 2025 el-Sisi pardoned the Egyptian-British writer Alaa Abd el-Fattah and five other prisoners, after the National Council for Human Rights petitioned on humanitarian grounds; Abd el-Fattah was freed the next day and, after a travel ban was lifted in December 2025, left to join his family in London (Amnesty International, PEN America and Human Rights Watch statements, 22–23 September 2025; UK Parliament written answers, September 2025).
  • Ordinary criminal process has reached military and state figures at the edges: in December 2024 a military court framework and civilian courts have, across his tenure, tried officers and officials in corruption and abuse cases publicized by the state itself — though, as the against-shelf records, no senior figure has been prosecuted for the protester killings of 2013 (state media reporting; HRW, 2014–2026).
  • In November 2025, when the first phase of the House of Representatives elections was marred by documented violations, el-Sisi publicly instructed the National Election Authority to scrutinize the appeals and "not hesitate" to annul results partially or entirely where the true will of voters could not be determined — an instruction followed by annulments in 48 of 70 first-phase constituencies and reruns (National Election Authority and Supreme Administrative Court rulings, November–December 2025; election concluded January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The founding act of the order he leads was processed by no law at all. On 14 August 2013, six weeks after Morsi's removal, police and army units under el-Sisi's overall command as defense minister and deputy prime minister for security affairs dispersed the Rabaa al-Adawiya and al-Nahda sit-ins. Human Rights Watch's year-long investigation documented a minimum of 817 people killed at Rabaa and 87 at al-Nahda, found the dispersal followed a plan that anticipated several thousand deaths, and concluded the killings likely amounted to crimes against humanity (HRW, "All According to Plan," 12 August 2014). The government's position is on the record: the state-appointed National Council for Human Rights put the Rabaa toll at 632 (624 protesters and 8 police officers), officials maintained that police gave warnings, that some protesters were armed — HRW confirmed only 15 firearms among tens of thousands of demonstrators — and that the dispersal was a necessary counterterrorism measure. No official or officer has been prosecuted for the Rabaa killings in the thirteen years since (HRW, 2014; NCHR report, March 2014).
  • The law's heaviest instruments fall on one side. Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International have estimated, since 2020, some 60,000 political prisoners in Egypt — an ESTIMATE by rights organizations, which the Egyptian government disputes, denying that it holds political prisoners at all (HRW and Amnesty International, January 2020, repeated through 2026; government statements). The mechanism is documented rather than estimated: mass pretrial detention renewed in 15-day increments, and the practice Egyptian lawyers call "rotation" — when a detainee approaches the two-year legal limit or wins a release order, prosecutors add them to a new, near-identical case, restarting the clock (Amnesty International and Egyptian Initiative for Personal Rights documentation, 2019–2025; the practice is expressly described in the civil-society critique of the 2025 Criminal Procedure Code, September 2025).
  • The release valve documented on the for-shelf runs at a fraction of the intake. In July 2023, members of the US Congress wrote that since the National Dialogue was announced, roughly 1,000 prisoners had been released through the Pardon Committee while, in the same period, Egypt detained approximately three new political prisoners for each one released and renewed the detention of nearly 5,000 (letter of US senators to the Secretary of State, 28 July 2023; House Foreign Affairs Committee letter, 10 August 2023). Egyptian rights organizations recorded that of 2,954 named cases they submitted to the Pardon Committee in 2022, 49 — under 2% — had been released (EIPR joint statement, 28 July 2022).
  • The law was rewritten around the man himself. The April 2019 constitutional amendments lengthened presidential terms from four to six years, extended el-Sisi's own running term, and made him eligible for a further term to 2030 — doubling the tenure the 2014 constitution allowed when he took office; the drafting was prepared inside the General Intelligence Directorate in meetings reported to have been headed by his son, a senior intelligence official (2019 referendum record: approved 88.83% on 44.33% turnout, National Election Authority; Reuters reporting on the drafting process, 2019). In his first term, parliament had twice voted the ordinary way on other men's immunities; for the president, the constitution itself moved.
  • Accountability for 2013-era violence runs one way: Morsi-era officials and Muslim Brotherhood members were prosecuted in mass trials — including a single 2014 Minya case in which 529 defendants were sentenced to death at first instance (later mostly commuted or overturned on appeal) — while the Rabaa file produced no defendant from the security side at all (court records, 2014; HRW, 2014).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the media regulator, the central bank, parliament, and the military's civilian counterparts keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts retain pockets of documented independence, and their rulings are executed. The Supreme Administrative Court's November 2025 annulment of results in 29 constituencies — on top of 19 annulled by the election authority itself — was final, unappealable, and carried out, with reruns held and a parliament seated in January 2026 in which eight opposition parties hold 53 seats (Supreme Administrative Court rulings, 29 November 2025; State Information Service composition figures, 6 January 2026). The same judiciary has periodically acquitted or ordered the release of detainees in individual cases throughout his tenure, and the prosecution released batches of pretrial detainees in 2022–2023 under the dialogue process (court and prosecution records).
  • The central bank executed the March 2024 exchange-rate float and a tightening cycle that brought inflation down from a peak near 38% in September 2023 to roughly 12% by early 2025, and the IMF records four completed reviews of the current program by 2025 — technocratic policy, publicly argued in the bank's own terms (IMF and Central Bank of Egypt records, 2024–2025; US State Department Investment Climate Statement, 2025).
  • Foreign correspondents operate in the open: the State Information Service states that roughly 1,000 resident foreign correspondents from 45 countries work in Egypt (SIS statement, 2025), and independent outlets publishing from abroad — Mada Masr among them — continued to report throughout, even while blocked at home.

Evidence against:

  • The judiciary's leadership is now chosen by the president, by laws he signed. The Judicial Bodies Law of April 2017 — passed over the public objection of the State Council Judges Club, which wrote to el-Sisi asking him not to ratify it — gave the president the power to select the heads of the Court of Cassation, the State Council, the Administrative Prosecution and the State Lawsuits Authority from nominees, replacing the seniority rule under which his role had been a formality; he ratified it the day after parliament passed it, and within months had used it to pass over the most senior judges in line, including the State Council's recommended head (Law 13 of 2017; Amnesty International statement, 27–28 April 2017; appointment decrees, June–July 2017). The 2019 constitutional amendments extended the same logic upward: the president appoints the head of the Supreme Constitutional Court and the prosecutor-general, chairs a Supreme Council of Judicial Bodies, and judicial bodies lost their independent budgets (2019 amendments, Articles 185, 189, 193 as amended).
  • The media system was absorbed, then policed. Since May 2017 the state has blocked more than 600 websites, including Mada Masr, human-rights organizations and news outlets, most without published court orders; the dominant broadcast and newspaper group, United Media Services, is owned by a company linked to the General Intelligence Service — the line, in one documented summary, "between owner and censor had all but disappeared" (RSF and Mada Masr documentation, 2017–2026; DAWN analysis, 2026). Egypt ranked 170th of 180 in RSF's 2024 World Press Freedom Index and 169th in 2026; the Committee to Protect Journalists counted 25 journalists imprisoned at October 2026 (RSF indices 2024, 2026; CPJ, October 2026).
  • The grip tightened in the most recent documented chapter: on 27 October 2026 a Cairo court ordered Mada Masr — the country's best-known surviving independent outlet — closed and its equipment confiscated, and fined editor-in-chief Lina Attalah the maximum 3 million Egyptian pounds for operating without a licence; Mada Masr states it applied for a licence in 2018, again in 2020, received an unexplained rejection in 2022 and has had an application pending since 2023. Attalah was detained in the courtroom until the fine was paid, and learned during the proceedings that she had been sentenced in absentia in 2024 to a year in prison on separate charges, with a retrial set for November 2026 (court ruling, 27 October 2026; Amnesty International and RSF statements; Mada Masr's own reporting). The same month, the entire six-journalist team of the fact-checking platform Matsadaash was arrested in raids and accused by the Interior Ministry of Brotherhood links — the platform had spent eight years debunking disinformation, including Brotherhood claims (Interior Ministry statement, 1 October 2026; CPJ and DAWN documentation).
  • Parliament's check is nominal. The 2015, 2020 and 2025 Houses have been dominated by pro-government parties (in 2025–26 the pro-government Nation's Future party alone took 227 of 568 elected seats); the chamber approved the 2017 judicial law the day after introduction-stage debate, ratified emergency renewals in three-month increments for four years, and passed the 2019 amendments 531 votes to 22 (parliamentary records, 2015–2026).
  • The military's economic arm operates outside the civilian count. The IMF's fourth review of the current program (published July 2025) counted 97 military-owned companies, 73 of them industrial, holding up to 36% market share in products such as cement, steel, marble and granite, and stated that the military's expanding economic involvement "needs to be corrected"; the same review recorded that privatization inflows had fallen from a projected $3 billion to about $600 million for fiscal 2024/25, and that promised listings of military firms — first proposed in 2018 — had still not put a single share on offer (IMF fourth review, 15 July 2025, as reported by Mada Masr; Carnegie Endowment analysis, January 2025).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the IMF, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clearest documented adjustment of his tenure is recent and was welcomed by his critics. On 21 September 2025 el-Sisi refused to sign the draft Criminal Procedure Code his own parliament had passed in April 2025 — a text condemned by UN experts, the OHCHR, the Lawyers' and Journalists' Syndicates and Egyptian and international rights groups — and returned it to parliament citing the sanctity of the home, defendants' rights, and the need for more alternatives to pretrial detention. A joint civil-society statement "welcomed the decision not to sign" (Amnesty International and co-signatories, 23 September 2025; Human Rights Watch, 24 September 2025).
  • In November 2025 he intervened against his own camp's electoral machinery: after documented vote-buying and tally violations in the first phase of the parliamentary elections, he publicly directed the election authority to scrutinize appeals and annul where the voters' true will could not be determined, later describing his intervention as a "veto" against practices he was "not satisfied" with. Annulments followed in 48 of 70 first-phase constituencies — the largest judicial correction of an Egyptian election on record — and reruns were held (presidential statements, November 2025; NEA and Supreme Administrative Court records; elections concluded 10 January 2026).
  • Under market pressure he ultimately took the adjustment he had resisted for two years: after defending an overvalued pound through 2022–23 while a parallel market widened, his government floated the currency in March 2024 alongside the augmented $8 billion IMF program, and inflation fell from its ~38% peak to roughly 12% by February 2025 (IMF program records, March 2024; CAPMAS inflation data).
  • On 25 October 2021 he declined to extend the nationwide state of emergency that had run, in three-month increments, since April 2017 — announcing that Egypt had become "an oasis of security and stability" — the first lapse of nationwide emergency rule in years (presidential statement, 25 October 2021).

Evidence against:

  • Each of the adjustments above was answered, in part, by codifying the exception. Days after the emergency lapsed in October 2021, parliament passed amendments writing emergency-style powers into ordinary law, including permanently expanded military-court jurisdiction over civilians for offenses around public infrastructure; a presidential decree the same month delegated open-ended powers in North Sinai to the defense minister (HRW, "Egypt: Emergency Provisions Made Permanent," 5 November 2021). The Criminal Procedure Code he returned in September 2025 was re-adopted on 16 October 2025 with changes the International Commission of Jurists assessed as cosmetic — the final text, which he signed into law on 12 November 2025 (Law 174 of 2025, effective October 2026), still "further empowered abusive law enforcement officials" and failed to curb rotation or prosecutorial extension of pretrial detention without judicial oversight (ICJ statement, October 2025; presidency statement, 12 November 2025).
  • The founding wobble was never answered at all. No investigation attributable to his government has revisited Rabaa; officials have consistently defended the dispersal, and in a February 2016 televised address el-Sisi told Egyptians, of the country's direction: "Please, don't listen to anyone else but me. I'm dead serious" (televised speech, 24 February 2016).
  • Pushback from the street is answered with the detention machinery rather than concession: the November 2013 protest law (issued while he was deputy prime minister) criminalized unapproved assembly and remains in force; documented waves of arrests followed the small September 2019 protests (roughly 4,000 detained in weeks, per rights groups' counts), the 2023 election period, and pro-Palestinian solidarity protests in 2024, in which most of about 100 people arrested in May 2024 were university students (EIPR documentation, 2024).
  • The IMF's structural pushback — the state and military footprint — has been answered with announcements rather than delivery across three programs (2016, 2020, 2022–2026): the July 2025 review records the privatization shortfall and the military's continued expansion into hospitality, energy and steel acquisitions in 2024 (IMF fourth review, July 2025).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include political prisoners and their families, Sinai residents, the poor, Copts, and journalists — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The material floor rose for millions, on documented aggregates. The World Bank-supported Takaful and Karama cash-transfer programs, launched in 2015, grew into the region's largest conditional cash system, reaching several million poor households; the "Decent Life" (Hayah Karima) initiative directed infrastructure spending at the poorest rural villages from 2019; and the government raised cash-transfer allocations in the 2014–15 budget even while cutting subsidies (World Bank program documents; MEI budget analysis, 2014).
  • On Gaza, his government held a line that counted Palestinians as a people with a homeland: from October 2023 el-Sisi categorically and repeatedly rejected any displacement of Gazans into Sinai — "in any shape or form," a position he stated to fellow heads of state and held through 2024–2026 against documented Israeli proposals and US pressure episodes — while Egypt co-mediated ceasefire talks with Qatar and moved aid through Rafah when the crossing operated (International Crisis Group, "Egypt's Gaza Dilemmas," 2024; Egyptian presidency statements, October 2023–February 2024). Whatever its security motives, the stated and held position was that Palestinians would not be counted out of their own land at Egypt's border.
  • Copts received gestures no predecessor had made: el-Sisi became the first Egyptian president to attend Coptic Christmas mass (January 2015, repeated annually), and his government passed the country's first church-construction law in 2016 and legalized thousands of previously unlicensed churches through its committee process (presidency records; Law 80 of 2016 and committee decisions).

Evidence against:

  • The counted-out begin with the ~60,000 (rights organizations' ESTIMATE; the government disputes the category, not the detentions' scale in individual documented cases). Families are inside the punishment's radius: rights organizations have documented the arrest of detainees' relatives, the refusal of identity documents and passports to critics abroad, and prison visits and medical care withheld — Alaa Abd el-Fattah's mother, Laila Soueif, conducted a hunger strike in London for most of a year after authorities refused to count his two years of pretrial detention toward his five-year sentence, a refusal documented as contrary to Egypt's own law; he was held until January 2027 on paper and freed only by the September 2025 pardon (Amnesty International statement, 22 September 2025; HRW documentation of document denial, March 2023).
  • North Sinai's residents were counted as terrain. Between late 2013 and July 2020 the army destroyed at least 12,350 buildings — mostly homes — and razed or closed some 6,000 hectares of farmland in the counterinsurgency against the Islamic State affiliate Wilayat Sina'; the city of Rafah was largely demolished for a buffer zone (at least 7,460 buildings by 2021), and Human Rights Watch estimated well over 100,000 of North Sinai's roughly 450,000 residents displaced, most evicted families uncompensated years later, concluding the demolitions likely amount to war crimes (HRW, "Egypt: Massive Sinai Demolitions Likely War Crimes," 17 March 2021). The government's position — that the evictions were a security necessity against an armed group that had massacred soldiers and civilians, including 311 worshippers at the al-Rawda mosque in November 2017 — is on the record and the insurgency was real; the count's failure is the documented absence of return plans or timely compensation, and the October 2023 episode in which residents protesting for their agreed return were dispersed with gunfire and at least nine arrested, after the army missed its own pledged return date (HRW, 2021; Egyptian rights organizations' joint statement, 26 October 2023).
  • The poor paid for the stabilization in documented sequence: subsidy cuts from July 2014 (fuel prices up as much as 78% overnight), the November 2016 float and VAT, and the 2022–24 crisis that drove inflation near 38% and the official poverty rate to 33.5% in 2021/22 on the World Bank's most recent household survey, with roughly 30% of Egyptians below the national poverty line through the adjustment years (World Bank poverty data; CAPMAS). The megaprojects proceeded in parallel — above all the New Administrative Capital, built by the military's Engineering Authority at a cost reported in the $45–58 billion range, and the 2015 Suez Canal expansion — while the IMF recorded the state's investment crowding out the private economy (IMF reviews; project reporting).
  • Copts remain counted unevenly beneath the gestures: the April 2017 church bombings that killed more than 40 Christians triggered the emergency; sectarian attacks in Minya and elsewhere were repeatedly resolved by customary "reconciliation" sessions rather than prosecution, and the 2016 church law's implementation has left many congregations still unlicensed a decade on (US State Department International Religious Freedom reports; Egyptian Initiative for Personal Rights documentation).
  • Journalists are counted as a security category: at least 18–25 imprisoned at any recent count (RSF/CPJ, 2024–2026), and pro-Palestinian protesters — Egyptians expressing the government's own stated position on Gaza — were arrested in 2024 (EIPR, 2024).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record since 2014, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The austerity itself was announced before it was imposed, and imposed as announced. In June–July 2014, weeks after taking office, el-Sisi cut fuel subsidies by up to 78%, publicly calling subsidy reform a "bitter pill" no predecessor had dared, and halved his own salary and pledged half his wealth as a signal of shared sacrifice (Reuters, July 2014; presidency statements). The reform sequence he promised the IMF in 2016 — float, VAT, subsidy phase-out — was substantially executed, and the March 2024 float completed the pattern (IMF program records, 2016–2024).
  • The Suez expansion was delivered on his stated clock: ordered in August 2014 to be finished in one year instead of three, the New Suez Canal opened in August 2015 (Suez Canal Authority records).
  • Security stabilization, his central 2014 promise, was substantially delivered on its own terms: the Sinai insurgency that killed hundreds of soldiers and civilians annually in 2014–2017 was degraded to a low level by the early 2020s, nationwide emergency rule was allowed to lapse in October 2021, and large-scale attacks inside Egypt's cities ceased (security reporting; HRW's Sinai documentation records the campaign's end state alongside its costs, graded in Test 4).
  • The Gaza red line was stated and kept: his October 2023 declaration that Egypt would never accept the forced displacement of Palestinians into Sinai was repeated at every pressure point through 2026 and never traded, including during the financing negotiations of early 2024 in which Egypt's need was greatest (presidency statements; Crisis Group, 2024).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The third term. In a November 2017 interview with CNBC, el-Sisi said he would respect the constitution's two-term limit and would not seek to remain: it was not appropriate, he said, for a president to stay "for another term against the will of the Egyptians." In April 2019 his parliament and referendum amended the constitution to extend his tenure to 2030, and in December 2023 he stood for and won the third term the amendment created (CNBC interview, November 2017; 2019 referendum record; National Election Authority results, 18 December 2023).
  • The patience appeals outran their dates. In 2014 he asked Egyptians for two years of hard work before they would feel improvement; in February–May 2016 he asked them to bear with him six months, after which Egypt would "become something else." A decade on, the documented aggregates are: poverty around 30–33.5% (World Bank), inflation that peaked near 38% in September 2023 before falling back, a currency that went from about 7 to about 48 pounds to the dollar across his tenure, and public debt service consuming the largest share of the budget — while growth (5.2% in the first nine months of fiscal 2025/26 on official figures) has resumed (World Bank; CAPMAS; IMF reviews, 2024–2026).
  • The 2023 election he promised would be a real contest was structured so it could not be. His only potentially serious challenger, former MP Ahmed Tantawy, withdrew in October 2023 saying officials and pro-regime men blocked his supporters from notarizing the required endorsements — the election authority dismissed the allegation — while dozens of his campaign members were arrested and Tantawy himself was later prosecuted over circulating endorsement forms; his phone had been targeted with Predator spyware in attacks Google and Citizen Lab attributed to a government-aligned operator (Tantawy campaign statements, October 2023; NEA response; Citizen Lab and Google Threat Analysis Group findings, September 2023; HRW, 19 December 2023). El-Sisi won 89.65% against three low-profile candidates.
  • The structural-reform word to the IMF — level the field, shrink the state and military footprint, privatize — has been given in three successive programs (2016, 2020, 2022) and remains, on the IMF's own July 2025 record, undelivered in its military dimension (IMF fourth review, July 2025; Carnegie, January 2025).
  • The National Dialogue's opening (2022) was announced as a political reset "for all Egyptians" except those with blood on their hands; its recommendations on pretrial detention were only partly and belatedly reflected in law, and new political arrests continued throughout its life at roughly triple the release rate (National Dialogue records; US congressional letters, 2023).

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Abdel Fattah el-Sisi has held Egypt for twelve years on a record that splits by altitude. At the level of the state he runs as an economy and a security file, the word was often kept: subsidies cut as announced, the canal dug on his clock, the insurgency degraded, the currency finally floated, and one red line — no displacement of Palestinians into Sinai — stated in 2023 and never traded. At the level of the state as a power over Egyptians, the same record fails the first two tests outright: the law that began with an unprosecuted mass killing at Rabaa now runs through presidentially chosen judges, an intelligence-linked media owner, 600 blocked websites, and a detention system whose rotation mechanism exists precisely so that release orders do not release; and the constitution itself was amended, by an intelligence-drafted process, to extend the one man's tenure past the limit he had publicly promised to respect. The wobble is genuinely mixed because the late adjustments are real — a flawed procedure code sent back, an election's results annulled in 48 districts on his own instruction, the most famous prisoner pardoned — but each arrived after years of the opposite practice, and the code he signed in November 2025 kept the machinery the return was supposed to fix. The counts file is the heaviest: some 60,000 estimated political prisoners whose category the state denies, a Sinai population displaced by the hundred-thousand with homes demolished and compensation unmade, and a poverty rate that rose through a decade of patience appeals. The corrections that would move these grades are the same ones the record itself names: prosecutions that reach the security side of 2013, a pardon process replaced by a rule, and Sinai families home or compensated.
