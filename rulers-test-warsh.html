The Ruler's Test — Kevin Warsh, Chair of the Federal Reserve

Subject: Kevin Warsh — Chair of the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System since 22 May 2026. The seat graded is the chairmanship, and the reason is stated plainly: the chair holds no vote more than any other member of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), but the chair sets the Board's agenda, leads the FOMC, speaks for the institution, and controls its senior staff and its communications — monetary policy power exercised through the FOMC, under the mandate Congress gave the Fed: price stability and maximum employment, held independently of political direction. The premise of the original brief is corrected on the record: Jerome Powell no longer holds this seat. Powell, chair since 2018, served a second term that ended on 15 May 2026 (Britannica Money, 2026; Reuters, 29 April 2026). President Donald Trump announced Warsh as his choice in January 2026 (formal nomination recorded in March 2026 in some accounts — the announcement and the paperwork are printed as the separate sourced facts they are); the Senate Banking Committee advanced the nomination 13–11 on 29 April 2026; the Senate confirmed Warsh as a governor, 51–45 on 12 May 2026, and as chair, 54–45 on 13 May 2026 — the most divisive, narrowest confirmation vote ever recorded for a Fed chair, almost entirely along party lines, with Senator John Fetterman (D-PA) the only Democrat in favour (AP via multiple outlets, 13 May 2026; Reuters, April–May 2026; Bloomberg via Nexgen, October 2026). Warsh, 56, a lawyer and financier who served as a Fed governor from 2006 to 2011 under Chair Ben Bernanke, was sworn in at the White House on 22 May 2026; his term as chair runs to May 2030 and his governor's term to January 2040 on one sourced account, with the governor's seat separately reported as a 14-year term running from 1 February 2026 (CoinStats/Fed biography summaries, May 2026; Senate reporting, May 2026) — the two term accounts are printed separately, not blended. He took the seat Stephen Miran had held on the Board. Tenure framing, stated plainly: Warsh is graded on a partial tenure of roughly six months — four FOMC meetings chaired (June, July, September, October 2026), with the December 8–9 meeting still ahead at grading. That is a thin file beside his predecessor's completed eight years, and this dossier says so where it matters: Powell's record appears as context — above all the independence fight of 2025–2026, which is the ground Warsh's chairmanship stands on — but Powell is not the subject graded. Where six months cannot yet carry a grade, the grade is NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE in reasoning even where a provisional grade is given, and no shelf is padded. The independence context is this: through 2025 Trump repeatedly attacked Powell, demanded lower rates, and floated removing him; on 25 August 2025 Trump attempted to fire Governor Lisa Cook, citing mortgage-fraud allegations made by Federal Housing Finance Agency Director Bill Pulte — allegations Cook denies, recorded here as ALLEGATION, never as a finding (Reuters, 29 June 2026; WSJ, June 2026); in November 2025 the U.S. Attorney for Washington, Jeanine Pirro, opened a criminal investigation into Powell over the Fed's headquarters renovation (projected cost grown from about $1.9 billion to about $2.5 billion on Fed budget documents) and whether his congressional testimony about it was false — an ALLEGATION Powell was never charged on (Reuters timeline, 24 April 2026; Wikipedia investigation record, 2026). On 13 March 2026 Chief U.S. District Judge James Boasberg quashed the DOJ subpoenas, finding they were issued for the improper purpose of pressuring Powell to cut rates or resign; on 24 April 2026 Pirro closed the investigation and referred the cost question to the Fed's inspector general, which had already reviewed the project — on one account twice, finding no wrongdoing (Reuters, 24 April 2026; NPR, April 2026). Senator Thom Tillis (R-NC) had blocked Warsh's nomination until the probe was dropped, calling it a "bogus investigation" and a "vindictive prosecution" (Britannica, 2026; AP, April 2026). Powell announced on 29 April 2026 that he would stay on the Board as a governor — his governor's term runs to January 2028 — saying he would "keep a low profile," not act as a "high-profile dissident," and hoping the Fed and the executive could "go back to respecting what the law says" (Reuters, 29 April 2026). On 29 June 2026 the Supreme Court, 5–4 in Trump v. Cook, Roberts CJ writing, refused to let Trump remove Cook without notice and a hearing, holding that accepting his position "would in effect transform the Federal Reserve's for-cause protection into at-will employment"; Cook remains a governor, her underlying case returned to the lower courts undecided on its facts (Reuters and Mayer Brown, 29 June 2026). Warsh therefore chairs a Board on which his predecessor sits, a governor the president tried to fire sits and votes — and, in October 2026, dissented — and only three of seven governors are Trump's appointees (WSJ, November 2026). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Each test closes with the grade's reason in plain words and a ledger naming who his actions on that test helped and who they hurt. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts — inflation and unemployment figures, dissent counts, balance-sheet figures from different sources — are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the mandate the office was given is never itself a failure: carrying out the platform the office was handed — here, the Fed's statutory dual mandate and the reform program Warsh himself stated to the Senate — is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5 (keeps his word). Marks are lost only for how power is held — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), refusing to consider another option when one becomes available (Test 3), counting out part of the population (Test 4). Word-keeping and outcomes are graded as separate axes and printed independently; neither launders the other. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier says so and grades the best possible choice available in the moment; that is not held against him. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The policy itself has been applied as one rule to the whole economy, with no carved-out beneficiaries documented. Under Warsh the FOMC held the target range at 3.50%–3.75% in June (12–0) and July (9–3), raised it a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00% on 16 September 2026 (12–0), and held it there on 28 October (10–2) (Federal Reserve FOMC statements, June–October 2026, via FOMC trackers). A single policy rate is by construction the same law for the president's allies and opponents alike — and this one was raised against the stated wishes of the president who appointed the chair (see Test 2).
  • The dissents cut in one direction only — toward more tightening — and they were published, named, and unpunished: in July, Presidents Beth Hammack (Cleveland), Neel Kashkari (Minneapolis), and Lorie Logan (Dallas) preferred a hike; in October, Governor Lisa Cook and President Hammack preferred a hike to 4.00%–4.25% (Reuters via meeting accounts, July and October 2026). The governor the appointing president tried to fire voted against the chair's majority position in the open, under the same published-vote rule as everyone else.
  • The June statement's closing line was rewritten to a single standard stated for all: "The Committee will deliver price stability" (FOMC statement, 17 June 2026). Warsh's own framing at his first press conference was that the committee was "unanimous and unambiguous" on price stability — one objective, announced once, applied to every borrower and saver alike (U.S. Bank summary of the June 2026 press conference).
  • His personal-interest file was put through the ordinary ethics door, on the record he gave the Senate: Warsh told the Banking Committee he would sell more than $100 million in assets within 90 days of confirmation under an agreement signed off by the Office of Government Ethics, and would recuse from matters involving firms he held stakes in (Reuters hearing reporting, 21 April 2026; hearing summaries, April 2026). His disclosed crypto-linked holdings were on the public record at confirmation (CoinMarketCap Academy, May 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The source of the seat did not operate by one law, and the chair is its product. Warsh was confirmed only after a criminal investigation of the sitting chair — opened by a Trump-appointed U.S. Attorney, found by a federal judge to be a pretext to pressure that chair on rates, and dropped on 24 April 2026 days before the committee vote — was withdrawn, and only after a Republican senator made dropping it his price (Reuters, March–April 2026; Judge Boasberg's ruling, 13 March 2026). Whatever Warsh's own conduct, the door he walked through was cleared by removing a legal threat against his predecessor; Senator Elizabeth Warren's characterization — that the confirmation was "another step in Trump's attempt to take over the Fed" — is recorded as an interested opponent's ALLEGATION-level characterization, and the 54–45 party-line vote, the narrowest ever for the office, is the documented fact beneath the dispute (AP, 13 May 2026; CFO.com, May 2026).
  • The ethics arrangement has an opacity the one-law test notices: at his hearing Warsh declined to detail what the $100-million-plus in assets were, or how and to whom they would be sold, and Democrats questioned "the lack of transparency around some of his financial holdings" (Reuters/SRN, 21 April 2026; AP, April 2026). The rule applied to him — ethics-office sign-off without public itemization — is the rule the system offers every nominee; that it leaves the public unable to check for favorites is a fact about the source, recorded at its weight.
  • The October hold drew a documented favoritism charge of timing, recorded as contested opinion, not finding: the 28 October 2026 hold came six days before the midterm elections, and at least one published analysis argued the pause was "capitulation, not prudence" — that a hike judged necessary in September was deferred in October while CPI inflation stood at 3.7%, with a December hike then expected after the vote (Main Street Independent column, 28 October 2026). Warsh's stated answer, on the record the same day, was that "politics played no role" in the decision (October press conference reporting, 28 October 2026). The charge and the denial are both printed; neither is a verdict, and the December meeting — after the election — is the file that will test them.

Why: On the policy itself, one law has held for six months: one rate, published votes, dissenters named and unharmed, and a hike imposed on the appointing president's own stated preference. What keeps the grade from HOLDS is the source: a seat reached through the narrowest party-line vote in the office's history, cleared by the withdrawal of a judicially rebuked investigation of the man he replaced, and an asset sale the public cannot itemize. The law he administers is single; the law that seated him was not visibly so. Helped: Savers and creditors generally, who face one published rate and one stated standard rather than directed credit; FOMC dissenters, whose named disagreement the published record protects; markets pricing a single rule, on the June statement's plain closing line. Hurt: No group is documented as carved out of the policy's burden — the burden itself (higher borrowing costs) is counted in Test 4. Hurt at the source: public confidence in the confirmation process, on the documented sequence of probe, judicial rebuke, withdrawal, and 54–45 vote; Jerome Powell personally, as the subject of a criminal investigation a judge found pretextual — an injury inflicted by the administration, not by Warsh, recorded here because Test 1 grades the source of the seat he holds.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The central fact of his six months runs against gripping the institution for the White House: the Fed under Warsh moved in the opposite direction from what Trump wanted. Trump had demanded cuts — on CNBC in April 2026 he said he would be "disappointed" if Warsh did not cut immediately, and immediately after the September hike he demanded rates of "1% or less" and called the hike "rather unfortunate" through the White House (CNBC via hearing reporting, April 2026; White House statements, September 2026). Warsh's FOMC held, then hiked unanimously on 16 September 2026 — the first increase since July 2023 — and in October Warsh stated on the record that "politics played no role" (press conference, 28 October 2026). On 20 November 2026 Trump was still demanding cuts, calling the Fed "very political," while pointedly saying he was "not blaming" Warsh — and officials were signalling a December hike (White House press remarks, 20 November 2026). A chair gripping the bank for his appointer does not produce this record.
  • The internal referees kept their autonomy on his watch. Powell stayed on the Board against the administration's interest — his staying denied Trump a fourth Board appointment and a possible majority (WSJ, November 2026; Reuters, April 2026) — and Warsh's documented posture toward him is Powell's own account: a low profile, no shadow chair, and a transition Powell publicly blessed ("When Kevin Warsh is confirmed and sworn in, he will be that chair," Powell, 29 April 2026). Cook kept her seat after the Supreme Court's 5–4 ruling of 29 June 2026 and used it to dissent in October. Trump has named only three of the seven governors, including Warsh (WSJ, November 2026). No governor, Reserve Bank president, or staff purge is documented under Warsh.
  • The five task forces he announced in June 2026 — on communications, balance-sheet policy, data sources, productivity and jobs in an era of transformation, and the inflation framework — were framed as independent reviews "enlisting some of the very best minds, both inside and outside the economics profession," with early findings due in the fall and completion by year's end (Warsh press conference, 17 June 2026, via Cbonds and U.S. Bank summaries). Opening the institution's own practices to outside reviewers is the opposite motion from sealing it.

Evidence against:

  • Inside the institution, the chair's grip on the information referees has tightened measurably. The post-meeting statement was cut sharply — the June edition ran about 131 words, the shortest outside the COVID emergency and a 45% reduction from March (NextFin analysis of FOMC statements, 2026). Forward guidance was removed by his stated design ("We've dropped forward guidance"; "Forward guidance is not the business we should be in" — Warsh, June–September 2026). He declined to submit his own projection to the Summary of Economic Projections in both June and September — the chair's dot withheld while 18 colleagues submitted theirs (SEP reporting, June and September 2026). Press conferences have been shortened — September's lasted about 30 minutes against Powell's roughly 45 (Main Street Independent, October 2026; NYT via Northwest Arkansas Democrat-Gazette, 14 November 2026). Former Fed officials and economists named in the coverage — including William English, former director of the Fed's monetary affairs division, now at Yale — argue the chair is "giving up a fair amount by communicating as little as he seems to want to communicate," and that the cost lands on the public's understanding (NYT reporting, November 2026). The AP's summary stands: Warsh "has now put that train in reverse" on three decades of rising transparency (AP, June 2026).
  • The meeting-calendar change concentrates the same grip in time. On 28 October 2026 Warsh announced that in 2027 one of the eight meetings will be a strategy session with no rate vote and no policy statement, and in 2028 only six meetings will set policy — the first change to the eight-meeting calendar since 1981 (Warsh press conference transcript, Federal Reserve, 28 October 2026; Reuters, 28 October 2026). Asked what disclosure would follow the strategy meetings, he answered "it's not obvious" (Reuters, 28 October 2026). Fewer decision points plus less communication between them is a centralization of the chair's discretion, whatever its deliberative merits — and Warsh holds unilateral authority over press-conference frequency, while the dot-plot overhaul would need colleagues' support (NYT via Business Report, November 2026).
  • The void has been filled by others, which cuts both ways and is recorded on this shelf at its weight: his colleagues "continued to vocalize their thinking," explicitly and often, while the chair stayed circumspect (NYT, November 2026) — evidence the institution is not silenced, and evidence that its public voice is no longer the chair's office speaking plainly.

Why: Against the White House, Warsh has held the bank's independence in the only way that finally counts — he raised rates against his appointer's loud, repeated demand to cut them, and the governors the president tried to remove or outlast are still seated and voting. Inside the building, the motion reverses: shorter statements, no guidance, no chair's dot, shorter press conferences, fewer voting meetings — the referees who check a chair between elections are the press, the markets, and the public record, and he has deliberately given all three less to check him with. Independence from the president: held. Independence of everyone else from the chair: narrowed by his own hand. Helped: The institution's operational independence — and everyone who depends on it, including future borrowers whose rates rest on the Fed's inflation credibility; Powell and Cook, whose seats survived with the chair's acquiescence rather than his assistance to their removal; outside reviewers given a formal door into the Fed's practices through the task forces. Hurt: The press and the public, who receive a 131-word statement, no forward guidance, and shorter press conferences in place of the Powell-era record; markets, which on the documented accounts charge for the uncertainty — the 10-year Treasury yield stood at 4.84% on 5 November 2026 (FRED via Boston Report tracker) and bond-market volatility rose on the guidance withdrawal (Motley Fool and NextFin analyses, 2026) — a cost that flows through to every mortgage priced off those yields (counted in money in Test 4); his own FOMC colleagues, whose individual voices now carry an explanatory burden the chair has set down.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The year's wobble was large and external: inflation re-accelerated during his first months — CPI rose 3.7% in the year to September 2026, up from 2.4% in February before the war with Iran drove energy prices sharply higher (energy +20.4%, gasoline +34.2% in the year to September, on the BLS figures carried in the October meeting accounts) — and the committee adjusted in the open, in stages: a unanimous hold in June, a divided hold in July while members waited for data, then a unanimous hike in September once, in Waller's account, the labor market had stabilized and progress on inflation had stalled (FOMC statements and minutes, June–September 2026; Waller remarks via FOMC tracker, October 2026). The September statement gave the reason in plain words: the move would "support a timelier return to 2% inflation."
  • Pushback inside the committee was answered by engagement, not discipline. After July's 9–3 split — three presidents preferring a hike — Warsh publicly welcomed the disagreement, calling it "a real family fight" (Yahoo Finance account of the July press conference), and the September decision that followed was unanimous. In October the split reversed field (10–2 for a hold, the dissenters wanting more), and the decision was explained against the data, with Warsh telling reporters the information arriving "will help us determine where we want policy to be" (Warsh press conference transcript, 28 October 2026).
  • When another option became available on the institution's own working methods, he considered it openly — that is the founding act of his chairmanship: the five task forces are a public, structured consideration of alternatives on communications, the balance sheet, data, jobs and productivity, and the inflation framework, announced at his first press conference with a timetable (June 2026). On forward guidance specifically, he considered the inherited practice and declined it with stated reasons — that guidance is "not well suited to the current policy conjuncture" and makes markets price the Fed instead of the data (Warsh, June 2026). Under the method, considering an option and declining it with reasons is not against-evidence, and it is not recorded as any here.
  • The no-good-choice clause applies to his central decision and is applied by its mechanics. In the autumn of 2026 the options that actually existed were: (a) cut, as the president demanded — into CPI inflation of 3.7% and headline PCE of 3.4% in August on one sourced series (FRED via meeting accounts), risking the inflation credibility the whole tightening rests on; (b) hold indefinitely — leaving inflation, on the September median projection, at 3.7% PCE for 2026 and not returning to 2% until 2029 on that projection (SEP, September 2026); (c) hike — imposing documented borrowing-cost harm (Test 4) to defend the target. Each door carried real harm; the constraint — an energy-driven inflation surge from a war he did not start, arriving months into his tenure — was not of his building. He took the least-harm door actually open on the committee's stated reasoning (a single quarter-point, unanimously, with a pause to reassess in October), and he was honest about the trap while in it: "inflation is too high and has been for too long" (Warsh, September 2026). The hike itself is therefore not held against him in this dossier; what remains gradable is the openness around it, which is what the shelves above and below record.

Evidence against:

  • The October pause is the wobble he answered least openly. Having hiked in September because inflation was too high, the committee held in October with inflation, on the same CPI series, still at 3.7% — and the published critique named the unexplained variable: the meeting concluded six days before the midterm elections, a December hike was already the market expectation, and the pause read to critics as the political calendar doing what the data had not (Main Street Independent, 28 October 2026). Warsh's answer — "politics played no role" — is a denial of motive rather than an account of what changed between September and October; the October statement's upgrade of activity from "solid" to "strong" sits oddly beside a pause, and the record assembled here contains no fuller explanation from the chair. Denial is the response this test grades lowest, and it is recorded at that weight, with the critique itself labeled contested opinion.
  • On communication, the wobble from markets and former officials — documented volatility, a former monetary-affairs director's public warning, sustained press criticism — has been met not with adjustment but with repetition of the design: at the October press conference he extended the same logic to the meeting calendar itself (fewer voting meetings from 2027). If another option becomes available — for instance, the communications task force's own findings, due by year's end — the record of whether he considers it is not yet written; at grading, the response to six months of transparency pushback is doubling down, openly stated as principle.
  • His room to answer wobbles at all has been narrowed by his own choices: with no forward guidance and no chair's dot, each data surprise lands unmediated — as on the October inflation release, when December-hike odds moved sharply on a single CPI print (market reporting, November 2026). That is a consequence he chose; it is recorded here, not as bad faith, but as the price of the stance when the wobble came.

Why: On the economy's wobble — an imported inflation surge — he adjusted in stages, in the open, with reasons, and under the no-good-choice clause the hike itself earns no demerit. On the institution's wobbles he splits: internal dissent he met with a welcome and a unanimous September; external pushback on his silence he has met with restatement, and the one decision that smelled of the political calendar got a denial where an explanation was owed. Adjustment where the data pushed; doubling down where the critics pushed. Helped: FOMC members in the minority in July, whose pushback visibly moved the committee to September's unanimous position; data-watchers and the public, who got the September reasoning in a plain sentence; future committees, if the task-force reviews land as genuine reconsideration. Hurt: Anyone who needed the October reasoning spelled out — markets repricing on single prints, and the public asked to take "politics played no role" on trust six days before an election; critics of the communications retrenchment, whose documented objections have so far received repetition rather than engagement.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Sunni, Kurdish, Turkmen, Christian, and Yazidi communities, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The mandate he repeats is the whole-count mandate, both legs, in the institution's own words: the October statement records the decision taken "in support of the Federal Reserve's dual mandate," with "job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and the unemployment rate has changed little" printed beside "inflation remains elevated" (FOMC statement, 28 October 2026). The employment leg has not been struck from the accounting.
  • On the employment figures as separately sourced: unemployment stood at 4.2% in the jobs account carried with the October meeting (BLS via meeting coverage, October 2026) and at 4.1% for October 2026 on the FRED UNRATE series (Boston Report tracker, updated 14 November 2026) — two sourced figures, printed separately, both low by historical standards, with one soft print inside the period (a 29,000-job month in the account read at the October meeting) and a stronger +165,000 monthly figure on the tracker's card; the conflict is in the prints' timing and series, and no blended number is offered. A chair tightening into a 4.1–4.2% unemployment rate is not, on these figures, tightening into mass joblessness — the September hike was taken with the jobs leg of the mandate, on the data, intact.
  • Price stability is itself a counting-everyone policy in its incidence, and his stated case rests on it: inflation is the tax that falls hardest on those with no assets to inflate — Warsh's hearing formulation was that Congress tasked the Fed with price stability "without excuse or equivocation," and that "inflation is a choice, and the Fed must take responsibility for it" (Senate Banking Committee, 21 April 2026). September's CPI detail shows why the burden is regressive: energy +20.4% and gasoline +34.2% in the year to September (BLS via October meeting accounts) are costs weighted heaviest in poor households' budgets.
  • His review agenda names the counted-out side of the labor market explicitly: one of the five task forces is charged with "productivity and jobs in an era of transformation" — the AI displacement question — and by November 2026 Warsh was publicly calling AI success "a make-or-break issue for the US" while cautioning, from his pre-chair record, against the Fed taking political sides on questions outside its mandate (Reuters, 24 November 2026). The jobs file of the newest technology is on his table, not off it.

Evidence against:

  • The tightening's costs are documented, specific, and fall downward. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate was 7.32% on 22 October 2026 (Freddie Mac via FRED), up from 6.51% in the week Warsh was sworn in on one sourced comparison and from 5.98% on 26 February 2026 on another — both printed, not blended (Nexgen, October 2026; MASEconomics from FRED, October 2026); the Mortgage Bankers Association's conforming rate reached 7.35% in the week ended 13 November 2026, with applications falling 2.3% (MBA via CrossCountry Mortgage update, 19 November 2026). On the worked example in the October coverage: a family signing a $400,000 30-year mortgage in late October pays about $355 a month more than one that signed in late February (MASEconomics, October 2026). The prime rate stands at 7.00%, credit cards average about 21% across accounts, while savings accounts pay well under 1% (October meeting accounts, 2026) — the borrower's side of the ledger is paying the anti-inflation bill in full, and the small saver's side is not being repaid for it.
  • The rhetoric of the chairmanship weights one leg of the mandate audibly heavier. At his first press conference Warsh mentioned "price stability" 12 times (U.S. Bank summary, June 2026); the June statement's new closing line names price stability alone — "The Committee will deliver price stability" — with maximum employment absent from the sentence the institution chose as its signature (FOMC statement, 17 June 2026). No policy act against employment is documented; the signature line is nonetheless the accounting the public was given, and it counts one mandate in full and the other not at all.
  • Inflation itself — the harm his policy exists to end — is still being borne, and borne regressively, on his watch and partly on his predecessor's and a war's account: CPI at 3.7% in the year to September 2026, headline PCE at 3.4% in August on the FRED series and core PCE at 3.0% for August on the tracker's card (figures printed separately by source and month, as sourced). Six months in, the counted-out-by-prices have not yet been counted back in; the September median projection did not reach 2% until 2029 (SEP, September 2026).
  • No distributional accounting from the chair himself appears in the record assembled here: no Warsh statement in the six months addresses who bears the tightening — renters priced out by 7.3% mortgages, card borrowers at 21%, small firms at prime-plus — by group. The task force on jobs and productivity may produce one; at grading it is recorded as an absence, not an act.

Why: Nobody has been counted out by design — the dual mandate is printed on every statement, unemployment on the sourced figures is 4.1–4.2%, and the anti-inflation cause he serves is genuinely the poor's cause in the long run. But the counting in his six months is uneven in fact: the costs are itemized and falling now on borrowers, first-time buyers, and card holders, while the benefits are a projection dated 2029; and the institution's new signature line counts price stability and leaves employment unspoken. Everyone is in the mandate; not everyone is yet in the results, or in the chair's own sentences. Helped: Households on fixed and low incomes in prospect — every point of inflation not entrenched is a regressive tax not collected, on the September projection's path back to 2% by 2029; workers, whose jobs leg held at 4.1–4.2% unemployment through a tightening cycle; savers holding longer instruments, with the 10-year Treasury at 4.84% (FRED, 5 November 2026). Hurt: First-time and lower-income homebuyers — 7.32–7.35% mortgage rates on the sourced series, about $355 a month more on the worked $400,000 example than in February; credit-card borrowers at about 21% average rates and small borrowers at a 7.00% prime rate; renters, whose would-be purchases the mortgage rates defer; small savers, paid well under 1% while the prime rate stands at 7.00% (October meeting accounts, 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 22 May 2026, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The independence word — kept, at the price that proves it. At his confirmation hearing on 21 April 2026 Warsh stated, under questioning: "The president never once asked me to commit to any particular interest rate decision, period. Nor would I ever agree to do so if he had," and "I will be an independent actor if confirmed as chair of the Federal Reserve"; "Monetary policy independence is essential" (Senate Banking Committee, 21 April 2026, Reuters and AP reporting). The record since: no cut delivered; a unanimous hike on 16 September 2026 against the president's publicly stated wish, repeated demands for 1% rates answered with a further expected hike, and on 20 November 2026 the president reduced to complaining about a "very political Fed" while exempting Warsh by name (White House remarks, 20 November 2026). This is crossing 1 and crossing 2 of the method's four, and both are named: the word was kept and it helped — the institution's credibility, and every holder of dollars, is the beneficiary — and the same keeping hurt borrowers in the short run (Test 4's ledger stands in full; the keeping earns this test's credit, the hurt is not laundered by it).
  • The price-stability word — kept as action, unkept as outcome, and the two are separated here. His hearing word: "Congress tasked the Fed with the mission to ensure price stability, without excuse or equivocation… Inflation is a choice, and the Fed must take responsibility for it" (21 April 2026). His June statement word: "The Committee will deliver price stability." The action record matches the word: holds in June and July, the September hike, an October hold with a December hike signalled, and Warsh in October: inflation "had been above the Fed's objective for close to six years" and the committee stood ready to act (press conferences, June–October 2026). The outcome is not yet the word: CPI 3.7% (year to September), the 2% return projected for 2029 (SEP, September 2026). A promise of delivery, six months in, is graded on whether the promiser is doing what he said he would do — he is — not on a 2029 arrival graded in 2026.
