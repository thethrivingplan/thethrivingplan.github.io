A clinical reasoning simulator

The Clinic

A patient walks in with a story. You ask the questions, read the vital signs, examine, order tests, build a list of what it could be, commit to what it most likely is — and decide what happens next. Every case teaches the reasoning underneath: why that question, why that test, what each answer rules in or out.

⚠ Read this first — honest limits, at full size

EDUCATIONAL ONLY. This game teaches how doctors reason. It is NOT medical advice, it does NOT diagnose any real person, and it is NOT medical training or a qualification.

Real symptoms mean a real clinician. Emergencies mean emergency services — now. If anything on the red-flag shelf below is happening to a real person, stop reading and get emergency help.

The cases are teaching simplifications of real medicine — real presentations, deliberately tidied. Real patients are messier, and real diagnosis belongs to real clinicians with the real person in front of them. Each case write-up labels its simplifications.

How a case works

  1. Story — the patient tells it in their own words.
  2. History — ask questions from a menu. Each costs from your budget. Some are red-flag screens; the game remembers whether you asked.
  3. Vitals — the vital signs, with normal ranges beside them.
  4. Examination — focused checks, each costing budget.
  5. Tests — bloods, dips, ECGs, scans. Each result says what it does and doesn't prove.
  6. Diagnosis — first cast wide: build the list of what it could be. Then commit to one.
  7. Plan — treat, refer on, reassure with safety-netting, or escalate to EMERGENCY now.

Grading is honest and plain: right / close / wrong diagnosis; questions and tests you needed vs wasted; red flags caught vs missed. Missing a red flag — or sending a true emergency home — is the worst outcome on this page, and it is named plainly, with why it kills. Dangerous choices are called dangerous.

Teaching dial: Off (no notes while you play), Light (short notes at key moments — the standard), Coach (the full why under every answer, result, and vital sign). Set it in Page settings on the compass bar below. Default: Light. Saved on this device.

Gestures on this page say what they do: folds read “Tap to open / Tap to close”. Where a control has more to say, hold it (or hover with a mouse) and it explains itself and offers more.

The stakes here are people

There is no money in this game — there never was. Nothing is priced, billed, or bought. What you win or lose is patients helped or harmed, and your standing in the clinic — the trust of your patients and colleagues. Patients helped is the score that matters.

Your standing (shown in your Record) rises when patients are helped well — right diagnosis, economical questions, red flags caught, honest safety-netting — and falls when they are harmed, wasted on, or sent away wrong. Every move of it is named with its reason in the case write-up. The question/test budget is time and attention — the patient's time, and yours; the cost of over-testing is delay, false positives, and a patient's worry, never a bill.

Shifts, triage, and capacity

You work in shifts. Each shift, the waiting room holds a set of patients and you can only take so many — the capacity is stated on the shift panel before the shift starts. Choosing who to take, and in what order, is triage, and it is a real strategy with honest trade-offs:

Triage strategyThe upsideThe trade-off
Sickest firstThe dangerous cases get your freshest attention.Their stories don't announce themselves — a quiet-looking patient can be the dangerous one, and a loud one can be safe. Triage from a waiting-room glimpse is a bet.
Quickest wins firstMore patients helped per shift; simple cases stop clogging the room.The complex patient waits — and waiting is sometimes the injury (see the appendicitis outcomes).
First come, first servedFair, simple, no favouritism.Ignores need entirely. Fairness of order is not the same as fairness of outcome.

Patients you don't take have honest outcomes, stated plainly at the end of the shift — some get seen elsewhere, some get worse. No melodrama, and no hiding it.

The ramp

The work gets harder on purpose, and the page says so: Shift 1 is common-and-clear presentations. Later shifts mix in the tricky ones — the anchoring traps, the fork cases, the emergencies hiding inside ordinary stories. Within a shift the order is yours (that's the triage); across shifts, the ramp is the design.

Your own energy

You have an energy level, and it is a real mechanic, not a poster. Working drains it. As it falls: your time-and-attention budget per case shrinks (a tired clinician has less to give), questions start costing more (everything takes longer), Coach's notes get shorter and are marked as tired, and the game will flag when you nearly miss something. Push past empty and the game sends you home — kindly, plainly, and your waiting patient is handed to a colleague. Resting (on the shift panel) restores energy — but the waiting room waits while you rest: patients' clocks keep moving, and a patient who waits through your rests may fare worse. Rest is triage too. A wrecked clinician is a dangerous clinician; the game practises that, it doesn't just say it.
