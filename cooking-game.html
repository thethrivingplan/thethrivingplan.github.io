On the Line

A cooking game played the way a real kitchen runs. Tickets come in, you work each dish through its real steps — prep on the board, heat on the stove or in the oven, a rest where the dish asks for one, then plating at the pass — and serve before the ticket's patience runs out. Food on heat has an honest done window: pull it early and it's underdone, pull it in the window and it's perfect, leave it and it burns. Nothing is dressed up.

    Strategies — more than one way to run a line

    Wherever real cooks disagree, this game teaches both ways and prints the trade-off. Neither is “the right answer” — each wins under different pressure.

    Batch the prep vs. cook to order

    Do every ticket's board work first, then fire dishes one by one — or take each ticket from raw to plated before touching the next?

    Batch the prepAll your knife work happens while nothing can burn. Calm, fast once firing starts. Cost: prepped food waits — herbs wilt, cut surfaces dry, and a ticket that arrived later may jump the queue badly.
    Cook to orderEvery dish is fired the moment its ticket lands, so nothing sits. Cost: you're chopping while something else is in a pan — the classic way dishes burn.

    Best when: batch when several tickets land together and share ingredients; cook to order when tickets trickle in one at a time.

    Fire the long dish first vs. clear the quick wins

    Two tickets on the rail: one needs the oven for a long bake, one is a two-minute plate. Which do you start?

    Long dish firstThe bake runs itself once it's in — that time is free for everything else. Cost: the quick ticket's patience drains while you set the long one up.
    Quick wins firstA served ticket can't be lost, and the rail feels lighter. Cost: start the long dish late and its bake may outlast its own patience no matter how well you cook.

    Best when: long-first when the long step is hands-off (oven, rest); quick-first when the long dish's early steps need your hands the whole time.

    Rest is a station vs. plate it straight from the pan

    Resting feels like doing nothing. It is the opposite: it's the step where the dish finishes.

    Rest itMeat keeps cooking off the heat (carryover) and its juices, driven to the centre by the heat, spread back out — slice too soon and they run onto the board instead. Cost: minutes where the ticket's patience is draining and the food is cooling.
    Skip the restFaster to the pass, hotter on the plate. Cost: a drier, tougher bite and juice on the board — the guest tastes the difference even if the clock doesn't.

    Best when: rest thick cuts always; skip it for thin, quick things — eggs, toast, stir-fry — where there's nothing to redistribute.

    Sequence two tickets vs. finish one, then the next

    The core skill of a line cook: while ticket A's dish is on heat or resting, your hands belong to ticket B.

    Sequence themHeat time and rest time stop being dead time. Two dishes finish close together and both go out hot. Cost: you must watch two windows at once — miss one and something burns while you chop.
    One at a timeNothing burns from neglect; every window gets your full attention. Cost: total time is the sum of both dishes, and the second ticket may die waiting.

    Best when: sequence when a step is hands-off (oven, rest, a long sear); stay single when both dishes are at fiddly, hands-on steps.

    Watch it done

    Technique videos that genuinely teach a step this game asks of you. Every link below was opened and checked before it shipped — the title and channel printed beside it are the video's own.

    • Basic Knife Skills — Tasty (YouTube)

      The pinch grip and the claw — the two hand shapes behind every Board step in this game. A secure board, fingertips curled in, knife rocking rather than slamming.

    • Mise en Place Explained (theory of food preparation) — French Cooking Basics Ep05 — French Cooking Academy (YouTube)

      What “everything in its place” actually means in a working kitchen, from a French culinary school curriculum — the habit the Coach keeps asking for before any heat goes on.

    • Searing Meat Is A Delicious Lie — SciShow (YouTube)

      The honest science of the sear: it does not “seal in the juices” — that's a myth — but the browning reaction is where the flavour is, and it only happens in a properly hot, preheated pan.

    • Does Resting Meat Matter? | Techniquely with Lan Lam — America's Test Kitchen (YouTube)

      A tested answer, with the experiment shown: what happens inside meat as it rests, how long is long enough, and when resting doesn't apply.

    Settings — the rules are yours to set

    Every setting below is saved on this device only. Anything that changes the standard game is labelled a variant, gets a banner at the top of the page while it's on, and is written into your record next to the dishes it touched — variant scores are never dressed as standard.

    Teaching dial

    Off: no tips, no Coach — just you and the rail. Light: one plain line of teaching on the step you're standing on. Coach: the full layer — why each step works, a live read of your rail, timers, patience order, and mise en place checks. How much teaching reaches you is always your dial.
