The Ruler's Test — Markwayne Mullin, Secretary of Homeland Security (United States)

Subject: Markwayne Mullin — the 9th Secretary of Homeland Security, in office since 24 March 2026, and still in office at grading: he testified as Secretary before the House Homeland Security Committee on 18 November 2026 (House Homeland Security Committee, Mullin testimony, 18 November 2026) and fronted the department's response to the 13 November 2026 nationwide FAA ground stop alongside Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy (White House and DHS releases, 13 November 2026). PREMISE CORRECTION, stated plainly: the seat changed hands during the period graded, and any dossier that graded only one holder would be grading a fiction. Kristi Noem — former governor of South Dakota — was confirmed by the Senate on 25 January 2025 by 59–34 and served as the 8th Secretary from 25 January 2025 (Senate roll call and DHS release, 25 January 2025). President Trump removed her on 5 March 2026, announcing the move on social media and naming her "Special Envoy for The Shield of the Americas"; Noem's own farewell message to the department, dated 5 March 2026, gave her last day as 31 March 2026 (DHS, "Message from Secretary Kristi Noem," 5 March 2026; AP and CNN reporting, 5–6 March 2026). Trump nominated Mullin — then the junior U.S. Senator from Oklahoma, a businessman and an enrolled citizen of the Cherokee Nation — the same day, 5 March 2026; the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee advanced the nomination 8–7 on 19 March 2026, with Chairman Rand Paul voting no; the full Senate confirmed him 54–45 on 23 March 2026, with Democratic Senators John Fetterman and Martin Heinrich voting yes and Senator Paul the lone Republican no; Attorney General Pam Bondi swore him in on 24 March 2026 (Senate record and contemporaneous reporting, March 2026). Because Noem held the seat for roughly fourteen months of the twenty-two months graded and Mullin for roughly eight, and because Mullin inherited, continued, paused, or reversed specific Noem policies by name, this dossier grades the seat's record from 25 January 2025 to 24 November 2026, attributing every act to the holder who held the seat when it happened. Where the record belongs to the President, to Congress, or to a component head rather than to the Secretary personally, the dossier says so. The seat graded is the standard executive seat for the homeland: border security and immigration enforcement through Customs and Border Protection (CBP) and Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), legal immigration services through U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, disaster response through the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA), protection of the President and other protectees and financial-crimes investigation through the Secret Service, aviation screening through the Transportation Security Administration (TSA), cybersecurity through the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA), and the Coast Guard. The office's limits, stated plainly: the Secretary executes statutes Congress writes and spends what Congress appropriates — the department ran unfunded for 76 days in 2026 when Congress withheld its bill (below); immigration judges sit in the Department of Justice, not DHS, and federal courts review removals, detention, and DHS policy and repeatedly ruled against this department in 2025–2026; major disaster declarations are formally the President's decision, with FEMA — inside DHS — assessing damage and recommending; under the Stafford Act states lead disaster response and the federal government supplements; and the Secretary serves at the President's pleasure, as Noem's removal demonstrated in a single social-media post. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record through 24 November 2026. Method: The ethic, printed verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that the seat passes, then the strongest evidence that it fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting figures (deportation totals, disaster-approval rates, citizens-detained counts from different sources) are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Carrying out the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one: it is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for HOW it was done — a second law for the administration's own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect this administration (Test 4). Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The border rule, as written, was applied to everyone who arrived: the CBP One appointment system was shut down on inauguration day 2025 for all nationalities at once, humanitarian parole programs were curtailed across the board rather than country by country as favors, and Border Patrol reported zero parole releases at the border for eighteen consecutive months through October 2026 (CBP operational statistics, February and November 2026; DHS release, 16 November 2026). Whatever else is graded elsewhere in this file, the border door itself was not held open for friends and shut for enemies.
  • Noem's most criticized internal rule was, in form, one rule for everyone: from June 2025 every DHS contract, grant, or expenditure above $100,000 required her personal sign-off, applied to FEMA disaster money and routine contracts alike, with no published exemption for favored states or vendors (CNN reporting, July 2025; Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee minority report, March 2026).
  • Mullin, on taking the seat, removed that rule rather than keeping a discretionary chokepoint he could have used selectively: he called it "micromanaging" at his confirmation hearing and rescinded the $100,000 sign-off requirement on 1 April 2026 (Mullin confirmation testimony, 18 March 2026; DHS action reported 1 April 2026). In the same first weeks his department reinstated fourteen FEMA employees — the signers of the August 2025 "Katrina Declaration" letter to Congress — who had been placed on administrative leave under Noem (reporting, 30 April 2026).
  • Money did move across the political map in 2026: on 22 October 2026 FEMA announced more than $896 million across more than 620 disaster recovery and resilience projects nationwide, plus a $400 million Flood Mitigation Assistance opportunity open to all states, territories, tribal nations, and the District of Columbia (FEMA announcements, 22 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Disaster declarations — the seat's most consequential favor-or-no-favor decision, formally the President's signature on FEMA's recommendation inside this department — split by the applicant's politics to a degree two independent analyses, printed separately and never blended, both found unprecedented. The Associated Press's analysis of FEMA data dating to 1989 found the President approved about 80% of major-disaster requests from Republican governors and about 60% from Democratic governors, approved more than three-fourths of requests from states that voted for him in 2024 and less than half from states that did not, denied a greater share of requests than any president since 1989, and took longer on average — about a month and a half — than any predecessor (AP analysis via Fast Company, July 2026). POLITICO's E&E News review of some 2,500 declarations since 1981, using a narrower class (states with a Democratic governor and two Democratic senators versus the Republican equivalent), found approvals of 23% for the Democratic-led group against 89% for the Republican-led group, average decision times of 80 days against 39, and about $250 million in aid to Democratic-led states blocked that the review calculated every previous president, including Trump in his first term (93% Democratic-state approvals then), would have granted (E&E News, 23 March 2026). The White House denied the decisions were political, saying each request received "great care and consideration" and a more thorough review than any prior administration (White House spokesperson Abigail Jackson, via AP, April 2026); DHS did not address the disparity itself in the responses on record. Urban Institute fellow Andrew Rumbach, analyzing the same FEMA data, cautioned that the sample is small and he had not concluded clear bias (AP, April 2026) — the caution is recorded; the two counts stand.
  • The department's public face was bought with a contract that bypassed the ordinary one-rule process. The $220 million advertising campaign featuring Secretary Noem — including on horseback at Mount Rushmore — was awarded without a standard competitive bidding process to firms run by longtime Republican operatives, and a subcontract went to a firm whose principal is the husband of a former DHS spokesperson (congressional hearing record and reporting, March 2026). Noem testified to Senator John Kennedy that President Trump had approved the campaign; Trump told reporters he had not known about it and "wasn't thrilled with it" (Kennedy and Trump statements via CNN, March 2026). The contradiction is printed as a contradiction; either version is a process failure — an unapproved $220 million spend, or an approved one later disowned.
  • Enforcement surges were concentrated where the politics pointed. DHS's largest operations of the period — Los Angeles, Washington, D.C., Chicago, Minneapolis–St. Paul ("Operation Metro Surge," from 1 December 2025), Charlotte, New Orleans — ran in jurisdictions whose governments opposed the administration, while the administration simultaneously pressed those jurisdictions as "sanctuary" violators and conditioned the Minneapolis drawdown on local cooperation (Migration Policy Institute, Policy Beat, 2026; AP reporting, February 2026). A rule enforced at full weight mainly on the other side's ground is the shape Test 1 grades.
  • The insider door stayed open at the top of the seat itself in November 2026. At the 18 November 2026 House hearing, Mullin acknowledged flying on a government plane to an LSU–Texas football game from the suite of the chief executive of Bollinger Shipyards, a firm holding a DHS icebreaker contract of about $2.2 billion (House Homeland Security Committee hearing coverage, 18 November 2026). The trip's propriety is contested and no finding against Mullin is recorded here — it is entered as ALLEGATION-level concern raised in oversight, not a verdict — but a secretary watching a game from a major contractor's suite while that contractor's contract sits in his department is, at minimum, an appearance of a second door for insiders that the seat did not close.

Why this grade: The border rule and the $100,000 rule were genuinely universal in form, and Mullin's repeal of the chokepoint and reinstatement of the FEMA letter-signers are real one-law acts. They are outweighed at the seat's two highest-stakes discretion points: disaster aid, where two separate counts show approvals and speed tracking the applicant state's politics with no departmental answer to the figures, and procurement for the secretary's own image, where $220 million moved without competitive bidding to politically connected firms. Where the seat had discretion, the record shows two tracks. That is FAILS.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: Republican-led states seeking disaster declarations, approved faster and oftener on both counts above; the advertising firms and subcontractors who received the $220 million campaign without competitive bidding; border communities and the administration's constituents to the extent a uniformly closed border door was the elected platform's stated aim.
  • Hurt: Disaster survivors in Democratic-led states whose requests were denied or waited — Colorado's wildfire and flood requests denied and the denial upheld on appeal in April 2026 (AP, 14 April 2026) among them; taxpayers who funded an unbid self-promotional campaign; residents of surge-targeted cities, who lived under an enforcement weight not applied at the same scale in cooperating jurisdictions.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, Congress, the inspectors, the media, and the department's own oversight offices keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized? For this seat the referees are the federal courts, Congress's statutory oversight access, the DHS Office of Inspector General, and the department's congressionally created oversight offices: the Office for Civil Rights and Civil Liberties (CRCL), the Citizenship and Immigration Services Ombudsman, and the Office of the Immigration Detention Ombudsman (OIDO).

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts did, in the end, hold — and the department, after resistance documented below, complied with the orders that closed the fights: Kilmar Abrego Garcia was returned to the United States on 6 June 2025 after the Supreme Court directed the government to "facilitate" his return (Supreme Court order, 10 April 2025; return reported 6 June 2025), and the April 2026 funding act that ended the shutdown carried a 20 percent increase for the DHS Inspector General and restored funding for the Office for Civil Rights and Civil Liberties (H.R. 7147, signed 30 April 2026, as summarized in congressional reporting).
  • Mullin came in promising to un-grip specific referees, and two of the promises were kept in visible form: he told the Senate he would re-engage with the Office of the Inspector General and stand up a legislative-affairs function to communicate with Congress (Carnegie Endowment summary of Mullin testimony, 24 March 2026), and his department reinstated the fourteen FEMA whistleblowers whom Noem's department had sidelined after they warned Congress of a Katrina-scale breakdown risk (reporting, 30 April 2026).
  • Oversight hearings happened, at length and on the record, under both holders: Noem testified before Senate and House committees on 3–4 March 2026 — the hearings that precipitated her removal — and Mullin testified before the House Homeland Security Committee on 18 November 2026 and answered, among other things, questions about his contractor-suite trip and the CISA cuts, declining to call the cuts a mistake but not refusing the questions (hearing record, 18 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The department's own statutory referees were switched off in a single day. On 21 March 2025 DHS ordered the staff of CRCL, the CIS Ombudsman, and OIDO — offices Congress created to investigate rights complaints, resolve immigration-service problems, and monitor detention conditions including excessive force, sexual abuse, and inadequate medical care — to stop work entirely, placing virtually all employees on administrative leave ahead of a reduction in force eliminating some 99% of roughly 400 positions; a DHS spokesperson said the offices had "obstructed immigration enforcement by adding bureaucratic hurdles" (plaintiffs' filings in Robert F. Kennedy Human Rights v. DHS, D.D.C., filed 24 April 2025; DHS statement, March 2025). Only after suit was filed did DHS announce on 23 May 2025 that it would not abolish the offices; the court denied the injunction as moot on 11 July 2025, and denied reconsideration on 27 March 2026 (case chronology, Democracy Forward). By May 2026 DHS was telling NPR that the Immigration Detention Ombudsman had shut down (NPR via contemporaneous tracking, 7 May 2026). A referee you must sue to resurrect has been gripped.
  • When a court stayed the department's block on Congress, the Secretary reimposed it in secret. Federal law (the §527 appropriations rider) guarantees members of Congress entry to ICE detention facilities for oversight without prior notice. ICE imposed a seven-day notice requirement in June 2025; Judge Jia Cobb stayed that policy on 17 December 2025. On 8 January 2026 Secretary Noem issued a previously undisclosed memorandum reinstating the requirement, re-based on One Big Beautiful Bill Act funds she argued the rider did not reach; members were then denied entry to a Minnesota facility while holding a valid court order, and on 2 March 2026 the court stayed the January policy too, holding the notice requirement likely contrary to the rider (Memorandum Opinion, Neguse v. ICE, D.D.C., 2 March 2026). Reissuing a stayed policy through a secret memo and a funding shell argument is the definition of gripping the referee.
  • The Secretary prejudged her own department's most sensitive investigations, on camera, within hours. After ICE agent Jonathan Ross fatally shot Renee Good in Minneapolis on 7 January 2026, Noem called Good's actions "domestic terrorism" the same day; after Border Patrol agents fatally shot Alex Pretti on 24 January 2026, she said he had attacked officers and was "brandishing" a gun and wished to inflict harm — accounts that bystander video, verified by Reuters, contradicted: Pretti held a phone, and CBP's own Office of Professional Responsibility notification to Congress mentioned neither brandishing nor approaching agents (Reason, 25 January 2026; House members' letter to Bondi and Noem, 4 February 2026). Acting ICE Director Todd Lyons later testified under oath that he had no knowledge that either Good or Pretti were domestic terrorists, and that public comments by officials would "put a bias" on the investigations; Senator Maggie Hassan asked the DHS Inspector General to investigate whether Noem's statements biased or impeded them (Senate hearing testimony and Hassan letter to Inspector General Joseph V. Cuffari, February 2026). Minnesota's Bureau of Criminal Apprehension was meanwhile blocked from the Pretti scene twice — the second time while holding a judicial warrant — and a federal judge had to enjoin federal officers from destroying or altering evidence (contemporaneous reporting and court order, January 2026).
  • Dissent inside the department was punished under Noem in the FEMA file: acting administrator Cameron Hamilton was removed on 8 May 2025, a day after telling a House panel he did not believe eliminating FEMA was in the American people's best interest (reporting, May 2025; Union of Concerned Scientists analysis, August 2026), and the fourteen Katrina Declaration signers were placed on leave and investigated after writing to Congress (reporting, 30 April 2026). Their reinstatement under Mullin is on the FOR shelf; that reinstatement was necessary at all is the against-evidence.
  • The cyber referee was left headless and thinned while its mission grew. CISA staffing fell from about 3,400 to about 2,200 on figures cited by House members at the 18 November 2026 hearing, against roughly 2,800 the members said was needed, and the agency had no Senate-confirmed leader from early 2025 through grading; USA Today reported more than 1,000 civilian cyber defenders pushed out (House hearing coverage and USA Today, 18 November 2026). Those numbers are the members' and the paper's, cited as such; DHS did not publish a competing count in the record assembled here.

Why this grade: A seat holds without gripping when the referees can say no and be heard. Here the statutory oversight offices were ordered silent, Congress's court-protected access was re-blocked by secret memo after a judicial stay, the Secretary announced verdicts in two fatal-shooting investigations her own ICE director testified were thereby biased, a FEMA chief was removed the day after candid testimony, and the cyber agency ran leaderless at two-thirds strength into the November 2026 outage week. Mullin's reinstatements and OIG promises are genuine counter-evidence and are recorded; they repair specific grips rather than rebut the pattern. That is FAILS.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: The enforcement program itself, which operated for over a year with its internal complaint, detention-oversight, and civil-rights channels switched off or thinned; the fourteen reinstated FEMA employees, under Mullin.
  • Hurt: Detainees and complainants whose statutory complaint doors (CRCL, OIDO) were closed while detention reached record size; members of Congress of the oversight committees, denied a statutory right twice; the families of Renee Good and Alex Pretti, whose investigations were publicly prejudged by the investigating department's own head; CISA's remaining staff and the infrastructure operators who rely on the agency, working at reduced strength (House figures, November 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Congress, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does the seat adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, when another option became available, the holder at least considered it openly: considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The street's and the country's pushback after Minneapolis was answered, within weeks, with a drawdown rather than only defiance. After the Good and Pretti shootings and days of protest, the President sent border czar Tom Homan to take over the Minnesota operation — widely reported as a rebuke of Noem's management — and Homan announced on 4 February 2026 that 700 of roughly 3,000 federal officers would leave immediately as state and local cooperation increased, and on 12 February 2026 that Operation Metro Surge would conclude, with more than 1,000 agents already gone and a return toward the original footprint (AP reporting, February 2026). The adjustment was late and followed two deaths; it was nonetheless an adjustment, announced in the open with numbers.
  • Mullin's confirmation hearing itself contained two open corrections of his own side's record. He walked back his earlier disparagement of Alex Pretti, said he was wrong to malign him, and pledged that as Secretary he would refrain from judging shootings before investigations conclude; and he told Senator Richard Blumenthal that "a judicial warrant will be used to go into houses, into place of businesses unless we are pursuing someone that enters in that place" (Mullin testimony, 18 March 2026; AP summary). In office he then paused — though, below, did not rescind — the practice: on 8 May 2026 he wrote to Blumenthal that ICE "has not rescinded the memorandum; however, I have paused the practice" of home entries on administrative Form I-205 warrants while the policy is assessed, and the government told the court no such entries would be executed during the pause (Mullin letter, 8 May 2026, as filed in Gibson Brown v. Mullin, D.D.C.).
  • His signature confirmation wobble-response was executed on schedule: the $100,000 rule he had called micromanaging was rescinded 1 April 2026, eight days after he was sworn in (above, Test 1).
  • The 13 November 2026 ground stop is this file's no-good-choice case, graded by its mechanics and not held against the holder. The options actually open that afternoon were: keep departing aircraft moving while radar and communications feeds were failing intermittently across air traffic control centers (harm: a mid-air collision risk no official could quantify), or stop departures nationwide while the cause was unknown (harm: roughly four to five hours of grounded flights, some 18,000 cancellations and over a million travelers affected, on hearing figures). Mullin and Transportation Secretary Duffy chose the stop, said publicly within hours that there was no indication of a cyberattack, and named the telecommunications failure as the working account (White House briefing, 13 November 2026; FAA statements). The trap was not of Mullin's building — the failure traced to a contractor router installation interacting with legacy FAA telecommunications equipment (Senate Commerce Committee letter summarizing briefings, 16 November 2026) — though whether CISA's reduced capacity (Test 2) slowed the ruling-out of an attack is a live oversight question, not a settled fact, and is recorded as such.

Evidence against:

  • Noem's answer to the Texas floods pushback was denial that the documented record supports. After the 4 July 2025 Kerr County floods killed at least 135 people, CNN and the New York Times reported from internal records that her $100,000 rule delayed Urban Search and Rescue authorization by more than 72 hours, that call-center contracts lapsed on 5 July leaving most survivor calls unanswered in the following days (NPR, from FEMA call logs: most calls from 6–9 July went unanswered; on the worst day roughly 10% of more than 15,000 callers got through), and that only 86 FEMA staff were deployed by the Monday night after the flood. Noem told NBC on 13 July 2025 the delay allegations were "absolutely false" (NBC, 13 July 2025). FEMA's then-acting chief David Richardson countered that a Texas-based task force and Coast Guard assets were on the ground 4 July and further support arrived within 24 hours of request (House testimony). The dispute is printed with its disputants; what is not in dispute is that the rule existed, the contracts lapsed, the call logs show the unanswered calls, and the Secretary's answer was a blanket denial rather than an accounting.
  • The "domestic terrorism" labels were never retracted. Confronted repeatedly in the March 2026 hearings — including six times by Representative Jamie Raskin, on the record assembled here — Noem declined to retract or apologize for characterizations her own acting ICE director would not endorse under oath (hearing record, March 2026; Hassan letter). When another option became available — correction, the cheapest adjustment in government — it was refused, not considered.
  • Congress's pushback produced the longest departmental shutdown in U.S. history rather than an early adjustment. After Pretti's death, Senate Democrats refused to fund DHS without enforcement guardrails (body cameras, identification, judicial warrants); the department's funding lapsed 14 February 2026 and stayed lapsed 76 days, until 30 April 2026 — the longest partial shutdown on record (Congress.gov, H.R. 7147; NBC News). TSA officers worked unpaid for weeks — more than 480–500 quit (figures from Acting TSA Administrator Ha Nguyen McNeill's testimony and CBS reporting, printed as the separate counts they are), checkpoint waits exceeded four hours, the longest in TSA's history (McNeill testimony, March 2026). The eventual settlement funded the department except ICE and the contested CBP operations — the oversight demands were not adopted; the fight was routed around, not answered. Mullin, to his credit in the middle of it, warned Congress plainly and early that the money would run out — "my payroll through DHS is just over 1.6 billion dollars every two weeks… I've got one payroll left" (Mullin on Fox News, 21 April 2026) — and executed the President's 27 March 2026 memorandum paying TSA officers from existing funds, with paychecks landing from 30 March. Candor about the trap, yes; adjustment of the policy that built it, no.
  • Mullin's November wobble was answered by deflection. Pressed on 18 November 2026 on whether pushing out 1,000-plus cyber defenders had been a mistake — after a week in which Microsoft services, Chase banking, United Airlines, and an NSA internal network also failed — he declined to say (USA Today, 18 November 2026). Within days, the New York Post reported he had been sidelined from the administration's outage response, with Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton fronting the briefings instead (New York Post, 19 November 2026) — reported as sourcing-based coverage, not an announced decision, and recorded as such. A secretary whose own White House routes around him in a crisis week has not answered the wobble; the wobble has answered him.

Why this grade: The seat adjusted, genuinely and in the open, at three points: the Minnesota drawdown, Mullin's Pretti correction and home-entry pause, and the $100,000 repeal — plus a least-harm call on 13 November that this dossier does not hold against him. It denied or refused at three others of equal weight: the Texas flood accounting, the unretracted terrorism labels, and a 76-day shutdown absorbed rather than negotiated, ending with the oversight questions deferred to a reconciliation track. Adjustment where the cameras and the body count forced it; denial where only the record forced it. That is MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: Minneapolis–St. Paul residents and remaining enforcement personnel alike, by the February 2026 drawdown's de-escalation; homeowners with final removal orders, by the May 2026 home-entry pause; TSA officers, paid from 30 March 2026 under the emergency memorandum Mullin executed; air travelers on 13 November 2026, by the cautious stop.
  • Hurt: Texas flood survivors who waited on rescue authorization and unanswered hotlines in July 2025 while the Secretary denied delay; TSA officers and travelers through 76 days of shutdown — 480–500 officers gone for good (separate counts above); the Good and Pretti families, denied any correction of the public record; the traveling and banking public in November 2026, given a "98% confident" assurance (Mullin, 18 November 2026 hearing) without an accounting of the cyber agency's reduced capacity to verify it.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include those who did not elect this administration — the detained, the misidentified, the citizen caught in an enforcement action, the disaster survivor in an opposing state, the department's own unpaid front line — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The department's protection and response duties were, in specific documented instances, performed for everyone in the path regardless of politics. Texas — a state whose request came through a Republican governor — received a major disaster declaration on 6 July 2025, and FEMA had approved 3,883 Individual Assistance applications and about $41.4 million in Individuals and Households aid for the Hill Country floods as of 14 July 2026 (FEMA disaster page DR-4879-TX). The October 2026 FEMA awards ($896 million, 620-plus projects) were nationwide in distribution (FEMA, 22 October 2026). The Secret Service's protective record across the period shows no successful attack on any protectee of either party, and in July 2025 six agents were suspended without pay (10 to 42 days) over the July 2024 Butler failures — discipline imposed, though Senator Rand Paul's July 2025 committee report noted no one was fired (Senate HSGAC report, July 2025; agency statements).
  • The border accounting, on the department's own published numbers, counted the flow honestly at the border itself: CBP published monthly encounter statistics throughout, and October 2026's 27,890 nationwide encounters and 8,288 southwest-border apprehensions are the agency's own figures, checkable against prior years (CBP, November 2026).
  • Mullin's confirmation framing was explicitly universal — "I can have different opinions with everybody in this room, but as secretary of homeland I'll be protecting everybody" (Mullin testimony, 18 March 2026) — and his FEMA posture ("restructured, not eliminated") kept a federal backstop in existence for states of both parties rather than abolishing the counting mechanism itself.

Evidence against:

  • Citizens were counted as enforcement targets in the hundreds, on counts printed separately and never blended: ProPublica documented 506 U.S. citizens held by immigration agents between January 2025 and September 2026, including at least 70 children and teenagers; a tracking compilation updated 29 October 2026 put the figure at 519 (ProPublica, 2026; compilation of the same series). The government does not itself track these cases — which is its own counting failure. Two citizens, Renee Good and Alex Pretti, were killed by the department's officers in Minneapolis in January 2026 (above). A five-year-old asylum seeker, Liam Conejo Ramos, was detained with his father in Minnesota in January 2026 and became, in the Migration Policy Institute's account, a symbol of the surge's reach into school communities (MPI, 2026).
  • The detained population's own composition contradicts the counting the department advertised. The Migration Policy Institute's October 2026 analysis of DHS's own FOIA-released data found about 980,000 ICE and CBP deportations from 20 January 2025 to 31 July 2026, of which 20 percent (about 190,000) involved a person convicted of a crime; 61 percent of ICE deportations (excluding border turnarounds) were of people who had been in the United States at least two years, and 15 percent (about 90,000) at least ten years (MPI Fact Sheet, October 2026, by Marc R. Rosenblum, former head of DHS's own Office of Homeland Security Statistics). Detention stood near 66,000 in July 2026, with roughly 71 percent of detainees having no conviction on TRAC's count of DHS case records — against ICE's position, cited in the same reporting, that about 60 percent have a conviction or pending charge; the two figures are printed as their sources gave them and not reconciled here.
  • People died in the department's custody at a record pace, on counts printed separately: the Guardian counted 32 deaths in ICE custody in calendar 2025, the most in more than two decades; Bloomberg Law reported 37 deaths in fiscal 2026, the most in a fiscal year on record, surpassing the previous record of 28 from 2004 (Guardian, 2025; Bloomberg Law, 30 September 2026). Senators Ossoff and Warnock wrote to Mullin and the acting ICE director on 13 April 2026 that of 49 deaths in custody since January 2025, ICE had issued the required interim death notice within 48 hours in only 15 (Senate letter, 13 April 2026) — the dead were not even counted on time.
  • The clearest single counting-out remains the seat's first spring: Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a Maryland resident under a 2019 immigration judge's withholding-of-removal order barring his return to El Salvador, was deported there on 15 March 2025 into the CECOT prison; the government conceded in court that the removal was an "administrative error" and that it had no legal authority for it, the Supreme Court ordered his return facilitated on 10 April 2025, and he was returned on 6 June 2025 — and promptly charged criminally in Tennessee, a prosecution a federal judge dismissed on 22 May 2026 after finding a presumption of vindictive prosecution (District of Maryland opinion in Abrego Garcia v. Noem, 2025; subsequent court record through May 2026). Every safeguard in the system said this one man did not count as deportable to that country; the department deported him anyway and took three months and a Supreme Court order to bring him back.
  • Disaster survivors in opposing-party states were counted at a discount (Test 1's two analyses: AP's roughly 80%/60% governor split and E&E News's 89%/23% full-control split, with decision times to match), and the department's own front line was counted as expendable in the shutdown: some 260,000 DHS employees were affected by the 76-day lapse, TSA's roughly 50,000 officers missed multiple paychecks, and hundreds resigned (congressional and union reporting, February–April 2026).

Why this grade: The FOR shelf shows a department that can still perform universal duties — declarations issued, aid paid, protectees kept alive, statistics published. But on the populations this test names, the documented record is a series of specific excludings: citizens detained by the hundreds and two killed, a detainee population roughly four-fifths without convictions against a "worst of the worst" public count, custody deaths at a record pace and reported late, one man deported in conceded violation of a court order, and disaster aid tracking the map of the 2024 election. The floor of this test is that everyone gets counted; the record shows whole categories counted out. That is FAILS.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: Disaster survivors in approved states, including Texas flood households who received $41.4 million in individual aid (FEMA, DR-4879-TX); protectees of both parties, unharmed on this department's watch in 2025–2026; communities near the border during a period of historically low crossings (CBP statistics).

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The border platform — delivered as run on (Noem, then Mullin). Noem pledged at her 17 January 2025 hearing to end CBP One, curtail mass humanitarian parole, reinstate Remain in Mexico, and prioritize removals of people with criminal convictions and final orders (AP hearing summary, 17 January 2025). Crossing 1 again: disaster grants and contracts moved without a secretary's signature queue, which helped state and local governments waiting on FEMA money — a kept word whose beneficiaries include governments that opposed his confirmation.
  • Mullin's FEMA-administrator word — kept in form, contested in substance. He promised the Senate a nominee with real emergency-management experience and said "absolutely" when asked (Kim statement summarizing the hearing, March 2026). Having pledged in March 2026 not to judge shootings before investigations, Mullin made no equivalent same-day characterization after the July 2026 fatal ICE shootings in Houston and Biddeford, Maine, in the record assembled here — an absence recorded as an absence, and a contrast with January 2026 that the file notes without overstating.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "Criminals first" — the priority word, not kept as stated. Noem's hearing pledge put migrants with criminal records first, "then" those with final orders; the platform's public form was "the worst of the worst." Beyond this instance, further crossing-3 cases are not documented in the record assembled here, and none are invented.

Overall Summary

The Secretary of Homeland Security seat in 2025–2026 was held by two people with opposite failure modes, and the dossier grades the seat across both because that is what the record is.
