The Ruler's Test — Paul Kagame, President of Rwanda

Subject: Paul Kagame — President of Rwanda since 2000 (interim president from 24 March 2000), and the country's de facto leader since 1994, when the Rwandan Patriotic Front he commanded ended the genocide against the Tutsi and he became vice president and minister of defence. He has won four presidential elections — 2003 (~95%), 2010 (~93%), 2017 (98.79%) and 2024 (99.18%) — and under constitutional amendments approved by referendum in 2015 he may remain in office until 2034. He remains president at grading, in his fourth term. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Anti-corruption enforcement has repeatedly reached inside his own state and party. From late 2008, permanent secretaries, directors general, prison directors and revenue-authority staff were arrested in waves, with prosecutors stating they had the president's backing to follow corruption wherever it led (US Embassy Kigali reporting, March 2009). The posture has been maintained across his tenure: in 2020 he publicly restated "maximum pressure on the corrupt," asset declarations are required of officials, and Rwanda consistently ranks among Africa's least corrupt states on Transparency International's index (government statements; The EastAfrican, July 2020).
  • The genocide-accountability process has run against defendants regardless of the political cost, and its courts have acquitted where the evidence failed: in January 2024 Wenceslas Twagirayezu, extradited from Denmark, was acquitted of genocide and crimes against humanity by a Rwandan court (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2025).
  • Clemency has at times been applied broadly rather than only to friends: the September 2018 executive clemency released 2,140 prisoners, including his most prominent imprisoned opponent, Victoire Ingabire (see Test 3).

Evidence against:

  • The constitution itself was rewritten for one man. Kagame was subject to a two-term limit expiring in 2017. A 2015 referendum — approved by a reported 98%, after a petition of some 3.7 million signatures and a parliamentary "national consultation" that reported finding only ten opponents in the country — exempted the sitting president by name in effect: it let him take a third seven-year term and then two five-year terms, potentially to 2034, and granted the president immunity from prosecution after leaving office, including for treason and constitutional violations. The general rule (two five-year terms) applies to everyone else; the exception applies to Kagame (Congressional Research Service, "Rwanda: In Brief," 2018–2019; US State Department testimony, 27 September 2017). A law with a personal exception at its center is the definition this test measures.
  • The candidacy rules operate asymmetrically in practice. In 2024 the National Electoral Commission barred Diane Rwigara — who said she had submitted 974 supporting signatures — for failing to provide a criminal-record statement, for signature shortfalls in eight districts, and for not proving Rwandan birth; courts rejected appeals by Victoire Ingabire and Bernard Ntaganda to clear the convictions that barred them (Reuters/AP reporting, June 2024). Rwigara had been disqualified in 2017 on forgery allegations, arrested with her mother and sister, held over a year, and then acquitted in December 2018; after her 2017 disqualification her family's business received a tax bill of some $6.5 million (contemporaneous reporting). The two candidates cleared to run in both 2017 and 2024 were the same two men, who took 0.50% and 0.32% in 2024 (NEC results).
  • Speech law reaches critics in a way it does not reach the powerful. Ingabire was sentenced in 2012 to eight years — increased to 15 by the Supreme Court — on charges including minimising the genocide, over remarks calling for recognition of Hutu victims alongside Tutsi victims; the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights ruled in November 2017 that Rwanda had violated her rights to freedom of expression and a fair trial, and ordered reparations in December 2018 (AfCHPR judgments, 2017–2018). She was rearrested on 19 June 2025 (Test 2). The government's position, printed here as its position: the laws against genocide ideology and divisionism are necessary safeguards in a country where ideology killed a million people in a hundred days, and are applied to conduct, not opinion.
  • The 2015 referendum process itself is contested: the Democratic Green Party, the only significant opposition party, was not permitted to campaign against the changes, and scholars document intimidation around the vote (SSRC, "Abusing Referenda to Consolidate and Extend Power"; Atlantic Council, October 2015). Kagame disavowed responsibility for the initiative, saying the people demanded it — a claim graded in Test 5.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, media, local governments, and the military keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Some institutional life persists in the open. The Democratic Green Party holds seats in the Chamber of Deputies and campaigns nationally; its leader Frank Habineza contested both the 2017 and 2024 elections and criticizes the president's longevity on the record. Courts have acquitted defendants the state prosecuted (Twagirayezu, 2024; Rwigara and her mother, 2018). Local government delivers services through elected councils, and Rwanda's administrative machinery is widely assessed as among the most effective in the region (World Bank and IMF country reporting).
  • The military's subordination runs the other way from most states in this series: the RDF is disciplined, professionalized, and has not intervened in politics against the president — because the president is its founder and former commander, a fact that belongs equally to the against-shelf.

Evidence against:

  • The electoral commission is not an independent referee on this record: it excluded the sitting president's most credible challengers in 2017 and again in 2024 on documentary grounds (Test 1), and announced results — 99.18% on 98.2% turnout in 2024 — that Human Rights Watch and other observers describe as the product of a system in which Rwandans "were not able to freely express their views and exercise their vote fairly and peacefully" (HRW, World Report 2025; NEC results, July 2024).
  • The judiciary's handling of the Ingabire file shows the grip at its clearest documented point. Ingabire was summoned as a witness in the trial of eight members of her party and a journalist on 17–19 June 2025; the three-judge panel, reportedly unsatisfied with her answers, ordered the prosecutor to investigate her directly and ordered her detained — and the same judges are to decide her case. The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found the detention of Ingabire and her co-defendants arbitrary (Opinion No. 3/2026); the European Parliament called for her unconditional release (resolution of 11 September 2025); on 27 March 2026 the Supreme Court rejected her constitutional challenge to the arrest, clearing the prosecution — which seeks a life sentence — to proceed (HRW, 24 June 2025; Human Rights Foundation statements, 2025–2026). The underlying "offence" documented by HRW: party members attended an online training on nonviolent resistance and read Srđa Popović's Blueprint for Revolution; the use of pseudonyms and an encrypted platform during the training is itself cited as evidence of crime.
  • Media autonomy is punished, not held. Journalist Dieudonné Niyonsenga (Ishema TV) was acquitted in March 2021, rearrested in November 2021 after the prosecution appealed and the acquittal was reversed, and told a Kigali court in January 2024 — visibly wounded, per court transcripts reported by VOA — that he is held in a flooded "hole," beaten frequently, and denied his trial documents; HRW records no judicial investigation of the torture allegations. Investigative journalist John Williams Ntwali died in January 2023 in what authorities called a road accident; HRW and press-freedom groups have called for a credible investigation and record that none has transparently accounted for his death. Journalist Théoneste Nsengimana has been detained since 2021 in the Ingabire co-defendants' case. In May 2024 Rwanda denied entry to a senior HRW researcher at Kigali airport (HRW, World Report 2025; HRW, "Rwanda: End Abuses Against Journalists," 2024).
  • Civil society's leash was shortened by law: the 2024 NGO law gives the Rwanda Governance Board discretionary power to approve or remove CSO leaders and suspend operations (CIVICUS/CEMIRIDE submission to the UN Universal Periodic Review, 2025). In August 2024 authorities closed more than 8,000 churches and prayer houses on compliance grounds and banned 43 religious groups (Local Government Ministry figures, via HRW).
  • The ruling party and the state are fused at the top: Kagame chairs the RPF, whose coalition took 68.83% of the parliamentary vote in 2024; he personally retires and dismisses generals in batches (a dozen generals and 83 senior officers retired on 30 August 2023, with further dismissals the next day — Political Chronicle of the African Great Lakes Region 2023). No institution in the record — court, commission, broadcaster, or barracks — is documented as having said no to him and made it stand.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He has adjusted, materially, under judicial and diplomatic pressure — twice on the two most famous prisoner files. After the African Court's November 2017 ruling in Ingabire's favor, Kagame pardoned her in September 2018, releasing her with 2,139 others (while warning, without naming her, that those who believed they had been freed by foreign pressure "could land back in prison" — contemporaneous reporting). In March 2023, after the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found Paul Rusesabagina had been illegally rendered, tortured and unfairly tried, and after the United States formally designated him wrongfully detained, Kagame commuted Rusesabagina's 25-year sentence; he was flown home via Doha (UN WGAD Opinion No. 81/2021, 18 March 2022; US Senate Foreign Relations Committee statement, 24 March 2023). The conviction was retained — the adjustment was real but partial, and was transacted, never acknowledged as correction.
  • He signed the Washington peace agreement with the DRC on 27 June 2025 and the Washington Accords with President Tshisekedi on 4 December 2025, committing Rwanda to disengagement of its forces in step with the neutralisation of the FDLR (agreement texts; Joint Oversight Committee statements, 2025). His government also adjusted to US migration policy in 2025, agreeing to accept up to 250 US deportees, the first of whom arrived in August 2025 (Reuters/AP, August 2025).
  • On the economic file, adjustment is routine and documented: his governments have repeatedly reformed under IMF and World Bank program reviews, and Rwanda's poverty and service-delivery gains (Test 4) are partly the product of that responsiveness.

Evidence against:

  • On the Congo file, the signature was not followed by the adjustment it promised. The June 2025 agreement's annex set a three-month horizon for FDLR neutralisation and the lifting of Rwanda's "defensive measures"; by mid-September 2025 implementation had stalled with no Rwandan withdrawal, and less than a week after the December 2025 Washington Accords, M23 seized Uvira. The UN Group of Experts' June 2026 report estimated 14,000–18,000 RDF troops in eastern Congo as of December 2025 — a larger presence than the ~6,000 estimated for early 2025 — and found no evidence of significant withdrawals; the area held by M23 grew by more than 35% between March 2025 and spring 2026 (UN Group of Experts reporting, 2025–2026; UN Special Envoy Huang Xia to the Security Council, 13 October 2026: "a genuine ceasefire has yet to materialize"). UN Security Council Resolution 2773 (21 February 2025) had already called unanimously for the RDF to stop supporting M23 and withdraw without preconditions. Rwanda's position, printed as its position: it does not support M23; its measures are defensive against the FDLR, a genocidaire force embedded with the Congolese army; and the DRC has not met its obligations.
  • The public posture toward pushback is denial and defiance, in his own words. After Patrick Karegeya's killing (Test 4), Kagame told a national prayer breakfast on 12 January 2014: "You can't betray Rwanda and not get punished for it… Anyone will reap the consequences. It is a matter of time" — while officially denying involvement; the US State Department said it was "deeply concerned" by the comments (Reuters, January 2014). In April 2025 he told critics of Rwanda's Congo policy they could "go to hell" (widely reported from the Kwibuka 31 address); Rwanda severed diplomatic ties with Belgium in March 2025 after Belgium pressed for sanctions. When the US Treasury sanctioned the RDF on 2 March 2026 and the Ministry of Defence and the military's Zigama bank on 28 October 2026, Kagame answered in a Jeune Afrique interview (3 April 2026) that "sanctions and threats are nothing but insults thrown in the face of my country," that Washington must push both parties evenly, and: "Don't expect me to lift our defence measures while you are doing nothing to stop what threatens my country" (US Treasury OFAC statements, 2026; Jeune Afrique interview as reported by regional press, April 2026).
  • Domestic pushback is answered the same way: Ingabire was rearrested in June 2025 while the European Parliament's and UN Working Group's calls were pending, and her constitutional challenge was rejected in March 2026 (Test 2). The pattern across three decades is consistent: concession when a transaction is available (a prisoner's release for diplomatic relief), denial where the underlying claim — that the system itself grips — would have to be admitted.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Hutu citizens, the Congolese in the east, critics and their families, the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The domestic development count is real, broad, and documented by sources independent of his government. Monetary poverty fell from 39.8% in 2017 to 27.4% in 2024, extreme poverty halved to 5.4%, and roughly 1.5 million people were lifted out of poverty in seven years (National Institute of Statistics EICV7, as reported by the UN Special Rapporteur on extreme poverty, Olivier De Schutter, April 2026 — who also criticized the model's weak job creation and rural reach). Community-based health insurance (mutuelles de santé) covers almost 90% of the population; life expectancy has risen to nearly 70 years (World Bank Human Capital Index Plus reporting, 2025). These gains are not ethnically gated: the unity framework formally abolished ethnic categories, and services reach Hutu and Tutsi citizens alike on the documented aggregate record.
  • Rwanda hosts and counts other countries' displaced: it has run the UNHCR Emergency Transit Mechanism for evacuees from Libya since 2019 (over 2,700 received by 2025) and hosts a large Congolese and Burundian refugee population (UNHCR reporting).

Evidence against:

  • The Congolese in the east are counted out at scale, on the UN's documented findings. The UN Group of Experts has reported for years — most recently in June 2026 — that the RDF operates in the DRC in support of M23, estimating ~6,000 Rwandan troops in early 2025 rising to 14,000–18,000 by December 2025; M23 took Goma (27 January 2025), Bukavu (February 2025) and Uvira (10 December 2025). The UN Human Rights Council's Fact-Finding Mission concluded in 2025 that all parties, including M23 and the RDF, have perpetrated violations that may constitute war crimes and/or crimes against humanity, including killings, sexual violence, torture and child recruitment (UN HRC Fact-Finding Mission, 2025; Global Centre for the Responsibility to Protect summary). Kagame's government denies supporting M23 and denies the troop presence as framed; its stated count is that Rwanda faces an existential FDLR threat the world ignores. Both positions are on the record; the troop estimates are the UN experts' documented findings, not a court's verdict.
  • Hutu citizens are counted in services and counted out of politics. The political figures prosecuted under the genocide-ideology and divisionism laws are disproportionately those who raise Hutu grievances: Ingabire's original prosecution rested partly on her call to commemorate Hutu victims; her party remains unregistered, its members imprisoned (Tests 1–2). The government's defense is printed in Test 1. What the record shows is that the one political question a Hutu-led movement might organize around — the distribution of power — is the one question the law makes dangerous to ask.
  • Critics' families are inside the blast radius. Rwigara's mother and sister were charged alongside her in 2017 (her mother was acquitted with her in 2018). Rusesabagina's family alleged in US federal court that Rwandan officials surveilled them; the court allowed claims against three named officials to proceed on 16 March 2023 — days before his release was announced (US District Court for DC, as reported). Gospel singer Kizito Mihigo, pardoned alongside Ingabire in 2018, was rearrested in February 2020 and died in a police cell days later; authorities said suicide, and his family and rights groups disputed the account and called for an independent investigation — the dispute is recorded here as a dispute, unresolved on the public record.
  • Transnational repression — ALLEGATION, stated at exact strength. Patrick Karegeya, Rwanda's former external intelligence chief, exiled in South Africa, was found strangled in a Johannesburg hotel on 1 January 2014. The Rwanda National Congress accused Kagame's agents; South Africa expelled three Rwandan diplomats in March 2014; a South African inquest process begun in 2019 heard evidence the presiding magistrate described as suggesting "direct involvement of the Rwandan government," and arrest warrants were later issued for two government-linked Rwandan suspects (Freedom House, Defending Democracy in Exile, 2022). No court has convicted anyone; the Randburg inquest was struck from the roll in January 2019 for want of produced suspects before being revived in reporting; Rwanda denies any involvement, calling Karegeya a self-declared enemy of the state. Former army chief Kayumba Nyamwasa survived a 2010 shooting in Johannesburg that his family attributes to Kigali and Kigali denies. These items are recorded as documented ALLEGATIONs with official denials — they are not findings against Kagame, and this dossier does not grade them as such; they are graded only as part of the documented pattern that exiled critics are not safe in the count.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record since 2000, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Development delivery — kept at unusual fidelity. The material promises of his rule — security, functioning services, rising incomes, infrastructure — were substantially performed: poverty 39.8% → 27.4% (2017–2024), health insurance to ~90% coverage, life expectancy near 70, sustained high growth across two decades (Test 4 sources; IMF/World Bank country data). Whatever else this dossier grades, the word he gave on building a working state was kept.
  • Anti-corruption — kept as posture and practice. "Zero tolerance" has been enforced against officials of his own system across his tenure (Test 1), and Rwanda's corruption indicators remain among the continent's best (Transparency International).
  • The retirement conversation — opened by him, repeatedly. In April 2023, beside Kenya's President Ruto, he called his retirement an "inevitability," said the succession discussion had run inside the RPF since 2010, and joked he might "join journalism in my old age"; at the RPF congress the same month he tasked the party with finding a successor and asked why, every time he is re-elected with 99.9%, "it can't be someone else" (KT Press and contemporaneous reporting, April 2023). In July 2024 he refused to anoint a successor: "I told my party that I would not anoint anyone. They have to find someone among many people" (KT Press, July 2024).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Term limits — the founding political promise, reversed. The United States engaged Kagame privately and publicly in 2015 "urging President Kagame to honor the commitment he had made to respect term limits when he first assumed office," and expressed "deep disappointment" when he instead accepted the referendum's personal exemption (US State Department testimony, 27 September 2017). He then ran in 2017 and again in 2024. The retirement talk of April 2023 was followed within five months by his September 2023 announcement, in Jeune Afrique, that he was "indeed a candidate" for 2024 (Political Chronicle of the African Great Lakes Region 2023). As of grading there is no successor, no timetable, and a constitution that permits him to stay until 2034 — the succession "discussion since 2010" has run for sixteen years without producing the one thing it discusses.
  • The Washington word — signed, then not performed on its own terms. The June and December 2025 agreements committed Rwanda to disengagement in step with FDLR neutralisation; the UN experts' finding that the RDF presence grew through December 2025, and M23's seizure of Uvira days after the December signing, are the documented record against the commitment (Test 3). Kagame's counter-position — that Congo failed its side first and the mediator applied the agreement unevenly — is on the record and is graded as his position, not as performance.
  • The denial that became the record. Rwanda has denied supporting M23 in every forum since 2022, while the UN Group of Experts, the UN Security Council (Resolution 2773), the HRC Fact-Finding Mission and, in 2026, the US Treasury have each documented RDF support and presence. A denial maintained against that weight of documented findings is itself a word-file fact: either the findings are wrong — which no Rwandan rebuttal has demonstrated to the experts' satisfaction — or the word was not kept. This dossier records the conflict; it does not resolve it.
  • The long arc, factually: the commander who ended a genocide in 1994 built the region's most effective state and its most complete presidency. His word splits along the same line as every other test in this file: on things a state delivers — roads, insurance, order, growth — his promises are among the most reliably kept on the continent; on the one promise that concerns power itself — that he would respect its limits and eventually hand it over — the record is a quarter-century of deferral.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Paul Kagame's Rwanda is the series' hardest file to grade honestly, because both shelves are heavy. On one side: a state rebuilt from genocide into the region's most capable administration, poverty cut by a third in seven years, health insurance for nearly nine in ten citizens, corruption prosecuted inside his own system, and a leader who has twice released famous prisoners when the pressure was properly applied. On the other: a constitution with his name written into its exception, an electoral commission that bars his challengers, judges who order a witness investigated and then sit on her case, journalists beaten in cells the courts will not inspect, a neighbour's two richest provinces held by a rebellion the UN's experts say his army staffs and supplies — with the troop count rising after he signed a peace agreement — and exiles who are not safe in Johannesburg. The tests measure the seat, not the achievement, and on the evidence assembled here the seat gives by exception rather than by one law, grips every institution that could check it, and counts its flock with named exceptions at home and across the border. The adjustments that exist are real but transactional — made when leverage is applied, never owned as corrections. What would move these grades is also on the record: a withdrawal the UN experts can verify, a challenger allowed to run, a successor named. None has yet occurred.
