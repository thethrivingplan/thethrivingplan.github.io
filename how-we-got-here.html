How We Got Here

A full day of work — research, a page, a film, and a rule that turned out to apply everywhere — started with one small video, honestly made, on a small account. This page gives the credit where it happened, in the open, and tells the chain in order.

The spark

It started on TikTok

A screen recording came in from TikTok: a video explaining a search for Dyson spheres — structures a civilization might build around a star, catching its light and giving the energy back as waste heat.

“Analysis of Potential Dyson Sphere Discoveries in Space”

The video that sparked the hunt — credited to Kalpana Pot and her page TokNerdyToMe (@toknerdytome); see The Credit for how that credit is worded, and why.

The video explains Project Hephaistos, a real research program (Suazo et al. 2024, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society 531:695) that searched roughly five million stars for Dyson-sphere candidates by combining three sky surveys:

Gaia

The foundation

Positions and distances of the ~5 million stars — where everything sits.

2MASS

The middle

The near-infrared bridge between starlight and heat.

WISE

The burn

The waste heat itself — the infrared glow a swarm would leave behind.

The moment it turned

“That sounds like a Lonsdaleite test”

The reaction, in substance: the three instruments together sound like a Lonsdaleite test. Three readers, three ways of looking, and a thing that only exists where all three agree. The reading that followed mapped the hunt onto the three modes:

  • Golden — the spread. Five million stars went in and nobody aimed at any one of them. A Dyson swarm is itself a spread, never a shell — energy caught where it's needed, not wrapped whole.
  • Platinum — the 1:1 test. The light missing from a star must equal the heat coming back extra. What goes out has to match what shows up.
  • Diamond — the weave. A candidate exists only where all three surveys agree — and the weave caught its own false positives: background galaxies standing behind the stars, wearing their heat.

What the research found

Seven maybes, zero confirmed

The same day, the seven Hephaistos candidates were checked against the follow-up work (Ren et al. 2024–2026; Zackrisson et al. 2026, Hephaistos IV, preprint; Blain 2024). Tap a letter to see where each one stands:

None of the seven stands as a confirmed Dyson candidate. Nothing was forced to make the count come out nicer — the honest result is the result.

What got built from it

One video, a whole day

  1. The Seven Maybes — a page mapping the seven candidates as a convergence map, not a leaderboard.
  2. “Three Eyes, Three Modes — The Dyson Hunt” — a narrated vertical video of the hunt and its honest result: seven maybes, zero confirmed, nothing forced.
  3. The follow-on question — “the line doesn't stop, so how do we follow it?” — answered with the same three modes: add readers, and weave them.
  4. The three-ish rule — named the same afternoon, below.

Where it landed

The three-ish rule

Three is the smallest number that makes a pattern — but the threes fluctuate, the way the matched frequencies did: hovering around a center none of them lands on exactly. So the rule isn't “exactly three, every time.” It's:

Look for the third. It will be there in some measure — two and a half, two and sixteen seventeenths — and the measure is the reading.

The same rule read the seven candidates honestly: three fully closed, two nearly closed, one weakened, one untouched — a live count hovering around one and a half to two and a fraction real maybes, moving every time a sharper eye looks. And it lands at every scale it touches: two people is a thread and the third is where a circle starts; an economy works when its third — the flow between having and needing — flows, and fails when that third gets dammed.

For something this big, the name is almost nonchalant. That's the tell. The load-bearing things say it plain and keep walking.
