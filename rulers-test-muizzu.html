The Ruler's Test — Mohamed Muizzu, President of the Maldives

Subject: Mohamed Muizzu — President of the Maldives since 17 November 2023; previously Mayor of Malé (2021–2023) and Minister of Housing and Infrastructure under President Abdulla Yameen (2012–2018). He won the September 2023 presidential runoff with about 54% of the vote as the PPM–PNC candidate, standing in for Yameen, who was barred from contesting by a corruption conviction. His People's National Congress (PNC) won 66 of 93 seats in the People's Majlis on 21 April 2024, a supermajority that grew to roughly 75–79 seats with allies and post-election party switches. He remains president in November 2026, his term running to November 2028; in April 2026 his party lost all five city mayoralties to the opposition MDP and voters rejected his constitutional referendum by 68.7% to 31.3%. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He broke the Maldives' governing pattern of prosecuting the predecessor. Since taking office in November 2023, Muizzu has brought no politically charged cases against former President Ibrahim Mohamed Solih, former President Mohamed Nasheed, or senior MDP leaders — a documented departure from a national history in which transfers of power were repeatedly followed by prosecutions, imprisonment, or exile of former leaders (Maldives News Network analysis, 2026; consistent absence across the public record).
  • The retrial of his own former patron proceeded against his political interest in both directions. Yameen — whose imprisonment made Muizzu's candidacy possible, and whose release Muizzu had pledged — was transferred to house arrest on 1 October 2023, and on 18 April 2024 the High Court overturned his 11-year bribery and money-laundering conviction for trial unfairness and ordered a retrial. The state has continued to prosecute that retrial, including petitioning the Supreme Court to admit new evidence against Yameen, even after Yameen left the ruling coalition, formed his own party, and became a vocal critic. Muizzu has repeatedly stated in public that he will not influence the courts, including when criticised for not freeing Yameen outright (High Court ruling, 18 April 2024; Sun and Atoll Times reporting, 2024–2025). Yameen's allegation that Muizzu never wanted him freed is an ALLEGATION by an interested party, printed here as his position, not as a finding.
  • The anti-corruption machinery was at least formally set in motion against the most protected figures in the system — the sitting Supreme Court justices (Test 2) — on the stated basis of a criminal investigation by the Anti-Corruption Commission (ACC), not by presidential order (JSC and ACC statements, February 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The one time the machinery moved at full speed, it moved against the judges who were about to rule on his government's amendment — and the underlying case then evaporated. The ACC investigation cited to suspend three Supreme Court justices on 26 February 2025 produced no charges; the ACC described it as still ongoing, as a "special priority," more than a year later, and in October 2026 the authorities dropped the corruption investigation citing lack of evidence of criminal wrongdoing (ACC statements via Sun, 2026; Sun reporting, October 2026). By then the justices had been out of office for nearly a year and a half. The sequence is documented; whether it was engineered is CONTESTED — the justices and the opposition say the case was manufactured as a pretext (an ALLEGATION, denied by the administration), while the administration maintains the institutions acted independently.
  • The outcome measure is flat. The Maldives scored 38/100 on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index in 2024 and 39/100 in 2025 (91st of 182), inside the same 38–43 band as the previous five years. Transparency Maldives attributed the stagnation to "politicized appointments," "opaque decision-making in state-owned enterprises," and legislative changes — including amendments to the Anti-Corruption Commission Act and the Elections Commission Act allowing the President to appoint the president and vice president of both commissions (Transparency Maldives statement, 10 February 2026). The ACC publicly disputes the CPI's perception-based methodology (ACC statement, 11 February 2026) — printed here as its position.
  • No major corruption case against any figure inside his own governing camp appears in the documented record of his first three years, while the October 2025 opposition protests centred on allegations of widespread graft (protest reporting, October 2025). The ACC itself "continues to face public criticism for failing to investigate major corruption cases," with several major cases pending without investigation (Sun, February 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the media regulator, the electoral authority, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Muizzu personally blocked the single most direct legislative instrument of grip. On 26 February 2025 his supermajority passed — under a three-line whip, 68–9 — a Judicature Act amendment cutting the Supreme Court bench from seven to five and requiring the Judicial Service Commission (JSC) to name two justices for removal as "incompetent" within days. On 11 March 2025 Muizzu returned the bill to Parliament without ratification, on the written advice of his Attorney General Ahmed Usham that it was unconstitutional; Parliament sent it back to committee on 17 March 2025 and it was never enacted (President's Office and Attorney General statements, March 2025; Atoll Times, March 2025). The bench stayed at seven.
  • The central bank governorship changed hands through the statutory process: Parliament's Public Accounts Committee recommended Governor Ali Hashim's dismissal for inadequate performance (63–9 in the Majlis, 22 July 2024), Muizzu dismissed him the same day, and his nominee Ahmed Munawar — a former finance minister then serving as an adviser to the finance minister — was interviewed by the committee and approved unanimously (78 votes) on 30 July 2024. Munawar told the committee on the record that he would not "follow the government blindly" and would resign if pressed to act against the country's interest (Atoll Times and President's Office records, July 2024).
  • Elections under his administration have been administered in a way the opposition could win: the April 2026 local elections and referendum (Test 3) were run by the Elections Commission and produced sweeping opposition victories that took immediate effect.

Evidence against:

  • The judiciary file is the heaviest in this dossier. On 20 November 2024 the PNC supermajority passed the Sixth Amendment — anti-defection provisions stripping MPs of their seats if they leave, resign from, or are expelled by their party — in under a day, with no public consultation, and Muizzu ratified it the same evening (Majlis records; Maldives Independent, November 2024). A constitutional challenge was filed; the full seven-justice bench heard arguments on 17 February 2025 and was due to rule on a stay of the amendment. On 26 February 2025 — about fifteen minutes before that hearing, and the same day Parliament passed the bench-cutting bill — the JSC suspended Justices Azmiralda Zahir, Mahaz Ali Zahir, and Husnu Al Suood, citing the ACC investigation. Five justices are required for constitutional cases; the challenge was frozen. Suood resigned in protest. The JSC then pursued misconduct cases against Azmiralda and Mahaz — alleging they conspired to influence a Criminal Court judge over the arrest of Azmiralda's husband (an ALLEGATION both justices deny as baseless). Parliament's own Counsel General, Fathimath Filza, advised the Judiciary Committee that the JSC had failed to follow due process; the committee denied the justices a chance to defend themselves; Parliament dismissed both on 14 May 2025, 68–11 (Maldives Independent explainer, May 2025; JSC, Majlis, and Bar Council records). Azmiralda has alleged she was pressured through a message conveyed from the Attorney General's office on 8 February 2025 — an ALLEGATION the government formally denies, stating neither the President nor the Attorney General influenced the justices (Foreign Minister Abdulla Khaleel's letter to the UN, 2025).
  • Every external referee who examined the sequence reached the same conclusion. The UN Special Rapporteur on the independence of judges and lawyers, Margaret Satterthwaite, wrote on 2 April 2025 that the proceedings appeared aimed at impeding judicial review of the anti-defection amendment and "appear to violate the principle that judges can only be dismissed on serious grounds of misconduct or incompetence and in accordance with fair procedures." The UN Human Rights Office said the dismissals raised "serious concerns about respect for the independence of the judiciary" (OHCHR, 19 May 2025). The Bar Council of Maldives raised due-process concerns throughout, and the Commonwealth Lawyers Association and other international legal bodies wrote jointly to Muizzu urging the impeachment be suspended; Parliament proceeded anyway. Muizzu's documented answer, at a 15-hour press conference on 3 May 2025, was denial: he had not interfered, and the replacement of his own representative on the JSC days before the probe was a "coincidence" (press conference reporting, May 2025). His two nominees to the vacated seats, Mohamed Saleem and Abdulla Hameed, were approved unanimously on 11 June 2025. The reconstituted court finally decided the anti-defection case in April 2026 — upholding the amendment and its application to sitting MPs, over a dissent; the MDP's statement that the bench that ruled "is not the bench before which the case was originally brought" is a matter of record (Supreme Court judgment, April 2026; MDP press release, April 2026).
  • The media were brought under a state-built regulator in 31 days. The Maldives Media and Broadcasting Regulation Act (Act No. 16/2025), introduced on 19 August 2025 by a government-aligned MP, dissolved the Maldives Media Council and the Broadcasting Commission and replaced them with a single Maldives Media and Broadcasting Commission: seven members, three appointed directly by the President, with Parliament empowered to remove even the members elected by media outlets by no-confidence vote. The commission can fine outlets (MVR 5,000–100,000), order corrections, and suspend registrations. Journalists were locked out of committee meetings on 9 and 15 September 2025; a petition signed by 151 journalists was dismissed; the Majlis passed the bill on 16 September and Muizzu ratified it on 18 September 2025, disregarding appeals from the Maldives Journalists Association (MJA), the Committee to Protect Journalists, and UN bodies (Maldives Independent timeline, September 2025; Act 16/2025). The MJA vowed civil disobedience and called the law "draconian." In 2026 the commission used the Act to order the takedown of a political cartoon (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026).
  • The pattern extends to the referees of elections and corruption themselves: amendments to the Elections Commission Act and the Anti-Corruption Commission Act allow the President to appoint the president and vice president of both bodies — a change Transparency Maldives identifies as politicizing "key oversight bodies" (Transparency Maldives, February 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, journalists, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He accepted the largest electoral rebuke of his presidency in public and at once. On 4 April 2026, at record 73% turnout for local elections, voters rejected his Eighth Amendment referendum — concurrent presidential and parliamentary elections and a shortened Majlis term — by 68.7% to 31.3%, and the MDP won more council seats overall (238 to the PNC's 216) and all five city mayoralties. On 5 April 2026 Muizzu congratulated the winners on social media, acknowledged the outcome as the people's will, and pledged to listen to "the aspirations and needs of the Maldivian people" (Elections Commission results; President's Office and Maldives Independent reporting, April 2026). The amendment died with the vote; no attempt was made to revive it by other means.
  • On the bench-cutting bill, the pushback worked on him directly: the Bar Council urged him not to ratify, his own Attorney General advised the bill was unconstitutional, and he returned it to Parliament rather than sign it (March 2025, Test 2) — a public reversal of his own parliamentary group's whipped vote.
  • After UN criticism of the justices' removal, his government formally invited the UN Special Rapporteur to visit and observe the judiciary, while denying the interference allegations in writing (Foreign Ministry letter, 2025). Protesters arrested during the October 2025 demonstrations (eight people) were subsequently granted amnesty (2025 Maldivian protests record).

Evidence against:

  • On the media law, every pushback was overridden: a 151-journalist petition, the journalist union's protests, locked-out committee hearings, a global chorus including UN recommendations — and the bill was still passed in 31 days and ratified within 48 hours of passage, triggering two months of street protests (20 September – 18 November 2025) that demanded, among other things, his resignation. CIVICUS's summary is documented fact: the government "ignores UN recommendations with passage of draconian media law" (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025–2026). Police disrupted opposition and fisherfolk protests with arrests the Monitor records as involving ill-treatment.
  • On the justices, the answer was denial without adjustment. Confronted with the UN Special Rapporteur, the Bar Council, his own Parliament's Counsel General, and a Sun poll in which 70% of respondents believed the ouster was politically motivated, Muizzu's documented responses were that he had not interfered and that the suspicious timings were "coincidences" (press conference, 3 May 2025; Sun poll, 2026). The dismissals proceeded on the original timetable.
  • His explanation of the April 2026 referendum defeat located the fault in his own party's campaigning, not the proposal: at Rasdhoo on 18 April 2026 he told supporters the PNC had failed "because of shortcomings within the party's own campaign teams" and a lack of adequate campaign work (Sun, 18 April 2026). The result was accepted; its message about the policy was not owned.
  • Transparency toward the IMF narrowed as the numbers worsened: the Fund's Executive Board concluded the 2025 and 2026 Article IV consultations, but in both years the staff reports went unpublished because, in the IMF's formula, "the authorities need more time to consider the publication of the staff report." The last published Maldives staff report dates from May 2024 (IMF records, March 2025 and September 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — migrant workers, the outer atolls, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His opponents' voters are counted, in the only way that finally matters: the April 2026 elections were held on schedule, at record turnout, the opposition won the cities and the seat plurality, its mayors took office, and the President congratulated them. No result was annulled, delayed, or litigated away by the state (Elections Commission; KAS country report, May 2026).
  • The flagship social pledge is universalist in design. Housing — his stated "foremost pledge" — is framed for every Maldivian over 18 without a home, native Malé residents and long-term residents alike, with no partisan or island test; agreements were signed for 825 housing units across 13 outer islands, and the Bank of Maldives was tasked with 3,000 further units (President's Office statements, November 2025).
  • Migrant workers — roughly a quarter of the population, over 90,000 of them Bangladeshi nationals alone as of December 2023 — were counted into a registry rather than simply expelled. Operation Kurangi (launched May 2024) collected biometric data from 191,723 expatriates; its second phase regularised undocumented workers, with Immigration reporting over 60% of expatriate workers brought into compliance; a dedicated centre and portal were established to protect migrant workers' rights, and compliant "System Trusted Employers" were given streamlined processes (UN Maldives migration governance review; Maldives Immigration statements, 2025–2026).

Evidence against:

  • The outer atolls were counted out of self-government. Decentralisation amendments under his administration transferred administrative and fiscal powers from elected island councils to central authorities — Transparency Maldives records the changes as "eroding local autonomy, limiting community oversight, and weakening participatory governance" (February 2026) — and a constitutional amendment abolished the elected atoll councils outright, effective 27 May 2026, shifting their powers to the central Local Government Authority. Restriction of local government authority was one of the three stated causes of the 2025 protests. The sequence has a direction: each layer of elected government between Malé and the citizen was weakened or removed on his watch, even as the April 2026 vote showed those same voters choosing the opposition where they still could.
  • For migrant workers, the enforcement side of the ledger is larger than the protection side. More than 9,000 foreigners were deported in his first two years — the government itself contrasts this with 2,165 deportations in the preceding five years, a 143% increase it presents as an achievement — rising to over 11,000–12,000 by 2026, with a final phase announced in 2026 to locate and deport anyone who had not submitted biometric data within six months (Home Ministry statements, November 2025; President's posts and Immigration statements, 2026). The UN review notes the underlying vulnerability the registry does not cure: by December 2023 only 36% of 139,000 issued work permits had corresponding fee payments, a system in which undocumented status is produced by employers as much as by workers (UN Maldives, 2025).
  • The poor carry the adjustment. The World Bank's 2026 update projects growth collapsing to 0.7% in 2026 (from 6.3% in 2025), the fiscal deficit blowing back out above the government's own target, and public debt climbing above 140% of GDP over the medium term; the IMF keeps the Maldives at high risk of debt distress, with reserves covering under a month of imports on some measures (World Bank and IMF, 2026). Rising prices were a stated grievance of the October 2025 protests. These are outcomes, not intentions — graded here because the test asks who is in the accounting when the bills arrive.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since November 2023, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Indian troops out — kept, on the deadline. The centrepiece of the 2023 "India Out" campaign was a pledge to remove Indian military personnel by 10 May 2024. India completed the withdrawal of its roughly 80 military personnel by that date, replacing them with civilian technical staff to operate the gifted helicopters and Dornier aircraft (Indian and Maldivian government statements, May 2024).
  • No default — kept. He inherited what his finance minister called a debt crisis and a US$500 million sukuk bullet due April 2026. The sukuk was repaid in full in April 2026; the ruling party documents US$1.29 billion in external debt repaid over two and a half years, and Moody's upgraded the Maldives to Caa1 in October 2026 (Finance Ministry statements; Moody's, 8 October 2026). The debt was serviced on schedule throughout — at the cost recorded under Test 4.
  • The China free trade agreement — delivered. Signed in 2016 under Yameen and long stalled, the Maldives–China FTA entered into force on 1 January 2025 under his administration, as pledged in his economic programme.
  • Yameen's freedom — delivered by the route he said he would use. He pledged Yameen would be freed; Yameen was transferred home the day after the runoff and freed by the High Court in April 2024. Muizzu refused demands — including from Yameen himself — to short-circuit the courts, and the retrial continues (Test 1).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "India Out" as a posture — reversed. The letter of the pledge (uniformed personnel) was kept; its spirit was inverted within eighteen months. Muizzu made a state visit to India in October 2024 and adopted a "Comprehensive Economic and Maritime Security Partnership"; on 25–26 July 2025 Prime Minister Modi was his guest of honour at the 60th Independence Day — the first head of government Muizzu hosted — announcing a US$565 million Indian line of credit for his government's "priority projects" (IDSA Issue Brief, 2025). A campaign run against Indian influence ended with Indian credit financing the government. Whether this is statesmanship or reversal is the reader's call; that the 2023 campaign's central implication did not survive contact with office is documented fact.
  • Housing — the foremost pledge is years behind its own announcements. By November 2025, two years in, keys had been issued to 84 recipients of the 4,000 FDC flats his administration inherited unfinished, with the remainder promised "before Ramadan." The Rasmalé centrepiece — 15,000 plots in the first phase, 25,000 in total — remained, at his second anniversary, a schedule of future announcements: reclamation to complete in April 2026, recipient lists to be finalised after that, applications to open 4 January (President's Office statements, November 2025). The pledge will be judged at term's end in 2028; on the documented midpoint record, delivery is a small fraction of the promise.
  • An independent judiciary — the pledge his presidency is measured against. Muizzu campaigned and took office pledging no political influence over the judiciary. The documented February–May 2025 sequence — suspensions fifteen minutes before the hearing on his amendment, a bench-cutting bill from his own party, dismissals over his Parliament's own Counsel General's due-process advice, and the underlying corruption investigation dropped for lack of evidence in October 2026 after the seats were filled — is set out under Test 2. His denial of interference is on the record and printed there. The word and the record do not match, whichever account of the machinery is believed.
  • The long arc, factually: Muizzu rose as Yameen's housing minister and Malé's mayor, won the presidency as the stand-in for an imprisoned patron on an anti-India platform, won a supermajority, and then governed as a centraliser: the anti-defection amendment binding MPs to their parties, the atoll councils abolished, a state media regulator, and a 2026 referendum to merge the elections that would have bound the legislature's calendar to his own. The voters' April 2026 answer — no, by more than two to one — is the largest single entry in his file, and to his credit it stands unchallenged in the record.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Mohamed Muizzu holds a supermajority, the presidency, and — since May 2025 — a Supreme Court whose composition his parliamentary majority determined in the middle of a case about his own constitutional amendment. The grip test fails on that sequence and on the media law that followed it: a single regulator with presidential appointees, built in 31 days over the organised objection of the country's journalists. Against that record stand facts of a different kind, and they are not small: he returned his own party's bench-cutting bill as unconstitutional; he has not prosecuted the men he replaced, breaking his country's ugliest tradition; he repaid the sukuk and kept the troops-out deadline to the day; and when the voters took his referendum apart and gave the cities to the opposition in April 2026, he congratulated the winners and let the result stand. The tests measure the seat, not the man, and this seat has two halves that do not reconcile: one that accepts the people's no, and one that made sure the referees would not say it first.
