Teaching: Light

Pitching

Throwing a baseball hard and accurately is the fastest whole-body movement most people ever learn. It is not an arm action with a body attached — it is a chain, and the arm is the last link.

The delivery as a chain: legs → hips → trunk → arm

1 · Legs
The stride and the drive off the rubber start everything. Ground force is the biggest energy source you own.
→
2 · Hips
The pelvis turns first. Hips opening while the shoulders stay closed stretches the trunk like a twisted rope.
→
3 · Trunk
That stretch releases: the chest rotates and the stored energy passes up and speeds up.
→
4 · Arm
The arm rides the rotation. Each link moves faster than the one before it, like the tip of a whip.

Why each link matters: energy is made in the big muscles (legs, hips, trunk) and only delivered by the small ones (shoulder, elbow, wrist). When a lower link is late or weak, the arm has to make up the difference — and the arm pays for it in stress and lost accuracy. Most “arm problems” in young pitchers are really chain problems: the hips never turned, so the arm dragged and muscled the ball there.

Light note

Feel the order in slow motion: step, turn the belt buckle, then let the chest follow, then the arm. If your arm moves first, start over slower.

Coach note — the why under the why

Coaches call the gap between hip turn and shoulder turn hip–shoulder separation. The trunk muscles and fascia store elastic energy when stretched under load, then return it for free — no extra effort, just timing. That is why two pitchers with the same arm strength can throw very different speeds: one uses the stretch, the other fights it. Separation is not “twist as far as you can” — it is turn the hips while the shoulders stay back, then let the shoulders come through on their own timing.

Arm action and timing, in plain words

  1. Balance and gather. Lift the front leg, stay tall, keep your weight back a moment. Rushing here ruins everything after it.
  2. Stride and separate. As the leg strides toward the plate, the hands separate: glove toward the target, throwing hand down, back, and up — thumb roughly under the ball.
  3. Foot plant, hips fire. The front foot lands slightly closed, the front leg braces firm, and the hips rotate. The throwing elbow comes up to about shoulder height — not forced higher.
  4. Layback. As the trunk turns, the forearm tips back. This is the stretch that loads the shoulder. It happens because the trunk turned, not because you cranked the arm back.
  5. Release out front. The arm whips through and the ball leaves the fingers in front of the body, on a downward path toward the target.
  6. Follow through. The arm continues across the body and the back leg comes through. A full, loose finish is the sign the chain fired in order.
Coach note

The single most useful timing check on film: at the moment the front foot lands, is the throwing hand already up and ready (ball facing away, roughly at head height or rising)? If the hand is still down by the hip at foot plant, the arm is late, the trunk has to wait or the arm has to sprint — both cost command and stress the elbow. “Arm on time at foot plant” fixes more deliveries than any grip change.

Grips — drawn here, with what each one does and why

A grip changes three things: spin direction, spin speed, and pressure on the seams. Moving air around a spinning ball is what makes pitches move; the seams grip the air. All diagrams are original drawings made for this page — a ball seen from the pitcher’s view, red seam, fingers as dark pads.

Command before velocity — the order that works

The documented order of development is: throw strikes first, then throw strikes harder, then add pitches. A pitcher who can put a fastball on either edge owns the at-bat before it starts, because the hitter must respect both sides. Velocity without command just makes the walks faster. Young arms also tolerate “throw it where you aim” far better than max-effort every pitch.

Light note

Score your bullpens by targets hit, not by how hard it felt. Eight of ten on the glove beats ten of ten at full effort sprayed.

Pitch strategy and sequencing

  • Work ahead. First-pitch strikes change the whole at-bat: ahead 0–1, the hitter must protect; behind 1–0, you must come in. Trade-off: grooving a “get-me-over” first pitch to a good first-ball hitter gets hit. Ahead is a goal, not a gift.
  • Tunnelling. Make two different pitches look identical for the first half of flight — same release, same early path — so the hitter must commit before the pitches split apart (fastball stays up, curve drops). Trade-off: tunnelling demands repeatable mechanics; a pitcher whose release point wanders cannot tunnel.
  • Changing eye levels. Fastball up, breaking ball down (or the reverse) forces the hitter to change both timing and swing plane. Trade-off: a high fastball missed low in the zone is the most hittable pitch in baseball.
  • Speed changes. The changeup works because it uses the fastball’s arm speed with a grip that kills spin and speed. Trade-off: a slow, lazy-armed changeup is just a batting-practice fastball — the arm must sell it.
  • Sequencing is a conversation. What you showed last pitch changes what this pitch means. Setting up an outside fastball with two inside ones works because the hitter’s feet and eyes have moved. There is no magic sequence — there is only what this hitter has seen and shown you.

Two honest schools of pitching mechanics

The Tall-and-Fall school

Stay tall, balance over the rubber, stride long and land under control; the arm works in a compact, repeatable path. Trade-offs: easier to teach, repeats well, kind to command. The cost: less momentum and drive, so some pitchers leave velocity unused.

The Drive-and-Rotate school

Push hard off the rubber, sink into the back leg, rotate aggressively and let momentum carry the body toward the plate. Trade-offs: more ground force and often more velocity. The cost: harder to repeat, timing errors are bigger, and a young or tired body pays more for mistakes.

Neither school owns the truth. Most good deliveries live between them, and the right mix is the one your body repeats under fatigue — because the 6th inning, not the 1st, is where mechanics are judged.

What Works — Pitching
  • Build the chain from the ground: legs and hips make the power, the arm delivers it.
  • Front leg braces firm at landing so rotation has something to turn against.
  • Repeatable release point, trained with target work, before chasing speed.
  • Fastball command to both sides before adding a second pitch.
  • Same arm speed on the changeup as the fastball.
What Really Hasn’t — Pitching
  • “Throw over the top” forced on every arm. Arm slot follows the shoulder’s natural path and the chain’s timing. Forcing a sidearm or three-quarter thrower straight over the top breaks their timing and often their elbow. Work with the slot the body repeats.
  • “Just throw strikes” by slowing down and aiming. Pushing the ball with a careful, muscled arm teaches a different, worse delivery. Command comes from repeatable full mechanics at 85–95% effort, not from guiding it.
  • Long-toss and max-effort year-round with no rest. Throwing builds arms; throwing exhausted and sore, all year, breaks them. See arm care below.
  • Wrist-curl and “snap” drills as velocity builders. The wrist adds almost nothing the chain has not already made; snapping hard at release mostly adds elbow stress.
What To Try — Pitching
  • Throw at 85% effort for a whole bullpen and count glove hits — many pitchers find their “85%” is their most repeatable, and barely slower.
  • One week of throwing only to the glove-side edge, one week arm-side, and notice which your body finds easier — that tells you where your release really lives.
  • Film five pitches, check “hand up at foot plant,” and adjust only that for a week before changing anything else.
Contested: weighted-ball programs — both shelves, same size Tap to open
The case for

Velocity evidence

Structured weighted-ball programs (throwing balls heavier and lighter than a standard ball, on a supervised plan) have repeatedly produced velocity gains in trained, mostly older-teen and adult pitchers in published program reports. The proposed mechanism is sound in principle: overload and underload train the arm path to move faster, the way overspeed training works in sprinting. For a healthy, mature pitcher with good mechanics who has plateaued, advocates argue it is the most direct velocity tool available.

The case against

Injury-risk argument

The same overload raises stress on the elbow and shoulder, and at least one widely cited study of a weighted-ball program found a notably higher injury rate in the training group than in controls over a single off-season. Critics add that most participants are still-growing arms, that gains often fade, and that velocity chased this way can outrun the command and tissue tolerance that make velocity usable. No long-term safety record exists.

This page’s honest position: contested, unresolved. Not for growing arms, not without medical screening and qualified supervision, and never while anything hurts. If the two shelves above do not balance for your situation, the tie-breaker is age and pain: young or hurting means no.

Arm care — workload, rest, and the pain rules

  • Count pitches, not innings. Workload is throws: game pitches, bullpens, warm-ups, and hard throws at other positions all spend the same arm.
  • Rest is part of training. Days off throwing after hard outings, and a real off-season away from competitive pitching each year, are when tissue adapts and gets stronger.
  • Fatigue is the warning light. When mechanics change late in an outing — elbow dropping, balls sailing high — command goes first and injury risk climbs. That is the moment to stop, not to push through.
  • The pain rules, plainly: pain while throwing = stop throwing today. Pain that changes how you throw = stop and get checked. Sharp pain, numbness, tingling, swelling, or pain still there tomorrow = see a professional before throwing again. This page never diagnoses; a professional examines.
  • Sleep, food, and general strength (legs, trunk, back, and the muscles behind the shoulder blade) support the arm better than any gadget.
Coach note

Why command goes first when you tire: fine timing is the most fatigue-sensitive thing the nervous system does. The big muscles keep producing force long after the small timing signals get sloppy — so the pitcher still feels strong while the release point wanders. “I feel fine, I just can’t locate” is fatigue talking.

Batting

A swing lasts about a seventh of a second, and the hitter must decide in less time than that whether the pitch is worth swinging at. Everything below serves two masters: bat speed and time — starting early enough to be adjustable.

The swing as a chain, too

Stance
Balanced, relaxed, weight even. Tension is slow.
→
Load
A small move back — hands and weight gather as the pitcher moves. This is your spring winding.
→
Stride
A short step toward the pitcher, landing soft on the front toe, head still.
→
Rotation
Back hip fires toward the ball; trunk and hands follow; the bat whips through last, like the pitching chain in mirror image.
→
Contact & finish
Meet the ball out front, then let the swing finish high and balanced — falling over means the chain fired out of order.

Stance, load, stride — the details

  • Stance: feet about shoulder width, knees soft, bat held up and back with the hands near the rear shoulder. Grip firm in the fingers, not squeezed in the palms — a death grip locks the wrists and slows the bat.
  • Load: as the pitcher starts, shift a little weight to the back side and take the hands back a few inches. Small and on time beats big and late.
  • Stride: short — a few inches — landing on the toe with the front knee soft, so the hip can still rotate. A long, hard stride pulls the head down and forward, and the eyes move with it.
  • Contact point depends on pitch location: inside pitch — meet it well out in front of the plate; middle — about at the front edge; outside — let it travel deeper and drive it the other way. One swing, three contact points: the timing changes, the mechanics do not.
Light note

If you are always late, do not swing harder — start your load earlier. Most “slow bats” are late starts.

Coach note — timing theory

A 90 mph fastball reaches the plate in about 0.4 seconds; the swing itself takes about 0.15. That leaves roughly a quarter-second to see, judge, and decide — near the limit of human reaction. Good hitters buy time three ways: (1) start the load during the pitcher’s motion, before release; (2) read the ball out of the hand — spin, height, and speed in the first third of flight; (3) keep the swing adjustable — a swing built on rotation can speed up or hold for a fraction longer, while an all-arms swing commits early and cannot change. “Start early, decide late” is the whole theory in four words.

Approach and strategy, with trade-offs

  • Two-strike approach: widen slightly, shorten the stride, choke up a touch if you like, and think “middle of the field, put it in play.” Trade-off: you give up some power and some pulled-ball damage to cut strikeouts. With a runner on third and less than two out, that trade is usually worth it; leading off an inning, less so.
  • Sit on a pitch — sometimes. Guessing a pitch and location can turn a good hitter into a dangerous one in hitter’s counts (2–0, 3–1). Trade-off: guess wrong and you have wasted the best count in baseball. Sitting is a tool for counts where a walk is fine, not a way of life.
  • Work the count. Taking close pitches early makes the pitcher show you his whole hand and raises his pitch count. Trade-off: passivity against a strike-thrower just puts you behind. Read the pitcher, not a rule.
  • Move the runner. With a teammate in scoring position, the at-bat has a job beyond your own line: a ball to the right side, a deep fly, contact over a hit. That purpose — getting your teammate home — is a real stake, and choosing the approach that serves it is strategy, not sacrifice of pride.

Two honest schools of hitting

Rotational hitting

Power comes from hip and trunk rotation; the hands stay back and whip through; the swing plane rises slightly to meet the ball’s downward path. Trade-offs: more power and better plane-matching against velocity. The cost: a bigger move that must start on time, and it can get long against good off-speed pitches.

Linear hitting

Weight shifts forward in a line, hands take a direct path to the ball, contact is emphasised. Trade-offs: simpler, shorter to teach, often more contact for beginners. The cost: less rotational power, and a downward chop plane fights the pitch’s downward angle, producing ground balls and late fouls against real velocity.

Modern coaching mostly lives in the rotational camp with a direct hand path — “turn hard, hands short.” Pure versions of either school fail somewhere; know which failure you are choosing.

What Works — Batting
  • Start the load on the pitcher’s move, not on the pitch.
  • Short stride, soft front side, head still — the eyes are the timing system.
  • Rotation from the hips with the hands connected, not an arms-only swing.
  • Contact point moves with pitch location; mechanics stay the same.
  • A real two-strike adjustment instead of the same full swing at 0–2.
What Really Hasn’t — Batting
  • “Squash the bug” as a literal cue. Screwing the back foot into the ground like squashing a bug locks the back side and kills hip rotation for many hitters — the back foot turns because the hip fires, not as the engine itself. Taught literally, it produces spinning-in-place swings with no drive.
  • “Swing down on the ball to create backspin.” A chopped-down swing meets a downward-travelling pitch at a glancing angle: grounders and pop-ups. The working plane roughly matches the pitch’s descent.
  • “Keep your elbow up” forced high. A jacked-up back elbow can drag the barrel and tense the shoulders. Comfortable and connected beats posed.
  • All tee work, no live timing. A tee trains positions; it cannot train the decision, which is half of hitting. See transfer notes in Drills.
What To Try — Batting
  • For one week, take the first pitch of every practice at-bat and just track it to the glove — many hitters find their timing improves everywhere from learning to see the ball deep.
  • Try a no-stride swing (front heel lift only) for a session: if your contact improves, your stride was costing you time.
  • Try choking up an inch with two strikes for a week and count hard contact, not feelings.

Coach note

Diagnose faults in chain order: stance and timing first, then stride, then rotation, then hands. Fixing the hands for a fault that starts in the stride is the most common wasted month in hitting practice.

Drills — with honest transfer notes

Read this first: a drill trains what it trains — nothing more. Each drill below says what it actually builds and what it does not. This page cannot see your form: film yourself (angles named per drill) and get a real coach’s eye when you can. Film is the check; this page is the map.

Pitching drills

Target bullpen (the glove game) Tap to open

Do it: catcher or partner calls a spot (low-outside, up-inside…). 20–30 throws at 85–95% effort. Score = targets hit.

Trains: command, repeatable release, focus under mild pressure. Does not train: max velocity, game pressure, or facing a hitter.

Film from: directly behind the catcher (release point and location) and from the open side (chain order, hand-at-foot-plant).

Towel drill (dry delivery) Tap to open

Do it: hold a small towel, make full deliveries snapping it toward a target at release distance.

Trains: chain sequence and release out front, with zero arm load — safe volume for mechanics. Does not train: grip, spin, or real release feel; a towel is not a ball.

Film from: open side, full body in frame.

Stride-and-freeze Tap to open

Do it: deliver at half speed and freeze at foot plant for two seconds: hand up, hips starting, front leg braced. 10 reps, then 5 full throws.

Trains: the foot-plant position and arm timing. Does not train: full-speed timing — always finish with real throws so the pattern transfers.

Film from: open side, close enough to see the throwing hand at the freeze.

Batting drills

Tee work with a moving contact point Tap to open

Do it: tee inside, middle, outside in rotation; meet each at its proper contact point.

Trains: swing path and contact points. Does not train: timing or pitch decisions — pair it with live or front toss every session.

Film from: behind the catcher’s position (plane and contact) and from the open side (load, stride, head).

Front toss, two-strike round Tap to open

Do it: front toss where every swing starts at 0–2: shortened approach, job is hard contact anywhere fair.

Trains: the two-strike adjustment under mild pressure. Does not train: full-count judgement or power approach.

Film from: behind a screen to the side, full swing in frame.

Tracking-only round Tap to open

Do it: stand in against real pitching (or a machine) and do not swing for 10 pitches; call “ball/strike” and the pitch height out loud as it crosses.

Trains: seeing the ball early and deep — the decision half of hitting. Does not train: the swing itself at all; that is the point.

Film from: not needed — the calls are the record. Note them in your practice log below.
