The Business of Belief — Side by Side

This page compares organizations, not faith.

What people believe is theirs. No page can grade that, and this one does not try. What institutions do — with money, labor, land, growth, and the rules — is a record anyone can check. That record is what sits side by side here.

On the left of every pair: how a successful business runs that mechanism (a mechanism is one working part of how an organization runs — how the money comes in, how it grows, how it keeps people). On the right: how religious institutions run the same mechanism. Under each pair, where the comparison fails, it says so — in a box labeled Where it breaks, at the same size as everything else.

No mockery of any faith or believer, anywhere on this page. The subject is institutional behavior — and the business shape, where it appears, sits on top of real faith and real charity, not instead of them. Both shelves are printed at full size.

How to read the labels

  • VERIFIED Verified — in a primary record anyone can check: a court record, a regulator's filing, a law's text.
  • ESTIMATE Estimate — a real figure from a named estimator, but not an audited public book. The estimator is named beside it.
  • CONTESTED Contested — serious people dispute the number or the method. Both sides of the dispute are printed.
  • CAN'T VERIFY Can't verify — no public record exists that could settle it. The label is the honest answer, not a dodge.
Mechanism 1 of 8

Recurring revenue

Recurring revenue means money that arrives again and again on a schedule, without having to be re-sold each time. It is the most prized shape of income in business — because it can be planned around. Watch for the same shape on the right.

Left — Business

How a successful business does it

  • Subscriptions and memberships. Software, gyms, streaming, warehouse clubs: charge monthly or yearly, renew by default.
  • Predictable income. A subscriber base lets a company forecast next year's revenue before it spends anything.
  • Churn management. Churn means the share of customers who cancel. Whole teams exist to measure it, predict who is about to leave, and win them back before they go.
The business idealRevenue that continues unless the customer actively stops it — and a department whose job is stopping them from stopping it.

Right — Religions

How religions do it

  • The tithe. A tithe is a set share of income given to the church — traditionally 10% — taught in many Christian churches and required for full standing in the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (the LDS Church, often called Mormon).
  • Zakat. In Islam, zakat is an obligatory charitable payment of 2.5% of wealth — note: of wealth held, not of income earned — owed by Muslims above a threshold, directed mainly to the poor.
  • Regular giving and pledges. Congregations run annual pledge drives, recurring online giving, and envelopes — the same scheduled-giving machinery, in church language.
Over $378 billion a yearWhat U.S. faith-based institutions take in combined, per year — with about 81% from individual donations. ESTIMATE Industry estimate (Giving USA / faith-giving research), not an audited total — churches file no public books; see Mechanism 7.
Where it breaks

A subscription is a contract: stop paying and the service stops, and the company can sue. A tithe is, in most churches, voluntary and unenforceable — no one is cut off from worship for an empty envelope, and zakat largely flows to the poor rather than into an institution's operating budget at all. The shape of the income matches. The legal teeth do not.

Mechanism 2 of 8

Growth & sales

Every organization that wants to be bigger next year needs a way to bring in new people. Businesses call it sales and marketing. Compare the job descriptions, not just the words.

Left — Business

How a successful business does it

  • Marketing departments that study the audience, shape the message, and design the first impression down to the lighting.
  • Advertising bought where the target customers already are.
  • Customer acquisition cost — what it costs, on average, to win one new customer — tracked and optimized like any other number.
  • Referral programs: existing customers rewarded for bringing friends. The cheapest sales force is a satisfied customer.
The business idealGrowth as a system: a budget, a funnel (the path from first hearing about it to signing up), and a number to beat next quarter.

Right — Religions

How religions do it

  • Evangelism and missions as the sales force. Evangelism means spreading the faith to new people; missionaries are sent out, full-time, to do exactly that — LDS missionaries, for example, serve about two years, unpaid (see Mechanism 6).
  • Literal marketing departments. Large megachurches (churches with roughly 2,000+ weekly attendees) employ marketing, branding, and media teams and run deliberate branding strategies — documented in the sociological literature (sociology is the academic study of how groups and institutions behave), cited here as such.
  • Referral is the main channel: most people arrive at a congregation because a member invited them — the referral program, without the discount code.
Lakewood Church, HoustonAbout 43,000 weekly attendees, about 400 staff, and an annual budget of about $90 million — run from a former sports arena, with broadcast production to match. ESTIMATE Widely reported figures; Lakewood publishes summaries, not audited public books.
Where it breaks

There is no price and no checkout. A convert is not a customer who bought something; joining usually costs the joiner (time, giving, changed habits) rather than paying the organization a purchase price. And much mission work is humanitarian — clinics, schools, disaster relief — with no conversion required. The growth machinery matches. The transaction underneath it does not.

Mechanism 3 of 8

Franchising & brand standards

A franchise is a system where a central brand licenses local operators to run under its name, its manual, and its inspections — so a customer gets the same product in any town. Scholars of religion use the franchise comparison themselves; this section uses their term, not a nickname invented here.

Left — Business

How a successful business does it

  • Brand manuals specifying the product, the look, the scripts, the standards.
  • Quality control: inspections, and the right to pull the sign down if standards slip.
  • Territory: who may open where, so outlets grow the brand instead of cannibalizing each other.
  • Instant credibility: a new local outlet opens already trusted, because the brand above it is known.
The business idealOne brand, thousands of doors, the same experience behind every door — enforced from headquarters.

Right — Religions

How religions do it

  • Denominations and multi-site churches. A denomination is a named branch of a faith with its own governance (Methodist, Baptist, Sunni, Shia, and hundreds more). Large churches now run multi-site campuses — one church, many locations, same sermon by video — which scholars in the field themselves call “multi-site franchising.”
  • Doctrine and creeds as brand standards. Doctrine is the official teaching; a creed is its short official statement. Hold the doctrine and you may carry the name; break it and the name — and the building, in some traditions — can be taken back.
  • Church planting is market expansion: a church plant is a new congregation started deliberately in a target area, often funded and coached by a network.
  • A schism is a spin-off: a schism is a formal split. The breakaway group keeps much of the product, changes the management, and competes for the same members — a startup founded by the old firm's own staff.
The shared tradeA local congregation gets instant brand credibility from the name above the door — and must hold doctrinal compliance to keep it. That is the franchise bargain, word for word.
Where it breaks

A franchisee pays fees and royalties upward and can be audited for profit. Most congregations pay modest dues, keep their own money, own their own buildings, and can — and regularly do — vote to leave the denomination entirely, which no franchise contract allows. Control in religion runs on shared belief more than on enforceable contract, and belief can walk out the door taking the “franchise” with it. That is what a schism is.

Mechanism 4 of 8 — the big one

The product

Every other mechanism on this page has a real parallel. This one is where the parallel ends — and it is printed larger than the rest, because it changes what all the other mechanisms mean.

Left — Business

How a successful business does it

  • The product must eventually be delivered — a car that drives, software that runs, a meal that arrives.
  • It can be tested by reviewers, regulators, and rivals before and after sale.
  • It can be reviewed publicly, by people who actually received it.
  • It can be refunded. If delivery fails, the law and the market both provide a remedy (a remedy is a fix the buyer can actually get — money back, replacement, a court order).
The disciplineDelivery is checkable, so failure is provable — and provable failure is what keeps every other mechanism on this page honest.

Right — Religions

How religions do it

  • The core deliverable — salvation, enlightenment, a place in the afterlife — is real to believers. This page does not grade that belief, in either direction.
  • Delivery is post-mortem or inward: after death, or as an inner state no outside observer can measure.
  • Therefore, structurally: no refund is possible, no review by a recipient can be filed, and no quality audit (an outside check that the thing promised was actually delivered, to standard) can ever be performed.
The structural factNot “the product is false.” The product is unverifiable — no delivery receipt can exist, for anyone, ever, on either side of the question.

This is the single biggest structural difference on this page.

A business with an undeliverable product gets caught — by refunds, reviews, regulators, and rivals. An institution whose core product is delivered after death, or inwardly, cannot be caught that way, whether it is honest or not. That is a fact about verifiability — about what can be checked — not a claim about truth. A true promise and a false one are, from the outside, identically unauditable. Every other mechanism on this page — the recurring revenue, the growth engine, the tax shelter, the exit costs — operates without the one discipline that constrains all of them in business: provable delivery.

Where it breaks — in fairness, both ways

Religions also deliver checkable, this-world goods: community, rites of passage, counseling, food, shelter, disaster relief (Mechanism 6). Those can be seen and judged, and often hold up well. And businesses sell unverifiable promises too — extended warranties, miracle supplements, “exposure” — and regulators chase exactly those. The difference is one of degree at the core: for a business the unverifiable product is the exception the law hunts; for a religion it is the center, protected — rightly, most democracies hold — as faith itself.

Mechanism 5 of 8

Real estate & assets

Assets are what an organization owns that holds value: land, buildings, investments. Accumulating them is normal for any long-lived institution. The question is only scale — and whether anyone outside can see the books.

Left — Business

How a successful business does it

  • Headquarters and flagship stores that double as advertising.
  • Land banking: buying land ahead of growth, holding it as it appreciates (grows in value).
  • Investment portfolios: surplus cash put into securities (stocks, bonds, and similar tradable investments) managed by professionals.
  • Public companies must report all of it, quarterly, in filings anyone can read.
The business ruleAccumulate freely — but the holdings of a public company sit in public filings, priced by strangers every trading day.

Right — Religions

How religions do it

  • The LDS Church — the extreme documented case. Its investment arm, Ensign Peak Advisors, held over $100 billion in securities. VERIFIED Per its SEC Form 13F filings — the quarterly holdings reports filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, the markets regulator.
  • In 2023 the SEC charged the church and Ensign Peak over two decades of shell-company filings — using separate shell entities (companies that exist on paper to hold or report things) to obscure the size of the portfolio; the church paid a $1 million penalty, Ensign Peak $4 million. VERIFIED SEC order, public.
  • Total LDS wealth estimated at $206–293 billion by independent analysts. ESTIMATE The church does not publish its finances; outside analysts (notably the Widow's Mite Report) build the figure from filings, land records, and leaks.
  • Land: about 1.7 million acres and about 16,000 U.S. property parcels. ESTIMATE From public land and tax records, assembled by researchers.
  • The waqf (Islam): a waqf is an endowment (property permanently set aside so its income funds a purpose) — land or buildings dedicated forever, their revenue funding mosques, schools, and charity. One of history's oldest asset-holding trust structures, centuries older than the modern corporation.
  • The Vatican holds a bank (the Institute for the Works of Religion) and a property portfolio across Rome and beyond; Tirupati temple in India, run by a Hindu temple trust, sits among the world's richest temples, receiving tons of donated gold and cash from pilgrims yearly.
The patternEndowments this large behave like endowments anywhere: professional managers, growth targets, and a strong institutional interest in never shrinking.
Where it breaks

Much religious property is restricted in ways corporate assets are not: a waqf is, in principle, irrevocable and unsellable — dedicated to God, not owned by managers; temple wealth at Tirupati is held for the deity and the pilgrims, and a congregation's building often cannot be sold without the members' vote. And the counterweight at full size: the typical congregation's “portfolio” is one aging building and a roof fund (see the median in Mechanism 8). The empires are real. They are not the median.

Mechanism 6 of 8

Labor

Ask any business owner their largest cost and the answer is almost always people. Now compare an organization whose people largely work for meaning instead of wages — and read both shelves of that fact, because both are true at once.

Left — Business

How a successful business does it

  • Wages are the price of labor, set by a market: pay too little and workers leave for a competitor.
  • Payroll is usually the largest single cost — commonly the majority of a service business's expenses.
  • Unpaid labor is, with narrow exceptions (a genuine internship, an owner's own hours), illegal — minimum-wage law exists precisely to close that door.
The business constraintEvery hour of work shows up as a cost. An organization that must pay market wages for every hour has a hard floor under its expenses.

Right — Religions

How religions do it

  • Much of the labor force is volunteer or below-market: clergy paid modestly, missionaries unpaid (LDS missionaries typically pay their own way), monastics (monks and nuns) working for room, board, and the life itself.
  • The motivation is the mission itself — which makes this, structurally, the cheapest workforce any organization can have: no wage floor, no competitor poaching with a better offer, because the pay is meaning.
  • Ushers, teachers, cooks, drivers, builders, counselors: in a typical congregation, most of the work that gets done is done by people who are not paid at all.
The cost advantage, plainlyAn institution that does not have to buy most of its labor at market price can run — and grow — on revenue no business could survive on.

The other shelf — at the same size

That same unpaid, mission-motivated workforce is also the source of much of the genuine good on this page's whole subject. Congregations run a large share of America's food banks and disaster-relief crews. Sikh temples run langar — free community kitchens that feed anyone who walks in, no belief, no payment, no questions — among the largest free-food operations on Earth, staffed entirely by volunteers. The labor is cheap for the institution and it is a real gift to strangers. Both sentences are true, and neither cancels the other. What would cancel the fairness is printing only one of them.

Where it breaks

A business's unpaid-labor problem is exploitation by definition; a volunteer's gift is, by definition, freely given and freely withdrawn — most volunteers can simply stop, and do. The analogy only holds where “volunteering” stops being free: where housing, family standing, or salvation itself is made conditional on the work. That line — gift on one side, leverage on the other — is the one to watch, and it connects directly to Mechanism 8.

Mechanism 7 of 8

Tax & regulatory shelter

Shelter here means legal protection from taxes and disclosure rules (disclosure means being required to show your records publicly) that apply to everyone else. Every large organization seeks favorable treatment. The difference is in what has to be shown in return.

Left — Business

How a successful business does it

  • Lobbies — pays advocates to argue for favorable rules — and uses every deduction and credit the law allows.
  • But a public company files public returns and reports: audited financial statements, executive pay, major risks, all readable by anyone.
  • Even a nonprofit business — a charity, a hospital — must file Form 990, the IRS's annual public financial disclosure, showing revenue, spending, and top salaries.
The business bargainFavorable treatment is argued for in the open, and the books are the price of legitimacy: show the numbers, then ask for the break.

Right — Religions

How religions do it

  • In the U.S., churches are exempt from income tax and (in every state) property tax on their religious property.
  • Uniquely, they are also exempt from Form 990 — the disclosure every other nonprofit must file — under Internal Revenue Code §508(c), the law's mandatory-exception provision for churches. VERIFIED The statute's text is public.
  • Result: churches are the only major money sector in American life with no public books — no required filing of revenue, assets, or salaries, at any size, from a storefront congregation to a $100-billion portfolio.
What the exemption costs — the honest rangeAbout $2.4 billion a year at the low end (Tax Foundation — federal income tax only) to about $71–83.5 billion a year at the high end (Cragun et al. — all-in, including property tax and other forgone revenue). CONTESTED Both shelves printed: the low figure counts one tax; the high figure counts everything and was published in an advocacy magazine (Free Inquiry), with its methodology disputed by other researchers. The fight over the number is itself part of the record — anyone quoting you a single figure, either way, is selling you half of it.
Where it breaks

The exemption has a stated reason no business can claim: the First Amendment's separation of church and state — the argument that taxing and auditing churches entangles government in religion, and history (in several countries, in living memory) shows that entanglement used as a weapon. Many churches also publish their finances voluntarily to their members, and some denominations require it. The break is real. It just does not restore the missing public record: voluntary disclosure ends exactly where an institution decides it ends — which is Mechanism 4's problem again, wearing a tax form.

Mechanism 8 of 8

Retention & exit costs

Retention means keeping the people you have. Every organization works at it. The honest question is what it costs a person to leave — because that cost, more than any mission statement, determines how much power the institution actually holds over a life.

Left — Business

How a successful business does it

  • Switching costs: your data, your history, your learned habits live inside the product; leaving means starting over.
  • Loyalty programs and community lock-in: points, status, and a social circle built around the brand.
  • At the honest end of the spectrum: cancel anytime — one click, no phone call, no exit interview, no consequence beyond losing the service.
The spectrum's honest endThe easier a company makes leaving, the more its retention has to be earned by the product itself — every single month.

Right — Religions

How religions do it

  • What a member holds inside the institution is not data. It is belonging, family, identity — often their whole social world, their marriage's community, their children's friendships, their account of what their life means.
  • In high-demand groups (groups that ask for unusually total commitment and control many parts of members' lives), exit can be formalized as shunning: members, including family, cut contact with the person who leaves. Leaving can cost a person their entire community at once.
  • Scientology — the documented extreme, from the court record. A 1984 U.S. Tax Court decision described the church as having “made a business out of selling religion.” VERIFIED Court record, public.
  • Founder L. Ron Hubbard's own letter of April 10, 1953 weighed adopting the religion form in explicit business terms — the “religion angle” as a strategic choice. VERIFIED The letter is a published historical document.
  • In 1993, after roughly 2,200 lawsuits against the IRS and its officials by the church and its members, the IRS signed a closing agreement granting Scientology full religious tax exemption. VERIFIED The agreement's existence and the litigation campaign are documented; its full text was later leaked and published.
The exit-cost factNo subscription on Earth can take your family with it when you cancel. At the high-demand end, a religion can — and that is a kind of retention no business is permitted to build.

The counterweight — at the same size, because it is most of the picture

Scientology is the extreme, and extremes are not the average. Most congregations are small and poor: the median U.S. congregation — the one in the middle, half smaller, half larger — takes in about $169,000 a year. ESTIMATE National Congregations Study / Hartford Institute research. Most clergy are not rich — many work second jobs. Most members of most faiths can, and do, quietly drift away with nothing worse than awkward holidays. The business shape documented on this page sits on top of real faith and real charity, not instead of them — and for the median congregation, the “business” is a bake sale trying to fix a roof. Grade the empires as empires. Do not let them stand in for the whole.

The close

The pattern, in one line

The closer an organization of any kind gets to all four of these, the more its behavior converges — whatever it calls itself.

  1. (a) Recurring revenue — money that arrives on a schedule, by default.
  2. (b) An unverifiable product — a core promise no delivery check can ever audit.
  3. (c) No public books — no required filing where strangers can see the numbers.
  4. (d) High exit costs — leaving costs a person more than the product is worth.

Score any organization — a church, a company, a movement, a platform, a government program — on those four, and you can predict its behavior better than from its name, its mission statement, or its founder's intentions. An organization with none of the four must earn its people every month, in the open, with a product that can be checked. An organization with all four does not have to — and, over time, mostly does not. The label on the door is the least informative thing about it. The four mechanisms are the most.

Why? → How? → Act. Why do such different institutions act alike? Because the incentives are alike. How can you tell, for any institution in your own life? Run the four checks above. Then act on what you find — stay, give, join, or leave with your eyes open. That part was always yours.
