The Ruler's Test — Hassan Sheikh Mohamud, President of Somalia

Subject: Hassan Sheikh Mohamud — President of the Federal Republic of Somalia. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: in Somalia's system the president is the executive center of gravity — head of state who appoints the prime minister (Hamza Abdi Barre, in post since June 2022), directs the constitutional and electoral program, and leads the war against al-Shabaab — and in this tenure the presidency has also been the author of the constitutional amendments, the electoral model, and the federal-member-state confrontations that define the record. Mohamud, born 1955, is graded on his second presidency: elected by parliament on 15 May 2022, defeating the incumbent Mohamed Abdullahi Mohamed ("Farmaajo"), after a first presidency from 2012 to 2017; the first term appears as context where his word over time is graded. Premise correction, material: the holder named in the brief still holds the seat in November 2026, but he holds it in open dispute. His four-year term under the 2012 Provisional Constitution ran to 15 May 2026. In March 2026 parliament approved amendments to eleven chapters — completing the constitution, moving to five-year terms — and Mohamud signed them on 8 March 2026; his government holds that the amendments carry his mandate to May 2027, and the electoral commission has scheduled parliamentary elections for 15 April 2027 and a presidential vote for 15 May 2027. The opposition Somali Future Council (SFC) — which includes former president Sharif Sheikh Ahmed and the Puntland and Jubaland administrations — holds that his mandate expired on 15 May 2026 and refers to him accordingly. On 5 November 2026 the Supreme Court's five-judge Constitutional Division ruled for the opposition's reading: the amendments were lawfully adopted as process (the court rejected Sharif Sheikh Ahmed's standing to contest the amending process itself) but cannot apply retroactively to sitting office-holders; the president's term ended 15 May 2026 and parliament's on 14 April 2026; acts taken by both after those dates under the extended mandates are "unconstitutional, unlawful and null and void"; the government is limited to caretaker duties; political leaders have 30 days to agree an electoral framework and elections must be held within 90 days. The government rejects the ruling — its Attorney General's office said on 6 November that the ruling had "no legal effect" — and Mohamud remains in Villa Somalia, exercising the office, as this dossier is graded. He is graded as the seat is actually held: in possession, under a court judgment that the possession is unlawful, with the federation partially withdrawn around him — Puntland withdrew recognition of the federal government on 31 March 2024 and again after the term lapsed in May 2026, Jubaland has been in armed confrontation with federal forces since late 2024, and South West State suspended recognition in March 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Casualty and displacement tolls are kept in separate, sourced counts and never blended. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic this Test applies is printed here in full: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one — Mohamud was elected by parliament in 2022 on a program of total war against al-Shabaab, debt relief, a completed constitution, and one-person-one-vote elections, and delivering that program is credited where it is due, above all on Test 5. What is graded is the holding: whether the law is one law, whether the referees are gripped, whether everyone is counted, and whether the word survives contact with his own interest. A note the mandate rule itself requires here: the "mandate" he now claims for the extra year is not a platform any electorate voted on — it is a document his own parliament amended and he signed, extending his own term — so it is graded under Tests 2 and 5 as an act of the seat, not excused as a platform. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as two independent axes; neither launders the other, and the crossings are named where they occur.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The state's own accountability machinery has published findings damaging to his government, in the open. Somalia's Auditor General found that 59 percent of audited federal agencies had broken procurement rules, producing more than $18.5 million in irregular spending — a finding produced by a state institution and circulating publicly against the government that appointed it (Auditor General's findings, as reported in Kormeeraha Magazine, November 2026). A seat running a pure favorites system does not usually leave its auditor publishing numbers like these.
  • When the law was allowed to run, it ran against his own side's wins. On 24 August 2026 the Supreme Court — under Chief Justice Bashe Yusuf Ahmed — annulled the 30 July 2026 Galmudug elections, in which the president's own Justice and Solidarity Party (JSP) had been declared the landslide winner, finding the vote "not administered in line with the constitution and the electoral law" and ordering a fresh election (Somali Mail / Radio Dalsan, 24 August 2026; The Voice News Magazine, August 2026). No documented move by the government to overturn that annulment appears in the record assembled here. The same court later ruled against his term extension (Test 2) — the point for this shelf is narrower: the judiciary's one law reached the ruling party's own victories before it reached the president.
  • The electoral program he championed is, in its stated design, a one-law instrument: voter registration for direct elections began in April 2025 — the first registration drive in the capital in roughly fifty years — and had reached approximately 900,000 registered voters by mid-2026, open in principle to every citizen in the areas where it ran (FIKIR Institute policy brief, 2026). The clan-based 4.5 allocation it is meant to replace is itself a formalized inequality — four "major" clan families at full share, all others at a half — and abolishing it would be a one-law reform in structure, whatever the disputes over its execution below.

Evidence against:

  • The courts were used as weapons in the federation fight, by both capitals — and his capital moved first in the documented sequence. After Ahmed Madobe's re-election in Jubaland on 25 November 2024, the Banadir Regional Court in Mogadishu issued an arrest warrant for a sitting state president on 27 November 2024, accusing him of treason, violating the constitutional framework, leaking classified information to foreign actors, and undermining national unity; Jubaland suspended relations the next day, and a Kismayo court retaliated with a mirror warrant against Mohamud himself (Good Governance Africa analysis, 2025; Jubaland crisis reference record, 2026). A treason warrant issued by the capital's regional court against a state president two days after an election the capital disliked is the shape of a second law: the criminal process as an instrument for insiders' disputes. Madobe's guilt or innocence of the charges is not found anywhere in this record; the warrant's timing and forum are the evidence here.
  • The Mogadishu public-land file: three former presidents — Sharif Sheikh Ahmed, Abdikasim Salad Hassan, and Mohamed Abdullahi Mohamed — declared publicly that the government's sales of public land in Mogadishu broke the rules: the land had not been declared surplus, the sales did not pass through the National Contracts Agency, and the proceeds did not reach the state treasury; 97 members of parliament accused federal leaders of auctioning public land for personal gain (Kormeeraha Magazine, November 2026). A July 2026 investigation by Baaritaan Digital alleged that more than 180 public properties, estimated by its sources at over US$1 billion, had been transferred to private buyers through questionable procedures (Baaritaan Digital investigation, reported by The Somali Monitor, October 2026). These are ALLEGATIONs by political opponents and investigative outlets, not court findings — no tribunal in the record assembled here has ruled on them — and they are recorded as such. What is not allegation is the measured environment around them: Transparency International's 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index scored Somalia 9 of 100, tied with South Sudan for last place in the world (Transparency International CPI 2025, as reported November 2026), and the same period's record shows nearly $53 million in unpaid salaries to civil servants, soldiers, and MPs (Kormeeraha Magazine, November 2026) — a state that cannot pay its soldiers on time while its auditor finds procurement law broken in most agencies is not running one law at the source, whatever the intent at the top.
  • Appointments at the center are credibly reported to run through his household. Published reports — carried by Kormeeraha Magazine (November 2026) and recorded here as contested claims, not findings — state that in 2023 he named his daughter Jihan Hassan Sheikh as senior adviser on foreign affairs, that his son-in-law Adam Roble directs the Somali Development and Reconstruction Bank, and that a son commands the presidential guard, with other relatives in diplomacy and protocol posts. The individuals named hold public posts and are named as the reports name them; whether each appointment was merited is not established in this record. The pattern as reported is the Test 1 question in its purest form — whether the surest path to the state's most sensitive posts runs through one family — and the government has not, in the record assembled here, answered it with a competing account of open competition for those posts.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the for-shelf items describe a law that exists — an auditor publishing, a court annulling the ruling party's win — while the against shelf describes where the law bends at the source: a treason warrant out of the capital's court for a state president who defied the capital, a land program three former presidents and 97 MPs say ran outside the contracts rules, the world's lowest corruption score, and the state's most sensitive appointments credibly reported as family placements. The exceptions prove a legal order survives; the pattern at the center is that the order's first exception is the seat itself and its circle.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — private buyers of Mogadishu public land (on the opposition's and investigators' accounts, at below-process prices); the presidential household and its appointees; the JSP's state-level machines in the aligned states, which govern under laws the capital writes. Hurt — Ahmed Madobe and the Jubaland administration, facing a treason warrant from the capital's court; Mogadishu residents evicted from public land whose sale proceeds are credibly alleged never to have reached the treasury; civil servants, soldiers, and MPs owed some $53 million in unpaid salaries while procurement rules were broken around them; and every Somali business that competes for state contracts without a connection to the source.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • For most of the tenure, the referees visibly functioned. The Supreme Court annulled his own party's Galmudug victory in August 2026 (Test 1) — a ruling no gripped court delivers — and the electoral commission (NIEBC) has run registration and state-level direct elections on a published timetable, announcing results that opposition parties then challenged through the courts rather than only in the street (NIEBC announcements, July–August 2026). Parliament debated and voted the constitutional program in the open across 2024 and 2026, and the Auditor General published against the government (Test 1).
  • Critical media operate and publish against him at volume: the reporting this dossier is built from — opposition outlets, investigative sites, critical magazines describing his government as a family business — circulates from inside Somalia's media ecosystem, and international and UN reporting on his government is published freely. Whatever pressures exist (below), there is no documented general closure of the press in this record.
  • The November crisis itself contains a for-item that should not be skipped: the court ruled. Five judges of the Constitutional Division heard a former president's petition, split it — denying his standing on the amending process, upholding the amendments' prospective validity — and voided only their retroactive application to sitting terms, including the term of the man who appointed the chief justice. That is a referee blowing the whistle on the seat, on the record, knowing the cost. The grip is graded on what the seat did next, not on whether the whistle existed.

Evidence against:

  • On 4 November 2026 — the night before the Supreme Court was due to rule on the constitutionality of his term extension — Mohamud dismissed Chief Justice Bashe Yusuf Ahmed by presidential decree, citing complaints from judges and lawyers about the administration of justice, and named his predecessor-but-one, Ibrahim Idle Suleiman, in his place. The dismissal followed neither of the constitution's routes: no proposal from the Judicial Services Commission and no approval by parliament. Two UN experts — Margaret Satterthwaite, Special Rapporteur on the independence of judges and lawyers, and Ben Saul, Special Rapporteur on human rights while countering terrorism — condemned the dismissal on 12 November as unlawful and a violation of judicial independence, and called for Ahmed's restoration (OHCHR press release, 12 November 2026). The UN statement further records that the government deployed security personnel to the Supreme Court in the ruling's aftermath — described by the experts as an attempt to intimidate the court — and that the five judges were left with their protection withdrawn and their travel restricted (OHCHR, November 2026, as reported).
  • When the court ruled anyway on 5 November — declaring the dismissal itself null and void and his term expired — the government did not adjust; it replaced the referee's head and denied the whistle. On 7 November Mohamud attended the swearing-in of Suleiman as chief justice at the presidential palace, with the prime minister and the parliamentary speakers present (HornPost, November 2026). The Attorney General's office declared the ruling of "no legal effect" (Attorney General's office statement, 6 November 2026). Suleiman has since called for "discipline" among the judges who ruled (FTL Somalia, November 2026). Six Supreme Court justices — Ahmed, Salah Sh. Ibrahim Diblaw, Abdikadir Idiris Essa, Abubakar Ahmed Ali, Abdikafar Hussein Isak, and Abdiaziz Mohamed Yusuf — have carried the file outside the country entirely, filing at the East African Court of Justice, which on 18 November gave the government 45 days to respond (FTL Somalia, November 2026). Somalia now has two men claiming to be its chief justice, and the one sitting in the chair is the one the president installed the week the court ruled against him.
  • The electoral referee's independence is contested at its root. The NIEBC operates a timetable — parliamentary elections 15 April 2027, presidential 15 May 2027 — that presupposes the very term extension the Supreme Court has voided, and its state-level elections have been run only in states aligned with the federal government, under rules the opposition states never agreed to. In Galmudug, the incumbent state president Ahmed Abdi Kariye ("Qoorqoor") withdrew from the race on 25 June 2026 after talks with federal officials, and Mohamud designated Liban Ahmed Hassan as the JSP's sole presidential candidate with no internal party contest, before the 30 July vote his party then swept (The Somali Digest, August 2026; FTL Somalia, August 2026) — the opposition parties' charge of federal interference is an interested party's claim, recorded as such, but the sequence it describes (incumbent withdraws after federal talks; president names the sole candidate; commission runs the vote; ruling party takes ~70 percent) is documented in the commission's own announced figures. Opposition parties also accused the commission of manipulating the Galmudug count (Towfiiq Party challenge, August 2026) — an ALLEGATION the Supreme Court's annulment partially corroborates in effect, the court having found irregularities that "seriously undermined the transparency, freedom and fairness of the elections and affected their outcome" (Supreme Court ruling, 24 August 2026).
  • Parliament, the referee of the executive's term, extended its own mandate in the same March 2026 instrument that extended his — both houses' terms having expired on 14 April 2026 on the court's reading, the legislature that would have to check the extension is itself a beneficiary of it (Supreme Court ruling, 5 November 2026). And the June 2026 record shows the coercive edge of the grip: federal forces restricted SFC leaders' movements and cracked down on public demonstrations in the capital (Critical Threats / Institute for the Study of War, Africa File, 12 November 2026), and on 3 June 2026 federal security forces fought clan militias guarding Sharif Sheikh Ahmed and former prime minister Hassan Ali Khaire in Mogadishu — the Banadir police account says forces responded to an attack on police; Khaire's account says the government directed forces to attack his residence and the former president's (UN Secretary-General's report S/2026/862, 29 October 2026). Al Jazeera's toll for the June clashes, as carried in subsequent reporting, was at least 13 killed and 189 wounded; the toll is recorded here as reported, with responsibility for the first shots contested between the two accounts above.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because November 2026 is the limiting case this test exists for. A court was about to rule on whether the president's term had expired; the night before, he removed its chief justice outside the constitutional procedure; when the court ruled against him anyway, he swore in a replacement, had his Attorney General declare the ruling void of effect, and left the ruling judges with their protection withdrawn and their travel restricted, while security personnel were deployed to the court. Everything on the for shelf — the auditor, the annulment, the functioning commission — is real, and all of it operated, in the UN experts' phrase, on sufferance: the moment the senior referee's decision touched the seat's own tenure, the seat reached for the referee. That is gripping, documented at the highest level of the judiciary, in the same week as the grading.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the presidency itself, which keeps its chair, its timetable, and a chief justice of its choosing; the JSP's state machines, whose referee challenges now route to a court headed by the president's appointee. Hurt — Chief Justice Bashe Yusuf Ahmed, dismissed by decree and claiming his office from outside it; the five Constitutional Division judges, protection withdrawn and travel restricted for a ruling; the six justices now litigating their own country's government in Arusha; voters in the 2027 process, whose electoral referee's timetable rests on a voided extension; and every future Somali litigant whose case touches the executive, who now knows the price a court pays for ruling against it.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His finest adjustment was foreign, and it was real. When Ethiopia signed its 1 January 2024 memorandum with Somaliland — sea access in exchange for prospective recognition — Mohamud's first answers were maximal: he demanded Ethiopia withdraw all its troops from Somalia and mobilized regional opinion against Addis Ababa (KAS analysis, March 2025). Then another option became available — Turkish mediation — and he took it, openly. The Ankara Declaration of 11 December 2024, signed by Mohamud and Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed under President Erdoğan's facilitation, reaffirmed each state's sovereignty and territorial integrity, left the memorandum behind, restored diplomatic relations, regularized Ethiopia's troop presence, and moved the sea-access question into bilateral negotiation under Somalia's sovereign authority (Ankara Declaration text, Turkish Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 11 December 2024). He walked back his own expulsion demand in public, in a signed document, and the walk-back de-escalated a crisis that had consumed, on the Africa Center for Strategic Studies' assessment, roughly a year of his government's focus (Africa Center for Strategic Studies, February 2026). That is the wobble answered exactly as this test asks: the pushback was absorbed, the position changed, the reasons were printed.
  • After the December 2024 Jubaland catastrophe — federal forces sent to Ras Kamboni defeated by Madobe's Dervish forces on 11 December 2024, with an estimated 600 federal soldiers retreating across the Kenyan border to be disarmed and hosted by the Kenya Defence Forces, and over 250 more reported surrendered (Jubaland crisis reference record, 2026) — he eventually changed register: he told lawmakers he would travel to Kismayo himself and spoke of changing "politics of confrontation into consensus-building" toward Madobe (reporting carried by The Free Library / Knowledge Bylanes, 2025). The flight ban on Jubaland towns that had paralyzed civilian movement during the confrontation belongs to the against shelf of the policy; the pivot to an offered visit, after a military defeat, is an adjustment, and it is recorded here as one.
  • Even in November 2026, the doubling-down has a moderating edge that the record should carry. Preliminary talks between federal security officials and SFC leaders were held on 10 November; the information minister stated afterward that the government still plans direct elections but is now "open to technical compromises," a formulation Critical Threats notes it "had not previously stated" (Critical Threats, Africa File, 12 November 2026). Mediated talks between the government and the SFC were announced for 14–18 November under US, Turkish, UK, and EU facilitation. A later report states the forum never opened and no new date has been set, with the court's 30-day framework deadline falling in early December (Kormeeraha Magazine, November 2026) — the two reports are printed together because the state of the talks is itself contested; the moderation of language is documented either way.
  • On the war's worst wobble — money — the no-good-choice clause applies, and its mechanics are stated here rather than the failure. By 2025–26 the options actually open to him were these: (a) halt the offensive and consolidate, ceding the initiative to an al-Shabaab that had already retaken towns across Middle Shabelle, Lower Shabelle, and Hiiraan and attempted his assassination in March 2025 (Africa Center for Strategic Studies, February 2026) — the harm: liberated populations returned to the group that taxes and conscripts them; (b) press the offensive with the forces at hand while AUSSOM's funding collapsed — the AU mission's spending ran a quarter below its approved budget in the year to June 2026 (UN Secretary-General's report, October 2026), the United States scaled back its contribution, and Uganda began withdrawing its bilateral contingent on 2 November 2026 and set a full exit by 2027 (Ugandan military statements, November 2026) — the harm: soldiers unpaid (Test 1's $53 million in arrears) and bases handed over faster than the Somali army can hold them, with al-Shabaab reported on 17 November 2026 to have recaptured 26 locations across seven regions following AU withdrawals (Baidoa Online report, carried by Eu SEE, November 2026 — a single-source report, recorded as reported, not independently verified); or (c) patch the gap bilaterally — the Uganda bilateral deployment of April 2025, Egyptian and Turkish support, intensified US airstrikes — the harm: deeper dependence on foreign forces whose timelines he does not control. He chose (b) with (c)'s patches, flew to Entebbe on 23 November 2026, and signed a joint communiqué with President Museveni calling for an increased offensive and a new funding mechanism for Ugandan troops (joint communiqué, Entebbe, 23 November 2026). No option on that list was good; the choice is graded as the best possible available in the moment and is not held against him. What remains gradable, and is graded on Tests 1 and 5, is the trap's construction: the 2022–23 offensive liberated territory faster than his government built the police, courts, and administrations to hold it — the stabilization gap the Middle East Institute and the Africa Center both identify as the opening al-Shabaab's counter-offensive walked through — and that was his government's planning failure, made in the good years, not the bad ones.

Evidence against:

  • The domestic wobbles were answered with force and denial at the moments they mattered most. When Puntland withdrew recognition on 31 March 2024 — the day after the first constitutional amendments passed — the answer was not renegotiation but completion of the program without it: further amendments in March 2026, adopted over the stated objections of two founding member states and the national opposition (FIKIR Institute, 2026; Kaab TV constitutional analysis, November 2026). When the opposition organized protests in Mogadishu in June 2026 against the term extension, the documented sequence is restriction of leaders' movements, a crackdown on demonstrations, one civilian killed at an SFC-organized protest (Genocide Watch special report, July 2026), and on 3 June open fighting in the capital between federal forces and the guards of two former national leaders (UN Secretary-General's report S/2026/862) — the worst violence in the capital in years, in which hundreds of families fled their neighborhoods (Al Jazeera reporting, June 2026). By the UN's account, mediation efforts by UNTMIS, Türkiye, the UK, the US, and the EU across May and July 2026 "did not achieve significant progress in bridging differences" (S/2026/862) — the door was held open by others for months; the bridging did not happen.
  • If another option became available domestically, the record shows him declining to look at it for most of the tenure. In June 2026 the SFC itself endorsed a compromise — limited direct voting inside the 4.5 framework as a bridge (Genocide Watch, July 2026) — which is to say the opposition moved toward his stated goal of direct elections and offered to meet on the bridge. Considering it and declining with stated reasons would have cost him nothing on this test. No documented federal engagement with that proposal appears in the record before the Supreme Court's November ruling forced the question; the November moderation (above) came after the court, the chief justice's dismissal, and the June fighting — adjustment under compulsion, late, and so far only in language.
  • The November ruling itself is the largest against-item: a wobble delivered by his own Supreme Court, in a reasoned judgment that split the difference (the amendments stand; their retroactive application falls), and his government's answer within 48 hours was that the ruling had "no legal effect" and a new chief justice sworn in at the palace (Tests 1–2). A ruler who answers the referee's whistle by replacing the referee has not answered the wobble; he has answered the referee.

Why: The grade lands on MIXED because the record genuinely splits, and the split runs along one line: outward, he adjusts — Ankara is a model answer, the Kismayo olive branch after a battlefield defeat is a real one, and the November softening of language, however late, is documented. Inward — toward the member states, the opposition, the street, and finally his own court — the answers were completion without consent, restriction and live fire in June, and rejection of the judgment in November. The war's funding trap is recorded under the no-good-choice clause and not held against him; the domestic traps were of his own program's making, and their answers are why the grade is not higher.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Somalia's sovereignty file and the whole country's diplomatic position (the Ankara de-escalation kept Ethiopia's sea access inside Somali law and restored relations); Jubaland's civilians, when confrontation gave way to an offered visit and the flight ban era ended; the residents of liberated towns whose defense continued under the clause-graded choice above. Hurt — the June 2026 dead and wounded in Mogadishu (Al Jazeera's reported toll: at least 13 killed, 189 wounded, responsibility for the first shots contested) and the families who fled the capital's fighting; Sharif Sheikh Ahmed and Hassan Ali Khaire, whose homes were surrounded and whose movements were restricted for organizing protests; Puntland's and Jubaland's publics, whose governments' rupture with the center hardened at each refusal to renegotiate; and the Somali public of November 2026, watching its president and its court deny each other's authority with the court's early-December deadline approaching and nothing built to meet it.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the member states that walked away, the displaced, the minority clans, the drought-stricken, and the people under al-Shabaab — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The direction of his flagship reform is toward counting everyone, and parts of it have actually happened. Somalia had not held a direct national vote since 1969; under his program, voter registration opened in April 2025 and reached roughly 900,000 people by mid-2026 (FIKIR Institute, 2026), and direct one-person-one-vote elections were in fact held — in the Banadir region, in South West State, and in Galmudug on 30 July 2026, where 16 political organizations contested 247 local-council seats across 11 districts and the commission counted some 193,000 valid ballots (NIEBC announcements, July–August 2026). Whatever the management of those votes (Tests 1–2), the citizens who cast them were counted as individuals for the first time in their state's history, and the 4.5 formula his model would retire is a system that counts some Somalis at half weight by design.
  • His government declared the drought a national emergency in November 2025 and its disaster agency has co-signed the warnings that quantify the uncounted: the IPC assessment carried jointly by the government and UN agencies projects some 6.5 million people — a third of the population — in crisis-level hunger or worse (IPC Phase 3+) into early 2026, two million of them in Phase 4 emergency, and 1.84 million children under five acutely malnourished in 2026 (IPC report, carried by the UN in Somalia, 2026). A government that publishes numbers like these against itself is, at minimum, counting the suffering on paper.
  • One community was counted in, structurally, on his watch: the Dhulbahante of Sool, Sanaag, and Cayn, whose forces expelled Somaliland's administration from Las Anod in 2023, were recognized in July 2025 as the North Eastern State, a federal member state inside Somalia; Mohamud traveled to Las Anod in mid-January 2026 for the swearing-in of its authorities, with Somali troops transferred to the new state (PISM Bulletin, 19 February 2026). For that community, federal recognition is the difference between being a contested borderland and being a counted state of the republic.

Evidence against:

  • The displaced of his own capital were counted out by his own government, at scale, with receipts. The EU Agency for Asylum records 143,548 people evicted in Mogadishu during 2025, including 63,132 from government properties, and 78 protests against land evictions between April 2025 and March 2026, up from eight in the preceding year (EUAA, June 2026). The Heritage Institute's research puts the government-land share of recorded evictions at 44 percent in 2025, up from 11 percent in 2021, within some 2.14 million recorded forced evictions across Somalia between 2017 and September 2026, Benadir accounting for roughly 64 percent (Heritage Institute, reported October 2026). Humanitarian monitors recorded over 140,071 IDPs forcibly evicted between January and June 2026 alone, in government-led land repossession (Genocide Watch special report, July 2026, citing humanitarian data). The evictions connect directly to Test 1's land file — the people removed are overwhelmingly the displaced poor; the land they are removed from is the land credibly alleged to be sold outside process. Named cases in the documented record include the reported killing of Axmed Xassan Rubac inside his home in Mogadishu's Suuq Bacaad area amid a disputed demolition in October 2026 (FTL Somalia, 28 October 2026) and the reported shooting of Abdullahi Abdi Raage while protesting evictions in Yaqshiid district in late November 2025 (Somali Salvation Forum statement, reported by Hiiraan Online, December 2025) — both recorded as reported by those sources, their circumstances contested by the authorities' silence in this record. Journalists and advocates documenting the evictions were themselves arrested, including a Himilo TV journalist in October 2025 (Salvation Forum statement, Hiiraan Online, December 2025).
  • Whole regions stand outside his count because the federation broke on his program. Puntland (withdrawn since March 2024), Jubaland (in armed confrontation since December 2024 — the reference record's casualty compilations for the constitutional crisis list, as separate counts, 126–235 killed in Puntland's theater, 515 killed in Jubaland, and 981+ killed in South West State; these are compiled estimates from the crisis's reference record, not adjudicated tolls, and they are printed separately and not summed here), and South West State (recognition suspended March 2026, its leadership then ousted and replaced by federal action in Baidoa amid ongoing clashes — Genocide Watch, July 2026) together hold millions of Somalis whose political counting — registration, elections, federal services — now runs, where it runs at all, outside his government's framework. The rupture's cause is graded on Tests 2 and 5; its incidence lands here: a president whose electoral roll reaches 900,000 in the aligned territories while three member states stand outside the count is not yet counting everyone, whatever the model's design.
  • Even inside the aligned map, the counting has left people out in documented cases. In Hirshabelle, demonstrations on polling day in east Beledweyne — dominated by the Hawadle clan, which had sought a power-sharing arrangement guaranteeing it the state presidency — ended with one reported civilian death and two security personnel injured (UN Secretary-General's report S/2026/862, October 2026): a community that asked to be counted in the state's leadership and was answered at the poll with force. And the largest uncounted population of all remains the one under al-Shabaab's parallel administration across the south and center — taxed, judged, and conscripted by the group his war has not dislodged (Test 5) — whose members appear in his government's accounting mainly as terrain.
  • The humanitarian ledger, stated separately from the political one: 4.8 million people in need in 2026 on OCHA's count, against a response plan of $852 million funded at 30.6 percent (OCHA Somalia, 2026); more than 4.6 million affected by the drought on authorities' estimates, with nearly half a million displaced from their homes since September 2025 (OCHA, 2026); over 700,000 displaced in 2026 so far, more than 500,000 of them by drought (Norwegian Refugee Council briefing, July 2026); some 3.3 million internally displaced in total (MSF, 2026); the World Food Programme's emergency food assistance cut from over two million people to just over 600,000 as funding collapsed (MSF, 2026). The funding collapse is a donors' failure as much as his — the largest cuts were made in Washington, not Mogadishu — and this shelf records that honestly. What is his is the priority order of the state he runs: in the same years, his government found its scarcest resource — political attention and coercive capacity — for constitutional amendments, state-leadership installations, and the eviction of the displaced from appreciating land, while the drought emergency he himself declared ran at a third funded.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the counting that works — registration, three states' direct votes, a new state recognized, the drought counted on paper — runs alongside a documented accounting in which the capital's displaced are evicted by the state at six-figure scale in a single year, three member states' populations stand outside the federal count after the federation broke on his program, a clan that asked for its share at a state poll buried a civilian, and the people under al-Shabaab are counted as terrain. The mandate rule is applied as written: he is not graded down for pursuing one-person-one-vote, the program he was elected on; he is graded on its incidence — and its incidence so far counts the aligned in and the displaced, the dissenting states, and the occupied out.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the roughly 900,000 registered voters and the citizens of Banadir, South West, and Galmudug who cast direct ballots for the first time; the Dhulbahante community of the North Eastern State, recognized as a counted state of the republic; private purchasers and developers of cleared Mogadishu land. Hurt — the 143,548 people evicted in Mogadishu in 2025 and the 140,071 IDPs evicted in the first half of 2026, overwhelmingly the already-displaced poor; the populations of Puntland, Jubaland, and South West State, outside the federal count amid armed confrontation; the Hawadle of east Beledweyne; the 6.5 million projected in crisis-level hunger whose declared emergency is funded at a third; and the populations under al-Shabaab, counted by neither government as citizens to be served.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 15 May 2022, with dates; his first presidency (2012–2017) stated as context for the word over time.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Debt relief — delivered, the tenure's cleanest kept word. He took office promising economic recovery and debt relief as first-100-days priorities (VOA interview, May 2022). In December 2023 Somalia reached the HIPC Completion Point: some $4.5 billion in debt relief, cutting external debt from 64 percent to under 6 percent of GDP (IMF / World Bank, December 2023, as summarized by the Africa Center for Strategic Studies, February 2026). In the same season Somalia took its seat for a two-year term on the UN Security Council (2025–2026). These were the program's promises, kept on schedule, and they are credited at full weight.
  • One-person-one-vote — genuinely set in motion. He pledged in May 2022 to scrap the clan-based formula and lead the country to one-person-one-vote elections, and repeated the commitment throughout the tenure, including the pledge of one-person-one-vote elections in 2026 (CRS / Congress.gov country report; Villa Somalia town-hall remarks, May 2024: "the government is firmly committed to a one person, one-vote election"). Registration began in April 2025; direct elections were held in Banadir, South West State, and Galmudug; the commission's timetable for national direct elections stands published (April–May 2027). The promise's direction was performed, at real political cost, against real resistance — the shelf records the motion in full. Its completion, and the terms on which it was pursued, are the against shelf below.
  • The total war — launched as promised. At the UN General Assembly in September 2022 he promised a "total war" against al-Shabaab — military, ideological, financial — and ruled out reconciliation with the group (UNGA address, September 2022). The war was in fact launched: the 2022–23 offensive, fought with the Macawiisley clan uprising, liberated significant territory in its first year (Africa Center for Strategic Studies, February 2026). The promise to fight was kept; the promise's object — elimination — is graded below.
  • The Ankara word — kept. The December 2024 declaration's commitments — restored relations with Ethiopia, sea access negotiated under Somali sovereignty, differences handled through dialogue with Turkish facilitation — were performed by his government through 2025–26, and Ethiopian forces' presence in Somalia was regularized rather than expelled (Ankara Declaration, December 2024; KAS analysis, March 2025). Notably, keeping this word required abandoning his own earlier stated position — the demand that all Ethiopian troops withdraw — and the abandonment is recorded on this shelf as the declaration's substance, not as a break: the signed word superseded the spoken one, and he kept the signed one.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The war's object — not delivered, and the trajectory reversed. "Total war" was promised as elimination; he assured Somalis repeatedly through the tenure that the fight would be finished. By 2025 al-Shabaab had retaken much of the territory liberated in 2022–23 across the Shabelle regions and Hiiraan, attempted to assassinate him in March 2025, and was threatening the capital's outskirts; by February 2026 the Africa Center for Strategic Studies' assessment was titled "Somalia at Risk of Becoming a Jihadist State" (Africa Center, February 2026). In November 2026 he was in Entebbe asking for a bigger offensive and a new way to pay for it (joint communiqué, 23 November 2026). Four and a half years after the promise, the group the war was to eliminate is reported recapturing locations vacated by withdrawing AU forces. A promise of effort was kept; the promise as made — elimination — was not, and the difference is the grade.
  • The inclusive agreement — promised first, abandoned in the doing. In May 2022 he named political stability a first-100-days priority in these terms: "We must come up with an inclusive agreement with the leaders of the federal-member states that federalizes the most important issues, including security" (VOA interview, May 2022). The constitutional program that followed was carried by the federal parliament over the objections — and finally the withdrawal — of the member states it most concerned: Puntland out from March 2024, Jubaland into armed confrontation, South West out by March 2026, the national opposition organized against it. The amendments may be good or bad law; that is not this test. This test grades the word: the agreement was to be inclusive, and the defining acts of the tenure were done without the inclusion promised, by the votes of the center alone.
  • The term itself — the promise underneath all the others. He was elected on 15 May 2022 under the Provisional Constitution's four-year term, in a system whose founding trauma is precisely this act: his predecessor Farmaajo's attempted extension produced armed clashes in Mogadishu in April 2021 and was reversed — the precedent Mohamud, of all Somali politicians, knew from the inside, having defeated Farmaajo partly on its strength. In March 2026 his parliament amended the constitution to five-year terms and he signed the change on 8 March, claiming a further year in office; in November 2026 his own Supreme Court ruled the change could not apply to his term and that his mandate ended on 15 May 2026. He remains in office on the strength of the extension the court voided. Whatever the amendments' merits as constitutional design — and the court upheld the amending process itself — the word a four-year mandate carries was not kept: he is serving, as graded, in a fifth year no electorate and no court has given him.
  • The first term's word, as context the seat carries: elected in 2012, he presided over a term in which the promised move to one-person-one-vote elections by 2016 did not occur — the 2016 process reverted to an expanded indirect model — and he handed over in February 2017 having told interviewers he had also handed over "a detailed plan that would have led the country to a different voting system" (VOA, May 2022). The pattern across fourteen years is consistent: the destination is always sincerely named, the schedule is always the casualty, and the reason is always the situation. This dossier grades the second term; the first is recorded because Test 5's title is "over time."

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the kept shelf, though real, is a shelf of deliverables — debt relief, a war launched, a declaration honored, a reform set in motion — while the broken shelf holds the words that underwrite the seat itself: the inclusive agreement promised in his first hundred days and abandoned in his defining acts, the elimination of al-Shabaab promised and reversed, and above all the four-year term he was elected under, extended by his own signature and voided by his own court, in a country that fought in its own capital five years ago over exactly this act by exactly his predecessor. A ruler's word about how long he will stay is the promise all his other promises stand behind; on the documented record, it has not held.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Somalia's treasury and every Somali who benefits from a state carrying 6 percent external debt instead of 64 (the kept debt-relief word); the citizens who registered and voted directly for the first time (the kept direction of the electoral word); Ethiopia and the region, by the kept Ankara word. Hurt — the electorate of 2026, promised a transfer of power and given an extension instead; the member-state publics whose leaders were promised inclusion in 2022; the soldiers and civilians of a war promised as elimination and now in its fifth year of reversal; and Mohamud's own historical position — the man elected to end the era of mandate extensions, now holding office under one.

The four crossings, named: 1. Kept his word AND it helped: debt relief — promised in May 2022, delivered at the HIPC Completion Point in December 2023, $4.5 billion of burden removed from every Somali's state. The Ankara commitments, kept through 2025–26, helped in the same direction: a sovereignty crisis de-escalated without a shot. 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt: the total war's no-reconciliation word. He promised at the UN in September 2022 to eliminate al-Shabaab and ruled out any negotiated track, and that word he has kept without deviation — no talks, no political track, through four years of reversal. The keeping is credited here; the hurt is recorded in full: with the military track failing by 2025–26 and no other door ever opened, the war's costs — the dead of the Shabelle offensives, the populations returned to al-Shabaab's administration, the assassination attempt on his own convoy — fell on a country whose government had promised itself, in public, exactly one instrument. Analysts across the spectrum (International Crisis Group; the "Exiting Somalia's Political Impasse" assessment) identify the foreclosed political track as a cost of the policy; the identification is analysis, recorded as such — the fact of the kept word and the fact of the reversal are the record's own. 3. Went against his word AND it helped: the Ethiopian troop demand. Through 2024 his stated position was that Ethiopia must withdraw all its forces from Somalia; at Ankara in December 2024 he signed the opposite — Ethiopian presence regularized, relations restored — and the reversal helped: it ended the year's most dangerous interstate crisis and kept an experienced counter-al-Shabaab force in the field. The break with his spoken position is recorded on this axis; the help is recorded on the other; neither launders the other. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt: the term and the inclusion promises (above) — broken together, and the hurt is the federation itself: three member states withdrawn or in confrontation, fighting in the capital in June 2026, a chief justice dismissed, and a president and a supreme court denying each other's authority as the court's December deadline approaches.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

One law — FAILS. The legal order survives — an auditor publishing against the government, a court annulling the ruling party's state election — but at the source it bends: a treason warrant from the capital's court for a defiant state president, a public-land program three former presidents and 97 MPs say ran outside the contracts rules with proceeds missing the treasury, the world's joint-lowest corruption score (9 of 100), unpaid salaries beside broken procurement rules, and the state's most sensitive posts credibly reported as family placements. Helped: land buyers, the household, the aligned state machines. Hurt: Madobe, the evicted, the unpaid, and every unconnected competitor for the state's business.

Without gripping — FAILS. The tenure's referees functioned until the senior one ruled on the seat's own tenure — then, in one November week: the chief justice dismissed by decree the night before the ruling, outside both constitutional routes (UN experts: unlawful); the ruling delivered anyway and declared of "no legal effect"; a replacement sworn in at the palace; the ruling judges' protection withdrawn and travel restricted; six justices litigating in Arusha. Add an electoral commission running a timetable that presupposes the voided extension, state elections managed in the aligned states, and a parliament that extended its own mandate in the same instrument, and the grip is not an inference but a sequence. Helped: the presidency and its party. Hurt: Chief Justice Ahmed, the five judges, the 2027 voter, and every future litigant against the executive.

The wobble — MIXED. Outward, he answers as well as anyone in this series: the Ankara Declaration — his own expulsion demand walked back in a signed document, a sovereignty crisis de-escalated — and an offered reconciliation in Kismayo after a battlefield defeat. The war's funding collapse is graded under the no-good-choice clause: every option open in 2025–26 carried real harm, his Entebbe choice is recorded as the best possible available in the moment, and only the trap's construction — territory liberated faster than the state learned to hold it — is graded, on Tests 1 and 5. Inward, the answers were completion without consent (2024–26), restriction and fighting in the capital (June 2026), and rejection of his own court's judgment (November 2026). The opposition's June 2026 bridge proposal — limited direct voting inside the 4.5 frame — was an option that became available and was not, on this record, openly considered until the court forced the question.

Counts everyone — FAILS. The design counts everyone and the pilots proved Somalis will vote directly when allowed — 900,000 registered, three states' direct ballots, a new member state recognized for the Dhulbahante. The incidence counts otherwise: 143,548 evicted in the capital in 2025 (63,132 from government land), 140,071 IDPs evicted in six months of 2026, three member states' populations outside the federal count amid armed confrontation, a Hawadle civilian dead at a Hirshabelle poll, 6.5 million in crisis hunger under a self-declared emergency funded at a third, and the al-Shabaab-administered population counted as terrain. Helped: the registered, the first-time voters, the North Eastern State. Hurt: the evicted displaced above all — the people with the least, removed by the state from the land with the most value.

His word — FAILS. Kept: debt relief (December 2023, $4.5 billion, 64→<6 percent of GDP), the war launched, Ankara honored, the electoral reform genuinely begun. Broken: elimination of al-Shabaab (reversed by 2025; "Somalia at Risk of Becoming a Jihadist State," February 2026), the inclusive federal agreement promised for his first hundred days (his defining acts done by the center alone), and the four-year term itself — extended by his signature in March 2026, voided as applied to him by his Supreme Court in November 2026, and served anyway. The crossings are named in Test 5: kept-and-helped (debt relief, Ankara), kept-and-hurt (the no-reconciliation war word), against-his-word-and-helped (the Ethiopian troop reversal at Ankara), broke-and-hurt (the term and inclusion promises, whose price is the federation).

Hassan Sheikh Mohamud is the only man Somalis have twice chosen to lead them, and the record shows why he was chosen twice: he delivers programs. Debt relief arrived; the war was fought; the constitution was completed; Somalis registered to vote directly for the first time in half a century. The same record shows the price list attached to the delivering: every program was carried by the center's will alone — parliament voting, courts warranting, soldiers deploying, the displaced evicted — and when the programs reached the one question they could not be allowed to answer, the length of his own tenure, the will that carried them all was turned on the referees: a chief justice dismissed in the night, a judgment declared void, a fifth year taken. He was elected in 2022 as the answer to Farmaajo's extension. As graded in November 2026 — in office past his term on his own court's reading, three member states withdrawn or fighting, al-Shabaab advancing into vacated bases, and mediated talks announced and unopened — he is the extension's second author. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the court's early-December framework deadline and 90-day election order are met or defied; whether the East African Court of Justice file restores the judiciary or hardens the split; whether the Security Council renews AUSSOM in December and on what money; and whether the talks with the Somali Future Council, when they finally open, produce the inclusive agreement he promised in his first hundred days — four and a half years late, and still the only door on the record that leads out.
