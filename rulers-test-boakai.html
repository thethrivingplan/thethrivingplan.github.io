The Ruler's Test — Joseph Boakai, President of Liberia

Subject: Joseph Nyuma Boakai Sr. — President of the Republic of Liberia since 22 January 2024. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: Liberia is a presidential republic in which the president is head of state, head of government, and commander-in-chief; there is no separate executive office above or beside him. Boakai, born 30 November 1944 in Worsonga, Lofa County, is a career official — agriculture minister under Samuel Doe (1983–1985), then Vice President under Ellen Johnson Sirleaf for twelve years (2006–2018) — who lost the presidency to George Weah in 2017 and won it on his second attempt. He took the seat by the rules: in the 14 November 2023 run-off he defeated the incumbent, Weah, by 50.64% to 49.36% on the National Elections Commission's count, and Weah conceded on 17 November 2023, before the final tally was announced — the second consecutive transfer of power between elected governments in post-war Liberia, and the context in which every grade below sits. He is graded on roughly thirty-four months in office. His age is a documented fact of the seat and is recorded only where the record documents it: he was 79 at inauguration, the oldest president in Liberia's history; during his inaugural address on 22 January 2024 he faltered in heat above 30°C, was helped from the podium, and did not finish the speech; his office stated the same day that he had suffered heat exhaustion, that his doctors had declared him "perfectly fine," and that he had resumed normal duties (AFP, Reuters and BBC reporting, 22–23 January 2024). No further episode of incapacity appears in the documented record assembled here; through November 2026 the Executive Mansion's own releases show a full working schedule, and he turns 82 on 30 November 2026. His predecessor Weah (president 2018–2024) appears as context where the record Boakai inherited — or the promises the seat carried into the election — bear directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. The mandate rule applies throughout: Boakai was elected on the Unity Party's "Rescue" platform and its ARREST agenda (Agriculture, Roads, Rule of Law, Education, Sanitation, Tourism), and governing on that platform is not graded as a failure in itself — carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how it is done. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier says so, names the options that actually existed and the harm each carried, and grades the choice made inside the constraint — while still grading whether he helped build the trap and whether he was honest inside it. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The asset-declaration law was enforced against his own government, at scale, on the record. On 12 February 2025 Boakai suspended 457 public officials for failing to declare their assets to the Liberia Anti-Corruption Commission (LACC) as the Code of Conduct requires — his own appointees, including the ministers of education and health, the minister overseeing the budget, ambassadors (including to the United States), Executive Mansion staff, and the entire interim management of the Liberia Electricity Corporation — for one month without pay or until they complied; the Civil Service Agency then froze the salaries of the 216 who fell under its authority (BBC, AFP and AP reporting, 12–13 February 2025; LACC published list, February 2025). The suspension followed a directive of 27 November 2024 that had given officials a ten-day grace period, and it was described in wire reporting as the first enforcement of its kind since the civil war.
  • He put himself under the same instruments first. On taking office he declared his own assets to the LACC, disclosed his salary (US$13,400 a year), ordered an audit of the presidency itself, and in July 2024 announced a voluntary 40% cut in that salary to US$8,000; the same period saw the General Auditing Commission (GAC) and the LACC publicly strengthened rather than starved (Executive Mansion statement, July 2024; BBC, July 2024).
  • Ministers of his own camp have faced consequences. Commerce Minister Amin Modad — a former chairman of Boakai's own Unity Party — resigned on 10 October 2024 after the purchase of a ministry vehicle for roughly US$96,000 through the Liberia Revenue Authority, against the US$45,000 cap the 2024 Budget Law sets for such purchases; Boakai accepted the resignation publicly, calling the purchase "poor judgment" and a "lack of sensitivity to the current economic environment," and ordered the vehicle transferred to the General Services Agency (Executive Mansion statement, 10 October 2024; Center for Transparency and Accountability in Liberia statement, October 2024). Modad denied personal misconduct, and later stated that audits and a Senate review found no malfeasance on his part — a claim recorded here as his, contested by the accountability groups that pressed the case. In early February 2025 Boakai also suspended the head of the National Oil Company, Rostonlyn Suacoco Dennis, on suspicion of corruption (AFP via News Central TV, February 2025).
  • His own appointees have been prosecuted, not only the other camp's. Abdullah Kamara, whom Boakai appointed acting chairman of the Liberia Telecommunications Authority (LTA) in 2024, was suspended and then indicted by the LACC in the Liberia Digital Transformation Project case (alleged disbursements of more than US$3.5 million to Tamma Corporation and others, per a GAC audit) — charges that are ALLEGATIONs; in September 2025 Criminal Court "C" dropped Kamara from the indictment, holding he had been improperly joined, and the Supreme Court dismissed the LACC's appeal (The New Dawn, 5 September 2025; Analyst Liberia, September 2025). The leadership of the Liberia Drug Enforcement Agency — his appointees — was dismissed twice over: Director General Abraham Kromah and two deputies suspended in June 2024 after a physical altercation at the agency's headquarters, and Director General Anthony Souh and both deputies dismissed outright on 28 August 2025 (Executive Mansion statements, June 2024 and 28 August 2025).
  • The flagship prosecution of the former regime was run to a verdict the government lost, and the loss stood. Former finance minister Samuel D. Tweah Jr. and four co-defendants were tried in Criminal Court "C" over some US$6.2 million in Financial Intelligence Agency funds; on 8 May 2026 a jury acquitted Tweah and Moses Cooper on all counts, convicted three co-defendants (including the former acting justice minister, Nyanti Tuan) on some counts, and the acquittals were given effect — Tweah walked free (Criminal Court "C" verdict, 8 May 2026; Liberian and regional reporting, May 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The criminal-prosecution map still runs overwhelmingly in one direction: against the defeated camp. The senior figures indicted, tried, or summoned since January 2024 are, in the documented record, almost all Weah-era officials or CDC figures — Tweah and his co-defendants; former LTA chairperson Edwina Zackpah; former speaker J. Fonati Koffa and three CDC lawmakers in the Capitol arson case (Test 2). No comparably senior figure of Boakai's own camp has been carried through a completed criminal process; the own-camp consequences on the FOR shelf are resignations, suspensions, and one indictment a court threw out. The CDC's charge that the campaign is a political witch-hunt is an interested party's characterization, recorded as an ALLEGATION — but the asymmetry it points at is itself documented.
  • Five days after Tweah's acquittal, the Asset Recovery and Property Retrieval Task Force (AREPT, created by Executive Order No. 161) summoned him again, on 13 May 2026, over an alleged US$20.5 million rice-subsidy diversion from 2021–2022 — an ALLEGATION he denies, calling the probe "bogus"; the timing is a fact whatever the merits (AREPT summons, 13 May 2026; GNN Liberia and FrontPage Africa reporting, May 2026). The CDC further alleged that US$275,000 was offered to jurors in the concluded trial through a ruling-party official — an ALLEGATION for which no corroborating evidence appears in the record assembled here, and which the government has not been shown to answer on the record.
  • The sequencing of the tenure removals (Test 2) also belongs on this shelf: after the Supreme Court blocked the direct replacement of tenured officials in April 2024, the government commissioned audits of the institutions concerned and then suspended the same officials on the audits' preliminary findings — a route the Liberian Investigator and other critics describe as reaching a predetermined destination by a second door. The GAC's Central Bank audit did document real irregularities for 2018–2023 (excessive expenditure, deficit financing, unauthorized payroll financing, procurement without competitive bidding); the question Test 1 grades is not whether the findings existed but whether the instrument was pointed at everyone or only at those already marked for removal. On the record assembled here, no audit-driven suspension of a Boakai-aligned tenured official appears.

Why this grade: The asset-declaration enforcement is the strongest one-law evidence in this dossier: a rule applied in one sweep to 457 of his own officials, ministers included, with pay actually stopped. Against it stands the criminal map, which after nearly three years still shows completed process on one side and administrative consequence on the other. One law at the level of declarations and dismissals; not yet one law at the level of prosecution. That is MIXED, and the open file that moves it is named: whether any senior figure of his own camp is ever carried through a court process to a verdict, and what becomes of the post-acquittal summons of Tweah. Helped: Compliant officials and the public — the declaration regime now has a cost attached for everyone in government; taxpayers, in the CSA's accounting (the agency reported savings of about US$104,000 from the February 2025 salary freeze of 216 officials; its director-general separately told AFP the freeze saved more than US$204,000 — the two figures are printed as their sources gave them, not blended). Hurt: The 457 suspended officials (one month's pay, including many local officials later found on an LACC list that was itself challenged as error-ridden); Weah-era officials held in long pre-verdict processes; Samuel Tweah, who bore a two-year trial ending in acquittal and a fresh summons within the week.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The first time the Supreme Court ruled against him, he complied in form. In February 2024 Boakai nominated replacements for the heads of five tenured bodies — the LTA, the Governance Commission, the National Public Health Institute, the National Lottery, and the National Identification Registry — while the incumbents' tenures still ran. The incumbents petitioned; on 24 April 2024 a unanimous Supreme Court held that statutory tenure does not violate the president's Article 56(a) appointment power, affirmed the writ of prohibition for four of the five petitioners, and ordered the nominees' names withdrawn (Supreme Court opinion, 24 April 2024, read by Chief Justice Sie-A-Nyene Yuoh). The names were withdrawn.
  • Courts have ruled against his government's position repeatedly since, without documented retaliation. Criminal Court "C" discharged his own LTA appointee from the LACC's indictment and the Supreme Court let that stand (September 2025); a jury acquitted the former finance minister his government most wanted convicted (May 2026); in the Capitol arson case the Supreme Court stayed the trial twice on defense petitions — September 2025 and 3 March 2026 — and the state waited more than seventeen months for the trial to proceed; when the Court finally ruled on 28 August 2026 it did so on the law of remedies, rejecting the defense's prohibition petition and ordering the trial resumed (Supreme Court judgment, 28 August 2026, per Chief Justice Yamie Quiqui Gbeisay Sr.).
  • The Central Bank fight ended in a negotiated legal settlement and a Senate confirmation, not a fait accompli left standing by force. Suspended governor J. Aloysius Tarlue's challenge was closed by a joint stipulation of discontinuance filed 13 January 2025 and approved by the Supreme Court; Henry F. Saamoi, who had acted since July 2024, was then nominated, faced Senate hearings, and holds the post substantively from 5 February 2025 on the Central Bank's own records (Supreme Court discontinuance, January 2025; Central Bank of Liberia board records, 2025).
  • The media referee still blows its whistle. Liberia stood 54th of 180 in RSF's 2025 World Press Freedom Index and 58th in 2026 (score 64.54, classified "problematic"); critical outlets — FrontPage Africa, the Liberian Investigator, The New Dawn — publish detailed attacks on the administration's conduct, including on the cases in this dossier, and no executive closure of an outlet appears in the record (RSF Index 2025 and 2026; New Dawn, 7 July 2026). The 2019 press-freedom act decriminalizing libel remains in force and unamended.
  • The electoral referee that seated him was independent of him — he won the 2023 election as the opposition candidate, on the NEC's count, against the incumbent who controlled the state (context for the seat; NEC final results, November 2023).

Evidence against:

  • The tenure doctrine was announced as policy before it was tested: at the cabinet's first meeting on 23 February 2024, Boakai's legal adviser announced that the cabinet had authorized the president to replace all tenured officials except those the Constitution itself protects, on the theory that statutory tenure violates Article 56(a) — the Unity Party adopting in office the position it had loudly opposed when the CDC proposed it in 2018 (legal adviser Bushuben Keita, February 2024; TLC Africa review). A declared intention to override tenure wholesale is a grip announced, even where courts later trimmed its execution.
  • After the April 2024 ruling blocked replacement, the same officials were reached by suspension. Governor Tarlue was suspended on 30 July 2024 on the basis of the GAC's Central Bank audit, although the Central Bank Act provides for a governor's removal only by impeachment by the Legislature and contains no suspension power; a Justice in Chambers issued a stay on 9 August 2024, and the full bench never ruled on the merits before the case was settled (Liberian Investigator, August 2024; Smart News Liberia, 9 August 2024). The destination the Court had barred by one door — a new governor of the president's choosing — was reached by another. The same audit-then-suspend sequence was applied at the LTA and the Governance Commission (Liberian Investigator, August 2024).
  • The speakership file is the heaviest item on this shelf. In October–November 2024 a "Majority Bloc" of representatives — anchored by Boakai's Unity Party and led by Richard Koon, an avowed Boakai supporter who called himself the "regime Speaker" — purported to remove Speaker J. Fonati Koffa (CDC) and elect Koon, sitting in the Joint Chambers while Koffa, present and available, presided elsewhere. The Supreme Court's 6 December 2024 opinion held any such sitting that contravened Articles 33 and 49 ultra vires without naming a winner; Boakai's attorney general issued an opinion treating the bloc's acts as lawful, which the Court later dismissed as irrelevant. On 23 April 2025 the Court ruled definitively: any sitting of the bloc from which the elected Speaker was excluded while present and not incapacitated is unconstitutional, and the bloc's acts — including its passage of the 2025 national budget, which Boakai had signed and under which the state was operating — were void. Boakai's response acknowledged the ruling and announced that his government would "work with the quorum that ensures the full functioning of our government" — in substance, continued recognition of the bloc the Court had held unconstitutional, not of the Speaker the Court had affirmed. The deadlock ended not by compliance but by attrition: Koffa, unable to assemble the 36-member quorum to conduct business under his own gavel, resigned on 12 May 2025, and Koon was elected Speaker in a lawful vote the next day, 43–26 (Supreme Court opinions, 6 December 2024 and 23 April 2025; Executive Mansion statement, April 2025; Liberian Investigator, 13 May 2025). Koffa's Rule of Law Caucus named the thing plainly in its farewell statement: "continuous defiance of the Rule of Law by the Executive" — an interested party's words, recorded as such; the sequence they describe is documented.
  • The street-level referees were gripped at the December 2024 protest. On 17 December 2024, police met the "White Tuesday" protest against the speakership maneuvers with tear gas and, allegedly, live ammunition, injuring and arresting demonstrators including a former presidential aide; those arrested were released without charge on 19 December (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). A month earlier, on 17 November 2024, ruling-party youth assaulted journalist Nyantee Togba as he covered a Unity Party meeting attended by Boakai; the Press Union of Liberia condemned the attack, and no prosecution of the attackers appears in the record assembled here (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). CEMESP, the Liberian media-development monitor, catalogued a 2025 pattern of police assaults and threats against journalists — including a BBC journalist allegedly assaulted by police at the national stadium and a newspaper's editors threatened with arrest by a deputy police inspector general over a story — and warned in May 2026 of "press freedom backsliding under Boakai" (CEMESP statement, 3 May 2026).

Why this grade: Two records run side by side. In the courts' own house, Boakai has mostly taken his losses: the 2024 tenure ruling was complied with, adverse verdicts were given effect, the bank governorship was regularized through the Senate. But at the two points where a referee stood directly across his party's path — tenured officials he wanted replaced, and a Speaker his bloc wanted gone — the referees were routed around: suspension where the law gives no suspension, and a quorum formula where the Court had given a judgment. MIXED, at the failing edge; the speakership sequence is what keeps it from HOLDS, and the documented compliances are what keep it from FAILS. Helped: The judiciary's formal authority — its biggest rulings were eventually given effect and its composition was changed only by constitutional process (Chief Justice Yuoh retired at the constitutional age in 2025; her successor was appointed and confirmed); the Central Bank, which got a confirmed governor and an ended dispute; newspaper readers — the press remains able to publish the case against him. Hurt: J. Fonati Koffa, whose lawful speakership ended by attrition after the Court affirmed it; the tenured officials suspended on audit findings without the removal process their statutes prescribe, Governor Tarlue first among them; protesters and journalists who met the state's hands in December and November 2024.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His first war-court appointment was reversed, openly, in the direction the pushback demanded. Boakai created the Office for the Establishment of the War and Economic Crimes Court by Executive Order No. 131 on 2 May 2024 and appointed Cllr. Jonathan Massaquoi to head it in June 2024. Victims' groups, civil society, and the Bar objected that Massaquoi had served as a lawyer for figures accused of war-era crimes — a conflict at the heart of the office. By August 2024 Boakai had dissolved the appointment, stating the process must be "subjected to open and broad-based public scrutiny," and re-ran the selection through a consultative search committee of civil-society organizations and the Liberia Council of Churches; its vetted nominee, Cllr. Jallah Barbu, took office on 1 November 2024 to broad stakeholder approval (DUBAWA, 2024; OHCHR Liberia report, 2024; New Narratives, November 2024). When another option became available — a consultative appointment instead of a unilateral one — he took it, at the price of admitting the first choice was wrong.
  • The rice wobble was answered by reversal within days. In May 2024, after India's export taxes pushed importers to demand US$20 for a 25kg bag, his Commerce Ministry announced an agreed increase from US$16.50 to US$18.50 (17 May 2024). Public reaction was immediate; Boakai summoned the importers to the Executive Mansion, disapproved his own minister's announcement, and the price was held — "there will be no increase in the price of rice" (The Analyst and Frontier Africa Reports, 21–23 May 2024; DUBAWA fact-check, 2024). The no-good-choice mechanics of that week are recorded under this test below.
  • The yellow machines saga shows a slow, public adjustment rather than a defended error. The May 2024 announcement of 285 earth-moving machines — presented at a cabinet retreat as arranged through a presidential associate, before any completed procurement — collapsed under legislative and civil-society challenge: the Public Procurement and Concessions Commission stated it had "zero involvement" and no record of the deal (PPCC Executive Director Bodger Scott Johnson, April 2025). The government's answer across 2025 was to concede the point in stages: the information minister acknowledged no final agreement had been consummated and negotiations continued (January 2025); Boakai wrote to legislative leaders that no contract had been finalized and no public funds disbursed; the acquisition was renegotiated — a vice-presidential review cut the projected cost from roughly US$43 million to about US$22 million — and restructured as an Ecobank-financed procurement through the National Road Fund and the Ministry of Public Works; the first 137 machines docked at the Freeport of Monrovia on 22 February 2026 (FrontPage Africa reporting, 2024–2025; The Liberian Investigator, 23 February 2026 and 6 March 2026). The adjustment took twenty-one months and the questions about the first announcement remain unanswered — a lawmaker was still demanding a full accounting of the 2024 unveiling in March 2026 — but the course change itself was made in the open and in the direction the critics demanded.
  • When the war-court office's starvation became a public rupture, he answered with money and deadlines. Through 2025 and into 2026, Executive Director Barbu disclosed that the office had received US$500,000 of the US$2 million pledged for its first year (US$368,000 of it a transfer from the Justice Ministry rather than a fresh allocation) and US$800,000 of the 2025 pledge, and in April 2026 he publicly accused senior officials of obstruction. Boakai's response was Executive Order No. 164 (signed by May 2026), renewing the office's mandate to 30 April 2027 with quarterly disbursements of US$500,000 ordered paid directly into the office's accounts, 60-, 90- and 120-day deadlines for mobilization and implementation frameworks, and — in late 2026, returning from a United States trip — a public 90-day challenge to move the enabling bills through the Legislature, alongside his January 2026 annual message confirming the draft court bills were complete and urging lawmakers to act (The Liberian Investigator, 4 May 2026; Executive Order No. 164; annual message, 26 January 2026; New Narratives, October 2026).
  • The street was answered differently the second time. The 17 July 2025 "Enough is Enough" march by the STAND movement and allied groups — against economic hardship, alleged police brutality, and perceived persecution — was permitted to proceed through Monrovia and present its petition, without the tear gas and arrests of December 2024 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). And in 2026 he has owned the delivery problem in front of his own cabinet rather than blaming it away: August 2026 warnings over implementation delays, and on 23 October 2026 the public caution that government is "not a friendship club" and officials who underperform will answer for it (Executive Mansion releases, August and October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the largest constitutional pushback of his tenure he did not adjust. The Supreme Court's 23 April 2025 judgment was not ambiguous; his government's answer was to keep working with the bloc it voided until the lawful Speaker resigned (detailed in Test 2). Whatever the practical bind — graded under the no-good-choice mechanics below — the public posture was not "the Court has ruled and we are trapped"; it was a formula ("the quorum that ensures the full functioning of our government") that treated the judgment as one input among others. On this wobble, the answer was to outlast the referee.
  • The tenure wobble was answered by changing the instrument, not the aim (Test 2): prohibition in April 2024, suspensions on commissioned audits by July 2024. Adjustment of method in service of the same destination is recorded on this shelf, not the other.
  • The war-court funding wobble ran for over a year before it was answered. Barbu's disclosures about missing money began in his first months (he took office in November 2024 to find, in his words, "no building, no stationery… just absolutely nothing" turned over, and no accounting for the 2024 allocation); the structural fix — direct quarterly disbursement — arrived only in May 2026, after the dispute had spilled into public accusations between the office and the Justice Ministry (FrontPage Africa/New Narratives, December 2024; The Liberian Investigator, May 2026).
  • December 2024's protest policing was never publicly answered for. The arrested protesters were released without charge — a quiet correction — but no documented inquiry, apology, or discipline for the tear gas and alleged live fire appears in the record assembled here.

The no-good-choice files, graded by the clause's mechanics:

  • The speakership, after 23 April 2025. The options that actually existed: (a) give the judgment full effect and transact only with Speaker Koffa — who could not assemble the 36 members Article 33 requires for a quorum, meaning no budget execution, no confirmations, a seized state; or (b) continue transacting with the bloc that held the numbers and not the law, keeping the state running on acts the Court had voided. Each carried real harm; no third door was open in the moment, and the constraint is recorded, not counted as a demerit in itself. What remains gradable, and is graded in Test 2: the trap's origin — the bloc was his own party's project to remove an opposition Speaker, launched while Koffa was abroad with the president in Rome — and the honesty inside the trap: the government did not say "we are trapped"; it said the quorum formula was lawful, after its own attorney general's supporting opinion had been dismissed by the Court as irrelevant. The least-harm door eventually taken was the lawful one — Koon's election by a properly presided vote on 13 May 2025 — but it was reached by the Speaker's resignation under deadlock, not by the executive's compliance.
  • Rice, May 2024. The options: (a) let the price rise toward US$20, loading the increase onto the poorest households' staple; (b) restore the previous administration's subsidy payments to importers — the same subsidy mechanism now under an AREPT investigation for alleged diversion (an ALLEGATION concerning 2021–2022 payments); or (c) hold the price by executive pressure and absorb the importers' threat of shortage. He took (c); supply held, and by December 2025 food prices were falling year-on-year (World Bank, Liberia Economic Update, November 2026). The trap predates him — an imported staple, a foreign export tax, an empty treasury — and is not of his building. Graded as the best available choice in the moment, and recorded as such.

Why this grade: The pattern is consistent and two-sided. On policy wobbles — an appointment, a price, a procurement, a starved office — Boakai adjusts openly, sometimes at real cost to his own announcements. On the one wobble that was a final court judgment against his party's central parliamentary project, he did not adjust; he endured the judgment until events dissolved it. MIXED. Helped: War-crimes victims and advocates, who got a consultatively chosen office head and, eventually, a funded mandate; rice consumers, whose staple's price was held in May 2024; taxpayers, in the renegotiated machines deal (roughly US$21 million below the first projected cost, on the vice president's committee's figures); protesters of July 2025, who marched unmolested. Hurt: Speaker Koffa and the Court's authority in the speakership file; the procurement system's credibility across the twenty-one months the machines announcement went unexplained; the war-court office's staff and the victims waiting on it during the year its funding wobble went unanswered.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition, the counties, the war's victims, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The war's victims were counted by act, not only by speech — after two administrations left the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's 2009 recommendation of a war crimes court unimplemented for fifteen years. Boakai created the establishing office within four months of inauguration (Executive Order No. 131, 2 May 2024); under Barbu the office ran a nationwide consultative road map (launched July 2025), produced draft bills for both a War and Economic Crimes Court and a National Anti-Corruption Court (submitted to the presidency in December 2025 and to the Justice Minister in January 2026), and was renewed three times, most recently by Executive Order No. 164 (Office records; FrontPage Africa, January 2026; The Liberian Investigator, May 2026). In November 2026 he signed a charter for a national Peace, Reconciliation and Memorial Museum and inaugurated its steering committee, saying "Liberia must confront its past to secure lasting peace" (Executive Mansion, 14–18 November 2026). The wars killed some 250,000 people (Civitas Maxima, 2025) — the constituency being counted is the largest in this dossier.
  • The accountability net was thrown over his own counties and camp: of the 457 officials suspended in February 2025, some 349 were local officials — superintendents, commissioners, mayors — spread across Lofa, Nimba, Montserrado, Grand Bassa, Sinoe, River Gee, Grand Kru and other counties (LACC list; Women Voices Newspaper, February 2025).
  • Distribution across the whole map is the design of his flagship programs: the yellow machines are allocated nineteen to each of the fifteen counties; in November 2026 he broke ground on electrification for Bomi, Gbarpolu and Grand Cape Mount counties under the assurance that "no county will be left in the dark" (Executive Mansion, 6 November 2026); the FY2026 budget of about US$1.211 billion — Liberia's first billion-dollar budget, roughly 94% domestically financed — was approved for 2026 (Ministry of Finance figures; Executive Mansion, November 2026).
  • Groups routinely counted out appear in his 2026 record by name: a National Autism and Disability Initiative launched with the First Lady (10 November 2026); a gender-responsive foreign policy launched 17 November 2026 (UN Women Africa, 18 November 2026); rural women addressed as a constituency in their own right (Executive Mansion, 14 November 2026). Drug-addicted youth — the "kush" generation — have been treated in his public framing as an "existential threat" to be answered with enforcement and institutional reform of the drug agency, and his government received 304 agricultural machines from China in October 2026 for the farming counties (Executive Mansion releases, August 2025 and October 2026).
  • The poor's basket got cheaper on its biggest line: after the May 2024 intervention, food inflation swung from 9.7% year-on-year in December 2024 to −1.7% by December 2025 (World Bank, Liberia Economic Update, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The counted economy and the lived economy have diverged. Real GDP grew 5.1% in 2025 on the World Bank's and IMF's shared figure (the African Development Bank's country note gives 4.6% for 2025 — the counts are printed separately, as their sources give them) — but the growth was driven by a mining sector that expanded 17%, and the World Bank's 2026 Public Finance Review reporting records Liberia's poverty rate rising through the same period (World Bank, 2026; Torch Media Africa summary of the Bank's September–November 2026 reports). The enclave is counted; the households outside it are, so far, counted in the speeches and not yet in the outcome.
  • The war's victims are counted most in words and least in cash. The office charged with delivering their court received US$500,000 against a US$2 million pledge in its first year and US$800,000 against the 2025 pledge, on its director's public accounting — funded, as the Liberian Investigator put it, at half the rate the TRC itself once received, irregularly, through channels its director described as obstructive (The Liberian Investigator, 4 May 2026). Seventeen years after the TRC reported, there is still no court, no enabling law passed, and no indictment; the director projects first indictments in 2027, and the office's mandate now expires on 30 April 2027 with the bills still before the Legislature (Civitas Maxima, 2025; Executive Order No. 164). If the deadline lapses, the group most solemnly counted will have been counted out by the calendar.
  • The opposition's home was demolished at dawn. On 23 August 2025, sheriffs backed by police demolished the CDC's national headquarters in Congo Town — offices, a vocational institute, and the sycamore tree that symbolized the party — enforcing an eviction the Sixth Judicial Circuit Court had ordered on 13 August 2025 after ruling the CDC had no legal claim to the 4.23-acre Bernard estate. The government's legal cover is real: a court ordered it, and the Supreme Court had upheld the eviction order while directing that the underlying ownership be probated. The CDC's counter is also on the record: it says its appeal was still pending when the bulldozers came, making the enforcement premature (its chairman, Janga Kowo, 1 September 2025). Boakai's public answer was "the law is the law… the police were ordered to carry out the action, and they did"; Weah called it "political mischief" directed from the top (The Liberian Investigator, 24 August 2025; FrontPage Africa, 26 August 2025). Both characterizations are printed as their makers'; the undisputed facts are the court order and the choice to execute it by pre-dawn demolition, against the principal opposition party, days before the elections commission's annual inspection of party headquarters.
  • His own county is alleged to be counted first. Opposition-aligned tallies published in November 2025 list more than 59 senior appointees from Lofa, Boakai's home county — including the Central Bank governor — under the headline of a "Lofa rescue government" (Lloyd FM/TV list, November 2025). The tally is a partisan compilation, recorded as such; no official rebuttal or official county-by-county accounting appears in the record assembled here.
  • Protesters and local journalists remain countable as problems at the margins: the December 2024 protest policing (Test 2), and, in April 2026, four journalists detained by traditional authorities in Margibi County while working, freed only after Press Union intervention (New Dawn, July 2026) — not the president's act, but an unremedied pattern inside the state he heads.

Why this grade: The design of his government counts everyone — fifteen counties, war victims, the disabled, the addicted — and several of those countings are real acts with money and law attached. But the two hardest countings run the other way: poverty rose while the enclave grew, and the victims whose court he promised have been funded at half rate against a deadline that may bury the promise. MIXED. Helped: The fifteen counties, in the machines allocation and rural electrification; war victims and their advocates, who have an office, a road map, and drafted bills where for fifteen years they had none; rice consumers; people with disabilities and rural women, named in launched programs in November 2026. Hurt: Liberian households outside the mining enclave, whose poverty deepened through two years of headline growth; the CDC as an institution, homeless by demolition; war victims again — in the gap between the court's promise and its funding; protesters of December 2024.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 22 January 2024, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The anti-corruption word — substantially performed. The 2023 Rescue platform and his inaugural address made corruption the headline enemy. The performed record: his own asset declaration filed and salary disclosed (February 2024); an audit of the presidency ordered; the GAC's audit of the Central Bank for 2018–2023 commissioned and published; the 40% cut in his own salary announced 6 July 2024 (Executive Mansion statement); the LACC's declaration regime enforced against 457 of his own officials (February 2025); a sitting minister of his own party gone over a vehicle (October 2024); prosecutions of the former regime carried into courtrooms and left to juries (2024–2026). Liberia's Corruption Perceptions Index score rose from 25 (2023) to 27 (2024) — movement, of the modest kind the word promised and the scale of the problem dwarfs (Transparency International CPI via CENTAL, February 2025).
  • The war-court word — kept as process, on the record. He promised accountability for war-era crimes where his two predecessors had let the TRC's 2009 recommendation sleep. Executive Order No. 131 was signed on 2 May 2024; the office exists, is staffed, consulted nationwide, and has drafted the enabling bills; he renewed the mandate in April 2025 and again, with direct funding ordered, in May 2026, and used his January 2026 annual message to press the Legislature to pass the bills. The outcome word — a court — is graded below; the process word has been kept at every point where keeping it was his alone to do.
  • The staple's price — kept in the moment it was tested. May 2024: against importer demands and his own ministry's announced increase, the price of rice did not rise (Test 3). Food-price inflation was negative year-on-year by December 2025 (World Bank, November 2026).
  • The macro word — delivered on the numbers he cites. The ARREST agenda's successor plan (the ARREST Agenda for Inclusive Development, 2025–2029) targets 5.9% average growth; the economy grew 5.1% in 2025 (World Bank and IMF figures), domestic revenue passed US$1 billion in 2026 on the Finance Ministry's reporting, and the FY2026 budget of about US$1.211 billion was approved with roughly 94% domestic financing (annual message, 26 January 2026; Ministry of Finance, November 2026). Liberia also took its seat as a non-permanent member of the UN Security Council for 2026–2027 — a diplomatic pledge of his first year, performed.
  • The health word, as far as the record reaches. He told the BBC during the 2023 campaign that he was in good health and that "age should be a blessing to this country." The documented record since: the inauguration-day heat-exhaustion episode of 22 January 2024, officially explained and followed by a same-day resumption of duties, and thereafter no documented incapacity across thirty-four months of a full public schedule. The word is graded on that record, neither credited beyond it nor debited by speculation.
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: Weah's completed tenure carried its own kept and broken words, but the fact that grades this seat's source is his last one — having lost, he conceded on 17 November 2023 and handed over power on schedule. The chair Boakai sits in was delivered to him by a kept word about the rules themselves.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "No car stuck in the mud" — broken on its own timeline. The roads mantra of the 2023 campaign and his first year promised that vehicles would no longer be stranded on Liberia's unpaved corridors; the instrument was the 285 machines, unveiled in May 2024 as if in hand. Cars were stuck in the mud through the rainy seasons of 2024 and 2025; the first machines arrived in February 2026, in the third year, under a different and smaller-structured deal than the one announced (The Liberian Investigator, February–March 2026). The government now reports paved national roads up from 12% to 20% (Executive Mansion figures, September 2026) — progress recorded; the promise as made was not kept when it was made for.
  • The war-court outcome — receding against his own deadline. The office's first (replacement) head spoke in November 2024 of courts established within two to three years; nine months of "very soon" preceded the draft bills' delivery (FrontPage Africa, January 2026); no enabling law has passed; first indictments are now projected for 2027; and Boakai's own Executive Order No. 164 fixes 30 April 2027 as the mandate's end, with a dissolution plan due 1 March 2027 — "either the court is established by then, or the process dies with the mandate" (The Liberian Investigator, 4 May 2026). A promise can be honestly pursued and still be lost; the record at grading is pursuit without the promised thing, against a clock he set.
  • The rule-of-law word — bent where his party's project was at stake. The inaugural pledge to uphold the rule of law sits against April–May 2025: a final Supreme Court judgment voiding his allies' House acts, answered with a quorum formula until the lawful Speaker resigned (Test 2). The word was kept in the corruption files and bent in the constitutional one; both are the same word.
  • The machines announcement itself. Whatever the eventual delivery, the May 2024 presentation stated as an arrangement what did not yet exist as a contract, procurement, or appropriation — his government conceded as much in January 2025. A word given at a cabinet retreat, walked back over twenty-one months, is a broken word that was later repaired at a lower price; the repair is credited in Test 3 and the crossings below, and the break is recorded here.

Why this grade: Boakai's word has a recognizable shape: where a promise can be kept by his own signature — declarations enforced, audits ordered, a price held, an office created and renewed — he has kept it, including against his own camp. Where a promise depends on institutions he does not control (a legislature that must pass the court bills) or on timelines he announced before the means existed (the machines, the mud), the word has run ahead of the record. MIXED. Helped: Citizens who needed the declaration regime and the audits to be real; rice consumers in May 2024 and through 2025's falling food prices; war-crimes advocates, whose fifteen-year-old recommendation became an office and draft law. Hurt: Rural road users who took the mud promise on its first two rainy seasons; war victims, whose wait lengthens against a deadline; Speaker Koffa, under the rule-of-law word; the public's credit in government announcements, spent in May 2024 and still being repaid.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Joseph Boakai came to the seat the way the Test most wants a ruler to come to it: he beat an incumbent at the ballot box by 1.28 percentage points, the incumbent conceded before the count was final, and power changed hands on schedule for the second time in Liberia's post-war history. Thirty-four months on, the record is that of a genuine reform program administered by a party man — and the grades turn, test after test, on which of those two is holding the pen. One law (MIXED): the asset-declaration rule was enforced against 457 of his own officials with salaries actually stopped, his own party's minister resigned over a vehicle, and his own appointees were dismissed and even indicted — while the completed criminal process still runs almost entirely against the defeated camp, and an acquitted former minister was summoned again within five days of his acquittal. Without gripping (MIXED, at the failing edge): he complied with the Supreme Court's 2024 tenure ruling, accepted a jury's acquittal of his government's chief target, and regularized the central bank through settlement and Senate confirmation — but tenured officials were suspended through a power their statutes do not contain, and when the Court voided his allies' seizure of the speakership in April 2025, he transacted with the bloc until the lawful Speaker resigned. The wobble (MIXED): on policy he adjusts in the open — a conflicted war-court appointee replaced through public consultation, a rice increase reversed at his own table, a phantom machines deal renegotiated into a real one at roughly half the first price, a starved justice office finally funded by direct order; on the constitutional wobble, he outlasted the referee instead. Counts everyone (MIXED): the design counts the whole country — fifteen counties, war victims, the disabled, the addicted — and the war-court office is a real act after fifteen years of neglect; but poverty rose while the mining enclave grew at 17%, the victims' office was funded at half its pledge against a deadline that may end it, and the opposition's headquarters came down at dawn under a court order he declined to soften. His word (MIXED): kept where his signature alone could keep it, broken or receding where it depended on time, money, and other institutions — the mud promise two rainy seasons late, the court promised and not yet built.

The two axes cross in his record in all four directions, and the dossier names them: kept his word and it helped — the declaration enforcement and the held rice price, promises performed at cost to his own side; kept his word and it still hurt — "the law is the law": the court-ordered eviction of the CDC was executed exactly as his government said it would be, and it left the country's largest opposition party homeless, its headquarters and its symbol tree destroyed, with its appeal — on its account — still pending; went against his word and it helped — the May 2024 machines announcement was abandoned as announced, and the renegotiated replacement cost roughly US$22 million against a first projection of US$43 million and actually arrived; broke his word and it hurt — the rule-of-law pledge in the speakership, and the mud promise in its first two years. The no-good-choice clause was applied twice, by its mechanics: to the post-judgment speakership bind (a Speaker with the law and no quorum; a bloc with a quorum and no law — the constraint recorded, the trap's origin in his own camp's project and the government's framing of it graded in Tests 2 and 3) and to the rice decision of May 2024 (a trap inherited, not built, where the door he took held both price and supply). The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the war and economic crimes court exists before its mandate dies on 30 April 2027; whether any senior figure of his own camp is carried through a criminal process to a verdict; what the retrial of the Capitol arson defendants — resumed after the Supreme Court's 28 August 2026 ruling, with the defendants' torture allegations (medical examinations in September 2025 found injuries in five defendants described as highly consistent with torture, a finding the prosecution's continuation of the case implicitly contests and no court has yet adjudicated) — does to the one-law record; and whether the poverty line turns before the growth line is all his government has to show.
