The Ruler's Test — Christopher Luxon, Prime Minister of New Zealand

Subject: Christopher Luxon — Prime Minister of New Zealand since 27 November 2023, leading the Sixth National Government, a three-party coalition of his National Party with ACT New Zealand (leader David Seymour) and New Zealand First (leader Winston Peters), formed after the 14 October 2023 general election (National 48 seats, ACT 11, NZ First 8 — 67 of 123). The seat graded is the premiership, which in New Zealand's unicameral MMP system carries the executive: Luxon chairs the cabinet that both of his coalition partners sit in, and the coalition agreements he signed on 24 November 2023 are the founding documents of his tenure. His first term is complete. The 2026 general election was held on 7 November 2026 and produced one of the closest results in New Zealand history: on preliminary results National won 42 seats on 29.21% of the party vote — actually finishing second in the popular vote to Labour (29.36%, 36 seats) while winning more seats, because National's electorate wins created an 11-seat overhang that expanded Parliament to 131 seats. The incumbent coalition holds 67 of 131 seats on the preliminary count, one above the 66 needed; around 399,000 special votes remain to be counted, official results are due on 27 November 2026, and Luxon himself declared the election too close to call on the night. He therefore sits at grading as a caretaker prime minister: the term is graded as a completed term (27 November 2023 – 7 November 2026), and his conduct since election day is graded as caretaker conduct. Two features of the term belong in the framing. First, Luxon's personal standing fell far and stayed down: net approval of −20.6 in the RNZ–Reid Research poll of April 2026 (preferred prime minister 17.3%, behind his Labour opponent) and −23 in the 1News Verian poll of September 2026, with 55% disapproving. Second, his own caucus twice voted on his leadership in 2026: a confidence vote he called himself on 21 April 2026, which he won by a margin media reported as 27–22, and an emergency caucus vote on 12 August 2026 against challenger Chris Penk, which he won by an unreleased margin. He is graded as the man who held the seat through all of it — neither removed by his party nor confirmed by the voters, with the final count still open. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The coalition's founding rules were enforced against the government's own friends, at the highest-stakes moment of the term. The National–ACT agreement committed National to support ACT's Treaty Principles Bill only to select committee; when the bill returned, National voted it down at second reading on 10 April 2025 by 112 votes to 11 — every ACT MP in favour, every National MP against — with Luxon on record beforehand that there was "nothing in the bill that he liked" (Treaty Principles Bill, voting record, 10 April 2025; contemporaneous reporting, April 2025). The limit written for a partner was applied to that partner, exactly as written.
  • The term's largest single spending correction ran to everyone, through the independent agency, not to a chosen list. After Budget 2024 omitted the promised cancer medicines (Test 5), the fix announced on 24 June 2024 put $604 million over four years into Pharmac — the independent drug-buying agency whose rankings the government cannot direct — funding up to 54 medicines for an estimated 175,000 people in the first year, precisely because the only lawful way to reach the promised drugs was to fund everyone ranked ahead of them (Broadcasting Standards Authority decision 2024-062, summarising the June 2024 announcement; RNZ reporting, June 2024).
  • The flagship fiscal measures were general laws of general application: the $14.7 billion income-tax package (Budget 2024) moved thresholds for all taxpayers by roughly 11.5%, and the tax, sentencing, and consenting programs were legislated in Parliament on recorded votes, not dispensed by discretion (Treasury, Budget 2024; Yeah Nah Yeah Vote fiscal tracker, 2026).
  • Where an independent referee did find a favorite, the finding was published and the process was then put right on the record. The Auditor-General's October 2024 letter on the Gumboot Friday contract (below) was released publicly; by November 2025 his office's audit work concluded the contract "was being managed appropriately against its terms and in accordance with good practice," and declined further investigation (Office of the Auditor-General, letter of 9 October 2024 and follow-up, November 2025).
  • The fast-track consenting regime's independent panels bit the hand that listed the projects: a fast-track panel rejected Trans-Tasman Resources' seabed-mining proposal — one of the 149 projects ministers had admitted to the list — on environmental grounds, citing unacceptable risk to Māui dolphins (Fast-track Approvals Act project record, summarised in the ACT infrastructure tracker, 2026). Referral by ministers did not guarantee approval by the panel.

Evidence against:

  • A named favorite was written into the coalition agreement itself and funded without the contest everyone else must enter. The National–NZ First agreement committed $24 million over four years to the I Am Hope Foundation's Gumboot Friday programme ($6 million a year), and the Ministry of Health contracted it directly, without open competitive tender, under an opt-out clause. The Auditor-General, John Ryan, wrote on 9 October 2024 that "several aspects of the process are unusual and inconsistent with good practice," and his office's later summary records that the supporting analysis "appeared designed to retrospectively justify an outcome that had already been decided." The letter expressly made no finding of wrongdoing by the charity or its founder and did not question the value of youth mental-health funding — the finding is about the process at the source, which is what this test grades (Auditor-General's letter, 9 October 2024; Office of the Auditor-General, November 2025).
  • The tobacco file shows a benefit flowing to one industry's product class, through a partner's minister, with the prime minister keeping the minister in place. In July 2024 Associate Health Minister Casey Costello (NZ First) halved the excise on heated tobacco products — a category dominated by a single manufacturer's devices — with a $216 million contingency set aside for forgone revenue over four years, defended by Costello as a switching trial (Costello, statement of 30 July 2024; coalition record summarised November 2026). This followed the February 2024 repeal, under urgency, of the smokefree-generation law, denicotinisation, and the retail-reduction scheme. Public-health bodies characterised the package as the tobacco industry's win — an interested parties' characterisation, recorded as such — while the documented core is narrower and sufficient: a tax concession for one product class, decided inside the coalition, against the direction of the government's own health advice tradition, and Luxon left its author in the portfolio for the whole term.
  • The restoration of full mortgage-interest deductibility for landlords cost $2.9 billion over four years against a pre-election costing of $2.1 billion — a benefit to a property-owning class that opposition parties noted includes the prime minister himself, a multiple property investor; that conflict framing is the opposition's, recorded as an ALLEGATION-level political claim about motive, while the costing overrun is Treasury-documented (Treasury figures via Yeah Nah Yeah Vote fiscal tracker, 2026).
  • The consenting system's direction of travel is toward ministerial discretion. The Fast-track Approvals Amendment Bill before Parliament in late 2026 would expand ministers' power over project progression and panel composition; the Environment Committee's report recorded 95% of submitters opposed, and Greenpeace's submission — an opponent's document, cited for the objection's content, not as a neutral finding — warns it "invites a clear risk of actual or perceived conflicts of interest whereby Ministers with ties to corporations, via political donations, are put in a position to influence decisions that benefit those donors" (Environment Committee report and submissions, November 2026). The bill had not passed at grading; the shelf records the direction, not a completed act.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The electoral referee ran the election that may end his government, untouched. The Electoral Commission administered the 7 November 2026 election; when the preliminary count left his coalition one seat above the threshold with ~399,000 special votes outstanding, Luxon declared it too close to call, placed his government under caretaker conventions, and waited for the official result due 27 November — no pressure on the count, no claim of victory, no attempt to govern beyond the caretaker limits (Wikipedia, 2026 New Zealand general election, summarising election-night statements; BBC and Electoral Commission reporting, November 2026).
  • The courts ruled against his government at the highest level of embarrassment and the machinery absorbed it without retaliation. In late October–November 2026 the High Court (Justice Radich, 63-page ruling) issued a declaration that his government's Equal Pay Amendment Act 2025 is inconsistent with the New Zealand Bill of Rights Act's freedom from sex discrimination; the constitutional consequence — the Attorney-General reporting the declaration to Parliament — proceeds as the law provides, and no move against the court or the judge appears in the record (High Court press summary, November 2026; NZEI Te Riu Roa statement, November 2026).
  • The Waitangi Tribunal ran urgent inquiries into his government's signature measures — the Treaty Principles Bill (August 2024: the bill breached principles including partnership and active protection) and the Māori Health Authority disestablishment (November 2024: the Crown failed to consult Māori and did not follow its own guidelines) — published its findings, and was not defunded, stacked, or abolished for them, though his coalition partners attacked its legitimacy in debate (Waitangi Tribunal, Ngā Mātāpono, August 2024; Haumaru/Crown-response reporting, November 2024).
  • The central bank governorship was refilled by the book after its crisis (below): the Reserve Bank Board ran a worldwide search identifying 300 candidates and nominated Dr Anna Breman, First Deputy Governor of Sweden's Riksbank; Willis appointed her for a five-year term from 1 December 2025, with the opposition notified and raising no objection (Beehive, 24 September 2025; Reuters, 1 December 2025).
  • Critical media operated at full volume for the whole term — his net approval of −23 and 55% disapproval were published by the state broadcaster's own poll in September 2026, mid-campaign (1News Verian poll, September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The central bank's independence was pressured through its budget, and its governor resigned over exactly that. Adrian Orr resigned on 5 March 2025, three years early, giving no reason; documents later released under the Official Information Act confirmed he resigned over a disagreement on funding — the board had agreed a five-year funding agreement at "considerably less money" than Orr judged necessary, following a 24 February 2025 meeting with Finance Minister Willis and Treasury officials after which Orr and the board chair negotiated his departure. Willis had publicly raised slashing the bank's funding the week before he resigned, and the government had campaigned against his stewardship for years. Board chair Neil Quigley also exited in the aftermath (Reuters, March 2025; RBNZ statement released under the OIA, June 2025; Reuters, December 2025). No document shows the government directing a monetary-policy decision; the grip documented here is the funding lever, applied until the governor left.
  • When a minister attacked a judge personally, the prime minister declined to enforce the standard — and the correction had to come from inside the coalition's other parties. NZ First minister Shane Jones called High Court Justice Cheryl Gwyn a "communist" in a meeting with seafood-industry representatives (officials' notes, May 2024) and later said some judicial decisions showed "elements of totalitarianism." The Cabinet Manual bars ministers from comments reflecting adversely on a judge's impartiality. Luxon's response was that the word was "descriptive not critical" and Jones was "perfectly free to do so." The Law Society wrote twice to the Attorney-General (23 and 30 August 2024) urging stronger action; the actual schooling came from Attorney-General Judith Collins, and Jones's own leader, Winston Peters, overruled Luxon's line in public: the comment "should not have been said," because "the separation between the courts and politics is a critical part to our democracy" (Law Society statements, August 2024; NZ Herald reporting via The Standard, September 2024). The referee was defended — by the deputy prime minister and the attorney-general, over the prime minister's shrug.
  • Health New Zealand's board was sacked in 2024 and replaced by a single commissioner (Professor Lester Levy) after the agency posted large deficits — an intervention the government defended as a turnaround measure; it is recorded here as what it was: the executive removing an arm's-length board mid-term and concentrating the agency in one appointee (Yeah Nah Yeah Vote health tracker, 2026; Health NZ financial reporting).
  • The Regulatory Standards Act 2025 (passed 13 November 2025, 68–55; in force 1 January 2026) created a Regulatory Standards Board appointed under the Minister for Regulation to review existing and proposed legislation against a fixed set of principles — a standing reviewer of Parliament's output seated inside the executive's own framework. Critics across the legal profession, including former prime minister Sir Geoffrey Palmer and the Law Society, raised constitutional objections, and Treasury judged the approach "unlikely to be the most efficient" route to its stated goals; supporters, including BusinessNZ and the NZ Initiative, defend it as a non-binding transparency measure. The board's recommendations are formally non-binding; the objection recorded is structural (Regulatory Standards Act 2025; NZ Politics Promise Tracker summary, 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The term's loudest policy outcry was answered with money and an apology inside a month. When Budget 2024 omitted the 13 promised cancer medicines, patients and advocates erupted; on 24 June 2024 the government announced the $604 million Pharmac package — larger than the original promise — and National apologised for its communication of the policy (Test 5; 1News and RNZ reporting, June 2024).
  • Faced with the biggest street mobilisation of the term, he neither rescued the bill nor pretended it lived. The Hīkoi mō te Tiriti brought a police-estimated 42,000 people to Parliament on 19 November 2024; the select committee received more than 300,000 submissions, ~90% opposed; the committee recommended the Treaty Principles Bill not proceed, and National voted it down 112–11 on 10 April 2025, with Luxon saying beforehand it was time to move on (police estimates and Justice Committee record, November 2024–April 2025).
  • He apologised, on the record, for his own captain's calls when his caucus pushed back: after pledging on talkback radio in August 2026 — without warning his MPs — a referendum on the MMP electoral system if re-elected, and after telling a Rotorua business audience that businesses had a parent-child mentality toward government, he apologised for both, then faced his caucus and won its confidence vote on 12 August 2026 (Wikipedia, August 2026 National Party leadership election; NZ Herald reporting, August 2026).
  • Election night 2026 was handled by the book: with his coalition's majority resting on uncounted special votes, he claimed nothing, conceded nothing improperly, and put the government into caretaker mode to await the official count (BBC; Electoral Commission, November 2026).
  • The Dunedin hospital wobble produced a partial, documented adjustment: after the September 2024 scale-back announcement brought an estimated 35,000 people — a quarter of the city — into the streets, the government confirmed in January 2025 that the new inpatient building would proceed on the new site under a fixed budget, signed the construction contract in mid-2025, and added $174 million contingency in May 2026 (Otago Daily Times and promise-tracker record, 2024–2026). The adjustment restored the project's existence; it did not restore its promised scope (Test 5).

Evidence against:

  • The pay-equity wobble was answered by doubling down at every stage. The Equal Pay Amendment Act was drafted in secret, introduced and passed under urgency on 6–7 May 2025 with no select-committee stage and no regulatory impact statement, retrospectively extinguishing 33 live claims covering some 180,000 mostly female workers; a People's Select Committee later called the process "a flagrant and significant abuse of power." Luxon defended the law throughout, denying it was budget-driven — "it's got nothing to do with the budget" — while conceding in the same interviews that it would save the government "billions," with $12.8 billion previously earmarked for settlements returned to Budget allowances. When the High Court declared the Act inconsistent with the Bill of Rights Act in November 2026, the government's position at grading remained defence of the law, not revision (Equal Pay Amendment Act 2025 legislative record; Stuff/OIA investigation of the 19 March 2025 ministerial meeting, March 2026; High Court declaration, November 2026). The government's substantive defence — that the prior settings were fiscally unsustainable and the new thresholds target genuine sex-based undervaluation — is on the record and was made throughout; what the shelf grades is that pushback from claimants, unions, a people's committee, and finally a court produced no adjustment at all.
  • The Regulatory Standards Bill was carried through against the most lopsided consultation record of the term: 88% of nearly 23,000 submissions opposed (the select committee's analysis put opposition at 98.7%), a petition of more than 156,000 signatures, and a Waitangi Tribunal finding that enacting it without meaningful Māori consultation breached Treaty principles. It passed 68–55 on 13 November 2025. Its champion, Seymour, publicly suggested the submission numbers were inflated by fake submissions — no audit substantiated that — and, asked whether the government's own urgent pay-equity bill complied with the standards the Act would impose, answered on RNZ: "It was breached because it didn't matter" (Ministry for Regulation submission analysis; RNZ interview, June 2025, as reported). Luxon neither required the consultation to be rerun nor distanced the government from the remark.
  • Two years of deteriorating polls were met mainly with message discipline. From the April 2026 polls onward Luxon's preferred-PM rating sat in the high teens and his net approval below −20; his standard answer to leadership questions was deflection — after the August 2026 challenge he told a reporter it was "lovely" that he wanted to "continue to noodle away" on the subject and refused further answers (Stuff stand-up transcript, August 2026). The April 2026 confidence vote he called in himself was won, on the widely reported figures, 27–22 — nearly half his caucus against him — and no policy reset followed either vote.
  • On Dunedin, the first answer to the street was a refusal delivered in person: confronted by protesters in the city in late 2024, Luxon held the line — "we are going to build a great hospital here in Dunedin, but it is going to be at $1.9 billion, not approaching $3 billion" — while nurses chanted "liar, liar" over the broken campaign pledge (Kaitiaki/NZNO reporting, October 2024). The January 2025 confirmation that the building would proceed was the adjustment; the scope cut stood, and in April 2025 RNZ reported the planned ICU beds had been quietly cut from 30 to 20 for opening day.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Māori, minorities, women, the homeless, those who share the streets — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The single largest counted-in result of the term belongs to the households nobody campaigns for. Emergency housing: 3,141 households in motel emergency housing in December 2023 fell to 591 by December 2024 and 444 by December 2025 — an 86% reduction, meeting the government's 75%-by-2030 target five years early — and the number of children living in emergency housing fell from 3,438 (December 2023) to 459 (February 2025), an 87% fall. The Priority One policy moved families with children to the top of the social-housing queue; 1,095 households with 2,340 children had been placed in social housing by December 2025, and Rotorua's contracted emergency-housing motels were emptied entirely by December 2025. Because Māori families were heavily over-represented in the motels, the fall counts Māori children in, in the thousands (DPMC Target 8 factsheets, June and December 2025; Beehive, 24 January 2025; National Party, November 2025).
  • When his own foreign minister told a Chinese-born MP to go home, the prime minister said the words out loud. In July 2026 Winston Peters told Green MP Lawrence Xu-Nan — born in Tianjin, raised in East Auckland — to "go back to your own country" in a parliamentary debate, adding "that's where they lie like a flatfish." The remarks drew a formal Chinese embassy protest and condemnation from the Race Relations Commissioner. On 31 July 2026 Luxon defended Xu-Nan's right to speak in Parliament and called Peters' comments "very offensive and completely inappropriate" (Reuters, 30 July 2026; Xu-Nan parliamentary record, July 2026).
  • The Māori-wards question was settled by counting Māori voters rather than by abolishing the wards from Wellington: the 2024 law required councils that had created Māori wards without a poll to hold binding referendums at the 2025 local elections; voters kept the wards in 18 councils and removed them in 24 (Local Government (Electoral Legislation and Māori Wards and Māori Constituencies) Amendment Act 2024; 2025 referendum results). The government's framing — that the decision belongs to local voters — is its own; the mechanism was a vote, and Māori voted in it.
  • Measured victimisation fell on his watch: the New Zealand Crime and Victims Survey (released 20 March 2026) recorded the share of adults victimised down from 30% (2018) to 28% (2025), with about 49,000 fewer victims of violent crime in the year to October 2025 than two years earlier. How much credit the government's policies deserve is contested — the decline began in late 2022, before its flagship measures — and the tracker records the contest (NZCVS, March 2026; NZ Politics Promise Tracker, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Māori file is the heaviest shelf in this dossier, and its weight is institutional, not rhetorical. The Māori Health Authority, Te Aka Whai Ora — created in 2022 to attack a documented life-expectancy gap — was disestablished by legislation passed under urgency in February 2024 and wound up on 30 June 2024. The Waitangi Tribunal found in November 2024 that the Crown breached Treaty principles in doing it: Māori were not consulted, the government did not follow its own policy guidelines, and Māori were not told what would replace the authority. The government's position — that all three coalition parties campaigned on abolition and that Hauora Māori funding was maintained ($749 million in 2024/25) inside the general system — is on the record; the Tribunal's finding is about the manner and the partnership, and it stands unanswered in substance (Waitangi Tribunal report, November 2024; Yeah Nah Yeah Vote issue tracker, 2026; a High Court challenge by four Māori health providers was heard in August 2025 with judgment reserved).
  • Section 7AA of the Oranga Tamariki Act — the statutory duty on the children's ministry chief executive to honour the Treaty and reduce disparities for Māori children in state care — was repealed on 3 April 2025, against the documented fact that Māori children are the majority of those in care. The government's stated ground was that the child's wellbeing must be the paramount consideration without a Treaty overlay; the repeal is recorded here as the removal of the one duty whose entire content was counting Māori children in (Oranga Tamariki (Repeal of Section 7AA) Amendment Act 2025).
  • The Treaty Principles Bill was carried on his signature for seventeen months. Whatever its April 2025 end (Test 3), the bill existed because Luxon signed the coalition agreement that created it, his party's votes passed its first reading, and the Waitangi Tribunal's urgent August 2024 report — finding the bill would breach partnership, reciprocity, active protection, and the Article 2 guarantee of rangatiratanga — did not move him to withdraw National's support early; support ended at the agreed line, on schedule, after the largest protest mobilisation of the term.
  • The pay-equity law counts against this test as well as Test 3: the 33 extinguished claims covered teachers, nurses, carers, and librarians — overwhelmingly women, in workforces the previous law had recognised as historically undervalued because they were female — and the High Court's November 2026 declaration names the result in law: inconsistency with the right to freedom from discrimination based on sex (High Court, Justice Radich, November 2026). The government's defence (fiscal sustainability; genuine-discrimination targeting) is recorded at Test 3; it does not answer the declaration.
  • Peters kept his job. Luxon condemned the "go back to your own country" remarks in the strongest words available to him — and retained their author as Foreign Minister, urging voters instead to elect more National MPs so coalition partners would hold fewer portfolios. Peters doubled down, calling his critics "pearl-clutchers" and "communist shills," and no consequence followed (Reuters, 30 July 2026; parliamentary record, July–August 2026). The counted-out MP was defended in words and outweighed in practice by the coalition arithmetic.
  • The penal settings fall where they always fall: the prison population reached record highs during the term under the restored three-strikes regime, the gang-patch ban, and longer sentences — a distribution whose incidence on Māori (a majority of the prison population) is long-documented and was itemised by critics including the New Zealand Medical Journal's editorial board at the term's start (NZ Politics Promise Tracker, crime issue, 2026; NZMJ editorial, February 2024). And on housing, the Aotearoa Human Rights Foundation's 2026 review warned the country is going backwards on housing outcomes and Treaty commitments, with emergency-housing applications being declined at significantly higher rates and Māori still disproportionately represented among those seeking help — the other side of the Target 8 numbers, recorded because both are documented (Waatea News, 11 August 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises and coalition agreements vs. the record, 27 November 2023 – 7 November 2026, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept):

  • Tax cuts — delivered, on time, at the promised scale. National campaigned on income-tax relief; Budget 2024 delivered a $14.7 billion package over five years, lifting thresholds by ~11.5% and expanding credits, effective 31 July 2024 (Treasury, Budget 2024).
  • The coalition agreements — kept to the letter, including against his partners' wishes. The deputy prime ministership rotated from Peters to Seymour on exactly the agreed date, 31 May 2025 (coalition agreements, 24 November 2023; swearing-in, 31 May 2025). The Treaty Principles Bill was supported to select committee and not one vote further, as the agreement said. The Gumboot Friday funding was paid as the NZ First agreement specified — the commitment was kept even though the procurement used to keep it failed the Auditor-General's standard (Test 1). National's foreign-buyer tax was dropped at the signing table, disclosed in the agreements themselves, rather than promised and quietly buried later (Sixth National Government coalition record, 2023–2025).
  • The 100-day plan — executed. The 49-action programme published at the swearing-in (repealing Fair Pay Agreements, disestablishing the Māori Health Authority, repealing the smokefree amendments, restoring the Reserve Bank's single mandate) was carried out on schedule in the first hundred days — a kept word whose contents are graded under other tests, but whose keeping is not in doubt (100-day programme, November 2023; legislative record, December 2023–March 2024).
  • Emergency housing — the target was beaten, not redefined. The government set a target of 75% fewer households in emergency housing by 2030 and announced it met in December 2024, five years early, extending to an 86% reduction by December 2025 (Beehive, 24 January 2025; DPMC factsheets, 2025).
  • Cancer medicines — broken in the budget, then kept late and larger. The June 2024 Pharmac package ($604 million, 26 cancer treatments, up to 54 medicines) exceeded the original $280 million costing of the 13-drug promise; seven of the 13 named drugs were funded directly and the rest replaced, on the government's account, by alternatives "as good or better" — a substitution cancer patients' advocates accepted only in part, recorded here as contested in its margins and delivered in its substance (BSA decision 2024-062; RNZ, June 2024).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The surplus — the fiscal promise that defined the government, missed on every published date. National's 2023 fiscal plan promised a return to surplus in 2026/27. Forecasts then walked it back in public: to 2028 (May 2024), to 2029 (December 2024), to 2030 (December 2025, when Treasury showed a $945 million deficit in the very year Budget 2025 had forecast a $214 million surplus). Budget 2026 brought the forecast back to a $2.6 billion surplus in 2028/29 — still two years later than promised, still only a forecast, with Treasury itself putting the chance of arrival at roughly 50–60%, and on a measure (OBEGALx) that excludes ACC, which critics say flatters the result. The actual 2025/26 result was an $8.667 billion deficit; net core Crown debt stood at $186.8 billion, 40.9% of GDP, at June 2026 — its first percentage-point fall since 2018/19, a genuine improvement the government fairly claims, inside a promise that was not kept (Treasury BEFU/HYEFU/PREFU series, 2024–2026; Financial Statements of the Government, 2026).
  • The 500 police — a coalition commitment with a date in it, missed on that date. The National–NZ First agreement committed to "training no fewer than 500 new frontline police within the first two years" — by 27 November 2025. The government formally conceded the miss on the deadline; by April 2026 the net gain stood at about 297 constables, with attrition to Australian police forces a documented driver, an $7.9 million recruitment underspend in 2024/25, and Treasury projecting the 500 would arrive around September 2026, nearly a year late (RNZ, 27 November 2025; Treasury report to the finance minister, September 2025; NZ Police figures, April 2026).
  • Dunedin Hospital — the clearest single broken pledge of the term. National campaigned in 2023 on restoring the full rebuild — "all the beds, operating theatres and imaging services that had previously been trimmed" — and Health Minister Reti confirmed the commitment in June 2024. In September 2024 the government announced a drastic scale-back (costs said to be approaching $3 billion). The hospital now proceeding — contract signed mid-2025, budget $2.1 billion by May 2026, first inpatients expected 2031 — opens with 351 beds, 45 fewer than the existing hospital it replaces, with ICU beds reported cut from 30 to 20 (NZ Herald campaign reporting, 2023; ministers' announcement, September 2024; NZ Politics Promise Tracker, Dunedin Hospital, 2026).
  • The ferries — the cancellation was his word; the bill is the record. Cancelling KiwiRail's iReX ferry project in December 2023 kept a campaign line about waste; the final documented cost of the cancelled project was $671 million, including $222 million to the shipyard for ships never delivered, and the replacement ferries and port works are costed at $1.86 billion arriving in 2029 rather than 2026 (KiwiRail/government settlement reporting, August 2025; Budget documents, 2026). Recorded as a kept cancellation with an unkept arithmetic.
  • An open file, stated as open: during the 2026 campaign Luxon repeatedly ruled out forming a coalition with the centrist Opportunity Party; on 9 November 2026, two days after the election made Opportunity's seven seats pivotal, he met its leader, Qiulae Wong. No agreement had been announced at grading (election coverage, November 2026; ElectioMap results service, 23 November 2026). If a deal follows, the ruling-out joins this shelf as a broken word; if none does, it leaves it. The record will grade it when it can.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Christopher Luxon is the first prime minister in this series graded at the exact moment his fate sits in someone else's count: a completed term behind him, a caretaker's chair under him, and 399,000 special votes between him and a second term. The record of the term is genuinely two-sided in a specific pattern. His word, where it was written down with a date — the coalition agreements' letter, the tax package, the 100-day plan, the deputy-PM rotation — was kept with unusual precision, including against his own partners; his word, where it was a number the economy had to produce — the 2026/27 surplus, the 500 police by November 2025, Dunedin's full rebuild — was missed on every date and re-dated in public. The referees mostly stand: the Electoral Commission ran his near-loss untouched, the High Court declared his pay-equity law inconsistent with the Bill of Rights and the constitution's machinery absorbed it, the Waitangi Tribunal published three findings against his government's methods and kept its doors open. The exceptions are named in the file: a central bank governor who resigned over the funding lever his finance minister was publicly threatening to pull, and a prime minister who called a minister's personal attack on a judge "descriptive not critical" and left the defence of the bench to his attorney-general and his deputy. On counting, the term holds the series' sharpest internal contradiction: the government that emptied the emergency-housing motels — 3,438 children in them when he arrived, 459 within fifteen months — is the same government that abolished the Māori Health Authority without the consultation the Treaty body required, repealed the one statutory duty to reduce disparities for Māori children in care, and retrospectively extinguished 33 pay-equity claims that a court later found discriminatory in effect. His coalition was the instrument of both halves, and his signature is on both. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: the official election result on 27 November 2026 and whatever government follows it, the Attorney-General's report to Parliament on the pay-equity declaration, the reserved High Court judgment on the Māori Health Authority, and whether the surplus forecast for 2028/29 is the first fiscal date of the term that arrives on time.
