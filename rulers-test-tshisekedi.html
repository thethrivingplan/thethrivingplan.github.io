The Ruler's Test — Félix Tshisekedi, President of the Democratic Republic of Congo

Subject: Félix Antoine Tshisekedi Tshilombo — President of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) since 24 January 2019, re-elected in December 2023 for a second five-year term. Premise verified, November 2026: the seat has NOT changed hands. Tshisekedi was still acting as president in the final week of grading — he attended the quadripartite summit at Oyo, Republic of Congo, on 22 November 2026 alongside Presidents William Ruto of Kenya, Denis Sassou N'Guesso of Congo, and Togo's Faure Gnassingbé, and was received at the Kremlin on 30 October 2026 as DRC president (People Daily reporting, 23 November 2026; Kremlin meeting record via GlobalSecurity, 30 October 2026). Prime Minister Judith Suminwa, appointed in 2024, heads the government under him; in the DRC's semi-presidential system the president is the executive seat that commands the armed forces, sets foreign and security policy, and appoints and dismisses the government, and it is that seat this dossier grades. He is graded on his full tenure, January 2019 to November 2026 — nearly eight years, not a completed tenure: his second term runs to January 2029 under the 2006 Constitution, and the live question of whether he seeks to change that is itself evidence in Tests 2 and 5. His route to the seat matters and is stated plainly: in the official results of the 30 December 2018 election the electoral commission (CENI) declared him winner with 38.6%, ahead of Martin Fayulu at 34.8% and Emmanuel Ramazani Shadary at 23.8%; the Catholic Church's CENCO observation mission, which deployed some 40,000 observers, said its tally did not correspond to CENI's, and leaked CENI data analysed by the Financial Times indicated Fayulu at 59.4% and Tshisekedi at about 19% (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2020; Financial Times analysis, January 2019). Those figures are printed as separate sourced figures and never blended; the widely reported assessment — recorded here as assessment, not as a court finding — is that Tshisekedi took office through an arrangement with outgoing president Joseph Kabila, whose FCC coalition kept 341 of 500 National Assembly seats and controlled his first government (Freedom House, 2020; International Crisis Group, January 2019). He broke that coalition in late 2020, built his own Sacred Union majority, and won re-election on 20 December 2023 with an official 73.47% (13,058,962 votes) against Moïse Katumbi's 18.32%, on official turnout of 42.65% — a vote the joint Catholic–Protestant observation mission said was marred by numerous irregularities, with voting extended beyond election day in places for up to six days (CENI results, affirmed by the Constitutional Court 9 January 2024; International Crisis Group, January 2024). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The Ethic: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them — above all on the eastern war, where UN findings and Rwandan denials are printed as disputants, not merged. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is NOT inherently a failure and is never graded as one; carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for HOW it is done — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). Seat-holders and those who chose the public eye are named; people below that line appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The single most senior corruption conviction of the era fell on his own household first. In June 2020 a court convicted his chief of staff and coalition partner Vital Kamerhe of embezzling almost USD 50 million of public funds from the president's own 100-days programme and sentenced him to 20 years' hard labour — at the time the most high-profile corruption conviction in Congolese history (contemporaneous court reporting, June 2020; Transparency International country coverage, 2024).
  • The audit machinery was turned on the previous regime's flagship deal, not only on small offenders. The General Inspectorate of Finance (IGF) audited the 2008 Sicomines minerals-for-infrastructure convention with China and found only USD 822 million of the promised USD 3 billion in infrastructure had been delivered while the Chinese partners had earned close to USD 10 billion — findings that drove the renegotiation concluded in January–March 2024 (EITI DRC evaluation, December 2021; IGF audit, 2023; fifth amendment signed 14 March 2024). Transparency International noted in 2024 that the IGF had spearheaded increased anti-corruption efforts over the previous five years (Transparency International, 2024).
  • A judicial investigation into alleged embezzlement of USD 138 million by former president Joseph Kabila and his associates — the "Congo Hold-up" bank-leak revelations covering 2013–2018 — was opened in Kinshasa in November 2021, i.e. the law was at least formally pointed at the most protected network in the country (European Investigative Collaborations / PPLAAF / Mediapart, November 2021; Kinshasa investigation reporting, November 2021).

Evidence against:

  • The Kamerhe case then reversed itself in a way no ordinary defendant could obtain: Kamerhe was released in December 2021, his sentence was cut on appeal and then overturned, and by May 2024 he was President of the National Assembly — the second office of the state — under Tshisekedi's Sacred Union (release reporting, December 2021; National Assembly election, May 2024). A law that convicts the president's chief of staff and then returns him to the speakership is two laws: one for the hearing, one for the insider.
  • The Congo Hold-up file produced no Congolese prosecution: a parliamentary commission was announced and never sat, and no prosecution of the Kabila-network figures documented in the leak was opened in the DRC (Congo Hold-up follow-up reporting, 2021–2024). By contrast, when Kabila himself was finally prosecuted in 2025 it was not for the documented financial file but by a military court, in absentia, without a lawyer, for treason and support to M23 — after the Senate lifted his lifetime immunity on 22 May 2025 — ending in a death sentence on 30 September 2025 (High Military Court verdict, 30 September 2025; Al Jazeera and Reuters reporting, September–October 2025). Kabila denies the accusations and calls the case political; his party calls the court an instrument of oppression. Those are a defendant's characterisations, recorded as such — but the procedural facts (military jurisdiction over a civilian former president, trial in absentia, no defence counsel) are documented and are themselves the Test 1 problem: the harshest process in the country was reserved for the president's chief political enemy, while the documented money file against the same man had sat untouched for four years.
  • Questions around the president's own family are on the public record as ALLEGATION, not findings, and are labeled so: in October 2026 Belgian police searched properties in Brussels in a money-laundering investigation which lawyers for civil-society complainants said concerned relatives and associates of Tshisekedi and mining revenues; Belgian prosecutors confirmed the searches, and no charge is recorded in the record assembled here (Belgian prosecutor reporting via Great Lakes Eye, October 2026 — ALLEGATION). Africa Intelligence reported payments from public funds in 2025 totalling about USD 4.6 million classified as compensation linked to his late father's prime-ministerial entitlements, and further payments to foundations headed by his brother, his daughter, and the First Lady, one foundation citing a scholarship programme and a government agreement (Africa Intelligence, 26 October 2026 — reported payments; the explanations are the foundations' own). Poorly explained spending around the first family in a state scored at 20 of 100 in Transparency International's 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index is, at minimum, a failure of the one-law appearance the test grades (Transparency International, CPI 2025).
  • Exclusive monopolies granted by the presidency recreated the insider pattern in new hands: Primera Gold was granted an exclusive artisanal-gold export monopoly for South Kivu in 2022, and The Sentry's 2024 report documented state revenue of about USD 40 million in its first year against an OECD field estimate roughly three times higher for the value that left through the channel; EGAL, created by presidential decree in 2021 with exclusive staple-food import rights, received USD 81 million in advances against undelivered goods on a Court of Accounts audit before losing the monopoly in October 2023 (The Sentry, 2024; Cour des Comptes audit reporting, 2023).

Why this grade: The audit tools were real and were used — against a predecessor's contract and, once, against his own chief of staff. But each time the law reached the top of his own house it bent: release and restoration for the insider, a dormant file for the predecessor's money, a military death trial for the predecessor's politics, and new exclusive monopolies for new insiders. That is not one law with imperfect enforcement; it is enforcement whose severity tracks proximity to the president.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the Congolese treasury and road users, if the Sicomines renegotiation's additional infrastructure envelope is delivered (Test 5 grades delivery); the IGF as an institution, given real work. Hurt — defendants and the public in the Kamerhe reversal, which taught that conviction at the top is negotiable; the eastern artisanal miners and traders of South Kivu, whose gold was routed through a monopoly that returned a fraction of estimated value to the state; and every ordinary Congolese defendant who watches a former president tried without a lawyer while documented embezzlement files go unprosecuted.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • He did not simply inherit and keep his predecessor's grip: in late 2020 he broke the FCC coalition that controlled parliament, the provinces, and much of the judiciary's patronage, dissolved the arrangement in a public address on 6 December 2020, and assembled a new majority — a president escaping a rigged inheritance rather than tightening it (presidential address, December 2020; contemporaneous reporting).
  • Opposition politics remains open in form: 19 presidential candidates stood in 2023, none was excluded from the ballot, the vote was comparatively peaceful by Congolese standards, and opposition figures — Katumbi, Fayulu, Delly Sesanga — campaign, hold seats, and denounce the president publicly, including in parliament in 2026 (CENI, December 2023; International Crisis Group, January 2024; Senate debate reporting, June 2026).
  • Critical media and monitors publish against him at length and are quoted throughout this dossier — Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, Congolese rights groups, and domestic outlets operate and report from Kinshasa (HRW and Amnesty reporting, 2024–2026).

Evidence against:

  • The referees of his own accession were never independent of the deal that seated him: CENI released only a national tally in 2019, CENCO's count contradicted it, and the Constitutional Court dismissed Fayulu's challenge on 19 January 2019 — the same court system whose composition he then reshaped in 2020 by appointing new judges in a contested process the FCC denounced as irregular (Freedom House, 2020; court reporting, 2019–2020). The 2023 election was run by a CENI whose president, Denis Kadima, was appointed in 2021 over the objections of the Catholic and Protestant churches, whose joint observation mission then documented numerous irregularities in a vote extended beyond its legal day; the Constitutional Court affirmed the result on 9 January 2024 (churches' statements, 2021 and December 2023; Constitutional Court, January 2024).
  • The military judiciary has become an instrument against civilians and opponents: opposition adviser Salomon Idi Kalonda was arrested on 30 May 2023 and tried before a military court on treason and M23-collusion accusations; candidate Franck Diongo was arrested by the Republican Guard in June 2023 and held by military intelligence before release without charge; journalist Stanis Bujakera was arrested in September 2023, held for months, and convicted in March 2024 over a leaked intelligence memo he had reported on (HRW, August 2023; Committee to Protect Journalists and court reporting, 2023–2024). Opposition spokesman and MP Chérubin Okende was found murdered in his car in Kinshasa on 13 July 2023; the government announced an inquiry and the documented record assembled here contains no concluded public accounting — recorded as an unresolved case, not as an attribution.
  • The grip tightened measurably in 2026. Human Rights Watch documented, for January–May 2026, intelligence agents hunting two journalists who had criticised government policy into hiding, the 9 January 2026 arrest by the National Intelligence Agency of a civil-society coordinator, and — on 24 March 2026 — police firing tear gas and using violence against a peaceful Kinshasa protest against constitutional change, arresting 15 opposition members and protesters (HRW, 2026). The UN Joint Human Rights Office had already documented that state actors were responsible for 90% of democratic-space violations in its 2023–2024 reporting period, including intimidation or reprisals against 387 human rights defenders and 67 journalists (UN Human Rights Council reporting, September 2024).
  • The term-limit lock itself is now being worked on by his majority. Article 70 limits presidents to two five-year terms and Article 220 expressly forbids amending the number or duration of those terms. In 2026 parliament passed — the National Assembly on 9 June, the Senate on 15 June by 89 votes to none — a referendum law which Tshisekedi signed as Law No. 26/045 on 9 November 2026; the law sets out how referendums, including in circumstances of "absolute necessity" a proposal for an entirely new constitution, can be put (People Daily, 16 June 2026; Africa Briefing reporting on Law No. 26/045, November 2026). Whether replacing the constitution wholesale could lawfully do what Article 220 forbids amending is disputed — supporters invoke Article 5 (sovereignty of the people); opponents, including Fayulu and Katumbi, call the process unconstitutional and a vehicle for a third term. The dispute is printed with its disputants; the documented fact is that the president signed the mechanism into law while saying, at a televised news conference on 6 May 2026, "I have not asked for a third term, but if the people want a third term, I will accept" (Africa Briefing, May 2026). Opponents also cite Article 219, which bars constitutional revision during a state of war — a condition the east arguably meets.

Why this grade: The forms of pluralism survive — candidates, critics, courts that sit — but every referee that touched his power was either seated by a bargain (2019), reshaped by him (the Constitutional Court, CENI's presidency), bypassed through military jurisdiction (Kalonda, Diongo, Kabila, Bujakera's prosecution), or, in 2026, handed a new rulebook that opens the one door the constitution welded shut. A holder who does not grip does not sign that law.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Tshisekedi himself and the Sacred Union, whose >90% National Assembly bloc after 2023 faces no institutional veto it did not appoint; the presidency as an office, now equipped with a referendum route around Article 220. Hurt — the named detained and prosecuted (Kalonda, Diongo, Bujakera — the latter two released, the fact of the process standing); the two journalists HRW documented in hiding in 2026 (role-only here, as they are below the public-eye line and at risk); Congolese voters in Beni, Butembo and Yumbi, whose 2018 presidential vote was postponed past inauguration on security and Ebola grounds and who were simply absent from the count that seated him; and the 2028 electorate, whose term-limit protection now depends on a legal argument rather than a locked article.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His largest adjustment was against his own founding bargain: when the FCC used its parliamentary majority to block his nominees and programme through 2019–2020, he did not merely denounce it — he dissolved the coalition publicly in December 2020, consulted nationally, and built the Sacred Union, accepting a new majority assembled in the open (presidential address, 6 December 2020).
  • On the eastern war he eventually took the door he had sworn never to take. For years his stated position was refusal of direct negotiation with M23, whom he characterised as a Rwandan proxy — a position with a real basis in the UN findings (Test 4) — while he cycled through military options: joint operations with Uganda, the East African Community Regional Force (admitted 2022, expelled by his decision in late 2023 after it would not fight offensively), the SADC SAMIDRC mission (deployed December 2023), Burundian troops, Romanian contractors, and pro-government Wazalendo militias. When Goma fell on 27 January 2025 and Bukavu on 16 February 2025 anyway, he pivoted to diplomacy on two tracks: the Washington track with Rwanda (Declaration of Principles, 25 April 2025; Peace Agreement signed by the two foreign ministers in Washington, 27 June 2025; Washington Accords signed by Tshisekedi and President Paul Kagame in person, 4 December 2025) and the Qatari-mediated Doha track with AFC/M23 itself (Declaration of Principles, July 2025; Doha Framework, 15 November 2025) (US State Department, April–June 2025; PA-X agreement records, 2025). In the words of this test: when another option became available, he at least considered it — and then took it, in public, with signatures. Considering it earlier might have mattered; that he took it at all is on this shelf.
  • The no-good-choice clause, applied where it genuinely fits — the Washington bargain of 2025. The options that actually existed after the fall of Goma were: (a) continue a war his army was losing, with two provincial capitals occupied and no ally willing to retake them; (b) negotiate directly with AFC/M23 from occupation, conceding legitimacy to an armed movement the UN documents as Rwandan-commanded; or (c) sign a US-brokered settlement with Rwanda that traded a regional economic-integration framework and a US commercial role in Congolese minerals for Rwandan disengagement and a DRC commitment to neutralise the FDLR — criticised at home as selling the minerals under duress. Each carried real harm; hindsight options (a reformed FARDC, a willing regional army) were not on the table in February 2025. The choice made — (c), with (b) pursued in parallel at Doha — is graded as the best available in the moment and is not held against him. Still gradable, and graded elsewhere in this dossier: whether he built the trap (in part, yes — seven years of un-reformed army, militia proxies and contractor stopgaps are his record, Tests 4 and 5), and whether he was honest inside it (largely, yes — the agreements were signed publicly and their terms published).
  • He has adjusted under street and institutional pressure at least once on a signature file: after soldiers killed protesters in Goma on 30 August 2023 (separate counts printed in Test 4), the government announced its responsibility for the victims' funerals, released those arrested, and put six officers, including Republican Guard officers, before a military court (government statements and court reporting, September 2023).

Evidence against:

  • The adjustment on the war came only after every doubling-down had failed and two capitals had fallen. The EAC force was expelled for refusing offensive operations rather than renegotiated; SAMIDRC was invited to do what the EAC force would not and was itself overrun at Goma; the death-penalty moratorium was lifted in March 2024 expressly for treason and military offences — a hardening presented as resolve while the front collapsed (government decree reporting, March 2024). The sequence is deny-and-double-down first, adjust last.
  • The peace he signed did not hold, and the response was largely rhetorical. Days after the 4 December 2025 Washington signing, AFC/M23 took Uvira (10 December 2025, per the UN Group of Experts reporting summarised in S/2026/466 coverage), displacing over 500,000 people in just over a week (UN OCHA / UN News, December 2025). Fighting continued through 2026 with both sides accused of violating commitments (PA-X process summaries, 2025–2026). No public revision of strategy followed; by October 2026 the diplomatic energy had moved to repairing relations with Kenya (Oyo summit, 22 November 2026).
  • Pushback at home on the constitutional file was answered with force, not consideration: the 24 March 2026 Kinshasa protest against constitutional change was met with tear gas, violence and 15 arrests (HRW, 2026), and protests in May 2026 against the revision push were likewise suppressed by police (Panafrican Visions reporting, July 2026). On this file, when another option became available — the option of leaving Article 220 alone — the documented record shows it was not openly considered; the referendum law was signed on 9 November 2026.
  • The 2023 election's documented irregularities were met with dismissal rather than remedy: the government rejected the opposition's rerun demand outright, resting on the Constitutional Court's affirmation, while the churches' mission's findings stood unanswered in detail (government statements, December 2023–January 2024; Crisis Group, January 2024).

Why this grade: Two opposite patterns share one tenure. Abroad, after catastrophe, he can move — coalition broken, diplomacy embraced, agreements signed in daylight, and the 2025 settlement is protected here by the no-good-choice clause. At home, where the pushback is about his own tenure's length, the answer has been tear gas and a new referendum law. The wobble is answered where the wobbler is an army; it is doubled down on where the wobbler is a voter.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Congolese diplomacy's credibility with Washington and Doha, converted into signed agreements and a US stake in Congolese stability; civilians spared a battle for retaking Goma by force, which the FARDC could not have won cleanly. Hurt — the population of Uvira and South Kivu, displaced in December 2025 under a peace signed six days earlier; SAMIDRC and FARDC soldiers and the Goma protesters of August 2023, who paid for the years of doubling down; Kinshasa protesters of March and May 2026, whose pushback was treated as disorder rather than as the wobble this test exists to measure.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the east and the west, the opposition regions, the displaced, the Congolese Tutsi and Banyamulenge, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The largest genuinely universal act of the tenure is his: free primary education, launched in 2019, abolished school fees nationwide and brought over 4 million additional children into primary school within two years — by some government counts more than 6 million — one of the largest access expansions in Africa in the decade (World Bank and UNICEF coverage of the gratuité reform, 2019–2021; Africa Education Review, 2025). It applied in opposition provinces as in loyal ones, and Kinshasa continued paying teachers' salaries even in AFC/M23-occupied territory through 2026 (RFI reporting, August 2026). Free maternity care and newborn care followed under the universal health coverage launch in September 2023.
  • The Sicomines renegotiation was framed and structured as a national, not a factional, claim: infrastructure financing raised from USD 3 billion toward USD 7 billion, a 1.2% royalty to Gécamines, and the right to market 32% of output (fifth amendment, 14 March 2024) — the country's patrimony argued up for all Congolese, at least on paper (Test 5 grades delivery).
  • He kept the state formally whole: no province was written off, elections were organised in 2023 across the national territory including most of the east, and the Washington and Doha texts commit to refugee and IDP return with UNHCR support (Washington Peace Agreement, 27 June 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The east was lost on his watch, and its people bear the tenure's central cost. M23, dormant since 2013, resumed operations in late 2021; the UN Group of Experts reported extensive and systematic Rwandan military involvement, estimating at least 6,000 Rwandan soldiers deployed on Congolese territory for the captures of Goma (27 January 2025) and Bukavu (16 February 2025), with 1,000–1,500 remaining in M23 areas as of April 2025 alongside thousands more on the border (UN Group of Experts report to the Security Council, made public 2 July 2025, S/2025/446). A later Group report (S/2026/466, summarised July 2026) estimated 14,000–18,000 Rwandan troops in the east — 6,000–8,000 in North Kivu and 8,000–10,000 in South Kivu — and found AFC/M23 responsible for the largest share of human-rights and humanitarian-law violations among armed actors, including as the leading perpetrator of UN-verified conflict-related sexual violence. The disputants are printed as disputants: Rwanda officially denies aiding M23 with troops and weapons and states its deployments are defensive measures against the FDLR, the Hutu armed group descended from the 1994 génocidaires, which it accuses the DRC of supporting — an accusation the Washington agreement itself partly honoured by committing Kinshasa to neutralise the FDLR (Rwandan government statements, 2025; Rwanda foreign minister's remarks at the Washington signing, 27 June 2025). The UN findings and the Rwandan denial stand side by side here; what is not contested by any party is the outcome for Congolese civilians.
  • The counts of the catastrophe, printed separately and never blended: more than 700,000 people displaced in January–February 2025 alone around Goma, Bukavu, Masisi, Sake and Minova (UN experts and humanitarian agencies, 2025); internal displacement peaked above 7.3 million in April 2025 (IOM Displacement Tracking Matrix / OCHA), with the 2025 Humanitarian Response Plan using a planning figure of 7.8 million IDPs; by mid-2026 recorded IDPs stood at about 5.8 million, a fall OCHA and HRW attribute in large part to some 1.9 million returns, many of them forced returns to insecure areas after AFC/M23 emptied the Goma camps that had held roughly 650,000 people (OCHA Global Humanitarian Overview 2026; HRW World Report 2026; IOM, 2025–2026); 28 million people were acutely food insecure by end-2025 (UNHCR Global Appeal 2026). In the December 2025 Uvira offensive, over 500,000 more were uprooted in just over a week (UN OCHA, December 2025).
  • His own forces and proxies are in the ledger, not only the enemy: on 30 August 2023 Congolese soldiers killed protesters of a religious sect in Goma — at least 48 civilians killed on the FARDC's internal document as reported, over 50 killed on Civicus/HRW counts, the two figures printed separately — and the state's answer relied on Wazalendo militias and foreign contractors paid USD 5,000–6,000 a month while ordinary Congolese soldiers received about USD 100–200, some unpaid for two months after Goma fell (Civicus Monitor, 2023; Guardian contract reporting and Great Lakes Eye salary reporting, 2025). UN reporting through the tenure has documented abuses by FARDC and allied militias alongside those of M23 and the ADF; a state that arms irregular proxies to hold its east has counted its easterners as expendable twice over — once to the invasion it could not stop, once to the methods it chose.
  • Congolese Tutsi and Banyamulenge citizens were counted out in speech and sometimes in safety: the war years brought documented hate speech and stigmatisation of Tutsi Congolese as foreigners — the precise exclusion M23 claims to fight — and UN and rights reporting through 2022–2026 records discrimination and violence against Banyamulenge communities in South Kivu, with no comparably documented presidential campaign against the stigma from a president whose own coalition's rhetoric at times fed it (UN Joint Human Rights Office and HRW reporting, 2022–2025; characterisation of coalition rhetoric is assessment, labeled as such).
  • The quality collapse inside his flagship: free primary education absorbed millions into classes of 80–100 pupils in Kinshasa without commensurate teacher hiring or materials, unofficial fees persisted at school level, and 3.5 million children remained out of school in 2024 (UNICEF estimate, 2024; Africa Education Review, 2025). Access counted everyone in; the schooling many received was nominal. In occupied North Kivu in 2026, AFC/M23 imposed its own school fees — 5,000–45,000 Congolese francs per term at primary level — formally reversing gratuité for the children least able to pay (AFC/M23 provincial decree as reported, August–September 2026).

Why this grade: One policy — free schooling — genuinely counted everyone, and it is not small. But the test's first question is whether anyone is counted out, and the tenure's answer is written in the east: millions displaced, two provincial capitals and then Uvira under an occupation his state could neither prevent nor reverse, his own soldiers among the killers of protesters, his proxies among the abusers, and a minority community stigmatised in wartime with too little protection from the top. A ruler does not pass a counting test on enrolment figures while the counted-out are counted in millions by the UN.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the 4 million-plus children, disproportionately poor and disproportionately girls, who entered primary school after 2019, and their families, freed of fees; mothers under free maternity care from 2023; teachers kept on the state payroll even under occupation. Hurt — the displaced of North Kivu, South Kivu and Ituri (7.3 million at the April 2025 peak on IOM/OCHA figures); the dead of Goma, August 2023 (48 on the FARDC document, over 50 on monitor counts — separate figures, unreconciled); survivors of conflict-related sexual violence, for whom MSF reported new restrictions on care in November 2026; Congolese Tutsi and Banyamulenge citizens stigmatised as foreigners in their own country; and Congolese soldiers sent unpaid against a better-equipped enemy.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 24 January 2019, with dates. The two axes are graded independently: word-keeping here, helped/hurt in the ledgers — neither launders the other.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Free primary education — promised 2019, delivered 2019, still standing 2026. His clearest word and his clearest keeping: fees abolished in his first year, enrolment up by over 4 million within two years, the policy defended through two terms and extended in promise to free 7th and 8th grade from 2027 (gratuité reform, 2019; government announcements, November 2026). This is crossing 1: kept his word AND it helped people — with the quality caveat recorded in Test 4, which belongs to the outcome ledger, not to the keeping.
  • Renegotiate the unbalanced mining contracts — promised, and partially delivered. He pledged "win-win" renegotiation, ordered the IGF audit, and signed the Sicomines fifth amendment on 14 March 2024 raising the infrastructure envelope from USD 3 billion toward USD 7 billion with a 1.2% royalty (presidential statements, 2021; amendment text, March 2024). The envelope is spread over 2024–2040 and conditioned on copper prices — delivery is a fraction of promise so far, and the gap is recorded, but the renegotiation itself happened as said.
  • Break the Kabila system's hold — promised obliquely, done by December 2020. Whatever the 2019 bargain was, he told Congolese he would govern, dissolved the FCC coalition on 6 December 2020, and did. A word kept against the man who may have made him president.
  • Diplomatic re-engagement — delivered. He promised to bring the DRC back into the world: US relations rebuilt (culminating in the 2025 Washington agreements), DRC admitted to the East African Community in 2022, climate and cobalt diplomacy sustained, and a Kremlin audience in October 2026 — balance maintained across patrons.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Peace in the east — his defining word, broken in full. In 2019 he pledged to restore peace and security in the east, speaking of being ready to die for it; he re-pledged it for re-election in 2023. The record: M23 resurgent from 2021, Bunagana lost 2022, Goma and Bukavu lost January–February 2025, Uvira lost December 2025 — six days after he personally signed the Washington Accords — and, on the UN Group's 2026 estimates, 14,000–18,000 foreign troops on Congolese soil. No Congolese president since the Second Congo War has lost more territory while promising its recovery. This is crossing 4: broke his word AND it hurt people — the hurt itemised in Test 4's ledger.
  • End corruption and impunity — pledged 2019, graded in Test 1. CPI score 20 of 100 in 2025, the insider reversal of the Kamerhe case, the dormant Congo Hold-up file. Crossing 4 again on the outcome axis for the public whose money it was.
  • Jobs — pledged at a scale the record cannot find. Campaigning in 2023 he pledged nearly 7 million jobs (about 1.5 million a year) over five years; youth organisations and Congolese reporting through 2026 record no tangible programme at that scale, with overall unemployment projected at about 84% in 2025 on Radio Okapi / Africa Coeur News figures — a contested, very high estimate printed as its source's figure, not blended with any official count, none being published (campaign pledge, 2023; youth-organisation statements, 2025–2026).
  • The army rebuilt — pledged repeatedly, answered with substitutes. Romanian contractors, Burundian and SADC troops, Wazalendo militias, and a March 2025 doubling of military salaries announced after Goma's soldiers had gone two months unpaid (Guardian contract reporting; government announcement, March 2025). The pledge was a national army able to hold the east; the record is a coalition of rentals that could not.
  • "No third term" territory — a word now being re-worded. He is not recorded in 2019 or 2023 as campaigning on extending his tenure; the constitutional settlement he swore to uphold twice fixes two terms and locks the lock (Articles 70 and 220). In May 2026 he told a televised news conference he had not asked for a third term but would accept one if the people wanted it, and on 9 November 2026 he signed the referendum law that creates the mechanism (Africa Briefing, May 2026; Law No. 26/045, November 2026). If a third-term bid follows, this becomes crossing 4 in full; as graded, it is a word being loosened in public, recorded before it is broken. For completeness, crossing 2 (kept his word AND it still hurt people) is documented in the death-penalty file: he pledged to restore order and, as promised by his government in March 2024, lifted the execution moratorium for treason and military offences — the keeping is real; the hurt is recorded in the Kabila death sentence it enabled and the chill HRW documents. Crossing 3 (went against his word AND it helped people) is documented in the M23 file: after years of pledging never to negotiate with M23, he authorised the Doha track with AFC/M23 in 2025 — the break is recorded here; the help, a signed framework where none existed, is recorded in Test 3's ledger in full.
  • Universal health coverage and the rest — announced, thin. Free maternity care launched September 2023 is real but partial; the broader coverage, infrastructure and "Germany of Africa" transformation language of 2019 has no comparable delivery record in the sources assembled here — recorded as unkept at scale, not as never attempted.

Why this grade: A president's word is graded on his biggest sentences, not his most convenient ones. Education — a big sentence — was kept, and this section records it first and in full. But peace in the east, an end to impunity, an army, and millions of jobs were said as plainly, to the same voters, and the documented record of November 2026 shows capitals occupied, the CPI at 20, proxies in place of an army, and no jobs programme at the pledged scale — while the one constitutional promise that protects everyone from every president is being re-opened by his own signature. The crossings are named because they are all present: he has kept his word and helped (education), kept his word and hurt (the death penalty's return), broken his word and helped (talking to M23), and broken his word and hurt (the east). A record containing all four is graded on where the weight sits — and the weight sits in the east.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — schoolchildren and parents (kept word, Test 4 ledger); the treasury's negotiating position with China; Congolese diplomacy. Hurt — eastern Congolese who believed the 2019 and 2023 peace pledges and planned their lives on them; young Congolese who believed the jobs pledge; future Congolese voters, whose two-term protection is weaker tonight than when he swore to it — twice.

Overall Summary

Test 1, one law — FAILS. The audits were real, but severity tracked proximity to the president: his convicted chief of staff was freed and made Speaker, the predecessor's documented money file slept for four years while the predecessor himself got a military death trial without a lawyer, and new monopolies enriched new insiders. Helped: the treasury's Sicomines claim. Hurt: equal justice's credibility, and every defendant without a presidency to return to.

Test 2, without gripping — FAILS. He escaped Kabila's grip only to take his own: a CENI and Constitutional Court seated through bargains and contested appointments, military courts for opponents and a journalist, intelligence-agency arrests documented into 2026, and — on 9 November 2026 — his signature on a referendum law that opens a route around the constitution's locked term limit. Helped: the presidency itself. Hurt: voters, journalists, and the detained named in the section.

Test 3, the wobble — MIXED. Abroad he can answer it: the FCC broken openly in 2020, and after Goma fell, the Washington and Doha tracks signed in daylight — a settlement this dossier protects under the no-good-choice clause, while still grading that he helped build the trap and that the peace was violated within a week at Uvira. At home the same man answered anti-revision protesters with tear gas in March 2026 and signed their opponents' law in November. Helped: a state that got diplomacy instead of a doomed counter-offensive. Hurt: Uvira's displaced, and Kinshasa's protesters.

Test 4, counts everyone — FAILS. Free primary education counted everyone in — over 4 million children — and stands as the tenure's best act. Against it: 7.3 million displaced at the 2025 peak, three eastern cities occupied, his own soldiers in the Goma killings of August 2023 (48 killed on the FARDC's internal figure; over 50 on monitor counts — printed separately, as found), proxies and contractors in place of protection, and Congolese Tutsi stigmatised in wartime. Helped: a generation of schoolchildren. Hurt: the east, in millions.

Test 5, his word — FAILS. All four crossings are present and named: kept-and-helped (education), kept-and-hurt (the death penalty's return), broke-and-helped (negotiating with M23 after swearing not to), broke-and-hurt (peace in the east, impunity, the army, the jobs). The grade follows the weight: the biggest promises — peace, impunity's end, an army — are the broken ones, and the term-limit word is being loosened in public as this is graded. Helped: the children of the gratuité. Hurt: everyone who planned a life on the peace pledge.

Félix Tshisekedi came to office in January 2019 through the most disputed count in Congolese history — 38.6% on the official tally, about 19% on the leaked one, the two figures still unreconciled — and the fairest sentence on the tenure is that he governed better than that beginning promised and worse than his own words did. He broke a predecessor's stranglehold, schooled a generation for free, and argued the country's copper up in price; he also lost the east to the largest foreign deployment since the Second Congo War, watched his referees become his instruments, and ends this grading, in November 2026, signing the legal machinery that could let him stay past the limit he twice swore to keep. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether Rwandan forces actually disengage under the Washington Accords, whether the Doha Framework reaches a final settlement, whether any referendum is called under Law No. 26/045 and on what question, and whether the Sicomines billions arrive as roads. The record is correctable, in the open.
