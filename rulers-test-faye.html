The Ruler's Test — Bassirou Diomaye Faye, President of Senegal

Subject: Bassirou Diomaye Diakhar Faye — President of the Republic of Senegal since 2 April 2024. The seat graded is the presidency, and what is being graded must be stated exactly, because this presidency has had two shapes. Faye, a former tax inspector and PASTEF's secretary-general, was elected on 24 March 2024 with 54.28% of the first-round vote as the stand-in candidate of Ousmane Sonko — PASTEF's founder and leader, who was barred from the race by his court cases and campaigned on the slogan "Diomaye mooy Sonko" ("Diomaye is Sonko"). Faye appointed Sonko prime minister, and for twenty-five months the executive ran as a diarchy: the president holding the constitutional seat, the prime minister holding the movement, the party, and much of the government's driving energy. That diarchy is over. After public friction from July 2025 and an open split over debt policy and the IMF, Faye dismissed Sonko on 22 May 2026 and dissolved the government; Sonko was elected President (Speaker) of the National Assembly four days later, keeping the leadership of PASTEF and its 130 of 165 seats; Faye launched his own party, Kiiraay–Les Patriotes Républicains, on 25 July 2026; and on 17 November 2026 PASTEF's National Council recorded Faye's "automatic loss of membership" of the party that elected him. The presidency is therefore graded across both configurations: Sonko's acts as prime minister (April 2024 – May 2026) are treated as part of this seat's holding, because the president appointed him, sustained him, campaigned as his other half, and is constitutionally answerable for the government he headed; Sonko's acts since May 2026 are graded as the counterweight the seat now faces, not as the seat's own. The predecessor, Macky Sall (president 2012–2024), appears as context where the record Faye inherited — above all the hidden debt and the 2021–2024 protest dead — bears directly on a test. He is graded on roughly two years and eight months in office. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Marks are lost only for how it is done. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The accountability drive against the Sall era ran through published audits and courts, not around them. The government ordered a general audit on taking office; the Court of Auditors' report of 12 February 2025 confirmed that the Sall government had understated the public finances for 2019–2023 — the average deficit revised upward by 5.6 points of GDP, central-government debt at end-2023 revised from 74.4% to 99.7% of GDP, hidden loans of 25.3 points of GDP identified — and the IMF's March 2025 mission corroborated the findings, its mission chief stating there had been "a very deliberate decision to underreport the debt" under the previous administration (Court of Auditors report, 12 February 2025; IMF Press Release 25/77, 26 March 2025; Reuters timeline, June 2026).
  • The flagship prosecutions followed the parliamentary route the constitution prescribes for ministers. On 8 May 2025 the National Assembly voted to refer five former Sall ministers to the High Court of Justice over the management of the 770 billion FCFA Covid-19 response fund, on the basis of a 2022 Court of Auditors investigation: Mansour Faye (the former president's brother-in-law), Aïssatou Sophie Gladima, Moustapha Diop, Salimata Diop Dieng, and Ismaïla Madior Fall. Two of the five were sitting MPs whose immunity parliament lifted. Mansour Faye was indicted on 26 May 2025 and remanded in custody, bail denied; Dieng was charged and released on bail under judicial supervision; Madior Fall was placed under electronic surveillance (Africa Risk Consulting, May 2025; AFP reporting, May 2025). Every charge in this file is an ALLEGATION until a court rules; no verdicts appear in the record assembled here.
  • The legal architecture was widened for everyone, not only aimed backward: a reform of the national anti-corruption office OFNAC, Senegal's first whistleblower-protection law (passed 26 August 2025), and an expanded asset-declaration requirement for public officials (Civicus Monitor, 2025; AFP reporting, 2025).
  • The law also moved against the former regime's security apparatus, through an ordinary citizen's complaint. On 22–23 September 2026, investigating judges laid Senegal's first charges over the 2021–2024 violence — crimes against humanity and complicity charges against four accused, arising from a complaint by a man who says he was abducted and tortured in June 2023; the accused are named in reporting from their own lawyers' statements and are presumed innocent (AFP via African Quarters and News Central, September 2026). The charges were possible because the Constitutional Council, while striking the government's amnesty revision (Test 5), had held that killings and torture are imprescriptible and cannot be amnestied at all — a ruling the governing party publicly welcomed (Reuters, 23 April 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The docket maps almost entirely onto one camp. The ministers referred, detained, or charged are Sall's; his brother-in-law is in custody; his son Amadou Sall has left the country amid a judicial inquiry (AFP reporting, 2025). In the record assembled here, no figure of the governing camp — no PASTEF minister, no official of the Faye administration — appears as facing the same instruments for the same classes of offence. The opposition's characterization of the campaign as a "witch hunt" is an interested party's claim, recorded as such; the asymmetry of the docket itself is a fact of the record, not a characterization (AFP reporting, May 2025; Africa Risk Consulting, May 2025).
  • The one law that most needed removing still stands. The amnesty law of 6 March 2024 — passed by Sall's parliament in his final weeks, covering all acts connected to "demonstrations or politically motivated events" between 1 February 2021 and 25 February 2024, shielding security forces and protesters alike — was the instrument that freed both Faye and Sonko from prison on 14 March 2024. Faye had promised its repeal "so that all the facts can be clarified." What parliament passed instead, on 2 April 2025, was an interpretative law narrowing it; the Constitutional Council struck that law's first article down on 23 April 2025. As of this grading, no repeal has been enacted and the amnesty remains in force for everything except the imprescriptible crimes the Council carved out by interpretation (Reuters, April 2025; Amnesty International, March 2025 and March 2026 statements). The law's beneficiaries include the men now governing; its shield still covers the chain of command of 2021–2024.
  • Speech offences are enforced against the new power's critics. Six journalists faced prosecution in 2025 on false-news and related charges, several of them for remarks about the prime minister personally; a former MP, Moustapha Diakhaté, was sentenced to two months' imprisonment for comments about the conduct of the president, the prime minister, and the Assembly's then-president at a state function (Media Foundation for West Africa, December 2025). No comparable file against a government supporter appears in the record assembled here.

Why this grade: The machinery of accountability genuinely ran — audits published in full, a parliamentary referral, courts deciding — and it ran upward against a former president's family and ministers, which is the direction one-law enforcement is supposed to run. But a law that gives by one measure must also reach the giver's own side and remove the giver's own shield; on this record the enforcement map and the political map coincide, and the amnesty that freed the winners and protects the old chain of command still stands on the winners' watch. That is one law applied with full force in one direction — more than a failure of the test, less than holding it. Helped: the victims' families and the public record — the true debt figures are published and citable by anyone; taxpayers, whose Covid fund is at last being accounted for in court. Hurt: the five referred ministers and their families, who face a special court on ALLEGATIONs not yet tried; the 2021–2024 victims' families, whose shield-law remains in force; journalists and commentators prosecuted for speech.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The referees have ruled against the governing camp again and again, and the rulings have stood. The Constitutional Council struck down the first article of PASTEF's amnesty revision on 23 April 2025; removed four articles from the National Assembly's new internal regulations on 24 July 2025; and on 9 July 2026 annulled outright the constitutional revision (Law n°18/2026) that Sonko's Assembly majority had adopted on 29 June 2026 — a text rewriting some thirty articles, strengthening the prime minister and the Assembly against the president, and barring a sitting president from leading a political party (Constitutional Council decisions 1/C/2025 and 6/C/2026; Reuters, 10 July 2026). The Supreme Court annulled the government's media-suspension orders on 11 December 2025 (West Africa Press Freedom Monitor 2025, July 2026). Sonko's response to the July 2026 annulment was a public statement that the Council's ruling "applies to all" — the loser obeying the referee in words, and in fact the revision has not been re-tabled (Benin Web TV and Reuters reporting, July 2026).
  • The electoral machinery has not been captured. Faye dissolved the National Assembly on 12 September 2024 — a constitutional power, exercised after the chamber refused his institutional bills — and the snap election of 17 November 2024 was run and certified with PASTEF at 130 of 165 seats and the opposition seated with the rest (Civicus Monitor, 2024). In November 2026 that same Assembly, under his rival, is blocking his budgets in the open — parliament is currently the strongest check on the president in the country, which is not what a gripped legislature looks like.
  • The central bank is the regional BCEAO, outside the presidency's gift; the debt files have been negotiated in the open with the IMF, whose spokeswoman stated publicly on 12 November 2026 that the Fund "was not a party" to the dispute between the government and parliament — the referee declining to be used by either side (Businessfront reporting, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The executive tried to shutter more than half the country's media by decree. On 22 April 2025 the communications minister signed an order suspending all broadcasting, publication, and content-sharing by outlets deemed non-compliant with the Press Code — 381 of the 639 outlets a state commission had evaluated — following a 2024 sequence of reversed tax-debt relief, frozen media bank accounts, and cancelled state advertising contracts that had already driven the press to a "press-free day" protest on 13 August 2024. The suspension stood for nearly eight months before the Supreme Court annulled it (MFWA/IFEX statements, April–May 2025; International IDEA democracy tracker, April 2025; WA Press Freedom Monitor 2025).
  • Journalists have been detained for speech about the seat's holders: an editor-in-chief detained in April 2025 over a shared article about the prime minister; a commentator held in custody from April 2025; a television journalist arrested on 9 July 2025 over on-air remarks about Sonko; two journalists arrested in late October 2025 in connection with an attempted interview with a corruption defendant; an Al Jazeera crew detained twice in one day in Casamance in April 2025 while reporting on displaced people's return; and a Walf TV journalist sentenced on 10 December 2025 to a suspended three months for "false news" (Civicus Monitor, 2025; MFWA, December 2025; AFP reporting, 2025). RSF has additionally documented coordinated online harassment of critics by ruling-party supporters, dubbed "72h PASTEF" (RSF via Civicus Monitor).
  • The Constitutional Council's own presidency was left vacant for over a year — its president, Mamadou Badio Camara, died on 10 April 2025 — and Faye filled it by decree n°2026-1318 on 13 July 2026, four days after the Council annulled the revision that threatened his powers, appointing Ousmane Diagne, a career magistrate and former justice minister under Sall. The vacancy is the presidency's defense — the appointment was legally his to make and overdue; the timing is the critics' point, and both are on the record (Presidency communiqué, 13 July 2026; Timbuktu Institute Fragility Alert, July 2026; Sahel Insider, August 2026).
  • Since the rupture, appointment power has been used as camp discipline: officials identified with Sonko have been removed from strategic posts — the Autonomous Port of Dakar, the 3FPT training fund, the National Vocational Training Office — as the two camps sort the administration between them (Timbuktu Institute, July 2026; ARC Briefing, July 2026). And the grip attempt of June 2026, though aimed at Faye, belongs to this seat's holding period: a 130-seat majority assembled under his coalition rewrote the constitution's balance in a text its critics — and the Council's procedural ruling — treated as a power instrument aimed at a named officeholder.

Why this grade: No referee has been removed, packed, or successfully punished, and the two biggest grip attempts of the period — the media suspension and the constitutional revision — were both annulled by courts whose decisions were obeyed. That is a system whose referees still blow their whistles. But the attempts were made, the media order stood for eight months, speech prosecutions are a running file, and the presidency's one appointment to the top court arrived four days after that court saved his powers. The hands have reached for the referees more than once; they have not closed. MIXED. Helped: the Constitutional Council and Supreme Court, whose authority has been tested and publicly obeyed by both camps; the National Assembly, now demonstrably able to check a president; voters, whose 2024 verdict was certified as cast. Hurt: the 381 suspended outlets and their staffs, who lost eight months; the detained and sentenced journalists; officials removed for camp affiliation rather than performance.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest wobble of the tenure — the discovery that the state he inherited was carrying some $13 billion of unreported debt — was answered by publishing it. The audit was commissioned, released in full in February 2025, and handed to the IMF, which suspended the $1.8 billion program in October 2024; the government published the corrected figures (debt at 132% of GDP by end-2024 on the wider measure) rather than disputing the auditors, and spent two years negotiating toward a successor program, reaching a staff-level agreement on a $2.2 billion, 36-month arrangement in September 2026 (Court of Auditors, February 2025; IMF statements, 2025–2026; Reuters timeline, June 2026).
  • Judicial pushback was accepted, including against his own camp. When the Constitutional Council struck PASTEF's amnesty revision in April 2025, the ruling party publicly welcomed the part of the ruling that kept grave crimes prosecutable rather than attacking the court (Reuters, 23 April 2025). When the Council annulled the June 2026 revision on the presidency's own referral, Faye's floated alternative — a popular referendum — was shelved once the court had ruled (African Quarters reporting, July 2026).
  • Parliamentary pushback in the autumn of 2026 was met, at first, with an open door. When PASTEF announced on 23 October 2026 that it would reject the revised 2026 budget, Prime Minister Ahmadou Al Aminou Lo held a press conference on 25 October calling on Sonko to engage in dialogue and asking PASTEF to submit amendments instead of blocking — although the constitution gives the government a route to adopt a budget without a vote, the government publicly chose to ask first (ARC Briefing, October 2026). The Assembly nonetheless blocked the revised budget by procedural motion on 3 November 2026, 126 votes, and its finance committee has since rejected parts of the 2027 draft; the file is open at grading (Businessfront and Europesays reporting, November 2026).
  • The Casamance file was answered with negotiation over force: the February 2025 Bissau agreement with a principal MFDC faction — disarmament, a presidential pardon for imprisoned rebels, amnesty and reintegration for fighters — signed by Sonko, himself from Casamance, carrying Faye's mandate (AP reporting, February 2025; ADF Magazine, March 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The wobble inside his own house was answered a year late, and then by rupture rather than adjustment. Sonko's public ultimatum of 10 July 2025 — accusing the president of a "lack of authority," demanding either backing or a free hand, declaring "if I were president, things would not happen like that" — was met with calls to "depersonalise" the party and months of drift while the debt disagreement hardened (Jeune Afrique via TN News and AFP reporting, July 2025). The adjustment, when it came in May 2026, was dismissal, a rival party, and — by Sonko's own account on 14 November 2026 — no contact between the two men since June, with the split declared "definitive" and a national-unity government ruled out (Sunugal24 and Senego via Europesays, November 2026). The country now runs a president without a party facing a party without a president, mid-debt-crisis.
  • If another option became available, it was not always considered openly. On the debt, the government's public line through Sonko was refusal — restructuring called a "disgrace," and in January 2026 a flat statement that Senegal would not need to restructure — while the presidency moved toward IMF engagement and, by autumn 2026, pursuit of an enhanced G20 Common Framework treatment, aiming for an agreement in principle with lenders by December (Reuters timeline, June 2026; ARC Briefing, October 2026). The option changed; the open consideration of it, with reasons stated to the public by both halves of the executive, did not happen — one half announced refusal while the other negotiated.
  • The victims' families' pushback has received no public answer. Families of the 2021–2024 dead objected from July 2026, and again after Faye's September 2026 speech at the United Nations backing former president Macky Sall's candidacy for UN Secretary-General — the man under whose government their relatives were killed, and whom PASTEF had once considered prosecuting. As of the reporting assembled here, the presidency has not publicly answered them (RFI via African Quarters, July–October 2026).

The no-good-choice clause, applied: The debt trap is the clause's case, and it is recorded as a constraint, not a demerit. The trap was built by the predecessor — $13 billion borrowed off the books between 2019 and 2024 — not by this seat. The options actually open in 2025–2026 were: an IMF program with fiscal consolidation and some form of debt treatment, at the price of austerity in a country where debt service already consumed 111% of revenue in 2025 and of breaking his own movement's no-restructuring word; refusal of restructuring and self-financing, at the price of mounting arrears (1.956 trillion FCFA by March 2025, on his prime minister's own figure) and closed markets; or disorderly default. Each carried real harm, and no hindsight option is counted against him. What remains gradable, and is graded above, is the honesty inside the trap — the numbers were published, which is to his credit — and whether the least-harm door was taken openly, which is where the two halves of his executive contradicted each other in public.

Why this grade: Courts were obeyed, auditors were published, parliament was asked before it was forced, and rebels were negotiated with — a real record of answering pushback in the open. Against it: the deepest pushback, from his own movement and from the victims' families, was answered late, by rupture, or not at all; and on the defining policy question of the tenure the executive spoke with two voices, one refusing in public what the other was negotiating. MIXED. Helped: creditors and citizens who plan on published numbers; Casamance's signatory communities; the Assembly, whose amendments were publicly invited. Hurt: public servants and suppliers waiting inside 1.956 trillion FCFA of arrears while the two palaces fight; the victims' families, unanswered; PASTEF's own voters, whose unified mandate was the thing wobbling.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition, the regions, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The previous era's counted-out were counted, officially and in money. The state's own assistance register, published by the Ministry of Family and Solidarities in January 2025, names the 2021–2024 protest toll as 79 dead and 2,172 former detainees to be assisted; financial assistance to arbitrary-detention victims began in 2024 and was extended in January 2025 to the families of those killed (Amnesty International, March 2025, citing the ministry's communiqué). The September 2026 crimes-against-humanity charges (Test 1) begin counting them in a courtroom as well.
  • Casamance — the region the Senegalese state has fought since 1982 and neglected longer — was counted in with a settlement and a budget: the February 2025 accord's pardon, amnesty, and reintegration provisions, and Faye's 53 billion FCFA plan to restore the state's presence in the region through demining, schools, health facilities, and government offices (ADF Magazine, March 2025; AP reporting, February 2025).
  • The count of votes has included everyone, including against the president: the 35 opposition deputies sit, speak, and — since November 2026 — the Assembly majority itself counts against the presidency on the budget, which is the counting mechanism working on the seat's own holder (Businessfront, November 2026).
  • The fiscal counting, for the first time, counts the whole debt: the corrected books include the hidden loans, the arrears, and the wider public perimeter — the precondition for counting everyone in the budget at all (Court of Auditors, February 2025; IMF, March 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The protest dead are counted in three different numbers that nobody has reconciled, and the instruction says why that matters: counting everyone starts with agreeing on the count. Amnesty International counts at least 65 killed between March 2021 and February 2024, the majority by firearms, with at least 1,000 wounded and over 2,000 arrested (Amnesty International, March 2024 and March 2025). The government's own assistance register counts 79 dead (Ministry of Family and Solidarities, January 2025). Officials have estimated the total above 80 (News Central, September 2026). Within the period, the counts also diverge and are kept separate here: March 2021 — 14 killed (Amnesty International); June 2023 — at least 23 killed including three children (Amnesty International), at least 29 killed and 390 injured (Senegalese Red Cross figures reported by Amnesty), 16 killed (the then-government's figure); February 2024 — at least 3 killed and 271 arrested in two days (Civicus Monitor; Human Rights Watch). Sall's government denied wrongdoing throughout; the dead were overwhelmingly protesters and bystanders of the movement that now governs — which makes the unreconciled count this government's own file to close, and it has not closed it.
  • Casamance's peace is partial in exactly the way the region is used to: the February 2025 accord was signed with the MFDC's Badiatte faction; the hardline Sadio faction, the movement's strongest armed wing, did not sign, and analysts on the record judge that the deal does not address the conflict's root causes (Vincent Foucher, CNRS, via AP, February 2025). The region is counted in — its strongest armed constituency is not yet.
  • The austerity arithmetic counts everyone on paper and lands downward in fact. Energy subsidies had to be tripled in the revised 2026 budget — from 250 billion to 790.3 billion FCFA — to shield households, while the growth forecast was cut from 5% to 2.7% and the deficit widened to 7.6% of GDP (ARC Briefing, October 2026). The recovery plan (PRES) totals 6,166 billion FCFA; domestic resource mobilization under it stood at 172.5 billion FCFA by August 2026 (ARC Briefing, October 2026). The young voters whose unemployment and cost-of-living anger carried this coalition to power appear in the record assembled here as still waiting: no documented dent in youth unemployment, and the IMF's 2026 growth forecast cut to 2.2% (IMF, April 2026).
  • Critics and journalists are counted as defendants in numbers the previous era would recognize (Test 2): six journalists prosecuted in 2025, an ex-MP imprisoned for remarks about the seat's holders (MFWA, December 2025).

Why this grade: This seat counted in two constituencies Senegalese governments have historically counted out — the protest dead of 2021–2024, named in an official register with money attached, and Casamance, given a settlement and a development plan. But the count of the dead is still three unreconciled numbers, the Casamance signature excludes the strongest faction, and the fiscal adjustment is distributed onto the young and the poor who were the mandate's core. Everyone is on the books; not everyone is yet in the settlement. MIXED. Helped: the 2,172 registered former detainees and the families of the 79 registered dead, who received assistance; Casamance's signatory communities and returning displaced people; households shielded by the tripled energy subsidy. Hurt: the families whose dead fall outside or between the three counts; Casamance communities under the unsigned faction; young Senegalese facing 2.2% growth and an unbuilt recovery plan; domestic suppliers inside the arrears.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 2 April 2024, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The audit of the old regime's books — kept, in full and in public. PASTEF campaigned on "reddition des comptes" — rendering of accounts. The audit was ordered on taking office, the Court of Auditors' findings were published on 12 February 2025 rather than buried, the IMF was invited to verify them, and the accountability cases followed (Test 1) (Court of Auditors, February 2025; IMF, March 2025).
  • Abolition of the CESE and the HCCT — kept. Faye campaigned on dissolving the two consultative councils as "budgetivores." After the old Assembly rejected the bill (83 against, 80 for, on 3 September 2024), he dissolved the Assembly, won his majority, and the councils were abolished by Law n°13/2024 on 14 December 2024, with the savings publicly assigned to education (Senegalese press and legislative record, September–December 2024). (The crossing this creates is recorded below: the same act broke a different word.)
  • Renegotiation of the resource contracts — in motion as promised. The pledge to re-examine oil, gas, and mining contracts was institutionalized in August 2024 with a commission of legal, tax, and energy experts; by March 2026 the government had terminated contracts on several petroleum blocks, moved to retake the Yakaar-Teranga gas block, and announced its intention to renegotiate the Greater Tortue Ahmeyim contract as "excessively favorable" to the operators (NRGI, 2024; Prime Minister's office statements via Ecofin Agency, March 2026). No completed renegotiation appears in the record assembled here; the process is the promise being worked, not yet the promise delivered.
  • Casamance — kept in form. The pledge to close the forty-year conflict produced the February 2025 accord (Test 4): negotiation held, pardon and reintegration offered, a development plan funded on paper.
  • Institutional housekeeping words — kept. Whistleblower protection (promised as part of the anti-corruption program) became law on 26 August 2025; asset-declaration obligations were expanded (Civicus Monitor, 2025).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The amnesty repeal — the signature justice promise, not kept. Faye promised to repeal the March 2024 amnesty law "so that all the facts can be clarified"; Sonko repeated the promise during the November 2024 legislative campaign. Two years on, the law stands. The April 2025 narrowing was struck down by the Constitutional Council, and no repeal has been enacted since; the September 2026 prosecutions route around the amnesty only for imprescriptible crimes, which is a judicial workaround, not the promised legislative act (Reuters, April 2025; Amnesty International, March 2025 and March 2026). The families of the dead name this, in their own statements, as the unkept word at the center of the file.
  • "Diomaye is Sonko" — the founding word of the mandate, broken by both halves. The 54.28% was won on an explicit promise of unity: one man in the seat, the other at his side, a single program. The record since: a public ultimatum (July 2025), a dismissal (22 May 2026), a rival presidential party (25 July 2026), a declaration that the divorce is "definitive" (14 November 2026), and a party council formally recording the president's loss of membership (17 November 2026). Whatever the provocations on either side — and both sides document them — the word given to the voters in March 2024 was the pair, and the pair no longer exists.
  • The no-restructuring word — given by the seat's own government, reversed by the seat. As prime minister, Sonko stated in November 2025 that Senegal would not restructure, calling it a "disgrace," and in January 2026 that Senegal would not need to. By autumn 2026 the presidency's government was pursuing an enhanced G20 Common Framework process — the architecture of debt treatment — alongside the IMF program (Reuters timeline, June 2026; ARC Briefing, October 2026). The reversal may be the right policy; the Test grades the word, and the word was reversed without ever being openly withdrawn.
  • The endorsement of Macky Sall — against the movement's word, in the open. PASTEF's program treated Sall as the author of the 2021–2024 repression and contemplated his prosecution. In July 2026 Faye hosted Sall in Dakar, and in September 2026 he backed Sall's candidacy for UN Secretary-General at the United Nations — days after Senegalese judges laid the first crimes-against-humanity charges over the killings of Sall's tenure. The victims' families objected publicly; the presidency has not answered them (ARC Briefing, July 2026; RFI via African Quarters, 2026).

The four crossings, named: 1. Kept his word AND it helped: the audit promise — the true debt figures were published against the government's own short-term interest, and every subsequent negotiation, including with the IMF, runs on numbers citizens can check. 2. Kept his word AND it hurt: the CESE/HCCT abolition — the campaign word was kept to the letter, and the same act broke Faye's signature on the "Sursaut citoyen" good-governance pact, which endorsed the Assises nationales framework recommending such consultative institutions be kept; two channels of citizen and territorial representation were removed by the keeping of the promise (Le Quotidien commentary, January 2025 — the pact signature is a matter of record; the characterization as a broken engagement is the critics', and is recorded as contested). 3. Went against his word AND it helped: the Sall endorsement is the contested instance — it broke the movement's stance toward the former president, and the presidency's camp presents it as statecraft that raised Senegal's diplomatic standing (Faye holds ECOWAS's rotating presidency in the same period). The help is claimed by his camp and disputed by the victims' families, who record the same act as a hurt; both are printed, and the crossing is recorded as contested rather than clean. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt: the amnesty repeal — promised by both men, unrepealed after two years, with the families of at least 65 dead (Amnesty's count) still waiting on the legislative act they were promised while assistance money, welcome as it is, is not the justice that was pledged.

Why this grade: The program words that cost effort were substantially worked — the audit, the councils, the contract reviews, the whistleblower law. The words that cost unity were not kept: the repeal that was the justice promise, the pairing that was the electoral promise, and the refusal that was the debt promise were each abandoned, two of them without an open withdrawal. A record that keeps the administrative words and breaks the founding ones is MIXED, and the open files that will move it are named below. Helped: citizens and creditors working from true figures; whistleblowers, now protected in law; Casamance signatories. Hurt: the victims' families, still owed the repeal; the 2024 electorate, whose chosen instrument was the pair; PASTEF's base, expelled-by-record along with its president.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Bassirou Diomaye Faye holds a seat that has changed shape under him: elected as one half of a pair, on the strongest first-round mandate in Senegal's history, he now governs as a president without a party, facing an Assembly run by the other half. Test 1 lands MIXED because the accountability machinery genuinely ran — published audits, a parliamentary referral of five former ministers, the first crimes-against-humanity charges over the 2021–2024 violence — while the docket's map coincides with the political map and the amnesty that freed the winners still shields the old chain of command; helped: the public record and the Covid fund's accounting; hurt: the untried accused and the families still behind the amnesty. Test 2 lands MIXED because every grip attempt on the record — the 381-outlet media suspension, the supermajority's constitutional revision — was annulled by a court and the annulments were obeyed, while the attempts themselves, the speech prosecutions, and the timing of the Constitutional Council presidency appointment (13 July 2026, four days after the ruling that preserved his powers, ending a vacancy of over a year) keep the grade from holding; helped: the courts and the Assembly; hurt: the suspended outlets and detained journalists. Test 3 lands MIXED because the debt revelation was owned and published, courts were obeyed, parliament was asked before it was forced, and Casamance was negotiated — while his own movement's pushback was answered a year late and by rupture, the families' objections went unanswered, and on restructuring the executive refused in one voice what it negotiated in the other; the debt trap itself is recorded under the no-good-choice clause as a constraint he inherited, not a demerit. Test 4 lands MIXED because the protest dead were entered in an official register (79 dead, 2,172 detainees assisted, on the government's own count — against Amnesty's at least 65 and officials' estimates above 80, all three counts printed unreconciled) and Casamance was settled with in part, while the unsigned Sadio faction, the tripled subsidy bill, and 2.2% growth leave the mandate's core constituency — the young — still waiting. Test 5 lands MIXED because the administrative words were kept (audit, councils, contract review, whistleblower law) and the founding words were not (the amnesty repeal, the pair itself, the no-restructuring refusal), with all four word×outcome crossings documented, one of them — the Sall endorsement — recorded as contested between his camp's claimed diplomatic help and the families' recorded hurt. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the IMF Board approves the program and on what budget; whether the 2027 budget passes, is forced, or brings the Assembly's dissolution; whether the High Court of Justice reaches verdicts; whether the crimes-against-humanity file climbs the chain of command; and whether the amnesty is ever repealed. The Sun remains the only complete hold in this series; Senegal's president is, on this record, a holder whose referees still work — including, in the end, against the man who seated half of them.
