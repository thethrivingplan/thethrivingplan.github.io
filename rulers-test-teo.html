The Ruler's Test — Feleti Teo, Prime Minister of Tuvalu

Subject: Feleti Penitala Teo OBE — Prime Minister of Tuvalu since 26 February 2024. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Tuvalu is a parliamentary constitutional monarchy in which the monarch is head of state and the prime minister is head of government, and executive power — the government, the budget, and the direction of the ministries — is exercised by the prime minister and cabinet. The head of state is King Charles III, King of Tuvalu since 8 September 2022, who does not reside in Tuvalu and acts through a Governor-General: Sir Tofiga Vaevalu Falani, a Church of Tuvalu pastor, held the office from September 2021 until September 2026 (including a twelve-month reappointment in September 2025), and Temukisa Auina Penehuro Hauma — a teacher of thirty years and president of the Tuvalu National Council of Women since 2016 — was sworn in on 30 September 2026 as the eleventh Governor-General and the first woman to hold the office substantively, beginning a four-year term (PINA, 30 September 2026; CIA World Leaders listing, updated 1 October 2026). The Governor-General is appointed by the King on the advice of the prime minister, declares the prime minister elected, appoints ministers on the prime minister's advice under sections 63, 64 and 68 of the constitution, and declares a state of public emergency under section 35 on the prime minister's advice — the roles are stated plainly here, and the monarch and the Governor-General are graded only where they bear on a test. Teo, born 9 October 1962, is a lawyer — the first Tuvaluan admitted as a lawyer, in 1986, Tuvalu's first Attorney General, later executive director of the Western and Central Pacific Fisheries Commission — and the son of Sir Fiatau Penitala Teo, Tuvalu's first Governor-General. He was elected to parliament for Niutao at the general election of 26 January 2024, in which Tuvalu's sixteen MPs were returned from eight two-seat island constituencies, all standing as independents — Tuvalu has no political parties and therefore no party platform, so the mandate graded in Test 5 is the word Teo's own government gave: its statement of twenty-one priorities published on 28 February 2024. The election removed the incumbent: Prime Minister Kausea Natano lost his Funafuti seat, as did three of his ministers and Dr Puakena Boreham, the parliament's only woman. On 26 February 2024 Teo was the only candidate nominated by his fifteen fellow MPs, and Governor-General Falani declared him elected prime minister without a vote (AP reporting via NPR, February 2024). His predecessor Natano (prime minister 19 September 2019 – 26 February 2024) appears as context where the record Teo inherited — above all the Falepili Union treaty Natano signed with Australia on 9 November 2023 — bears directly on a test. Teo is graded on his own tenure, 26 February 2024 to November 2026 — roughly two years and nine months — and he was still in office, addressing COP31 in Antalya and the UN General Assembly, at grading. No motion of no confidence against him appears in the record assembled here, in a system whose history is marked by frequent ones. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test, printed verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure — the Test grades the holding, never the platform; in Tuvalu's case there was no platform, every MP sitting as an independent, so the word graded in Test 5 is the prime minister's own stated commitments. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The election law that seated him removed his own side's predecessor and spared no incumbent. At the 26 January 2024 election the sitting prime minister, Kausea Natano, lost his seat; three of his ministers lost theirs; the parliament's only woman lost hers; two Nukulaelae MPs were returned unopposed because no candidate contested them — the same written rule producing removal, election, and uncontested return in different constituencies (2024 general election results; RNZ reporting, January 2024). Teo himself took the premiership by the constitution's own sequence: sole nomination by the sixteen MPs, declaration by the Governor-General, cabinet appointed the next day, 27 February 2024 (PINA, 28 February 2024).
  • The Falepili mobility pathway — the largest single benefit his government administers — is allocated by random ballot, one rule for every applicant, with none of the exclusions other migration systems apply: no job offer required, no upper age limit, and no disability exclusion (Migration Amendment (Australia–Tuvalu Falepili Union Treaty Visa) Regulations 2025, in force 1 May 2025; DFAT country brief, 2026). The first ballot was heavily oversubscribed — the registration and application figures conflict and are printed separately, never blended: 8,750 registrations, equal to 82 percent of the 2022 census population of 10,643, counting family members (Reuters-based reporting, December 2025); and, on another sourced count, more than 5,000 applications, over half the population (Asylum Insight, 2026). Whoever the count, the allocation mechanism documented is a ballot, not a favour list.
  • The money is published, down to a citizen's version, and carried to the outer islands. The national budgets for 2025–2026 and 2026–2027 are published in full by the Ministry of Finance; on 9 October 2026 the ministry launched a Citizen Budget Guide for 2026/27 alongside the mid-term review of the national strategy Te Kete, expressly "presenting budget information in a simple and accessible way," and its team then took the guide to island communities beginning with Niutao, Vaitupu and Nukufetau (Ministry of Finance and Economic Development, October 2026).
  • The standing governance record shows no second law in operation. Transparency International's U4 overview of Pacific corruption (January 2026) records that there is little data or evidence of corruption in Tuvalu, that the US State Department reported no reports of serious government corruption, and that the government generally implements anti-corruption laws effectively; Tuvalu has no Corruption Perceptions Index score at all — recorded here as an absence of measurement, not as a clean bill. Freedom House scores Tuvalu 93 of 100, Free, in its 2026 edition (political rights 37 of 40, civil liberties 56 of 60).

Evidence against:

  • The .tv domain — one of the state's three great revenue sources — runs on a contract the public cannot check. The 2026–2027 budget estimates "Dot TV Marketing" revenue at 10,881,770 budget dollars inside a commercial-contracts total of 13,081,770, and states that the estimate takes into account a planned review of the existing contractual arrangements with GoDaddy Registry, the operator chosen in December 2021; the budget does not disclose the contract's terms, what must actually be paid, by whom, or on what trigger (2026–2027 National Budget, section 3.4.4, presented 22 June 2026; BTW Media analysis, November 2026). A revenue stream worth roughly a tenth of the state, governed by an unpublished contract and an unscoped review, is a place where one law cannot be verified to run — the gap is documented, and it sits at the source.
  • The treaty his government carried into force was made, by his own government's admission, without the public being told. The Teo government's statement of 28 February 2024 supported the Falepili Union's broad principles while acknowledging "the absence of transparency and consultations" in informing the general public about the deal under the previous administration, and vowing to address the "process issues" (government statement via Simon Kofe, 28 February 2024; VOA reporting, 28 February 2024). The treaty then entered into force on 28 August 2024 without any Tuvaluan public consultation or vote documented in the record assembled here. The process flaw is inherited — the signature was Natano's — but the carrying-forward was Teo's, and the one-law question it raises (a decision of this size made inside the room) was answered with an explanatory memorandum negotiated with Australia, not with a consultation of Tuvaluans (Test 3 and Test 5).
  • The treaty's own terms give an outsider a standing the insider does not have in reverse. Article 4(4) requires Tuvalu and Australia to "mutually agree" any arrangement Tuvalu enters with another party on security or defence-related matters — former prime minister Enele Sopoaga, now leader of the opposition, calls this a hand-over of sovereignty and "neo-colonialism at its worst," and former foreign minister Simon Kofe criticised the same clause before joining Teo's cabinet (PINA, 12 April 2024; American Journal of International Law essay, Green and Guilfoyle, 2024). The clause is contested — the May 2024 explanatory memorandum records the joint understanding that it does not require Australia's permission for Tuvalu to talk to other partners and does not limit Tuvalu's diplomatic relations (Test 3) — but the treaty text itself was never amended, and a rule that binds Tuvalu's security choices to a partner's agreement, with no reciprocal binding of the partner, is not one law for both sides of the table. That asymmetry was signed by his predecessor; Teo, who sat on the eminent persons panel that built the treaty's groundwork before he was an MP, chose to proceed with it rather than reopen it — a choice graded in Test 3, recorded here because Test 1 grades the source.

Why this grade: On every domestic process the record can check — the election that unseated his predecessor, the ballot that allocates the Falepili visas, the published budgets and their citizen guide — the same rule visibly runs for insiders and outsiders, and no documented act of favouritism by Teo appears anywhere in the record assembled here. The grade stops at MIXED for two documented reasons at the source: a tenth of the state's revenue flows under a contract whose terms the public cannot read, and the largest decision of the tenure was carried into force on a process his own government admitted had shut the public out, without a documented Tuvaluan consultation to repair it.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — every Falepili ballot applicant, who faced a random draw with no age, job or disability screen; citizens and island communities who received the budget in a citizen's guide taken to the outer islands. Hurt — no group is documented as hurt by a favouritism act of this government; the hurt recorded is to public verification itself — Tuvaluans who cannot read the .tv contract behind a tenth of their revenue, and who were not consulted on the treaty that now governs their mobility and security.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The referees that seated him acted independently of him, and the one he inherited mid-stream was reformed rather than captured. The 2024 election was run under the electoral provisions by the election commissioner, produced a result against the incumbent government, and was accepted by all sides; in October 2025 his government launched a full review of the electoral laws and processes — priority number four of its own twenty-one priorities — led by two expert consultants, Tauasaa Taafaki and Mose Saitala, with nationwide consultations beginning in Funafuti and continuing across all island communities, aimed at passing amended laws well before the 2028 election (PINA, 8 October 2025). A government that opens its own election law to public consultation two years before it must face it is not gripping that referee on this record.
  • No documented interference with the courts, the prosecution, or the media by Teo appears in the record assembled here. Freedom House's 2026 edition — covering 2025 — rates Tuvalu Free at 93 of 100 and records that elected officials develop and implement policy without improper interference from unelected entities (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026, as summarised in the U4 overview, January 2026). RSF-derived listings place Tuvalu's press-freedom score at 65 points (Nomadino compilation of RSF 2026 data). Tuvalu's media is small and substantially state-run — the environment, printed as environment — but no ban, prosecution of a journalist, or closure under Teo is documented here.
  • The Governor-General's office changed hands on the constitutional track, in the open, at the end of a term rather than by removal: Falani was reappointed for twelve months in September 2025 in a public ceremony administered by the Acting Chief Justice, served out the year — including a June 2026 official programme in which Deputy Prime Minister Panapasi Nelesone acted as prime minister in Teo's absence — and handed over to Hauma on 30 September 2026, sworn by the Acting Chief Justice with Teo in attendance (PINA, September 2025 and September 2026; DFAT country brief, 2026). The monarch's part in the tenure is this appointment machinery and nothing else documented: no royal act directed at Tuvalu's courts, elections, or media appears in the record.
  • The state's bank paid its dividend through the published budget process rather than around it: the National Bank of Tuvalu's $3 million interim dividend to the government was announced by the finance ministry as national revenue, with the deputy prime minister's statement on the record (Ministry of Finance, 2026). Tuvalu has no central bank — it uses the Australian dollar — so the monetary referee this test usually examines does not exist to be gripped; the National Bank is state-owned, and the documented flow ran toward the treasury in public.

Evidence against:

  • The chief justiceship has been "acting" for the whole of his tenure's second half, held on short contracts extended through the prime minister's own office. Sir John Muria was appointed Acting Chief Justice on 21 December 2024, succeeding Sir Gordon Ward, who had himself been acting since 2019 pending the appeal of Charles Sweeney QC against the termination of his appointment as chief justice by a resolution of parliament — a termination that predates Teo and is recorded as the seat's history, not his act. Muria's contract was extended for one year from 21 December 2024, and the extension was announced by the Office of the Judiciary "operating under the auspices of the Office of the Prime Minister" (Solomon Star News, January 2025; High Court of Tuvalu record). As of grading, Tuvalu's highest judge still sits in an acting capacity on a renewable contract, in a judiciary administered under the prime minister's office. No act of interference with a case is documented; the structure itself — a referee whose tenure renews annually at the executive's end of the table — is the grip risk this test exists to name, and two years of it is his tenure's fact, not his predecessor's.
  • The emergency power was used, lawfully and briefly — and its use shows where the lever sits. On 13 April 2026 Governor-General Falani, under section 35 of the constitution and acting on Teo's advice, declared a state of public emergency for Funafuti over electricity instability and fuel-supply risk; it ran its stated fourteen days and was lifted, with the energy minister reporting publicly as it ended (PINA, 14 and 27 April 2026). The declaration is on the FOR side of the ledger in one respect — time-limited, constitutional, and allowed to expire — and it is recorded here because the mechanism is the head of state acting on the prime minister's advice, which is exactly the channel this test watches. No extension, and no use of emergency powers against any person or institution, is documented.
  • Power in the cabinet is concentrated by design and unchanged: nine ministers including the prime minister govern a sixteen-member parliament in which the government holds ten seats and the opposition six, every minister is drawn from the government's own ten, and the prime minister's office carries the judiciary's administration and the Falepili file alongside the premiership (Cabinet of Tuvalu listings, 2024–2026; CIA World Leaders, 1 October 2026). In a state this small some concentration is arithmetic; the dossier records that Teo has loosened none of it, and that the one structural reform he did launch — the electoral review — has produced consultations, not yet law.

Why this grade: No referee in Tuvalu is documented as punished, removed, or overruled by Teo — elections ran against his predecessor and were accepted, the electoral law was opened to consultation, the emergency expired on its own date, and the Governor-Generalship turned over on schedule. The grade is MIXED rather than HOLDS because of one standing structure he owns and has not changed: the chief justice of Tuvalu has sat as an acting appointee on short renewable contracts, administered under the prime minister's office, for the whole period since December 2024 — a referee held on a short string even where no pull on the string is documented.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — voters and candidates in 2028, whose election law is being reviewed in public consultation before the poll rather than after a dispute; the public record itself, through published budgets, a published dividend, and an emergency that expired in the open. Hurt — the judiciary's institutional standing, which carries an acting head on annual contracts; no individual judge, journalist, or official is documented as personally hurt by an act of grip under Teo.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • The tenure's defining wobble — the Falepili Union — was answered by considering, in the open, the option that stopped short of both poles on offer. The pushes were real: the treaty's signer had just lost his seat over it in part; the opposition leader, Enele Sopoaga, had vowed to repeal it and called it a sovereignty hand-over; Teo's own incoming foreign-policy critics, Kofe among them, had pledged renegotiation. Teo's government neither scrapped the treaty nor ratified it untouched. In his first interviews he named the exact clause he wanted revisited — the "mutually agree" security clause — and stated the test he would apply: "[if] there is a way that stops short of revising the treaty that guarantees the integrity of the sovereignty of Tuvalu, then we will certainly explore those options" (Teo interviews, February–March 2024). This is the other-option rule in its exact form: when another option became available — an interpretive instrument rather than revision or repeal — he at least considered it openly, and then took it. On 9 May 2024 he and Australian Foreign Minister Penny Wong signed a joint statement recommitting to the Union and released an explanatory memorandum recording the joint understandings: Tuvalu does not need Australia's permission to talk with other partners; Australia has no unqualified right of access to Tuvalu's territory or airspace and no right to establish military areas; Article 4(4) does not diminish Tuvalu's standing in global fora; and the treaty "would not limit Tuvalu's liberty to enter into diplomatic relations with other States" (Joint Statement on commitment to the Falepili Union, DFAT, 9 May 2024; Teo–Wong press conference, Funafuti, 9 May 2024). Teo also corrected the public record in person, against the critics' framing: "This treaty came about because Tuvalu Government requested Australia, not the other way around, as some critics of the treaty attempt to misinform the public. It was entirely an initiative of the Tuvalu Government" (press conference, 9 May 2024). The treaty entered into force on 28 August 2024 with those understandings attached, a joint committee was established under Article 5 to meet at least every twelve months, and implementation followed in the open: the visa regulations in force from 1 May 2025, the Development Partnership Plan 2025–2030, and the Te Ao economic-governance partnership's inception meeting in Funafuti on 9 September 2025 (DFAT, 2024–2025; Australia–Pacific Partnerships Platform, September 2025).
  • The no-good-choice clause applies to the Falepili file, and is applied by its mechanics. The options that actually existed in February 2024 were: proceed with the treaty as signed, accepting a security clause critics across Tuvalu's own political spectrum called a veto; repeal or refuse it, losing the world's first treaty recognition of continuing statehood despite sea-level rise, the Australian assistance commitment for disaster, pandemic and aggression, and the only mobility pathway on offer — 280 permanent-residence places a year — for a nation whose highest point is about 4.6 metres; or reopen the text with Australia, risking the whole instrument a month after an election fought partly on it. Each door carried real harm. The trap's origin is shared and recorded, not hidden: the treaty was requested and signed under Natano, and Teo himself had served on the eminent persons panel that built its groundwork — he helped design the instrument he then had to defend, which is part of the record, and it means the "inherited trap" framing is only half true. He took the least-harm door actually open — proceed, with a signed interpretive memorandum narrowing the clause he had publicly identified — and was honest in public about both the treaty's Tuvaluan origin and his own reservation. That choice is graded as the best possible choice available on the documented options, and the residual sovereignty argument (Test 1) is recorded as a cost of the treaty, not as a failure to answer the wobble.
  • The April 2026 energy emergency was answered with a time limit, public numbers, and a named fix. When generator instability and Middle East-linked fuel risk threatened Funafuti's supply — with fuel supply reported "not assured" beyond June (RNZ, 15 April 2026) — the government declared a fourteen-day emergency on 13 April 2026, and as it expired on 27 April the energy minister, Simon Kofe, reported in public that generation was stable, that recent blackouts were network faults rather than generator failures, that an ADB-funded rental generator would arrive on 28 May as three-to-six months of backup, and that procurement of permanent generators would begin in early May (PACNEWS, 27 April 2026). The emergency was not extended. At COP31 in November 2026 Teo was still answering the same wobble in the open, presenting Tuvalu's Energy Transition Masterplan — built with Australian support to cut dependence on imported fossil fuels — as the long-term reply (PINA, 16 November 2026).
  • The deepest pushback of all — the sea — is answered with adjustment rather than denial, in both directions at once. His government continued the Tuvalu Coastal Adaptation Project's land-raising on Funafuti (the figures conflict and are printed separately in Test 5), carried the continuity-of-statehood campaign to the UN Political Declaration on Sea-Level Rise endorsed on 24 September 2026, and simultaneously refused the abandonment framing the pathway invites: "We are not planning to abandon our homeland. We are planning to protect our people, our identity and our future" (Teo at COP31, PINA, 16 November 2026), while developing a National Plan on Climate Change Displacement and Human Mobility for those whom movement reaches anyway. Holding both doors open — defend the land, dignify the leaving — is adjustment to the wobble in its purest documented form.

Evidence against:

  • The adjustment on Falepili was made with Australia, not with the Tuvaluan public whose exclusion his government had itself named. The February 2024 statement promised to address the "process issues" of a treaty negotiated without transparency or consultation; the documented remedy by November 2026 is an explanatory memorandum, Australian parliamentary scrutiny (the treaty was tabled in Canberra on 26 March 2024 and examined by the Joint Standing Committee on Treaties), and a joint committee of the two governments. No Tuvaluan public consultation on the treaty itself appears in the record assembled here. The wobble from his own voters was answered over their heads, however openly the answer was published afterward.
  • The electoral wobble is answered slowly. The January 2024 election's frustrations — the stated reason for the review — were met with a review launched in October 2025, twenty months into the tenure; as of grading the consultations continue and no amended law has been passed, against the government's own aim of completing the reforms well before the 2028 election (PINA, 8 October 2025). The door is open and the direction is right; the pace is the against-shelf fact.
  • The energy answer, for all its openness, records a failure arriving on his stated top watch: electricity was a named development challenge from his first month, yet by April 2026 the capital's generation was unstable enough to require a state of emergency, a rented generator, and emergency procurement — the system reached the edge before the fix was ordered. Explanation was prompt and candid; prevention was not there. The shelf records the gap between the answering and the outcome, as it does for every ruler in this series.

Why this grade: Test 3 grades the answering, not the outcome, and on the answering this record is the strongest in the dossier: on the treaty he named his objection precisely, stated the condition an alternative had to meet, took the alternative when it became available, and defended the result in person; on the energy emergency he used a constitutional, fourteen-day instrument, let it expire, and reported the fix with dates. The against shelf is real — the Tuvaluan public was never itself consulted on the treaty, and two reforms move slowly — but it records incompleteness of consultation and pace, not denial or doubling down, neither of which appears anywhere in the documented record. HOLDS.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Tuvaluans who wanted the treaty neither scrapped nor swallowed whole, who got its sovereignty understandings in a signed public document; Funafuti households and services, whose emergency was managed inside fourteen days with a dated backup plan. Hurt — Tuvaluan voters whose objection to the treaty's making was never put to them directly, even by the government that agreed the objection was justified; outer-island communities still waiting, at grading, for the electoral law the 2024 frustrations promised them.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the outer islands, women, the poor, people with disabilities, and those who leave — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The structure he governs under counts every island equally, and his cabinet is drawn from across it. Parliament's sixteen seats are two for each of the eight island constituencies, from Funafuti (over half the population) to Nukulaelae (the smallest) — a count by island, not by head. Teo's nine-member cabinet seats ministers from six of the eight constituencies: Niutao (Teo; Sa'aga Talu Teafa, natural resources), Vaitupu (Paulson Panapa, foreign affairs; Maina Talia, home affairs, climate change and environment), Funafuti (Simon Kofe, transport, energy, communication and innovation; Tuafafa Latasi, health), Nukufetau (Panapasi Nelesone, deputy prime minister and finance), Nanumaga (Hamoa Holona, education) and Nanumea (Ampelosa Manoa Tehulu, public works) (Cabinet listings, 2024–2026). His government's stated development challenge from its first month was "the need to improve medical and educational services to Tuvalu's outer islands" (Teo, March 2024).
  • The mobility pathway counts the people other systems count out first. The Falepili visa carries no upper age limit and no disability exclusion, requires no job offer, and grants permanent residence with access to Australian health, education and income support on arrival (2025 Regulations; DFAT, 2026). The first arrivals in December 2025 — the first group numbered 27 in the contemporaneous reporting — included a dentist, a trainee pastor, and Tuvalu's first female forklift driver (Euronews Green / DFAT statements via December 2025 reporting). The 280-a-year cap is itself a counting decision, documented as designed to prevent the mass loss of the skilled people Tuvalu still needs (DFAT; December 2025 reporting).
  • Women were counted at the very top of the state on his advice, and in his government's public accounting of the climate burden. The Governor-General appointed on the prime minister's advice and sworn in on 30 September 2026, Temukisa Hauma, is the first woman to hold the office — Teo marked it himself: "It is a historic moment for Tuvalu, as she becomes the first woman to hold this position" (PINA, 30 September 2026). At COP31 he put the unpaid resilience work of women — monitoring shellfish, rationing rainwater, moving crops inland — at the centre of Tuvalu's case, in terms: "Calling our people agents of change gives no one the right to ask them to carry more. They did not cause this crisis. Resilience has a limit, and beyond that limit is survival — and many of our women live at that limit now" (Teo, COP31, November 2026; UN News, November 2026). The displacement plan his government is developing expressly includes women, young people, persons with disabilities and older people in planning and decisions (PINA, 16 November 2026).
  • The digital count reached everyone at once: the Vaka Cable, Tuvalu's first international undersea cable, was activated on 24 October 2025, connecting Funafuti to Google's Bulikula system in a project of about US$56 million grant-funded by Australia, Japan, New Zealand, Taiwan, the United States and partners; the state carrier cut mobile plan prices by 50 percent at launch (AIFFP update, 2025; Data Center Dynamics, 2025; DevPolicy, 4 December 2025). Connectivity is the physical layer of the digital-nation policy — the means by which a dispersed people's records, culture and government services are preserved and delivered regardless of island.

Evidence against:

  • Women are counted out of the elected count entirely, and his tenure has not moved it. The 2024 election returned sixteen men and no women — the only woman MP, and the only woman candidate of the two who stood in 2019's pattern, lost her seat; only three women have ever been elected to Tuvalu's parliament (IPU Parline: 16 men, 0 women, 0.0 percent; IPU, September 2024). Teo's cabinet of nine is likewise all men, and the electoral review launched in October 2025 has produced no documented reserved-seat or quota proposal as of grading — the 2005 Pacific Islands Forum recommendation of two reserved seats for women remains unimplemented two decades on. The Hauma appointment is real and sits on the FOR shelf; it is an appointment at the head of state's office, where the elected and executive counts stand at zero.
  • Two islands have no seat at the cabinet table: Nui and Nukulaelae — Nukulaelae being the smallest constituency, whose two MPs were returned unopposed in 2024 — supply no minister, and the opposition's six MPs, including former prime minister Sopoaga, hold no portfolio (Cabinet listings, 2024–2026). In a ten-seat government some exclusion is arithmetic; the dossier records which islands the arithmetic left out.
  • The adaptation count is concentrated where the people already are. The raised, flood-free land is on Funafuti — necessary, since over half the population lives there, and the Long-Term Adaptation Plan (Te Lafiga o Tuvalu) itself envisages the raised Funafuti land eventually sheltering the whole national population — but the documented effect in this tenure is protection built first, and so far only, for the capital atoll, while the outer islands' documented receipts are coastal works under the earlier TCAP phases on Nanumea and Nanumaga and the budget-guide visits of October 2026. The plan counts everyone in the end; the record to November 2026 counts Funafuti in fact.
  • The leaving itself cuts both ways in the count, and the cost side is documented. At 280 Falepili places a year plus other pathways, close to 5 percent of the population a year can take permanent residence abroad (DevPolicy analysis, July 2026); the IMF names rising emigration among the forces moderating Tuvalu's growth (IMF Article IV, September 2025); and among the first to arrive in Australia was one of Tuvalu's few dentists — the skills drain the cap was designed to slow, visible in the first cohort. Those who leave are counted, with dignity, as the FOR shelf records; those who remain inherit a thinner service base, and no documented government measure addressing that specific loss appears in the record assembled here.

Why this grade: Within the seat's reach, the counting is unusually deliberate — island-equal representation honoured in a six-constituency cabinet, a mobility pathway with the disability and age exclusions deliberately removed, a woman placed at the head of state's office for the first time in the country's history, and a cable that connects all nine islands' people at half the previous mobile price. Against it stand the two counts he has not moved: zero women elected and zero in cabinet, in a parliament whose electoral review he controls the timetable of; and an adaptation programme whose built protection, so far, covers the capital island while the outer islands hold the plan. Deliberate counting and an unbroken zero on the same page: MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — people with disabilities and older Tuvaluans, admitted to the Falepili pathway on the same terms as everyone else; the first 27 documented arrivals and the cohorts following them; women in public life, by the Hauma appointment; every island's internet users, by the Vaka Cable's price cuts. Hurt — women as a class, whose elected and cabinet representation stands at zero for the whole tenure; Nui and Nukulaelae, unrepresented in cabinet; the patients and communities at home who lose scarce skilled workers — a dentist in the first cohort — to the pathway that helps the leavers.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 26 February 2024, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Taiwan — the word given on day two, kept and built on. The government's statement of 28 February 2024 "reaffirm[ed] its commitment to the long-term and lasting special relationship between Tuvalu and the Republic of China, Taiwan," against open pre-election speculation that the new government might switch recognition to Beijing; Teo's first interview grounded it: "Our ties with Taiwan are purely based on democratic principles, and they have been very loyal to us" (government statement, 28 February 2024; Teo interview, February 2024). The word was then carried past reaffirmation into law: after President Lai Ching-te's state visit to Tuvalu in December 2024, at which the two leaders pledged to strengthen the partnership through an expert team, Teo and Lai witnessed the signing in Taipei on 18 November 2025 of the Taiwan–Tuvalu Kaitasi Treaty — the first treaty of its kind between the two, converting political commitments into legal ones, with Taiwan recognising Tuvalu's perpetual statehood and maritime boundaries regardless of climate impacts — alongside a fisheries cooperation agreement and a sports exchange letter of intent (Taiwan Presidential Office and MOFA records, 18–20 November 2025). At COP31 on 12 November 2026 Teo was still spending the word in public, commending Taiwan's climate contribution and insisting no willing partner be left behind (COP31 national statement, 12 November 2026). On the Test's two axes this is crossing 1, named plainly: the word was kept, and it helped — Tuvalu gained a treaty partner for its statehood-continuity campaign and continued development support.
  • The Falepili process word — kept in the form he actually gave it. Teo never promised to repeal or to revise the treaty; he promised to explore arrangements that "stop short of revising the treaty" while guaranteeing Tuvalu's sovereignty, and on 9 May 2024 the explanatory memorandum and joint statement delivered exactly that form (Test 3). The treaty he confirmed Tuvalu desired to proceed with — Australia's Pacific minister Pat Conroy told the Australian parliament on 26 March 2024 that "the new government of Tuvalu has confirmed its desire to proceed with the Falepili Union" — entered into force on 28 August 2024 and has been implemented on the published timetable since: visa regulations 1 May 2025, first ballot 2025, first arrivals December 2025, joint committee established, development partnership plan to 2030 (DFAT records, 2024–2026). This is also crossing 2, and it is named as such rather than averaged away: the word to proceed was kept, and the keeping carries the documented hurt on the other axis — the Article 4(4) mutual-agreement clause stands unamended in the treaty text, and the opposition's sovereignty objection to it stands unanswered in law, whatever the memorandum records in interpretation (Tests 1 and 3).
  • Climate as the stated top priority — the record matches the ranking. In March 2024 Teo named climate change his government's top priority. The tenure's centre of gravity stayed there: TCAP land-raising continued on Funafuti — the reclaimed-area figures conflict and are printed separately, never blended: 15.3 hectares reclaimed with a further 10 hectares being reclaimed (Teo, UN General Assembly, 25 September 2026, via UN News); TCAP has reclaimed 20.8 hectares (PINA, 16 November 2026) — the Vaka Cable was activated on 24 October 2025 as the digital nation's physical backbone, and the campaign Teo fronted reached its stated legal goal when UN member states endorsed the Political Declaration on Sea-Level Rise on 24 September 2026, affirming continuity of statehood and permanence of maritime zones; Teo, who had been invited to open the high-level meeting, called it "a monumental and gratifying achievement" and stated its meaning plainly: "Tuvalu's nationhood is not negotiable, that our maritime rights are not erasable" (UNGA address, 25 September 2026; PINA, 2 October 2026). The declaration provides no physical protection — Teo said that too, in his independence address — and the shelf records the advocacy delivered, not the sea stopped.
  • Electoral reform — promised as priority four, launched as promised, unfinished. The twenty-one priorities listed electoral reform fourth; on 8 October 2025 the government launched the full review with named consultants and nationwide consultations, aiming to pass amended laws well before the 2028 election (PINA, 8 October 2025). A promise in motion on its own stated timetable — graded as such, neither kept nor broken yet.
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: Natano's completed tenure carried the two commitments this tenure inherited. His government delivered the Constitution of Tuvalu Act 2023, in force 1 October 2023, declaring Tuvalu's statehood perpetual "notwithstanding the impacts of climate change or other causes resulting in loss to the physical territory of Tuvalu" — the legal foundation every Teo climate claim stands on. And his signature on the Falepili Union on 9 November 2023 was given, on his own account, as essential to preserving Tuvalu's identity — a word whose keeping fell to his successor, at the price recorded under crossing 2 above. The seat Teo holds was shaped by a predecessor who kept his two largest words and lost his seat anyway, in part over the second of them.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The consultation word — the clearest unkept item. The 28 February 2024 statement acknowledged the Falepili treaty's "absence of transparency and consultations" and vowed to address the "process issues." Two years and nine months on, no Tuvaluan public consultation on the treaty — the largest instrument of the tenure — appears in the documented record; the remedies on record are intergovernmental (memorandum, joint committee) and Australian (parliamentary scrutiny in Canberra). On the two axes this is crossing 4 in a limited, precisely bounded form: a process promise not kept, and the hurt is the one his own statement identified — a public that learned of its mobility and security settlement after it was made. No crossing-3 case (a word broken that helped) is documented in the record assembled here, and none is invented to complete the set.
  • The fiscal word is spoken in two voices, and the figures conflict on the page. The 2026–2027 budget book projects total revenue of $143.9 million and total expenditure of $155.8 million, an overall fiscal deficit of $11.9 million (2026–2027 National Budget, presented 22 June 2026); the budget speech delivered the same day projects the same revenue against expenditure of $154.4 million and a deficit of $10.5 million (Budget Speech 2026–2027, 22 June 2026). Both figures are printed here separately, as sourced, and not blended. A government whose word on its own deficit arrives in two versions in its own documents has a receipts problem on this line, whichever version is right. The 2025–2026 budget, by contrast, was presented as balanced at $114.1 million revenue and expenditure (2025–2026 National Budget, 23 June 2025).
  • The trajectory under the words is adverse, on the IMF's published baseline. The Fund's 2025 Article IV (board conclusion 3 September 2025) records the fiscal balance improving from a deficit of about 7 percent of GDP in 2024 to a projected surplus in 2025 on higher grants, then deteriorating to a deficit of 6.8 percent of GDP by 2030, with sovereign-fund withdrawals insufficient to close the gap and Tuvalu assessed at high risk of debt distress — this in a state whose gross public debt is only about 7.5 percent of GDP and whose Trust Fund stands near 2.9 times GDP, the distress rating being driven by climate and revenue volatility rather than by borrowing. The Fund also records the revenue structure the words must survive: fishing licence fees at 41.6 percent of GDP in 2025 on its tables, grants volatile, growth moderating below 2 percent over the medium term on sluggish productivity and rising emigration. None of this breaks a specific Teo promise; all of it is the documented distance between "Sustaining our Economy, Securing our Future" (the 2026–2027 budget's own theme) and the baseline his finance ministry is steering against.
  • The outer-island services word — stated, not yet documented as delivered. Improving medical and educational services to the outer islands was named a development challenge in his first month (March 2024). The record assembled here documents the intention, the budget-guide visits, and the connectivity delivery, but no completed outer-island medical or education programme with dates and outcomes attributable to this government. Recorded as an absence, not an act: the word stands open, and the file will grade it when the receipts exist.

Why this grade: His two largest foreign words were kept in their exact form and on the record — Taiwan reaffirmed on day two and carried into a signed treaty in November 2025, and the Falepili sovereignty exploration delivered, in May 2024, precisely the sub-revision instrument he had described. His climate priority was not a slogan: the tenure's effort, travel, and legislative diplomacy all ran through it, to a UN declaration in September 2026. Against that, the one promise he made to his own public about process — to fix a treaty's shut-out consultation — has no documented fulfilment, and his government's own budget states its deficit two ways on the same day. A word kept abroad in full and unkept at home in the one place he named it himself is MIXED, with the crossings named above rather than averaged away: kept-and-helped (Taiwan; the statehood campaign), kept-and-hurt (proceeding with Falepili under its unamended clause), broken-and-hurt (the consultation promise, in its bounded form), and broken-and-helped — not documented.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Tuvaluans seeking a durable future either at home or in Australia, who hold a statehood declaration, a mobility pathway in operation, and a second treaty partner's legal recognition of perpetual statehood; the state's credibility with partners, who received the government Australia and Taiwan were told to expect. Hurt — the Tuvaluan public on the consultation promise specifically; future Tuvaluan budgets, which inherit the IMF's documented trajectory toward a 6.8-percent-of-GDP deficit by 2030 on current settings.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: HOLDS · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Feleti Teo came to the premiership by the quietest door in this series — a first-term MP, a former attorney general and fisheries official, elected unopposed by fifteen colleagues a month after an election that had removed the incumbent — and the dossier's central finding is that the tenure's strength and its limits sit in the same place: he is a careful lawyer-administrator governing a state of 10,643 people whose land is disappearing, and the record is strongest exactly where law and process are the work. One law is MIXED because the visible domestic processes run evenly — an election that removed his own predecessor, a random ballot with the usual exclusions deliberately stripped out, budgets published with a citizen's guide carried to the outer islands — while a tenth of the state's revenue flows under an unpublished .tv contract and the tenure's largest instrument was carried into force without the public consultation his government admitted it was owed. Without gripping is MIXED because no referee is documented as punished or overruled — the electoral law was opened to consultation, the April 2026 emergency expired on its fourteenth day, the Governor-Generalship turned over on schedule to the country's first woman in the office — while the chief justice has sat as an acting appointee on short contracts administered under the prime minister's own office for two years. The wobble is HOLDS because the answering is the record's best shelf: faced with a treaty his predecessor signed, his critics vowed to repeal, and his own side wanted renegotiated, he named the clause, stated the condition, and took the interpretive door when it became available, defending the result in person; faced with a failing power grid, he declared a time-limited emergency and let it lapse with the fix announced in public. Counts everyone is MIXED because the counting is deliberate where he designed it — island-equal seats honoured in the cabinet, disability and age counted into the pathway rather than out of it, a woman at the head of state's office — and untouched where he did not: zero women elected, zero in cabinet, and built protection that so far covers Funafuti while the outer islands hold the plan. His word is MIXED because the words given abroad were kept to the letter — Taiwan reaffirmed and treaty-bound, the Falepili exploration delivered in its promised form, the climate priority carried to a UN declaration on statehood in September 2026 — while the word given at home about consulting his own people on that treaty stands unkept, and his budget states its own deficit two ways. The monarch and the Governor-General stand behind this record as the constitution writes them — the King as head of state acting through a Governor-General appointed on the prime minister's advice, who declares prime ministers elected, appoints ministers, and, on that same advice, declared the April 2026 emergency — graded here only where they bear on the tests, as the channel through which this prime minister's advice becomes the state's act. The no-good-choice at the tenure's heart is recorded in Test 3: a nation of 26 square kilometres and a highest point under five metres does not get a clean door, and the one he took — defend the land, legalise the leaving, and write the statehood down where the sea cannot reach it — is graded as the best possible choice available on the documented options. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the electoral review becomes law before 2028 and what it does about women's representation, whether a substantive chief justice is appointed, whether the .tv contract and its review are ever published, whether any Tuvaluan consultation on the Falepili Union ever takes place, and whether the outer-island services word acquires receipts.
