On the Air — CB & Walkie-Talkie

Two things live on this page, and they are not the same thing. There is a real voice link that connects you to other people on this page through the internet, the way a walkie-talkie works — hold to talk, one voice at a time. And there is the real radio teaching: how actual CB and walkie-talkie radios reach each other through the air, which is the part that matters when networks go down. A practice radio sits between them so you can learn the manners of the air before you ever key a real microphone.

Part one

The Real Link — talk to people on your channel

This is a working push-to-talk voice link. You and your people open this page, agree on a channel code (any word you make up together, like elm-street), type the same code, and join. Whoever holds the talk button holds the floor — everybody else hears them, one voice at a time, like a real walkie-talkie. When a transmission ends, you hear a short roger beep.

The honest limits — read these at full size

This link rides the internet. It is not radio. Your voice travels through a connection service on the web, not through the air, so it is not limited by distance — it can reach the next street or the next country, and it works only while a network does.

In a true shutdown — no internet, no local network — no web page can connect anybody. Not this one, not anyone's. Anyone who tells you a web page works with no network at all is selling you something. What a page can honestly do for a shutdown is teach you real radio (Part Three below) and work for as long as any network lives.

Press and hold the button to talk; let go to finish. On a keyboard: tab to the button, then hold Space. Why one voice at a time? Real radios share one channel: two people transmitting at once just garble each other. The floor rule isn't politeness decoration — it's what makes a shared channel work at all. This link copies the rule on purpose, so the habit you build here is the habit a real radio needs.

Coach note: practise the real shape of a transmission even here — listen a moment, hold, say who you are and who you're calling, talk, say "over," let go. The people on the other end will thank you, and on a real radio that discipline is the difference between a channel that works and a wall of noise.

The battery drains fast while you transmit and slowly while you listen, like a real handheld. Run it dry and the session ends until you swap batteries — short transmissions are a real radio habit, and this is where it comes from.

Squelch — tap and slide

Squelch is a gate on the speaker. Signals weaker than the gate are silenced — along with the static. Raise it and weak, far stations drop out. Lower it and the static comes back, but so do the faint voices. Try it: slide it all the way up and watch the far stations below drop off the air; slide it down and listen to the static return.

These three settings change your range the way they change a real radio's range — the numbers come straight from the teaching in Part Three. Height does the most. Weather does the least. That ordering is itself the lesson.

Part three

Learn Real Radio — the genuine shutdown preparation

Everything below is about real radios — the kind that work with no network at all, because they talk through the air itself. This is the half of the page that still works in a true shutdown, and it's the half worth learning before you need it. Each shelf opens with a tap.

How range actually works — and why height beats power Tap to open

Radio waves at CB and walkie-talkie frequencies travel in roughly straight lines. The Earth curves. So the real limit on most handheld conversations is not the radio's strength — it's the horizon. Your signal runs straight until the ground falls away beneath it.

The horizon distance grows with height, and the maths is simple enough to carry in your head: horizon in miles ≈ 1.23 × the square root of your antenna height in feet.

Antenna heightYour horizonTalking to the same height
Held in your hand, ~6 ft~3 miles~6 miles on a perfect day
On a car roof, ~5–6 ft~3 miles~6 miles on a perfect day
Rooftop, ~30 ft~6.7 miles~13 miles on a perfect day
A hill or tower, ~100 ft~12 miles~24 miles on a perfect day

Those are perfect-day, nothing-in-the-way numbers. Real life is less: honest typical ranges for a handheld are roughly half a mile to 2 miles in woods or city streets, 1 to 3 miles in open country, and up to about 5 miles over flat open ground or water. Trees, buildings, and hills eat signal; a valley can cut you off from a station a mile away while a hilltop reaches twenty.

Why height beats power

Power spreads out as it travels — double the distance and the same energy is smeared over four times the area, so doubling your range by power alone takes roughly four times the wattage, and sixteen times for triple. Meanwhile a wall, a ridge, or the curve of the Earth blocks a strong signal just as completely as a weak one. Height removes the blockage itself. Ten extra feet of antenna regularly does more than ten extra watts — and it costs no battery. Get high first; add power second.

The honest exception: skip

CB frequencies can sometimes bounce off a charged layer of the upper atmosphere ("skip") and land hundreds of miles away. It's real, it's fun, and it is weather in the sky — it comes and goes by the hour and cannot be scheduled or relied on. Never plan an emergency around it.

The bands in plain words — CB, FRS, GMRS, ham Tap to open

A "band" is a neighbourhood of frequencies set aside for a kind of use, with its own rules. The four that matter for ordinary people:

The honest rider: these rules are set by the FCC and they change over time. What this page says is the shape of things as generally understood — check the current rules before you transmit, especially power limits and which channels allow what. Transmitting on a band you're not licensed for, or with power above its limit, is against the rules even in an emergency-minded spirit; genuine life-and-death emergencies are the recognised exception in the rules themselves.

Procedure — how to behave on the air Tap to open

Procedure is not etiquette for its own sake. A channel is one shared conversation space, and procedure is what keeps it usable when many strangers share it.

Listen first

Before you transmit, listen for a few seconds. Why: you cannot hear anyone while you are transmitting — a radio talks or listens, never both. Keying up blind means talking over somebody, and in an emergency that somebody might be the call that matters.

Identify yourself

Open with who you're calling and who you are: "Hilltop, this is Copper Fox, do you copy?" Why: on a voice-only channel, your callsign is your face. People decide whether to answer — and whether to trust what you say — by whether they can place you.

"Over"

Say over when you've finished a thought and expect a reply. Why: the other side can't see your face or your hand leaving the button. "Over" is the sound of the floor being handed over — without it, both sides sit in silence wondering whose turn it is, or talk at once.

"Roger" and "copy"

Roger means "I heard and understood." Why: on a crackly channel, the sender needs to know the message landed; a bare silence could mean anything, including a dead battery.

"Break"

Say break to join a conversation in progress with something urgent — and then wait to be invited in. Why: it asks for the floor instead of seizing it. Save it for things that genuinely can't wait; a channel where everyone breaks for trivia soon has no floor worth breaking into.

Close properly

End with your callsign and "clear" or "out": "Copper Fox clear." Why: it tells everyone listening that the channel is free again — and tells the station you were talking to that you're gone, not just quiet.

Keep it short

Say the thing, then stop. Why: your battery, their battery, and everybody else's wait all drain while you talk. The practice radio above meters this on purpose.

The phonetic alphabet — all 26, as a learnable shelf Tap to open

Common 10-codes — and the honest case for plain speech Tap to open

Ten-codes are shorthand from the early days of police radio, when transmissions were weak and every saved syllable helped. You'll still hear these on CB:

CodeMeans
10-1Receiving poorly — you're coming in weak or broken
10-2Receiving well — loud and clear
10-4Okay — message received and understood (the famous one)
10-7Out of service — signing off / unavailable
10-9Repeat — say that again
10-10Transmission complete, standing by
10-20Location — "what's your 20?" means "where are you?"
10-33Emergency traffic — clear the channel

The honest note: codes were never standardised everywhere — the same code can mean different things in different agencies and counties — so emergency professionals now recommend plain speech when it matters. "I need help at the Elm Street bridge" lands with everyone; "10-33" lands only with people who share your codebook. Use the codes for flavour on the highway if you like; use plain words when anything is actually at stake.

Squelch, channels, and interference — the three puzzles Tap to open

Squelch

An open radio with nobody talking hisses — that hiss is the sound of the receiver amplifying empty air. Squelch is a gate that keeps the speaker silent until a signal stronger than the gate arrives. Set it just above where the hiss stops and you have it right: quiet when the channel is empty, open when a real signal lands. Set it too high and weak, far voices get gated out with the hiss — that's exactly what the slider on the practice radio does to the far stations. Too low and you listen to static all day, which trains you to ignore the radio — the most dangerous setting of all.

Channels

A channel is one agreed frequency inside a band. Everyone on channel 19 hears everyone else on channel 19 — there is no privacy on analogue radio, and anyone with a receiver can listen. Why it matters: pick your channel like you pick a room — 19 for the highway and general calling, 9 for emergencies, a quieter channel for your own group's working talk once you've found each other.

Interference

Signals compete. A nearer or stronger station covers a weaker one ("capture"), power lines and cheap LED lights spray noise across whole bands, and two transmitters at once garble into each other. When a channel turns to mush: move (a few feet can do it), get higher, wait for a gap, or change channel — in roughly that order of effort. The one cure that never works is shouting louder into the same pile-up.

Buying & setting up — a basic real radio shelf Tap to open

Information only — nothing here is for sale and nothing on this page costs anything. Prices, where given, are rough general ranges as of late 2026, the kind of span you'd expect across ordinary stores; check current prices yourself before believing any of them.

The simplest start: an FRS pair

Two handhelds in a blister pack, batteries or a charging cradle, done. Roughly $25–$60 a pair in general. No licence, no setup — charge them, pick the same channel on both, talk. This is the right first radio for a family, a campsite, or two cars travelling together.

A basic CB setup

A mobile CB radio (roughly $50–$120 in general) plus the part that actually decides your range: the antenna (roughly $25–$60). A cheap radio on a good, well-mounted, properly tuned antenna will out-talk an expensive radio on a poor one — that sentence is the whole shelf in miniature, and it follows straight from the height-and-blockage lesson above. A handheld CB (roughly $80–$130) trades range for walk-anywhere convenience.

GMRS, when you're ready

Handhelds roughly $30–$80 each in general, plus the licence fee (a government fee, not a store price — check the current amount with the FCC). The draw is repeaters: if your area has one on a hill or tower, your few-mile radio becomes a whole-county radio.

Whatever you buy

Budget for batteries or a way to charge off a car, keep the manual (channel plans differ), and — the step everyone skips — practise with it on an ordinary day. A radio you have never keyed is not equipment; it's a hope in a box.
