The Ruler's Test — Pete Hegseth, Secretary of Defense of the United States

Subject: Pete Hegseth — United States Secretary of Defense since 25 January 2025. The seat graded is the secretaryship, and its limits are stated plainly at the start because they shape every test below: the Secretary of Defense is the President's principal assistant on defense and exercises authority, direction, and control over the Department of Defense, but he is not the Commander-in-Chief — that is the President (Donald J. Trump, in office since 20 January 2025) — and he cannot declare war, authorize force, or appropriate a dollar; Congress holds the war declaration, the authorization, and the purse. Hegseth, a former Army National Guard officer and Fox News host, was announced as the nominee on 12 November 2024, testified before the Senate Armed Services Committee on 14 January 2025, was advanced by that committee 14–13 on 20 January 2025, and was confirmed on 24 January 2025 by 51–50 — a 50–50 Senate split (50 Republicans for; all 45 Democrats, both independents, and Republicans Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, and Mitch McConnell against) broken by Vice President JD Vance, who swore him in the next day (Congress.gov, Congressional Record, 24 January 2025; Senate roll call vote 119-1, No. 15; Ballotpedia confirmation record, 2025). It was among the narrowest confirmations of a defense secretary in the office's history. He has held the seat continuously since — there has been no change of holder in 2025–2026 — and he is graded on roughly twenty-two months in office, through November 2026. Since Executive Order 14347 of 5 September 2025 he also uses the secondary title "Secretary of War" and the department the secondary name "Department of War"; the order itself states that statutory references to the Department of Defense and the Secretary of Defense "shall remain controlling until changed subsequently by the law," and Congress has not enacted that change (Executive Order 14347, 5 September 2025; CBO letter, 14 January 2026). The statutory seat graded here is therefore Secretary of Defense. The record of his tenure includes three wars or war-scale operations run under presidential direction — the Yemen strikes of March 2025, the Caribbean and Pacific boat-strike campaign from September 2025, the capture of Venezuela's president on 3 January 2026, and the war with Iran begun 28 February 2026 and still running in November 2026 — and, in November 2026, eight articles of impeachment introduced against him by Representative Thomas Massie (Republican, Kentucky), which the House voted 211–204 on 10 November 2026 to refer to committee, effectively ending the effort for that session (CNN, 10 November 2026; WOSU/NPR reporting, 10 November 2026). President Trump publicly defended him throughout. Where an act was the President's order executed by the secretary, this dossier says so; the secretary is graded on his own exercise of the office — what he ordered, signed, defended, dismantled, or refused to reconsider — not on the President's record as such. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test, printed verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one: carrying out that platform is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for HOW — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect the administration (Test 4). Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The standards program was, on its face, written as one rule for everyone. At Quantico on 30 September 2025, addressing more than 800 generals, admirals, and senior enlisted leaders summoned from commands worldwide, Hegseth announced that every member of the joint force at every rank would take a physical fitness test twice a year and meet height and weight requirements twice a year, that combat-arms positions would return to "the highest male standard," and that grooming rules — clean-shaven, no beards outside narrow waivers — would apply from the youngest private to a four-star general, himself included by his own framing: "If the Secretary of War can do regular, hard PT, so can every member of our joint force" (Hegseth Quantico address, transcript, Air & Space Forces Magazine, 30 September 2025). His confirmation pledge was the same shape: standards "high, and they will be equal (not equitable)," and every general or flag officer "selected for leadership based purely on performance, readiness, and merit" (Hegseth opening statement, Senate Armed Services Committee, 14 January 2025).
  • One documented act ran the rule against his own party in the room. On 11 November 2026 at Arlington National Cemetery, members of his own party — his wife, his chief of staff, and aides — were reprimanded by a Tomb Guard sentinel for laughing and talking after photographs at the Tomb of the Unknowns. Hegseth did not punish or shield around the sentinel; he brought the soldier, Specialist Iglesias of the 3rd Infantry Regiment, to the Pentagon and personally awarded him the Joint Service Commendation Medal for the execution of his duties (CNN, 12 November 2026). The rule the sentinel enforced against the secretary's own circle was honored, publicly.
  • The COVID-19 vaccine-mandate reversal was applied as a general restoration, not a selective one: troops separated solely for refusing the since-rescinded 2021 mandate were invited back as a class, with adverse actions taken solely for refusal to be removed (Department of Defense, "First 100 Days," 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The removal and promotion record does not distribute evenly, on the documented counts. By September 2026 CBS News had counted at least 20 generals, admirals, and civilian defense leaders fired or pushed out since Hegseth took office (CBS News count via Take Care Ledger, updated 22 November 2026). The first night of removals, 21 February 2025, took six senior officers at once, including General CQ Brown Jr., Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff — only the second Black officer to hold that post — sixteen months into a four-year term, and Admiral Lisa Franchetti, Chief of Naval Operations and the first woman to sit on the Joint Chiefs, relieved the same day without a stated performance reason; the services' top uniformed lawyers — the Army, Navy, and Air Force judge advocates general — were also removed in the early purge (contemporaneous reporting, February 2025; Take Care Ledger, 2026). Hegseth had written and said before taking office that any general or admiral involved in diversity, equity, and inclusion efforts needed to go — a criterion of association, not of individual performance (pre-nomination statements, cited in 2025–2026 reporting).
  • Promotion boards' results were overridden from the secretary's office in a pattern his critics document and the department disputes. Reporting sourced to current and former officials states that Hegseth personally struck officers from Army and Navy promotion lists after boards had selected them, that at least eight Navy captains were removed from consideration for rear admiral, and that the Navy one-star list released 22 May 2026 contained 22 names and no women, with no woman promoted from captain to rear admiral since June 2025 although women are roughly 21% of active-duty sailors (Task & Purpose reporting, cited November 2026; NPR reporting, March 2026, and AP reporting, April 2026, as compiled in November 2026 coverage). An advocacy-compiled figure in the impeachment-era record states that nearly 60% of the senior officers fired or sidelined were women or Black officers, although they make up less than 20% of the general and flag ranks (Democrats Abroad VMF Caucus resolution, June 2026 — an interested party's compilation, recorded here as its claim, with the underlying firings themselves independently documented as above). The department's position is that every removal and list change was merit-based; that dispute is named, and the outcome distribution — including, by late 2026, no active-duty four-star women — is a documented fact whichever motive is credited.
  • One class of service members was removed by category, not by individual standard. The Hegseth policy of 26 February 2025 generally disqualifies from service anyone with gender dysphoria, a history of it, or a history of related interventions — including, as the D.C. Circuit later put it, anyone who has ever "attempted to" transition, including by changing clothing or pronouns — regardless of whether the individual member met every fitness and performance standard of their job (Hegseth policy, February 2025; D.C. Circuit, Talbott v. United States, June 2026, and Judge Wilkins's statement, 17 November 2026). A rule that removes people who pass the stated standard, because of who they are, is a second law in the Test's sense, whatever its policy rationale.
  • Retaliation-shaped acts against named critics sit on this shelf as well. In January 2025 Hegseth revoked the security clearance and protective detail of retired General Mark Milley, the former Chairman and a public critic of the President, and ordered an inspector-general inquiry into Milley's tenure (Department of Defense, January 2025). On 5 January 2026 he issued a formal letter of censure against Senator Mark Kelly — a retired Navy captain and a sitting senator exercising oversight — and opened proceedings to reduce Kelly's retired grade and pay, after Kelly appeared in a video reminding service members of the existing legal duty to refuse unlawful orders (Pentagon announcement, 5 January 2026; see Test 2 for the court's answer). No comparable censure of an allied lawmaker or officer for public speech appears in the record assembled here.

Why this grade: In plain words: the written standards really were written for everyone, and at least once — the Arlington sentinel — Hegseth honored a rule enforced against his own circle. But the office's discretionary acts, which are where one-law is actually tested, ran in one direction: the officers removed first and without stated cause were disproportionately the historic "firsts" he had criticized before taking office, promotion-board results were set aside from his office with a documented skew in who disappeared from the lists, one category of member who met the standards was expelled by identity, and the two most prominent individual punishments of his tenure fell on two named critics of the administration. A secretary does not control who the President fires; he does control his promotion lists, his policies, and his censures. On those, the law was not one.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: Service members separated solely under the COVID vaccine mandate, restored as a class; Specialist Iglesias, honored rather than overruled; recruits and serving members who wanted higher, uniform fitness and grooming standards and got them in writing.
  • Hurt: The transgender service members removed by category, including the 27–28 plaintiff members whose cases reached the courts (the plaintiffs' brief says 28; the Associated Press figure is 27 — both figures printed as sourced, not blended); the senior officers removed without stated performance cause, among them General Brown and Admiral Franchetti; the Navy captains struck from promotion lists, including, on the reporting cited, an officer whose disqualifying history was service as a diversity liaison nearly two decades earlier; Senator Kelly and General Milley, each punished in his retired status after criticizing the administration.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

For this seat the referees are: Congress (authorization, appropriations, oversight), the federal courts, the Department of Defense Inspector General, the services' judge advocates general — the military's own independent lawyers — and the press that covers the Pentagon.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The referees still exist and have ruled against him, in public, on the record. The DoD Inspector General's report on his Signal use was completed under Acting Inspector General Steven Stebbins, delivered to Congress on 2 December 2025, and publicly released days later with findings adverse to the secretary (see Test 5) — the watchdog investigated the secretary himself and published (DoD IG report, December 2025; NPR and CNN reporting, 3 December 2025). Hegseth testified repeatedly before congressional committees, including the 21 July 2026 Senate Appropriations hearing on the Iran supplemental, where senators pressed him on cost and on his contradictory statements about the war (Senate Appropriations Committee, 21 July 2026). The impeachment articles of November 2026 were processed through the House's ordinary rules and defeated by an ordinary vote, 211–204, not suppressed (House vote, 10 November 2026).
  • Where courts issued orders he disliked, the department's compliance, while grudging and litigated, is documented rather than defied outright: after the Supreme Court on 23 December 2025 refused to let the Chicago National Guard deployment proceed, the administration ended the Los Angeles, Portland, and Chicago efforts and withdrew those troops in January 2026 (Supreme Court, Trump v. Illinois, December 2025; see Test 3). No documented instance appears in this record of the department simply ignoring a final court order.

Evidence against:

  • The press referee was expelled from the building. In September 2025 the Pentagon required credentialed journalists to pledge not to obtain or use unauthorized material — including unclassified information not approved for release — on pain of losing credentials as a "security or safety risk." On 15 October 2025, 40 to 50 correspondents from nearly every major outlet — the Associated Press, The New York Times, and the broadcast networks including Fox News among them — turned in their badges and walked out together rather than sign; only One America News Network signed among major outlets (AP reporting, 15–16 October 2025; U.S. Press Freedom Tracker, 2025–2026). The New York Times sued Hegseth and the department in December 2025. On 20 March 2026 U.S. District Judge Paul Friedman ruled that the credential-revocation provisions violated the First and Fifth Amendments and ordered seven Times journalists' credentials restored; three days later the department announced journalists would be moved out of their Pentagon offices into a separate annex and escorted — and on 9 April 2026 the same judge ruled the revised policy "continues to flout" his order, calling the revisions "transparent attempts to negate the impact of this Court's Order" (U.S. Press Freedom Tracker, updates of 23 March and 9 April 2026). The department appealed. A press corps that must sign a loyalty-shaped pledge or leave, and a court order answered with a workaround the court itself named as evasion, is gripping in the Test's plain sense.
  • The military's own lawyers were removed at the start. The judge advocates general of the Army, Navy, and Air Force — the officers whose statutory job is independent legal advice inside the chain of command — were fired in the February 2025 purge, and the Massie articles and Senate critics charge that civilian-harm mitigation offices required by Congress were dismantled in the same period (February 2025 reporting; Massie articles of impeachment, November 2026 — the dismantling charge recorded as an article's ALLEGATION, with the JAG removals themselves documented fact). At Quantico, Hegseth announced an "overhaul" of the inspector-general process itself, describing it as "weaponized, putting complainers, ideologues and poor performers in the driver's seat," alongside changes shortening how long adverse information stays on personnel records (Quantico address, 30 September 2025). The DoD Inspector General he inherited, Robert Storch, had already been removed by the President in the January 2025 inspector-general dismissals — a presidential act, stated here as context, whose effect was that the secretary was investigated by an acting watchdog rather than a Senate-confirmed one.
  • Oversight from Congress was answered, in the Kelly case, with punishment of the overseer. Senator Kelly sits on the Senate Armed Services Committee. After the 18 November 2025 video — in which Kelly and five other lawmaker-veterans restated the existing duty to refuse unlawful orders — Hegseth censured him on 5 January 2026 and opened retired-grade proceedings that would cut his pension. Kelly sued; in February 2026 a federal judge preliminarily enjoined the Pentagon, finding Kelly likely to succeed on his First Amendment claim, and the department's appeal was still pending in November 2026 (Kelly litigation record, January–November 2026; Arizona Republic, 19 November 2026). Whatever the video's politics, using retired-pay proceedings against a sitting senator who oversees the department is the office gripping a referee.
  • Records — the substrate every referee works from — were not kept. The Inspector General found that Hegseth's Signal messages about the March 2025 Yemen strikes were sent from his personal phone, that he provided only a few of his messages to investigators, forcing reliance on The Atlantic's screenshots, and that communications were shared with unauthorized persons and not preserved as federal law requires (DoD IG report, December 2025, as reported by CNN and NPR, December 2025).

Why this grade: In plain words: the referees were not abolished — the IG published against him, judges ruled against him, Congress questioned him — and that is why this is a record of gripping rather than of capture completed. But on every referee within his own reach, the documented direction is the same: the press pledged or expelled and a court order worked around, the services' independent lawyers fired in the first month, the IG process publicly slated for overhaul by the man it had just investigated, a senator-overseer censured through his pension, and the secretary's own operational messages kept off the record systems the referees inspect. Independence was not ended; it was punished, narrowed, or routed around wherever the office could reach it.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: The department's leadership itself, which gained a friendlier in-building press corps (some 60 journalists, largely from smaller and conservative outlets, signed and were credentialed by 22 October 2025) and fewer internal legal and watchdog frictions; commanders who wanted adverse-information retention shortened.
  • Hurt: The expelled Pentagon press corps and the public that relied on its in-building reporting; the judge advocates general removed from posts designed to be independent; Senator Kelly, censured in his capacity as an overseer; Congress and the courts, whose oversight record was thinned by unpreserved Signal communications; future investigators of any secretary, who inherit the annex-and-escort precedent.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? Test 3 also grades whether, when another option became available — if another option becomes available — the ruler at least considered it openly. Considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • On the domestic deployments, the pushback was ultimately answered with withdrawal rather than defiance. After Judge Charles Breyer ruled on 2 September 2025 that the Los Angeles deployment violated the Posse Comitatus Act, after district courts blocked the Portland and Chicago deployments (Judge Karin Immergut found the President's determination for Portland "simply untethered to the facts"), and after the Supreme Court refused on 23 December 2025 to stay the Illinois block, the administration announced on 31 December 2025 that it was ending the Los Angeles, Portland, and Chicago efforts, and the troops were withdrawn in January 2026 (court record, September–December 2025; deployment histories, 2025–2026). Earlier, on 15 July 2025, Hegseth had already ordered 2,000 of the Los Angeles Guard troops withdrawn, citing reduced unrest — a partial adjustment made on stated operational grounds while litigation was still running (Pentagon announcement via Asian Journal reporting, 15 July 2025). The deployments governors consented to — Memphis, and Washington, D.C., where the President commands the Guard directly — continued, which is a distinction the record supports: where the legal ground held, the mission held; where courts removed it, the troops came home.
  • On Venezuela, the office moved from force to partnership in the open. After the 3 January 2026 raid (Tests 4–5), Hegseth traveled to Caracas on 18 November 2026 — the first such visit — met interim President Delcy Rodríguez, and signed an agreement reestablishing military ties, training, and intelligence sharing, saying Venezuela had gone "from our most aggressive regional threat to a compliant security partner" (CNN and Air & Space Forces Magazine, 18 November 2026; DoD news release, 20 November 2026). Whatever the raid's legality (contested; Test 4), the follow-on was diplomacy conducted publicly, not a doubled-down occupation.
  • On the Minab school strike (Test 4), the secretary's first public answer was acknowledgment of an investigation rather than a flat denial: on 4 March 2026 he stated the United States was "investigating" the strike (Hegseth statement, 4 March 2026, quoted in Senate correspondence, March 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Iran war is the tenure's central wobble, and it was answered by redefinition, not adjustment. The war began 28 February 2026 without congressional authorization. When the War Powers Resolution's 60-day clock ran out at the end of April 2026, Hegseth told senators on 30 April that the ceasefire the President ordered on 7 April meant "the 60-day clock pauses or stops," and the President's 1 May letter declared the hostilities that began 28 February "have terminated" — while a U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports, first imposed 13 April, was lifted 18 June and reimposed 14 July, and U.S. jets were still striking Iran, including Qeshm Island on 2 November 2026 (War Powers record, 2026; Silent Dossier war-powers compilation, November 2026). In June 2026 both chambers of Congress passed H. Con. Res. 86 directing the removal of U.S. forces from hostilities with Iran — the House 215–208 on 3 June, the Senate 50–48 on 23 June, the first time both chambers had done so under the Resolution — and the White House treated it as without significance and the forces stayed (congressional record, June 2026). A concurrent resolution does not bind as a statute does, and the administration's legal position is stated here fairly; but the Test grades the response to pushback, and the response to the strongest pushback Congress can send short of a funding cut was to declare the war already over while continuing to wage it. If another option becomes available — authorization sought from Congress, or withdrawal on the Resolution's terms — the record shows it was not considered openly; it was defined away.
  • The Signal affair followed the same shape: deny, then be corrected by the referee, then declare exoneration. Hegseth first said no classified information and no war plans had been shared; the Inspector General's December 2025 report found the information came from CENTCOM emails classified SECRET / not releasable to foreign nationals, that he disclosed "the quantity and strike times of manned U.S. aircraft over hostile territory over an unapproved, unsecure network approximately 2 to 4 hours before the execution of those strikes," and that using a personal phone this way "risks potential compromise of sensitive DoD information, which could cause harm to DoD personnel and mission objectives" — while also acknowledging his declassification authority and not finding a violation of law (DoD IG report, 2 December 2025). The Pentagon's public answer, through spokesman Sean Parnell, was that the report was a "TOTAL exoneration" — a characterization the report's own findings do not carry, as national-security archivists and legal experts noted at the time (ABC News via National Security Archive, 3 December 2025). The finding was not absorbed; it was relabeled.
  • The September 2025 boat strike drew shifting accounts rather than one settled one. After The Washington Post reported in late November 2025 that a second strike had killed two survivors of the first strike on 2 September 2025, Hegseth called the report "fabricated, inflammatory, and derogatory," then stated he had watched the first strike live but had left before the second and had not seen survivors, describing the sequence as the "fog of war," while defending Admiral Frank Bradley's decision to re-engage (Hegseth statements, November–December 2025; Military.com analysis, 8 December 2025). Lawmakers who viewed the unedited video split on the record: Representative Jim Himes described "two individuals in clear distress, without any means of locomotion, with a destroyed vessel, who were killed by the United States"; Senator Tom Cotton described two men "trying to flip a boat, loaded with drugs bound for the United States, back over so they could stay in the fight" (Reuters, 4 December 2025). The factual dispute is named and unresolved in public; what is documented is that the secretary's own account moved — from the report being fabricated, to his absence from the room, to defense of the decision — as the evidence arrived.
  • The press-policy court loss (Test 2) was answered the same way: within three days of Judge Friedman's 20 March 2026 order, the department announced the annex-and-escort revision that the judge then ruled, on 9 April 2026, was a transparent attempt to negate his order (U.S. Press Freedom Tracker, March–April 2026). And the voters' pushback — the November 2026 midterms, fought substantially over the Iran war and returning Democratic majorities in both chambers pledged to investigate the Pentagon — was answered by Hegseth with a "We Stand, We Fight" social-media campaign and a Pentagon press conference framing impeachment as persecution, not with any announced change of course (CNN, 6 and 10 November 2026). His former chief of staff Joe Kasper — his own first chief of staff — publicly called in November 2026 for his resignation, saying his "ability to be effective has been constrained" and criticizing his firings, his Iran war handling, and his relations with Congress; the Pentagon responded with a statement of support for the secretary (Military.com, 24 November 2026).

Why this grade: In plain words: when a court took the legal ground away entirely, as in Chicago, the troops came home — the office can adjust, and did. But on the three largest wobbles of the tenure — a war Congress voted to end, a watchdog report against his own conduct, and a court order for the press — the pattern is denial first, redefinition second ("the hostilities have terminated"; "TOTAL exoneration"; a revised policy the judge called evasion), and no documented open consideration of the other door in front of him. The no-good-choice clause was considered for the Iran clock: it does not apply. The options that actually existed in April–June 2026 — seek authorization, comply with the concurrent resolution, or state plainly that the administration rejected Congress's direction and why — each carried political cost but none carried the kind of harm on every side that the clause requires; and the constraint that did bind (a concurrent resolution's non-binding form) was a feature of the chosen route, not a trap he was placed in. The clause is therefore recorded as considered and not applied, not as a demerit in itself.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: Residents of Los Angeles, Portland, and Chicago, and the Guard members withdrawn from those missions in January 2026; Venezuela's interim government and the U.S. services that gained a training and intelligence partnership in November 2026; the administration's war policy, which continued without interruption.
  • Hurt: U.S. service members kept in a war past the statutory clock and past both chambers' direction — more than 20 U.S. service members had been killed in the Iran war by the impeachment record of autumn 2026 (figure as stated in the June–November 2026 accountability record; no fuller official toll appears in the record assembled here); Congress, whose June 2026 direction was treated as without significance; the expelled press corps, whose court win was worked around; Admiral Bradley, left carrying the public weight of the 2 September second strike while the secretary's account of his own role shifted.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the communities the policy falls on — service members of every kind, the civilians under the bombs, the cities under deployment, the allies and the adversaries' noncombatants — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The force itself was counted in material ways. All five active-duty services met or exceeded their fiscal 2025 recruiting goals — the Army about 62,050, the Navy about 44,096, the Air Force about 30,166, the Marine Corps and Space Force meeting theirs — the strongest department-wide result in about 15 years, with retention also above goal (DoD recruiting releases, 2025; Senate Armed Services testimony, 11 February 2026). The fiscal 2027 budget request of April 2026 proposed tiered pay raises weighted to the lowest ranks — 7% for E-5 and below, 6% for E-6 to O-3, 5% for O-4 and above — and Hegseth created a Barracks Task Force on 6 October 2025 to address barracks conditions (White House FY2027 budget release, April 2026; NGAUS summary, April 2026; Army testimony, February 2026). The raises are a request, not an enactment — Congress had enacted neither the budget nor the authorization by November 2026 — and are recorded as proposed, not delivered.
  • The COVID-separated were counted back in (Test 1), and the November 2026 Venezuela agreement counts a former adversary's military as a partner rather than a permanent enemy (CNN, 18 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Minab school strike is the heaviest single entry in this dossier. On 28 February 2026, the first day of the Iran war, a U.S. Tomahawk strike destroyed the Shajareh Tayyebeh Elementary School in Minab, Hormozgan province. The death toll is printed here as the separate sourced figures the record carries, never blended: 156 killed, including 120 schoolchildren, with 95 injured (United Nations, Amnesty International, Forensic Architecture, Bellingcat, and New York Times investigations, 2026, as compiled in the Minab record); at least 168 killed, mostly children (letter of 46 senators to the President, March 2026); at least 175 killed (Senators Heinrich and Luján's letter to Hegseth, March 2026, citing reporting at the time). Independent investigations concluded the strike was American; the Pentagon's preliminary internal findings, as reported by The New York Times, likewise determined the United States was likely responsible, the school having been on a U.S. target list and possibly mistaken for the adjacent naval facility from which it had been walled off since at least 2016; as of September 2026 the internal investigation had not concluded and neither government had taken formal responsibility (Minab investigation record, 2026; Senate Armed Services Committee request letter, 27 March 2026). Hegseth's public framing of the operation three days after the strike was that Operation Epic Fury would have "no stupid rules of engagement" (Hegseth statement, 3 March 2026, quoted in Senate correspondence). The Massie articles charge that civilian-harm mitigation offices were dismantled before the war — an ALLEGATION in an impeachment article, recorded as such; the strike itself, the tolls, and the absence of a concluded public accounting by grading day are documented.
  • The boat-strike campaign counted its targets without knowing who they were. From 2 September 2025 the military struck alleged drug boats in the Caribbean and Pacific; officials acknowledged not knowing the identities of everyone aboard before striking (CNN, 28 November 2025). The first strike killed 11 people, including — on the documented record of the follow-on strike — two survivors of the first hit, killed in the water beside a destroyed vessel (Tests 3 and 5). The DoD Law of War Manual forbids attacks on shipwrecked persons who abstain from hostilities (Law of War Manual, cited in Reuters, 4 December 2025). Whether the two men were attempting to recover cargo, as Senator Cotton stated, or were in clear distress, as Representative Himes stated, is contested and named as contested; that they were shipwrecked survivors of a U.S. strike, killed by a second U.S. strike, is the common ground of both accounts. Roughly 80 people had been killed across the campaign by late November 2025 (States Newsroom reporting, November 2025). No public accounting naming those killed, or establishing each boat's cargo and each person's affiliation beyond the administration's assertion, appears in the record assembled here; Hegseth's assertion was categorical: "Every trafficker we kill is affiliated with a Designated Terrorist Organization" (Hegseth social-media statement, November 2025).
  • The Venezuela raid's dead were counted by others. Operation Absolute Resolve, 3 January 2026, captured Nicolás Maduro and Cilia Flores without U.S. deaths — a precision the department documented in detail — while about 80 people were killed on the Venezuelan and Cuban side, mostly soldiers (reporting of the raid, January 2026; The Times, November 2026). The raid was executed under presidential direction to enforce a New York indictment; its legality under international law and the absence of congressional authorization are contested, with the Massie articles charging the seizure was without legal authority (Massie articles, November 2026 — ALLEGATION as to illegality; the operation itself is documented fact).
  • Inside the force, whole categories were counted out. Transgender members who met every standard were removed by category (Test 1). The Quantico grooming directives rescinded religious exemptions for facial hair — ending accommodations under which Sikh, Muslim, Jewish, and Norse pagan service members had served — and narrowed the medical shaving waivers used disproportionately by Black service members with pseudofolliculitis barbae; CAIR's objection on First Amendment grounds is on the record (Quantico directives, 30 September 2025; Government Executive and Defense News reporting, 30 September 2025). The combat standard set at "the highest male standard" was stated by Hegseth in terms of who should serve beside his son — "troops who are out of shape or in combat units with females who can't meet the same combat arms physical standards as men" — a standard women may meet individually, recorded here as a standard, with its disparate effect on women's combat service stated rather than assumed (Quantico address, 30 September 2025).
  • The domestic deployments fell on named communities. The Los Angeles deployment — eventually some 6,000 California Guard federalized over Governor Gavin Newsom's objection, plus 700 Marines — accompanied immigration operations, with troops present on about 75% of ICE operations in the city through mid-July 2025 (deployment record, 2025–2026). The Congressional Budget Office estimated the 2025 deployments cost $496 million from June through December 2025 (CBO estimate, January 2026). In Memphis, where the deployment continued with the governor's consent, Guard soldiers shot and killed a resident, Tyrin Johnson, in July 2026 (Take Care Ledger deployment record, updated November 2026 — recorded as the ledger's documented report; no fuller official account appears in the record assembled here). The D.C. deployment grew to nearly 5,000 troops by July 2026 and was extended, on the same record, to Inauguration Day 2029.

Why this grade: In plain words: the secretary counted the force's volunteers, its junior enlisted pay, and its barracks — real counting, recorded on the FOR shelf. But the Test asks whether the accounting includes everyone the power falls on, and on that question the record is one-sided: an elementary school full of children struck off a target list with no concluded public accounting nine months later; boat crews killed without their identities known, two of them after they were already shipwrecked; a category of serving members expelled for who they are; religious accommodations withdrawn from minorities of the force; and American city neighborhoods patrolled in an immigration mission a court found unlawful. The people with the least ability to answer back are the people the accounting most often skipped.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: New recruits and serving junior enlisted members (recruiting success, proposed weighted pay raises, barracks attention); troops restored after COVID-mandate separations; U.S. special operations and aircrews whose 3 January 2026 mission was planned to bring them all home, and did.
  • Hurt: The children and staff of Shajareh Tayyebeh Elementary School and their families in Minab — 156 killed on the UN/Amnesty-compiled figure, with the higher sourced figures of 168 and 175 printed above, unblended; the boat crews of the Caribbean and Pacific campaign, roughly 80 killed by late November 2025, including the two shipwrecked survivors of 2 September 2025; the roughly 80 Venezuelan and Cuban personnel killed in the 3 January 2026 raid; transgender, Sikh, Muslim, Jewish, and Norse pagan service members, and Black service members reliant on shaving waivers; residents of the deployed-to cities, including the family of Tyrin Johnson in Memphis; Iranian civilians under a continuing war and blockade, whose wider toll is asserted in the thousands in the accountability record but is not documented to a single sourced figure in the record assembled here and is therefore not printed as one.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Confirmation pledges and public statements vs. the record since 25 January 2025, with dates. Under the mandate rule, carrying out the platform the voters elected is FOR-shelf evidence here — the keeping is credited; its hurt, where documented, is recorded in the ledgers in full and graded on Tests 1 and 4, not deducted twice.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The warrior-ethos program — delivered as promised, at speed. His charge from the President, stated at his hearing on 14 January 2025, was to restore the warrior ethos, rebuild the military, and reestablish deterrence, with the department "laser focused on warfighting, lethality, meritocracy, standards, and readiness" (opening statement, 14 January 2025). The standards reviews, sex-neutral combat standards at the male level, twice-yearly fitness testing, grooming enforcement, the end of DEI programs and of the Women, Peace and Security program, the "Department of War" secondary branding (Executive Order 14347, 5 September 2025), and a reduction of general and flag officers — about 10% removed and billets cut by a further 10%, for roughly 20% overall, as he announced on 30 September 2025 — all happened, on the schedule he set (Quantico address and directives, 30 September 2025; DoD "First 100 Days," 2025). Under the mandate rule this is credited as word-keeping: it is the platform the elected administration ran on, executed.
  • Recruiting — the measurable promise, met. He promised to address "the recruiting, retention and readiness crisis." In fiscal 2025 all five services met or exceeded their goals for the first time in about 15 years (figures in Test 4), and the Army met its fiscal 2025 goal four months early (DoD releases, 2025; Senate testimony, February 2026). Attribution is shared — the rebound began with the November 2024 election, before he took office, as his own supporters' accounts note — but the promise was made and the numbers came in on his watch.
  • The transgender policy — kept, and recorded on both axes. The February 2025 policy disqualifying transgender service was the administration's stated platform, issued within a month of his swearing-in and defended through the courts since (Hegseth policy, 26 February 2025; Shilling stay, Supreme Court, 6 May 2025; Talbott, D.C. Circuit, June and November 2026). This is crossing 2 in the Test's terms: the word was kept AND it hurt people — the keeping earns its credit here; the hurt is recorded in full in Tests 1 and 4 and is not softened by the credit.
  • The audit — not yet due, and stated honestly as a target. He told Senator Ernst at his hearing he would prioritize ensuring the Pentagon can pass an audit. The department failed its eighth consecutive audit in December 2025 — 26 material weaknesses, on $4.65 trillion in assets — and restated the statutory target of a clean opinion by 2028, with Hegseth committing publicly to that date (DoD Agency Financial Report FY2025, December 2025; Reuters, 19 December 2025). A 2028 promise cannot be graded broken in 2026; it is recorded as open, with the trajectory — one weakness closed, one consolidated, the GAO assessing the 2028 target as unlikely (GAO assessment, April–September 2025) — stated against it.
  • Crossing 1 (kept his word AND it helped people): the recruiting and standards promises, kept, with the helped ledger of Test 4 — volunteers who joined a force that met its goals, junior members offered weighted raises. Crossing 3 (went against his word AND it helped people): the first version of the September 2025 press pledge required journalists not to release even unclassified material without approval; the revised version removed that approval-for-release provision (Iowa Journal of Gender, Race & Justice analysis, October 2025, comparing the versions). The softening departed from his own announced policy and marginally helped the press corps that remained — a small crossing, recorded at its size; the pledge regime itself, and what followed in court, stands on Test 2.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "No classified information was shared" — not sustained by his own watchdog. Through spring 2025 Hegseth denied that war plans or classified material had been put on Signal. The Inspector General's report of 2 December 2025 found the strike details came from CENTCOM emails classified SECRET / NOFORN, were sent 2–4 hours before the strikes to two Signal groups — one including a journalist added in error, one including his wife, his brother, and his personal lawyer — over an unapproved network from a personal phone, in violation of department policy on personal devices and records preservation, while acknowledging his declassification authority and making no finding of illegality (DoD IG report, December 2025). The Pentagon then described that report as a "TOTAL exoneration," which it was not on its own text. A secretary's word includes his characterizations of the record; both the original denial and the exoneration claim fail against the document.
  • The bipartisan-working pledge — overtaken by the record. He opened his confirmation statement pledging to work with the Armed Services Committee, "Senators from both parties," and told the committee "I pledge to do the same for you. All of you." (opening statement, 14 January 2025). The documented course since includes the censure of a Democratic member of that committee through his retired pay (Test 2), the expulsion of the press corps that covers the committee's work, and — by his own former chief of staff's public account in November 2026 — a failure to work well with Congress that Kasper warned could impair the department's funding (Military.com, 24 November 2026). His $1.5 trillion fiscal 2027 request — the largest in history, a 42–44% increase, split between roughly $1.15 trillion in discretionary funding and $350 billion through reconciliation — was enacted in neither part by November 2026; the NDAA passed the House in July and failed a Senate procedural vote, the government ran on a stopgap from 1 October 2026, and the incoming Democratic majorities announced plans to hold defense near $1 trillion (DoD FY2027 budget release, 21 April 2026; Military.com, 9 November 2026). A budget request is the President's, and its failure is Congress's act as much as his; what is graded here is the narrower pledge of a working relationship, and on that the record runs the other way. This is crossing 4 in part: the pledge receded AND the hurt is documented — in oversight friction and in a department entering fiscal 2027 on a stopgap amid a war.
  • The Iran statements — a word that moved with the week. Hegseth's characterizations of the war shifted across 2026: the 7 April ceasefire "pauses or stops" the statutory clock (30 April); hostilities "have terminated" (the President's 1 May letter, defended by the department) while the blockade was reimposed in July and strikes continued into November; cost figures given to Congress moved as well, with the department telling appropriators in May 2026 the war had cost about $29 billion while seeking first a reported $200 billion in March and then an $87.6 billion supplemental in July, $67.1 billion of it for DoD, while Senator Murray placed on the record that the department still held roughly $75 billion in unspent reconciliation funds (Reason, 16 July 2026; Senate Appropriations Committee, 21 July 2026 — the figures printed as each source states them, not reconciled). Senators of both parties pressed the contradictions on the record at the 21 July hearing. Crossing 4 (broke his word AND it hurt people) is recorded here in its clearest form in the Signal matter above: the assurance that nothing classified was shared did not hold, and the IG found the sharing itself risked harm to the personnel flying the mission.
  • The drinking pledge — not documented either way. At his confirmation he denied a drinking problem and pledged not to drink if confirmed. No documented breach of that pledge appears in the record assembled here, and none is alleged in this dossier; it is recorded as not documented, per the Test's rule, rather than scored.
  • The "kill everybody" order — ALLEGATION, unresolved. The Washington Post reported in November 2025 that Hegseth gave a verbal order to kill everyone aboard the 2 September 2025 boat; Hegseth denies issuing any such order, the President said he believed him, and Admiral Bradley's role is documented in Test 3. The allegation is printed as an allegation, with its denial, and no grade in this dossier rests on it.

Why this grade: In plain words: the programmatic word — the platform he was confirmed, 51–50, to execute — he kept, quickly and measurably: standards, DEI's removal, the transgender policy, the flag-officer cuts, the recruiting rebound. That is a real FOR shelf, and the mandate rule forbids grading the platform itself against him here. Against it stand the personal and institutional words: the Signal assurances, contradicted by his own inspector general and then misdescribed as exoneration; the pledge to work with both parties on the committee, answered with a censure of one of its members; and a running set of Iran characterizations that Congress documented contradicting themselves and the facts of a continuing blockade and continuing strikes. The two axes are printed independently, as the Test requires: his word-keeping is strongest exactly where the helped/hurt ledger is most contested (crossing 2), and weakest where he spoke about his own conduct (crossing 4). A secretary who keeps the program and not the account of himself lands at MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: The administration's voters and the force's volunteers, who got the program they were promised, on schedule; recruits and junior enlisted members (Test 4); the President, whose defense platform was executed without public daylight between the Pentagon and the White House at any point in twenty-two months.
  • Hurt: The transgender members removed under a kept promise — credited as kept here, hurt recorded there, both in full; Congress and the public, who received "TOTAL exoneration" for a report that found policy violations and risk to troops, and "terminated" hostilities in a war that continued; Senator Kelly; the department itself, which entered a war's second year on a stopgap budget with its authorization stalled.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Pete Hegseth holds an office with stated limits — he is not Commander-in-Chief, he cannot declare war or fund one — and the first finding of this dossier is that he never changed seats: confirmed 51–50 on 24 January 2025, in office continuously since 25 January 2025, defended by the President through an impeachment effort the House referred to committee, 211–204, on 10 November 2026. One law (FAILS): the written standards applied to everyone, and a Tomb sentinel who reprimanded his own party was decorated — but the discretionary record ran one way: the February 2025 purge took the first woman on the Joint Chiefs and the second Black Chairman without stated cause, promotion lists were overridden with a documented skew, one category of member meeting every standard was expelled by identity, and the tenure's two named individual punishments fell on critics, Milley and Kelly; hurt — the removed officers, the struck captains, the expelled transgender members. Without gripping (FAILS): the referees survive — the IG published against him, judges ruled against him — but the press was pledged or expelled and a court order worked around in a way the judge himself called evasion, the JAGs were fired in the first month, the IG process was slated for overhaul by its subject, and a senator-overseer was censured through his pension; hurt — the expelled press corps, the removed lawyers, Senator Kelly, and every future investigator working from unpreserved records. The wobble (FAILS): where courts removed the legal ground entirely, in Chicago, the troops came home, and Venezuela moved from raid to partnership in the open — but on the war, the watchdog, and the court order, the answer was denial, then redefinition: hostilities "terminated" during a blockade, a critical IG report renamed "TOTAL exoneration," a boat-strike account that shifted as evidence arrived; the no-good-choice clause was considered for the Iran clock and does not apply, because authorization, compliance, or a plain stated refusal were all real doors; hurt — the more than 20 U.S. service members killed in a war past its clock, and Congress, whose first-ever bicameral War Powers direction was treated as without significance. Counts everyone (FAILS): the force's volunteers, junior pay, and barracks were genuinely counted — and the accounting then skipped the people with the least voice: Minab's schoolchildren (156 killed on the UN/Amnesty-compiled figure; 168 and 175 on the senators' sourced figures, printed unblended, with no concluded public accounting by grading), roughly 80 boat-crew dead by late November 2025 including two shipwrecked survivors, roughly 80 Venezuelan and Cuban dead in the January raid, transgender and religious-minority members of the force, and the patrolled neighborhoods of Los Angeles and Memphis. His word (MIXED): the programmatic word was kept — standards, removals, the transgender policy, recruiting goals met for the first time in fifteen years — and the Test's mandate rule credits the keeping while the ledgers carry its hurt in full (crossing 2); the personal word was not kept — the Signal assurances against his own IG's findings (crossing 4), the bipartisan pledge against the Kelly censure — with crossing 1 in the recruiting record, crossing 3 in the small softening of the press pledge, the audit promise open to its 2028 date, and the drinking pledge not documented either way. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: the concluded Minab investigation and whether its findings are published; the Supreme Court's disposition of the transgender policy in Talbott; the incoming Congress's investigations from January 2027; whether the Iran war gains an authorization, an end, or a funding reckoning; and whether the 2028 audit target — the one promise whose date has not yet arrived — is met.
