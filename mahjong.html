How to play

The one rule that runs everything

A tile is free when two things are both true:

1. Nothing sits on top of it — no other tile overlaps it from a higher layer.
2. At least one long side is open — the left edge or the right edge has no neighbour at the same height. Blocked on both long sides means not free, even with nothing on top.

Tap a free tile to select it. Tap a second free tile with the same face to remove both. Flowers all match each other (any flower with any flower), and seasons all match each other — that is part of the documented tile set. Everything else must match exactly.

Removing tiles frees the tiles underneath and beside them. Clear every tile to win. If tiles remain but no two free tiles match, the board is dead — the page declares it, and you can undo, shuffle (a variant), or end the board and have it recorded honestly as a dead board.

The tiles

Faces follow the documented suits, drawn here in our own hand as original artwork: Dots 1–9, Bamboo 1–9, Characters 1–9, the four Winds (East, South, West, North), the three Dragons (red, green, white), four Flowers, and four Seasons. Each tile also carries a small printed code in its corner (like B4 for Bamboo 4) so faces can be told apart by more than colour alone.

Gestures, in plain words

Tap a tile to select or match it. Tap a selected tile again to put it back. Every button on this page says its tap on its face. There are no hidden gestures.

The deal

Faces are shuffled onto the layout at random, so a deal is not promised solvable — that is true of the documented game too. The honest tools for a stuck board are undo, a fresh deal, or the labelled shuffle variant.

Strategies — more than one way, each with its trade-off

The Coach uses these on your live board. None of them is always right; the trade-offs are the lesson.

1. Free the most-buried tiles first

Prefer the pair whose removal uncovers the deepest, most-covered tiles. Buried tiles hide the most unknown faces, and every tile you can see is information.

Trade-off: the pair that frees the most is not always available — chasing it can mean passing up a pair that opens a long row right now.

2. Open long rows before short stacks

A tile in a long row is blocked by neighbours on both sides; only end tiles are free. Working a long row from both ends opens many tiles in sequence.

Trade-off: rows open slowly, one end at a time. A short stack can collapse in a single pair and drop a whole layer — sometimes the stack is the faster door.

3. Watch the three-of-a-kind trap

When three copies of a face are free and the fourth is buried, which two you take decides what gets freed. Take the pair that uncovers the most — taking the wrong pair can leave the fourth copy buried behind the very tile you left standing.

Trade-off: there is no undoing a careless pick except the undo help, and the undo is counted in your record.

4. Keep a pair of last resort

A free pair that uncovers nothing new will still be there later. Leaving it untouched keeps one guaranteed move in hand while you spend your other moves opening the board.

Trade-off: a saved pair can also be a blocker — if one of its tiles is sitting on something you need, saving it saves the problem too. Check what each tile covers before you save it.

5. Spend flowers and seasons carefully

Any flower matches any flower, so flower and season tiles are flexible keys — they can rescue a board that is one match short.

Trade-off: that same flexibility makes them easy to waste early on a pair that opened nothing, exactly when a stricter pair would have done the same job.

About this game

Mahjong solitaire is the documented tile-matching solitaire: the first computer version was written in 1981 for the PLATO system, and the form spread from there. This page is our own version of that documented form — the layouts are arranged by us (the Turtle is our own arrangement of the classic turtle shape; the Bridge and the Fortress Steps are originals), and every tile face is drawn by us as original SVG artwork in the documented suits. No commercial game's tile art, layout files, or text are used.

The rules are settings, not stone: helps can be turned down or off, the timer can be switched off, and the one true variant — the shuffle — is labelled as a variant on the board and in the record whenever it is used.
