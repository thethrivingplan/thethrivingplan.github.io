Cribbage

The classic card game, played in full: six cards dealt, two to the crib, the cut, pegging during the play, and the show. First to 121 — skunks honoured. This is the proven, documented game: cribbage as recorded since the 1600s (the invention is credited to the poet Sir John Suckling) and kept today in the standard rules of the American Cribbage Congress. Nothing here is an invented variant dressed as the classic — where a rule can change, it sits in the settings, labelled.

It is really two games in one: the play, where cards peg points as they land, and the show, where hands are counted. New players drown in the counting — so the teaching layer counts out loud, with its reasons, as much or as little as you want.

Documented strategies — tap to open. The proven plays, with where they come from

Cribbage strategy is a documented field — tournament players of the American Cribbage Congress have published and tested these plays for decades (a standard text is Play Winning Cribbage by DeLynn Colvert, a champion of the game). The coach uses them, and names its trade-offs:

Keep your 5s — and don't hand them over

Every 10-value card (10, J, Q, K — sixteen of them) pairs with a 5 to make fifteen. A kept 5 is the most likely card in the deck to gain points from the cut; a 5 thrown to their crib is the most generous gift in the game. Trade-off: sometimes breaking a pair of 5s is still right when the other keep is a sure big hand — the coach shows both numbers when it happens.

Lead from a pair

When you lead a card in the play and hold its mate, if your opponent pairs your lead you pair right back on top — pair royal, six points. Trade-off: leading from a pair spends the pair's show value if the hand were re-counted — it isn't; the play is its own game, which is why the lead is usually worth it.

Never lead a lone 5

Lead a 5 and any 10-value card makes fifteen against you. It is the most punished lead in the documented game. The one honest exception: when the 5 is paired or the count race is nearly over and the points race matters more — the coach says so when it applies.

Play for the go, guard the 21

Counts of 21 are dangerous: your opponent holding mid cards can run to 31. Strong players track what the count lets the other side do, not just what it gives them — the Pro bot does exactly this, and the coach shows its arithmetic on your turns.

The 29 hand

Three 5s and a jack in hand, the fourth 5 cut, jack matching the cut's suit: 29 — the highest hand in cribbage, documented odds of about 1 in 216,580. It is in this game's engine and test set, like every other score on this page.

How the bot levels were checked — tap to open

The four levels are four different brains, not one brain with a handicap: Learning throws and plays at random, like a first-week player. Steady keeps obvious points, keeps its 5s, and takes any points the play offers. Sharp values every discard over all 46 possible cuts and prices the crib. Pro does all of that with a deeper crib model and plays the pegging two moves deep — its points now, minus your best expected reply, plus the value of its own follow-up, sampled over the cards it cannot see.

How the counting works — tap to open

The deal and the crib

Each player is dealt six cards and throws two into the crib — a second hand that belongs to the dealer. The non-dealer (the pone) cuts, and the top card of the remaining deck is turned up as the cut (starter). If the cut is a jack, the dealer pegs 2 at once — his heels. The deal alternates every hand.

The play (pegging)

Players alternate laying cards, keeping a running count that may not pass 31. Points peg as they happen: making the count exactly 15 scores 2; a pair 2, three of a kind 6, four of a kind 12; a run of three or more scores its length (cards need not land in order, but a run already scored doesn't score again shorter); making exactly 31 scores 2. If you cannot play without passing 31 you say go; when neither side can play, the last to play scores 1 for the go — and the last card of the whole play scores 1. The count then resets and the other player leads what remains.

The show

Hands are counted in order — pone's hand first, then the dealer's hand, then the crib (order matters: a game can be won in the show before the crib is ever counted). Each hand counts with the cut as a fifth card: every combination making 15 scores 2; pairs 2 / 6 / 12; runs score their length (a doubled card in a run counts the run once per combination, plus the pair); a flush scores 4 in hand, 5 if the cut matches — in the crib, only all five matching scores, for 5; a jack of the cut's suit in hand scores 1 — his nobs.

Winning and skunks

First to the target — 121 standard, 61 in the short game — wins the moment the peg lands, even mid-play. In a 121 game, a loser still short of 91 is skunked; short of 61, double-skunked. In a 61 game the skunk line is 31. The peg board shows both lines.

Muggins

The classic claim-it-or-lose-it rule, off by default here. With muggins on, you announce your own show count; anything in your hand you failed to claim is pegged by your opponent instead. It applies to the show counts on this page, and the page says so where you turn it on.
