The Ruler's Test — John Mahama, President of Ghana

Subject: John Dramani Mahama — President of Ghana since 7 January 2025. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: Ghana's 1992 Constitution vests the executive authority of the state in the president, who is head of state, head of government and commander-in-chief. Mahama, born 29 November 1958, is in his second, non-consecutive presidency — he previously held the office from 24 July 2012, succeeding on the death of President John Atta Mills, until 7 January 2017 — and he is the first Ghanaian president democratically elected to a non-consecutive second term. He won the 7 December 2024 election with 56.55% of the valid votes against the incumbent vice-president, Mahamudu Bawumia, who conceded the morning after the election; his National Democratic Congress (NDC) won 183 of 276 parliamentary seats, a two-thirds majority it has since added to in by-elections — which means the checks on this seat are mostly institutional rather than parliamentary. The constitution bars him from any further term: he must leave office on 7 January 2029, and no initiative to alter that exists at grading. He is graded on the current tenure, January 2025 to November 2026 — roughly twenty-two months — with his first presidency admitted as context where it bears directly on a test: the 2016 defeat that followed the dumsor power crisis, the Airbus investigation that hung over his opposition years, and his own earlier public positions, which his opponents now quote back at him. His predecessor Nana Akufo-Addo (2017–2025) appears as context where the record Mahama inherited — a defaulted public debt, an IMF programme that had veered off track, and the former officials his anti-corruption drive now prosecutes — bears directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption drive he launched on taking office — Operation Recover All Loot (ORAL), whose preparatory committee reported in February 2025 that more than US$20 billion in allegedly looted funds might be recoverable — has been run through courts and independent offices rather than by decree, and it has reached verdicts. The drive's most prominent conviction is of a senior figure of the opposing party: Bernard Antwi Boasiako ("Chairman Wontumi"), the NPP's Ashanti Regional Chairman, was arrested by the Economic and Organised Crime Office (EOCO) in May 2025, tried from October 2025, and on 20 July 2026 convicted by the High Court (Justice Audrey Kocuvie-Tay) of assigning mineral rights without ministerial approval and purposely facilitating an unlicensed mining operation on his company Akonta Mining's Samreboi concession; he was sentenced to 20 years' imprisonment with hard labour and a GH¢120,000 fine, with his appeal and a bail application pending at grading (certified judgment reviewed by DUBAWA, July 2026; Ghanaian Times, 21 July 2026). The NPP rejects the conviction as politically motivated — its characterization, recorded here as the party's position, not a finding.
  • The same courts have not simply ratified the prosecution's cases. In the trial of former National Signals Bureau director-general Kwabena Adu-Boahene — charged with stealing GH¢49.1 million in a purported cybersecurity procurement, charges he denies and which remain ALLEGATIONs — the prosecution closed its case in September 2026, and on 12 November 2026 the High Court acquitted him on two counts while ordering him to open his defence on nine others (Ghanaian Chronicle, 13 November 2026). Other predecessor-era cases are in open process: former National Service Authority director-general Osei Assibey Antwi and his deputy Gifty Oware-Mensah were charged in October 2025 in the "ghost names" payroll case (charges are ALLEGATIONs, denied, untried at grading), and Wontumi separately pleaded not guilty in October 2026 to amended charges of causing GH¢30 million in financial loss through a Ghana EXIM Bank loan (Daily Guide reporting, October 2026).
  • The highest-value target is being pursued by an office the president does not control. Former finance minister Ken Ofori-Atta was declared a fugitive by the Office of the Special Prosecutor (OSP) on 12 February 2025 after failing to appear for questioning; an INTERPOL Red Notice followed in June 2025; the OSP — headed by Kissi Agyebeng, an appointee of the previous administration — has since charged him in the Strategic Mobilisation Limited revenue-assurance case; he was detained by US Immigration and Customs Enforcement in January 2026 over his immigration status, and extradition remained contested and unresolved at grading. He denies all charges, sued over the fugitive declaration, and says he was abroad for medical treatment; every charge against him is an ALLEGATION (Financial Times, 9 June 2025; BBC, February 2025; GhanaWeb, January 2026).
  • The law has been applied inside his own camp, at the level the seat can reach directly. His Code of Conduct for political appointees, launched 5 May 2025, was enforced against his own ministers: more than 40 appointees who missed the 31 March 2025 asset-declaration deadline forfeited four months' salary, under a published rule that further non-compliance meant automatic dismissal (Presidency, May 2025; press reporting, May 2025). On 7 February 2026 he recalled Ghana's High Commissioner to Nigeria, Baba Jamal Mohammed Ahmed — his own appointee — after allegations of vote-buying in the NDC's Ayawaso East primary, the presidency stating that although several candidates faced similar accusations, Baba Jamal was the only serving public officer among them and was therefore the one the Code reached; the vote-buying itself remains an ALLEGATION, which Baba Jamal denies (Presidency statement, 7 February 2026).
  • The man himself has stood where his targets now stand, under the same machinery. As opposition leader he was "Government Official 1" in the Airbus bribery affair; after a four-year investigation the OSP announced on 8 August 2024 that it had found no evidence that he or any other official was paid a bribe, closed the case and withdrew the related arrest warrants and Red Notices (OSP report of investigation, 8 August 2024). The office that cleared him on evidence is the office now prosecuting his predecessor's finance minister.
  • His Attorney-General also discontinued the previous government's prosecution of 53 Democracy Hub protesters arrested in 2024 — the discontinuation machinery (below) cutting, in that instance, in favour of people who had protested against the camp now prosecuting (Attorney-General's press conference, February 2025).

Evidence against:

  • In the same weeks, the discontinuation machinery freed almost exclusively his own side. From late January 2025, Attorney-General Dominic Ayine entered nolle prosequi or withdrew charges in the pending trials of a series of NDC figures prosecuted by the previous government: former COCOBOD chief executive Stephen Opuni; former minister Collins Dauda (Saglemi housing case); former SSNIT director-general Ernest Thompson; NDC chairman Samuel Ofosu Ampofo; the ambulance case against Cassiel Ato Forson — acquitted by the Court of Appeal in July 2024, after which the state abandoned its appeal — who is now Mahama's Finance Minister; and Johnson Asiamah, whose two bank-collapse trials (UT Bank and uniBank) were discontinued in the same weeks that Mahama named him Governor of the Bank of Ghana, a sequence the previous Attorney-General, Nii Ayikoi Otoo, publicly called unjustified. Ayine stated publicly that Mahama gave him no instruction and that he took "absolute responsibility" for the decisions, citing defective charges, conflicts from his own prior role as defence counsel in two of the cases, and an internal ministry memo on the Asiamah file — a memo his office later declined to release to Parliament, citing legal-advice exemptions in the Right to Information Act (Attorney-General's press conferences, February 2025; News Ghana reporting, June 2026).
  • The Duffuor settlement built a second, purchasable track. On 22 July 2025 the Attorney-General discontinued the trial of former finance minister Kwabena Duffuor and seven others over the collapse of uniBank after announcing a general prosecutorial threshold: where at least 60% of state losses are recovered, prosecution may be reconsidered. The Attorney-General's reconciliation had reduced the alleged loss from GH¢5.7 billion to GH¢2.8 billion, and some GH¢824 million in assets had been surrendered. Former Attorney-General and first Special Prosecutor Martin Amidu published a detailed attack calling the settlement "high corruption" dressed as discretion and noting it bypassed the statutory plea-bargaining procedure — his characterization, recorded as an ALLEGATION-level critique, not a finding. Transparency International Ghana's February 2026 statement on the CPI named the same act in measured language: "the discontinuation of corruption cases involving members of the ruling government through a 60:40 settlement raised eyebrows among Ghanaians and dampened expectations raised by the publication of Operation Recover All Loot" (Transparency International Ghana, 10 February 2026). No equivalent settlement track has been documented for any defendant of the predecessor camp.
  • The drive's own throughput figures show where its edge currently stops. EOCO reports more than GH¢600 million recovered, but of 462 cases it investigated only 15 have reached prosecution, and Em News found no reported prosecution of a sitting minister of the current government — which does not prove none is under investigation, and is recorded as the state of the public record, not as proof of protection (Em News investigation via myemonline, November 2026).
  • The outcome measure has not moved: Ghana scored 43 of 100 in the 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index (76th of 182), one point above 2024's 42 and the same score the country has held, within a point, since 2020 (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released February 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The electoral referee who declared his 2020 defeat still holds her office, after a removal process he could have slow-walked or fed and instead simply transmitted. Ten petitions reached the presidency in late 2025 — seven seeking the removal of Electoral Commission chairperson Jean Mensa and her deputies Bossman Asare and Samuel Tettey, three seeking the removal of the Special Prosecutor. Mahama forwarded all ten to the Chief Justice as Article 146 requires, his spokesperson stating that "the process has left the hands of the President." On 18 February 2026 the Chief Justice determined that the petitions against the EC leadership established no prima facie case, and the process ended; Jean Mensa, appointed by President Akufo-Addo and chairperson of the commission that declared Mahama the loser in 2020, remains in post (GhanaWeb, December 2025; Presidency announcements, February 2026).
  • When his own parliamentary leadership moved to abolish the Special Prosecutor, he stopped them in public. On 8 December 2025 the Majority Leader, Mahama Ayariga, and the Majority Chief Whip introduced a private member's bill to repeal the OSP Act — days after the OSP had detained a prominent lawyer and critic, Martin Kpebu, an incident the NPP Minority links to the bill's timing (the Minority's allegation of coordination is recorded as such). Mahama publicly called the repeal premature at a National Peace Council engagement and formally requested the bill's withdrawal; it was withdrawn. The Centre for Democratic Development (CDD-Ghana) praised the intervention as a commitment to accountability institutions (Presidency statement, December 2025; CDD-Ghana, December 2025).
  • The courts have decided against state actors in both directions on his watch, in public: the High Court's partial acquittal of Adu-Boahene (Test 1); a High Court ruling of 15 April 2026 holding that the OSP lacks power to prosecute without the Attorney-General's authorization — a judgment against the anti-corruption office's autonomy claim, now the subject of a Supreme Court constitutional challenge (suit J1/3/2026) brought by a private citizen, which the courts will decide; and the ECOWAS Court of Justice's dismissal, on 19 November 2025, of the removed Chief Justice's application for provisional measures against the government (below), with her substantive case permitted to proceed by amendment granted 30 January 2026 (ECOWAS Court rulings, November 2025 and January 2026).
  • The media referee was reopened by his intervention, not closed by it. On 12 June 2025 the National Communications Authority suspended 62 radio stations for regulatory breaches; Mahama directed the Communications Minister to work with the NCA to reinstate the affected licences, and critical and opposition media operate openly — the Minority petitions him in Parliament, fact-checkers grade his promises, and a fact-check publicly corrected an NPP claim exaggerating his judicial appointments (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025; GhanaFact, November 2026).
  • The central bank has run a tightening-then-easing policy that delivered inflation from 23.8% at end-2024 to 3.3% by February 2026 — outcomes consistent with an operational central bank, though the governor is his appointee (see against) and the IMF has flagged quasi-fiscal losses on the Bank's balance sheet from the Domestic Gold Purchase Programme it inherited and continued (IMF Article IV and sixth ECF review, 15 May 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The tenure's defining institutional act is the removal of a sitting Chief Justice — the first completed removal of a Chief Justice under Article 146 in the Fourth Republic — executed in a sequence that concentrated the referees around his office. Three citizen petitions reached Mahama in March 2025; after consulting the Council of State he suspended Chief Justice Gertrude Torkornoo on 22 April 2025; the five-member committee chaired by Justice Gabriel Pwamang (its members included former Auditor-General Daniel Domelevo) heard the case from 15 May 2025 and found "stated misbehaviour" established — including, per the committee's report excerpts, holiday travel expenses charged to the Judicial Service (Tanzania with her husband, the United States with her daughter, with per diems) and interference in judicial appointments. On 1 September 2025, bound by Article 146(9) to act on the recommendation, Mahama removed her — not only as Chief Justice but, on the committee's recommendation, as a Justice of the Supreme Court as well. Torkornoo denies the findings and has challenged the process in the High Court and at the ECOWAS Court; her case is pending (committee report and Presidency warrant, 1 September 2025; ECOWAS Court procedural rulings, 2025–2026).
  • The process's fairness is contested at the highest professional level, and the government's answer addressed the critics' standing rather than their points. On 14 August 2025 the Bar Council of England and Wales and the Commonwealth Lawyers Association issued a joint statement calling the continued suspension prima facie a breach of the executive's constitutional duties, citing among other things the committee's permission for petitioners to rely on witnesses rather than testify themselves, and called for her reinstatement. The Attorney-General replied that the statement echoed arguments Ghana's courts had rejected and defended Article 146 compliance; other government figures dismissed the intervention as "colonialist insults" (BCEW/CLA joint statement, 14 August 2025; Attorney-General's response, 15 August 2025).
  • The succession compounded the structural problem. The most senior justice, Paul Baffoe-Bonnie, became Acting Chief Justice on Torkornoo's suspension — standing, as former Attorney-General Martin Amidu documented, in the position of inheriting the substantive post if the removal succeeded. Mahama nominated him on 23 September 2025; Parliament approved by 164 votes to 69; he was sworn in on 17 November 2025. In September 2026, remarks attributed to Baffoe-Bonnie at a Minerals Income Investment Fund engagement were reported by Amidu: the Chief Justice described Mahama as "the old man" and said, "The old man's agenda is one that will benefit Ghana. We should do everything to ensure that he succeeds." Amidu's conclusion — that the Chief Justice has become the executive's "surrogate" — is his characterization, recorded as a published critique by a former officeholder, not a finding; the reported remarks themselves are the documentary item (Ghana Guardian reporting of Amidu's analysis, September–October 2026). Baffoe-Bonnie reaches the mandatory retirement age of 70 on 26 December 2026; a further succession, reported but unconfirmed at grading to favour Justice Emmanuel Yonny Kulendi, is pending (The Herald, November 2026; GhanaWeb via Ghana News Online, November 2026).
  • The bench beneath the Chief Justice has been substantially reshaped by this one president in under two years. Mahama swore in seven Supreme Court justices on a single day, 3 July 2025, then 21 Court of Appeal justices on 2 October 2025 and 37 High Court judges on 24 October 2025 — 65 presidential judicial appointments in eighteen months, with three further Supreme Court nominations in August 2026 taking the Supreme Court bench to roughly nineteen or twenty. The NPP's claim of "120 judges" was fact-checked false (it counted Circuit Court judges appointed by the Chief Justice), but the verified figure still stands against the cap of fifteen Supreme Court justices that the NPP cites from the NDC's own 2024 manifesto (GhanaFact fact-check, November 2026). A 2026 directive from the Chief Justice's office requiring specified judges to sit through the legal vacation drew a formal protest from the Ghana Bar Association and from defence counsel in the ORAL trials, who called it unprecedented and discriminatory — the protest is recorded as the Bar's position; the directive is the Judicial Service's (GhanaFact, November 2026; Daily Guide reporting, 2026).
  • The security and investigative leadership was replaced wholesale on his arrival — Inspector-General of Police, Chief of Defence Staff, EOCO and National Security leadership — each within the president's lawful appointment powers, and each meaning that by mid-2025 every coercive referee in the state answered to a Mahama appointee. The Special Prosecutor is the exception that proves the structure's tension: Agyebeng survives and acts independently (Test 1), but he does so while removal petitions against him sit with a Chief Justice who owes his own seat to a process this president initiated, and while a constitutional suit against his office's prosecutorial powers — announced publicly by the Deputy Attorney-General — proceeds in the Supreme Court (The Publisher, December 2025; Kumasi Mail reporting of Minority statement, 21 April 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The forest-reserve file is a complete, documented adjustment arc. His 120-day contract promised a ban on mining in forest reserves; the government's first answer to pressure was partial — L.I. 2501 (June 2025) amended the 2022 instrument L.I. 2462 to strip the presidency's power to license mining in protected forests, but campaigners documented that some 80% of reserves remained exposed. The pressure continued — seventeen civil-society organisations, the Forestry Commission's own recommendations, sustained media campaigns — and the government conceded the full point: Lands Minister Emmanuel Armah-Kofi Buah laid the revocation instrument on 31 October 2025, describing a "listening government," and L.I. 2462 stood revoked with effect from 10 December 2025 (L.I. 2505; CSO coalition statements, December 2025).
  • His single largest early overreach was corrected by his own review machinery, in the open, with numbers. The Chief of Staff's letter of 10 February 2025 directed every state institution to annul all appointments made after 7 December 2024 — a blanket order that swept up teachers and health workers and drew the Minority Leader's charge, quoting Mahama's own 2017 words against him, that it was unjust. Mahama's government then constituted a presidential committee that reviewed 2,080 cases institution by institution and, in findings announced 3 March 2026, upheld 1,539 appointments as procedurally compliant — restoring hundreds the blanket directive had already cancelled (879 institutional revocations preceded the review; the committee's final revocation figure was 541) — and extended clemency to persons with disabilities recruited into the Education Service whose paperwork fell short (Presidency briefing, 3 March 2026).
  • Misses were owned in his own voice, with reasons, on a date he had set for the accounting. In his 120-day broadcast of 7 May 2025 Mahama stated plainly that the COVID-19 levy had not been scrapped within the promised window because of its linkages to the IMF programme, and committed it to the VAT reform in the next budget; the 2026 Budget of 13 November 2025 abolished it (120-day address, 7 May 2025; Citi Newsroom budget reporting, 13 November 2025).
  • The holder of this seat has twice absorbed the largest wobble a democracy administers, before this tenure began. Defeated in December 2016, he conceded on election night and handed over power. Defeated again in 2020, he took his case to the Supreme Court, lost unanimously in March 2021, and accepted the ruling publicly while recording his disagreement — the conduct that left him eligible, in the voters' eyes, to be returned in 2024 with 56.55% (Britannica; contemporaneous reporting, 2016 and 2021). Within this tenure, the street's verdict so far runs the other way: Global InfoAnalytics measured his approval at 71% in June 2026 (8,784 respondents, ±2.5 points) — the highest in its tracking series since 2020 — with 66% saying the country is headed in the right direction (Global InfoAnalytics National Tracking Poll, June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the tenure's deadliest file, the seat has not answered at all. Through 2025 and 2026 the Catholic Bishops' Conference, organised labour, civil-society coalitions and the parliamentary Minority pressed him to declare a state of emergency in the galamsey zones; he refused throughout, holding that the fight is gradual and that existing law suffices. On 11 November 2026 a NAIMOS operation moving seized excavators through Manso Keniago, in the Ashanti Region, ended in a clash in which residents were shot dead — the toll is CONTESTED: three dead and thirteen injured per MyJoyOnline, Graphic Online and the town's chief; five dead per the small-scale miners' association GNASSM; six dead per the NDC's own Manso Adubia constituency executives and NPP statements. NAIMOS states its personnel fired warning shots in self-defence after an armed mob attacked, wounding two of its officers, and its statement did not mention civilian deaths; the chief of Manso Keniago states residents were fired on without provocation and has challenged NAIMOS to release its drone footage. The Lands Ministry opened an investigation; Parliament's Human Rights Committee on 17 November 2026 demanded an independent probe and a review of the task force's rules of engagement; the governing party's local branch demanded NAIMOS's withdrawal from the area; his own Ashanti Regional Minister publicly called for NAIMOS operations to be reviewed. As of grading, no statement by Mahama on the deaths appears in the documented record, and NAIMOS has publicly committed to continuing operations unchanged (MyJoyOnline and Graphic reporting, November 2026; Parliamentary Human Rights Committee statement, 17 November 2026; NAIMOS statement, November 2026).
  • The Ablekuma North adjustment stopped at his own appointees. After the violent 11 July 2025 parliamentary rerun — in which former MP Mavis Hawa Koomson and the NPP's candidate were assaulted, journalists were attacked, and a police officer slapped a GHOne TV reporter — Mahama stated that anyone responsible must face justice; police interdicted and charged the officer, and eight suspects were arraigned in August 2025. But two serving officials of his government publicly celebrated the violence — Minerals Development Fund chief executive Hanna Bissiw called it justified retaliation ("violence begets violence"), and Youth Employment Agency acting chief executive Malik Basintale praised Koomson's assailant in a Facebook post — and despite a formal Minority petition of 24 July 2025 and a Minority Women's Caucus demand for their dismissal, both remained in post at grading (DUBAWA reconstruction, 2025; Minority Caucus petition, 24 July 2025).
  • State force against the press was answered with investigations ordered, not yet with published outcomes. On 30 July 2025 at least seven military officers attacked a JoyNews anchor and cameraman covering a warehouse demolition in Accra, smashing equipment and detaining the journalist for some 45 minutes; Mahama directed the security services to investigate. Journalists were also assaulted at EOCO's premises in May 2025 and at Ablekuma North in July 2025 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). The pattern of the tenure is that the order to investigate is documented; the investigation's findings, in these files, are not.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's voters, the mining communities, the revoked appointees, LGBTQ Ghanaians, cocoa farmers, students, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The flagship inclusion programmes were delivered at national scale, across party lines. The No-Fees-Stress policy, launched 4 July 2025, reimbursed the academic fees of 152,698 first-year students in public tertiary institutions for the 2024/25 year — a cohort admitted before he took office — and the Student Loan Trust Fund reports 168,982 beneficiaries paid so far for 2025/26, with GH¢452.9 million allocated for the first cohort alone (Student Loan Trust Fund statement, August 2026; DUBAWA fact-check, 2026).
  • A group most systems count last was counted by name. Free tertiary education for persons with disabilities — full tuition, automatic coverage without individual application — was launched on 24 October 2025, funded through GETFund, with 1,623 students with disabilities receiving full tuition and residential support in 2025/26 and loan forgiveness under consideration for those already in the loan portfolio (Presidency, October 2025; SLTF, 2026). The appointments-review committee likewise carved out clemency for recruits with disabilities caught by the revocation directive (Test 3).
  • Cocoa farmers — some 800,000 farm families, concentrated in regions that vote heavily for the opposition — received the promised share: on 4 August 2025 the producer price was set at US$5,040 per tonne, exactly the pledged 70% of the US$7,200 gross FOB value, up from US$3,100, with free fertiliser, insecticides and spraying machines reintroduced (COCOBOD, 4 August 2025).
  • The top of the state itself widened: Jane Naana Opoku-Agyemang serves as Ghana's first female Vice-President. His predecessor's flagship Free Senior High School policy was retained and continued rather than abolished, and the Ghana Medical Trust Fund ("Mahama Cares"), established in 2025, directs resources to chronic-disease care for those outside insurance coverage (Presidency and parliamentary record, 2025).

Evidence against:

  • One class of Ghanaians is counted out by name, with his signature promised. The Human Sexual Rights and Family Values Bill — which would imprison a person for up to three years for identifying as LGBTQ and impose three to five years for promoting or supporting LGBTQ activity — was passed by Parliament on 29 May 2026. Mahama has repeatedly stated his position ("marriage is between a man and a woman… that is our position," to the Christian Council at Jubilee House, 18 November 2025) and pledged to assent; on 1 June 2026 at Chatham House he said the Attorney-General and the presidency's legal team would review the passed text. On 10 November 2026 Parliament rescinded its third reading to correct legal inconsistencies, the Majority Leader confirming the corrected bill would reach the president within the month and that "the President has [not] shifted goalposts." The bill is domestically popular — carried by MPs of both parties and backed by the major religious bodies — and is condemned by international human-rights organisations; both facts are recorded, and neither changes the test's arithmetic: at grading, the seat's stated intention is to sign a law under which a defined minority of Ghanaians becomes criminal by identity (Parliamentary record, May–November 2026; Rightify Ghana reporting, November 2025; Ghana Guardian, November 2026).
  • The 541 Ghanaians whose appointments were finally revoked (Test 3) were counted out by a directive of his own government — teachers, health professionals and revenue officers among them, per the Minority Leader's parliamentary account — under a policy the president himself had condemned in identical terms when the other party applied it: "These workers are all citizens and, like all Ghanaians, have rights which must be protected" (Mahama, 26 May 2017, quoted in Parliament by Minority Leader Alexander Afenyo-Markin, 2025). The committee review restored many; it did not restore all, and the 541 figure is the government's own final count (Presidency briefing, 3 March 2026).
  • The mining communities bear the enforcement side of the galamsey fight while its financiers largely remain ahead of it. The Manso Keniago dead (Test 3) were residents of a mining town shot during an operation by the state's own task force, whose first public statement did not record their deaths at all; the counts of the dead still differ by source because no official figure has been published. Civil-society assessments through 2026 record the same asymmetry the DYMOG protesters carried into the street: labourers and excavators are seized while the financiers and political protectors of illegal mining — alleged by campaigners to exist in both parties, an ALLEGATION recorded as such — are rarely prosecuted (CIVICUS and CSO statements, 2025–2026; fifty4news analysis, 2026).
  • The poor's line items are the ones shrinking in his government's newest budget, on the Minority's documented reading: allocations for the LEAP cash-transfer programme, school feeding and the capitation grant fall from GH¢3.46 billion in 2026 to GH¢2.26 billion in the 2027 Budget — a figure the government has not, at grading, publicly re-based (Minority Finance Committee statement on the 2027 Budget, November 2026; recorded as the opposition's reading of the budget document, contested by the government's overall framing).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Manifesto and 120-day social-contract pledges and public statements vs. the record since 7 January 2025, with dates; his first presidency (2012–2017) and his own earlier public positions stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The 120-day social contract — mostly delivered, and independently scored. Mahama published 26 dated commitments before taking office. The cabinet list was submitted within the promised 14 days; the lean-government pledge cut ministers from over 120 to 60 and ministries from 29 to 23; the E-Levy, the 10% betting tax and the emissions levy were repealed by assent on 2 April 2025, inside the promised 90 days. BudgIT Ghana's independent audit scored delivery at 72% — 17 of 26 promises fully delivered — while his senior advisor claimed all 25–26 fulfilled; both counts are printed because the gap between them is itself part of the record (BudgIT Ghana audit, 2025; Daily Graphic, 2025; DUBAWA reality check, May 2025).
  • The reset's macroeconomic word — kept at a scale few governments in this series match. He inherited an IMF Extended Credit Facility that had veered off track, a fiscal deficit of 7.9% of GDP and public debt of 61.8% of GDP. By the programme's sixth and final review (IMF staff-level agreement, 15 May 2026), debt had fallen to 44.7% of GDP — hitting the legislated 45% anchor eight years early — inflation had fallen from 23.8% to 3.4% (April 2026), the cedi had appreciated 40.7% against the dollar in 2025, reserves stood at about US$14.5 billion, and Fitch had raised Ghana from restricted default to B with a positive outlook. Ghana exited the programme in May 2026, and Mahama's January 2026 declaration — that this must be the last IMF bailout in Ghana's history — was followed by the choice of a non-financing Policy Coordination Instrument rather than a new loan (IMF press release 26/152, 15 May 2026; Ecofin Agency, June 2026; Finance Minister's statement to Parliament, 28 May 2026).
  • The cocoa word — kept on the formula, in writing. The 70%-of-FOB pledge was delivered exactly as specified on 4 August 2025 and raised again in October 2025 to GH¢3,625 per bag (Test 4); when the world price collapsed, the February 2026 adjustment still paid farmers 90% of the achieved FOB, and Cabinet directed COCOBOD to pay all arrears and to legislate an automatic price-adjustment mechanism guaranteeing a minimum of 70% (COCOBOD, 12 February 2026) — the formula bent with the market rather than being quietly abandoned, though the season's outcome for farmers is recorded on the against shelf.
  • The anti-corruption word — machinery built and running. ORAL was stood up within weeks; the Code of Conduct was launched with sanctions actually applied to his own appointees (Tests 1–2); the National Cathedral file he promised to expose has been audited twice — a Deloitte audit published July 2025 found procurement breaches and an unexplained GH¢4.93 million discrepancy in payments to the project's architects, with total commitments of US$97 million against the US$58 million previously admitted — the secretariat and board were dissolved, the site handed back in October 2026, and the completed forensic audit sits with the Attorney-General for advice (Presidency communications, July 2025 and October 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The dated misses, on his own contract's terms. The COVID-19 levy was promised gone within 90 days; it was abolished only in the budget of 13 November 2025 — the independent Votum tracker rates the pledge broken under its deadline rule, and Mahama's own May 2025 broadcast concedes the delay while explaining it (Votum promise tracker, checked 15 November 2026). The forest-reserve mining ban, promised within 120 days, was delivered by revocation on 10 December 2025 — roughly seven months late (Test 3).
  • The flagship jobs promise — the word the tenure will be graded on, currently uncounted by his own secretariat. The 24-Hour Economy, his signature 2024 pledge, was launched on 2 July 2025 targeting US$4 billion mobilised and 1.7 million jobs by 2028; the Authority Act was assented to in February 2026. On 19 October 2026 the 24-Hour Economy Secretariat stated it could not provide a definitive jobs figure while it built a measurement framework with the Ghana Statistical Service; four linked projects carried about 160,000 projected opportunities. In August 2026 Mahama announced a "New Economy" programme — US$10 billion over four years — into which the 24-Hour Economy's projects were folded; the Minority's charge that this is a rebranding of an undelivered promise is an opposition characterization (Oppong Nkrumah press conference, 10 November 2026), but the missing jobs count is the government's own documented position (24-Hour Economy Secretariat via Minority statement, October 2026; Ghana Guardian, November 2026).
  • The cocoa season's second half. After the October 2025 increase, the producer price was cut on 12 February 2026 from GH¢3,625 to GH¢2,587 per bag — a 29% reduction mid-season — as the world price fell below the cost of production and farmers went unpaid for a period, requiring an emergency cabinet meeting and a directive to pay arrears (COCOBOD, 12 February 2026). The 70% formula was honoured; the income farmers were promised by the season's opening headline was not.
  • His own earlier words, returned as evidence. In May 2017 Mahama condemned the incoming NPP government's dismissal of public servants appointed after election day as an unacceptable precedent; in February 2025 his Chief of Staff ordered the annulment of all appointments made after 7 December 2024. The committee review (Test 3) softened the act; it does not reconcile the words. Likewise his first presidency is the standing context for every reset promise: he pledged then to fix the dumsor power crisis, and while supply eased by late 2016 it did so at high cost alongside an IMF programme — and the electorate removed him in December 2016, the only verdict on a first term this series records from the voters themselves (Britannica; GhanaSummary political record).
  • The cathedral word — process delivered, accountability pending. Twenty-two months in, no prosecution has been announced on the previous government's flagship scandal file despite two audits and a CHRAJ finding that the main construction contract was void from the start for procurement-law breaches; the forensic report has been with the Attorney-General since August 2026 and the government's stated position is that decisions await his advice (Christian Daily International reporting, 2026; CHRAJ findings via press reporting, 2025). Not yet broken; nowhere near closed.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

John Mahama is the first subject in this series to be graded on a second mandate — a man the voters removed in 2016, investigated in opposition, and then returned with 56.55% and a two-thirds parliament — and the record of his first twenty-two months is genuinely two-sided in a specific pattern: the delivery machinery is the strongest in the recent batches, and the referee machinery is where the weight collects. On the word, the dated promises mostly landed — taxes repealed on schedule, the lean cabinet, the cocoa formula, the IMF programme completed with the debt anchor hit eight years early — and the misses (the COVID levy, the forest ban, above all the uncounted 1.7 million jobs now folded into a renamed programme) are specific, dated, and partly confessed in his own broadcasts. On the law, the drive against his predecessor's camp runs through real courts that convict and acquit, while his own camp's files end in discontinuations and a published 60% settlement threshold that Transparency International Ghana named, in terms, as a second track for the ruling side. On the grip, he left the electoral chair who declared his defeat in office and publicly saved the Special Prosecutor from his own majority — and he removed a Chief Justice, seated her acting deputy in the substantive post, and appointed sixty-five judges in eighteen months, while the new Chief Justice is reported pledging the judiciary's help to make "the old man" succeed. On the count, 150,000-plus students and every tertiary student with a disability were counted in with money attached, and LGBTQ Ghanaians are counted out by name in a bill he has promised to sign. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether he signs the Family Values Bill when it reaches his desk, who succeeds Baffoe-Bonnie at the end of December 2026, whether the Manso Keniago investigation names what happened and the seat speaks to it, whether any ORAL case ever reaches his own side of the aisle, and whether the jobs his flagship promised are ever counted at all.
