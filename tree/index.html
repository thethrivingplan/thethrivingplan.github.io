Your name can be anything you can typeThe law protects names — it doesn't police them▾

A name itself is unrestricted. Any language, any script, emoji, symbols, spaces, punctuation, scripts mixed together, long or short — anything a text input can hold can be your name. Be as creative as the keyboard allows.

Your name is kept exactly as you wrote it. Names are stored and displayed exactly as written, every decoration intact — the raised ⁷, the emoji, the spelling, all of it. Nothing here trims, tidies, or “corrects” a name.

Folding is only a measuring stick. When the registry checks whether a name is already claimed, it folds written forms down to compare them — that folded form is only a comparison key for the check. It never alters your name, and a name is never rejected for its shape.

Only two refusals exist. A name is refused only if it folds onto somebody else's live claim (and you don't hold their code — a handed name, with the code, is the open door), or onto a retired name, which stays retired forever because somebody's history is under it. Everything else is yours to type.

The conferred look — it cannot even look the sameThe look is conferred, not carried — a copy stands naked▾

So it cannot even look the same. A claimed name's special rendering is conferred by the page, never carried by the string. The letters themselves hold no mark and no motion — anyone can type them, paste them, or screenshot them, and what they get is only the letters.

When the look appears — both jaws, together. The page confers the look only when (1) the string on the page is a byte-exact codepoint match with the string recorded in the registry — the true name, character for character — and (2) a code proof passes: the code entered on this device fingerprints to the fingerprint recorded in that registry entry. A name match alone never confers anything; a fold match never confers anything. Then the page renders the name with its standing: its registry mark beside it, plus its own styling, recorded in its entry.

When it does not. The identical string typed by anyone else renders plain — no mark, no motion. A text copy loses the life, because the life was never in the text. A screenshot freezes one frame of it, and the frame still carries no standing on any page. Set a copy next to the original on a page that knows the law, and the copy stands visibly unmarked: a copy stands naked.

This applies to any claimed name, on equal terms. The registry mark is conferred for every proven claim; a per-name styling is conferred where that name's registry entry defines one — and every claimant chooses their own styling at claim time, from the same kit (see “The full stack” below). Unproven, a name is still protected — it still cannot be taken — it simply renders plain, like any other words.

The full stack — the standard for every claimed nameClaim, code, watermark, exactness, conferred look — the same for everybody, available to everybody▾

Take the best of all worlds — and make it available to everybody. Every claimed name gets the whole stack, not just Entry #1. No tier, no favourite: what one claimant can have, every claimant can have, on the same terms.

1 · A distinctive visible string. Your name, stored and displayed exactly as you typed it — every letter, symbol, and decoration byte-for-byte. The registry can show its exact codepoint spelling on request (see the registry below — “Show exact codepoints”).

2 · An optional watermark — declared, never secret. The watermark is hidden in the name, and it is not a secret that it's there. At claim time the owner may embed a payload of their own choosing: invisible Unicode marks (zero-width characters) placed inside the name string itself. The marks themselves are invisible in the string — and their presence is public knowledge, declared right on the registry entry with a visible “Watermarked” note, like the watermark in paper money: there for safety, and no secret to anyone. The registry stores the name byte-for-byte, watermark and all, publishes its exact codepoint spelling for anyone to check against (match / folds-only / no match), and these pages never strip invisible marks.

Its safety is provenance and detection — not secrecy. A hand-made fake lacks the watermark; a copy carries it and confesses its source. Honestly, both ways: a paste carries the watermark, which proves the copy came from the true string — and some outside apps strip invisible marks, so a copy that passed through those apps may arrive with the watermark gone, which the public codepoint check shows at once. The only secret in the whole system is the claim code. The watermark never was one.

3 · Exactness checking at the gate. Two different questions, two different answers — the two-jaw check, spelled out in the next fold. A string that merely folds onto a claim is an imitation and is refused. Only an exact codepoint match is the true string.

4 · The conferred look — designed by you. At claim time you choose your own conferred styling from a small built-in kit, and it is recorded in your registry entry: a mark style (for example ◆, ★, or ●, with your entry number or name on it) and a motion treatment — rise, glow, wave, or steady (mark only, no motion). Entry #1's recorded design is the launch treatment above: silver streaks, a pulsing arrow, a solid name, climbing 7s, and its Entry #1 mark. Unproven names always render plain, whatever design is recorded.

The whole stack — claim, code, watermark, exactness, conferred look — is the same for everybody.

The two-jaw check — a lookalike has nowhere to liveJaw 1 folds and refuses imitations; Jaw 2 confers the look only on the exact string plus a passing code proof▾

Jaw 1 — FOLD. Any string that folds onto a claimed name is treated as an imitation of that claim and refused without the owner's code. Folding strips away the disguises: lookalike letters from other alphabets (Cyrillic, Greek), decorated and fullwidth digits, separators slipped in or out, and case. An imitation can never register as a “different” name — the fold is checked first, before any new claim is taken.

Jaw 2 — EXACT. Only the byte-exact codepoint string recorded in the registry is the true name. And the conferred look — registry mark plus the name's styling and motion — is applied only on an exact codepoint match and a passing code proof: never on a fold match, never on a name match alone.

The worked example — the Cyrillic а. Take the name with its second a swapped for the Cyrillic letter а. To your eyes the two strings below are identical. To the page they are not the same string at all: the true name's a is Latin a, codepoint U+0061; the costume's is Cyrillic а, codepoint U+0430 — a different character wearing the same face.

The true string (Latin a, U+0061):~~~>Tristan⁷⁷⁷

The costume (Cyrillic а, U+0430) — looks identical, is not the name:~~~>Tristаn⁷⁷⁷

So a lookalike has nowhere to live. Offered as a new name, Jaw 1 folds it straight onto the claim and refuses it as an imitation. Worn as a costume, Jaw 2 gives it nothing: different codepoints, no code, no look — it renders plain and unmarked, next to a name it can imitate but never be.

A name cannot be replicated just by using a Cyrillic version of an A — or of any letter in it. The fold map covers the confusable twins of every letter in Entry #1's name, and the decorated-digit family for its 7s.

How a claim travels to every tableThe honest mechanics — version by version, settled by the record▾

Claim anywhere. A claim can be made right at any table, or on this page. It is recorded in that table's record and in the registry's append-only history, with its date. The history only ever grows — claims are added, never quietly removed.

Claims travel with the pages. Registry updates ship with new published versions of the pages. From that version on, every table enforces the name everywhere — refused to anyone but its owner, in every language and every form of symbols, exactly as on this page.

First recorded claim wins. If the same name is claimed at two tables before an update ships, the first recorded claim wins and the later claim must choose another name. No argument, no auction: the registry's append-only history — dates and all — settles it.

In the gap, the room protects you. Until an update lands, the room-level rule still holds: the first claim at a table is final for that table's run. So a fresh claim is protected where it was made from the first minute, and everywhere else from the next published version forward.

How claiming works — the claim form & the string checkerOne name, one owner, one code only you know▾

Claimed on any table = claimed on all tables. A claim made at any table, or on this page, becomes a claim on every table and tree as registry updates ship — see “How a claim travels” above.

Claim once. Anyone can claim their name once, through the public door: the Poster Board ↗ — free, no account. Claimed names join a public registry; only the fingerprint of your code rides in the code of the pages. The names stand in the open; the codes never do.

Your code is yours to invent — a phrase, a number, any string of characters you'll remember. It is not generated for you and not assigned. Nothing releases or renames your name without it. Lose it, and the name stays yours forever anyway — it just can never be given up.

Any language, any symbols. A claim protects the name, not one spelling. The pages fold every written form down to the name itself — a lookalike letter from another alphabet, a decorated ⑦ for a ⁷, spaces or dashes slipped in — all of it reads as the same claimed name. A name claimed in Arabic, Japanese, Spanish, any script at all is protected in that form and every form that folds into it.

One claimed name per device. Your device holds a single claimed name. Your characters, roles, and tree cards under that one name are untouched — this binds the name, not the person.

Picked is picked. Only the owner can release a name, and only with their code. A rename takes a new, untaken, never-claimed name. A released name retires forever — nobody else can ever wear it, because your history is under it. And history does not erase with a name change: everything under the old name stays under it, the change itself goes on the record, and the card reads now this, formerly that, in the open.

Names can be handed. The owner can hand a name to someone else — it takes the owner's code, the handing goes in the registry in the open (handed from, handed to, the date), the receiver sets their own new code, and the giver's era under the name stays the giver's. Or the name is carried with a suffix or prefix — a Jr., a II, a mark of the house — a new claim linked to the old one, the registry showing of the line of. A stranger bolting a prefix onto a claimed name to sneak around it still folds straight into the claim and gets refused: a suffix makes a new name only when it comes through a handing.

The Backlog — file your own public pastAn option, not a force. Only you can backlog yourself, only with your code, only as far as you choose.▾

An option, not a force — open as much of your past as YOU choose. If you hold a claimed name, you may attach things you have publicly said or done — public posts, public statements, the public record — to your record, filed under your claimed name. You choose the scope: how far back, which sources, all of it or some of it.

Entirely optional. The Backlog is never required, and never a condition of joining this tree or holding a claim. A name with an empty backlog stands in full standing, exactly as claimed — and joining the tree asks nothing about your past at all.

Nobody can backlog anybody else's past. Filing is the owner's work alone, proven the same way as everything here: your own code, your proven claim. There is no form, anywhere on this page, for putting something on another person's record.

Every item is labeled self-filed, with its source and its date — what it is, where it stands in public, and when it was originally said or done, printed beside it. The label is part of the item and never comes off.

The record law applies: the backlog is append-only. An item, once filed, is not erased; if something filed turns out wrong, the correction is filed beside it, in the open, and both stand.

Public means public. The backlog is a door the owner opens onto their own already-public past. It reaches nothing private, and opening it a crack opens exactly that crack — no more.

How this works — the whole thing, plainly

Your first five minutesA checklist — do these in order and you have a tree▾
  1. Join yourself (the “Start” section above). You land at the center. Write only what you choose.
  2. Add one ancestor — a parent or grandparent who has died — as a record entry, and pick its label honestly: verified, family-told, or not sure.
  3. Plant one invitation leaf for a living relative — just a first name. Notice the form gives you nowhere to add their details. That's the law working.
  4. Save a copy on the Your Saves shelf, and download your tree file as a spare.
  5. Pass it on — send the file or link to one family member, with the one warning that matters: anyone holding the link can view the tree, so it goes to family, not the world.
Inviting, and what an invitation leaf isA held place — never a filled-in person▾

To invite someone, plant a leaf: their first name (or “child of …”), connected to whoever they're kin to. That's all a leaf can hold — no dates, no places, no photos, no details, for adults and children alike.

Then send them the tree (file or link). They open it, find their leaf, and tap “This is me — join” on it. The leaf becomes their card, and they write what's on it. If they never open it, the leaf just stays a leaf: invited, not joined. Nobody is on this tree by somebody else's say-so.

Ancestors and the three labelsVerified / family-told / not sure — printed where the fact sits▾

People who have died belong to the record, and anyone in the family can add what is known about them. Every record entry carries one label, chosen by whoever adds it and shown on the card:

verified — a document or public record backs it: a certificate, a census line, a dated record.

family-told — passed down in the family: told by someone who knew, carried by memory.

not sure — best knowledge, honestly shaky. A “not sure” is worth more than a guess dressed up as a fact.

Why → How → Act applies here too: the label says why the entry claims what it claims, so the next person knows how much weight to put on it.

Sharing, joining, and merging — and their limitsThe tree travels; copies grow; nobody's own card is ever overwritten▾

Sharing: download a tree file, or copy your tree code — paste it to family as-is, or add it to this page's address after #tree= to make a link that carries the tree inside it. Anyone holding either can view the whole tree — there is no password and no account, so choose who you send it to the way you'd choose who gets a key.

Joining: open the tree, find your leaf (or join fresh at the center of your own copy), fill in your own card, save your copy, pass the grown copy on.

Merging: when you load a grown copy over your own, new people and new record entries are added. Matching is by person. A joined person's own card is never overwritten by an import — if two copies disagree about a joined card, both versions are shown to you and you decide, card by card. Conflicts are never silently resolved.

The honest limit: copies can drift. Two branches of the family can grow two versions, and merging brings them together — but there is no master tree anywhere, by design. The tree is the family's, held by the family.

Privacy, in plain wordsNothing uploaded, no account, yours to forget▾

Nothing on this page is uploaded anywhere. There is no account, no sign-in, and no server copy of your tree. Your tree exists in exactly three places: on your device (autosave and your named saves), and in the files and links you choose to send.

You can forget the tree off this device at any time (see “Forget” in the Save section) — that clears the working copy and its autosave; your named saves stay until you remove them, and copies you already sent stay sent.

Living people's details exist on a tree only if those people wrote them. That's not a setting — it's the shape of the tool.

How the mic works, plainly — read this before you tap: it starts with one tap (your browser asks permission once). Dictation is done by your browser's speech service, not by this page. This page itself never records, receives, or stores your audio — but in most browsers (Chrome, Safari, Edge) the browser sends the audio to its maker's servers — Google, Apple, or Microsoft — to turn it into text, and the text comes back into the box above. So while the mic is on, your voice does leave your device, to your browser maker's speech service. When you stop, the box stays fully yours to edit — fix a misheard word before you send it. Browsers pause their speech service on their own after a while or a long silence; when that happens the mic stops, the red “Listening” pill disappears, and the status line here says so — tap the microphone again to keep talking. If this browser or in-app viewer can't do live dictation, the button above will say so plainly — a real browser is the reliable door.
