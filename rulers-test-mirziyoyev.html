The Ruler's Test — Shavkat Mirziyoyev, President of Uzbekistan

Subject: Shavkat Mirziyoyev — President of Uzbekistan since December 2016. He served as Prime Minister under his predecessor Islam Karimov from 2003 to 2016, became acting president on Karimov's death in September 2016 through a parliamentary appointment that bypassed the constitutional line of succession, and won the December 2016 election with a reported 88.6%. He was re-elected in October 2021 (80.1%) and again, after a constitutional reset, in July 2023 (87.7%). His current seven-year term runs to 2030, and the 2023 constitution leaves him eligible for one further term to 2037. He remains in office in November 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machinery processes officials in volume. The Prosecutor General's Office reported that 4,904 officials faced criminal charges in 2024, up 37% on the previous year, and the Anti-Corruption Agency he created by decree on 29 June 2020 reported more than 6,500 people tried on corruption charges in 2023 (Prosecutor General's Office, January 2025; Anti-Corruption Agency report, December 2024). Transparency International recorded Uzbekistan's Corruption Perceptions Index score rising every year from 17 in 2013 to 33 in 2023, crediting the agency's creation, strengthened legislation, and charges filed against numerous officials (Transparency International, CPI releases 2013–2024).
  • The law reached the former first family. Gulnara Karimova, the previous president's daughter — held under house arrest from the Karimov era — was prosecuted and convicted in Uzbek courts under Mirziyoyev, receiving a 13-year sentence in March 2020 in a fraud and money-laundering case, alongside parallel Swiss and US proceedings over telecoms bribes (court reporting, March 2020; US Department of Justice filings). The predecessor's household was not placed above process.
  • The cotton-labor prohibition was enforced against the state's own apparatus: the abolition of the state order system and criminalization of forced labor were applied to the local administrations that had run the harvest mobilization for decades, and the ILO verified the result on the ground (ILO Third-Party Monitoring, 2021 — see Test 4).

Evidence against:

  • In August 2026 the law itself was written in two tiers. A constitutional law "On the Presidential Administration," passed by the lower house and Senate one day apart and signed by Mirziyoyev within a week, formalized the Administration — headed since June 2025 by his eldest daughter, Saida Mirziyoyeva — as a body subordinate only to the president, with authority to coordinate state bodies, oversee regional governors, and exercise judicial-reform and anti-corruption powers; the head of the Administration automatically became deputy chair of the Security Council (Saida Mirziyoyeva assumed that post on 14 August 2026), and members of the Administration and their families were granted lifetime immunity from criminal prosecution, questioning, arrest, and even administrative detention (constitutional law as reported by Mezon Central and Frontier Review, August–November 2026; the Administration's stated rationale is institutional stability and independence of the office). A class of office-holders — his own household's office — is by statute unreachable by the law that reaches everyone else.
  • The family's placement is structural, not incidental. His younger daughter, Shakhnoza Mirziyoyeva, has run the preschool-education department for nine years and is first deputy director of the National Agency for Social Protection. His elder daughter's deputy in the Administration is Komil Allamjonov. Reporting by RFE/RL and others on business interests of his sons-in-law — including Oybek Tursunov's reported acquisition of a stake in Kapitalbank — is ALLEGATION-level: reported, not adjudicated, and denied by implication by the absence of any official proceeding; no documented case has ever reached a member of his family in either direction (Mezon Central family profile, November 2026; RFE/RL reporting).
  • The insult law is one law for the president and another for the citizen. In March 2021 Mirziyoyev signed amendments criminalizing online "insult or slander" of the president. Blogger Sobirjon Babaniyazov was sentenced to three years in prison for insulting the president online; blogger Valijon Kalonov was arrested in August 2021 on the same charge after criticizing the president and calling for an election boycott (European Parliament resolution A9-0227/2023; Human Rights Watch, World Report 2022). Criticism of officials below him is policed as ordinary slander; criticism of him carries a dedicated criminal article.
  • The outcome measure has turned: after a decade of annual improvement, Uzbekistan's CPI score fell to 32 in 2024 (121st of 180) and 31 in 2025 — the first declines since 2012 — while the prosecutions counted by his own agencies continued to rise, a combination consistent with enforcement that processes the apparatus but does not reach its top (Transparency International, CPI 2024 and 2025).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, media, the electoral authority, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Elections are observed, and the observers' findings are published unedited inside the country. ODIHR deployed full missions for the 2023 referendum, the 2023 early presidential election, and the 2024 parliamentary elections, and its critical final reports are publicly available; the government has implemented some ODIHR recommendations in the legal framework (OSCE/ODIHR final reports, December 2023 and February 2025).
  • The appellate courts are not wholly inert: in June 2023 the Supreme Court, while upholding Dauletmurat Tazhimuratov's 16-year sentence, modified some of his co-defendants' sentences, and sentences of others convicted over the July 2022 events were reduced or overturned on appeal (Supreme Court reporting, June 2023; ICCPR submission to the UN Human Rights Committee, 2026).
  • The Central Bank has run a professional inflation-targeting framework, unified the exchange rate in September 2017, and changed governor once in a decade through an ordinary appointment — Mamarizo Nurmuratov (2017–2024) succeeded by former finance minister Timur Ishmetov in December 2024 — with no documented interference scandal (Central Bank of Uzbekistan records; World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, April 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The electoral authority administers contests without contestants. No new political party has been registered since 2003, apart from one created with official support; the unregistered opposition party Truth, Progress and Unity failed registration requirements again in 2024, and all five parties seated in the Legislative Chamber are pro-government (ODIHR 2024 final report; IPU Parline). ODIHR concluded the 9 July 2023 presidential election was "technically well prepared but took place in a political environment lacking genuine competition," noted indications of ballot-box stuffing and seemingly identical signatures on voter lists, and recorded that no registered candidate publicly criticized the president (ODIHR final report, 21 December 2023). Of the 27 October 2024 parliamentary elections it concluded the environment "remained constrained, not providing voters with a genuine choice," with counting procedures not followed in over half of observed polling stations (ODIHR final report, February 2025).
  • The prosecution operates as an instrument in the cases that matter most to the seat. The July 2022 Karakalpakstan cases (Test 4) were tried 600 km from Nukus in Bukhara; the attempted-assassination case against the men accused of shooting at former administration official Komil Allamjonov's car on 26 October 2024 was tried in a closed Military Court, which on 12 February 2025 sentenced ten defendants to terms of up to 23 years — among them a former head of the Presidential Security Service's internal-security directorate and a former deputy head of Tashkent's police — with five defendants' names and terms undisclosed (Supreme Court press service, February 2025; Fergana News). A legal-advocacy organization, Due Process International, condemned the verdicts as baseless and politically motivated; that characterization is an interested party's ALLEGATION, but the closed forum, the undisclosed defendants, and the reported 2026 prison suicide of one of the convicted men (Ismail Jahongirov, per RFE/RL's Uzbek Service) are documented features of the process, and the question of who ordered the attack has never been publicly answered.
  • Media autonomy narrowed by statute and by prosecution. Beyond the March 2021 insult amendments (Test 1), March 2021 amendments to the Informatization Law prohibited online calls to protest "in violation of the established order"; in June 2021 the Foreign Ministry denied accreditation to a Tashkent-based foreign correspondent and in November 2021 blocked her entry to the country (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2022). Freedom House scores Uzbekistan 12/100, "Not Free," noting the president exercises significant control over the legislature and judiciary and that the Oliy Majlis provides neither an effective check on the executive nor a venue for genuine political debate (Freedom in the World 2024; CRS overview).
  • The August 2026 Administration law (Test 1) is also a grip instrument: it gives the presidential Administration — not the Cabinet or Parliament — formal oversight of the government and regional governors, implementational authority over judicial reform, and anti-corruption powers, concentrating in the president's household office functions the constitution assigns elsewhere (Mezon Central; Frontier Review, 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the street, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The fastest concession in this series' record is his. When mass protests erupted in Nukus on 1 July 2022 against draft constitutional amendments that would have deleted Karakalpakstan's sovereignty and its right to secede by referendum, Mirziyoyev flew to the region and on 2 July announced the disputed provisions withdrawn; he visited twice over the weekend and publicly castigated Karakalpakstan's own pro-government figures for not telling him about public opposition beforehand (protests record, July 2022; Wikipedia compilation of contemporaneous reporting). The offending text was his own proposal; he withdrew it within roughly a day of the street's answer.
  • The early presidency institutionalized responsiveness as its stated doctrine — "it is not the people who serve the state bodies, but the state bodies must serve the people" — and paired it with open criticism sessions in which he publicly rebuked and dismissed officials, including a documented November 2019 rebuke of officials who pressured bloggers (presidential addresses, 2017–2019; Human Rights Watch reporting).
  • On labor rights, international verification was invited and its findings accepted: the ILO's third-party monitoring ran at the government's request through 2021, and when the Cotton Campaign lifted its boycott in March 2022 on the strength of verified findings, the government treated the verdict as binding evidence its reform had worked (ILO, March 2022; Cotton Campaign statement, 10 March 2022).

Evidence against:

  • The Karakalpakstan concession was paired with a state of emergency, an internet blackout across the region, and lethal force (Test 4) — and the accountability he promised for the deaths never arrived. Prosecutors announced an investigation into excessive force in December 2022, and two police officers were prosecuted for torture connected to the events in August 2023; but two years on, Human Rights Watch recorded that no senior official had been held accountable for the excessive force, the Ombudsperson-led commission's findings were never transparently published, and repression of Karakalpak activists had increased rather than eased (HRW, 1 July 2024; US State Department Human Rights Report 2022).
  • The pushback on elections has been answered with repetition, not adjustment. ODIHR's core recommendations — on party registration, fundamental freedoms, defamation's criminal sanctions — have been restated by successive missions in 2021, 2023, and 2024 as unaddressed, and each subsequent contest was run on the same terms (ODIHR final reports, 2021–2025).
  • The pushback on speech was answered by doubling down in the same season it was given. Weeks after blogger Miraziz Bazarov was beaten by unidentified assailants outside his home in March 2021, Mirziyoyev signed the law criminalizing online insult of the president; Bazarov himself was placed under house arrest on slander charges and in January 2022 sentenced to three years' restricted freedom for social-media posts (HRW, World Report 2022; UPR submission, 2023). When the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found blogger Otabek Sattoriy's detention violated international law and called for his immediate release (Opinion published February 2023), he remained in prison serving six and a half years for reporting on local corruption.
  • The 2026 trajectory is away from adjustment: the response to accumulated criticism of family power was not to disperse it but to constitutionalize it, with lifetime immunity, in August 2026 (Test 1).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Karakalpakstan, labor migrants, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The poor were counted, measurably. The World Bank records the national poverty rate falling from 17.0% in 2021 to 8.9% in 2024 and 5.8% in 2025 — 3.7 million people lifted out of poverty — with real incomes of the bottom quintile growing 26% in 2024–25 against 13% for the top, and real GDP growth averaging about 6% a year in 2017–2025, reaching 7.7% in 2025 (World Bank, Uzbekistan country page and Macro Poverty Outlook, April 2026). Mirziyoyev's own August 2026 claim — poverty down from 35% in 2017 to 4% — uses a different methodology from the Bank's series and is not comparable to it; both figures are printed here at their own strength.
  • The cotton fields were emptied of coercion, and the count included the pickers. The ILO's 2021 monitoring, based on 11,000 interviews, found 99% of harvest workers working voluntarily and concluded Uzbek cotton was "free from systemic child labour and systemic forced labour" — an estimated two million children and half a million adults removed from coerced labor since the reform began (ILO, March 2022). The Cotton Campaign ended its boycott on the same findings.
  • Political prisoners of the prior era were released in the opening years — among them Muhammad Bekjanov, one of the world's longest-imprisoned journalists, freed in February 2017 — and thousands benefited from successive pardons (Human Rights Watch and press reporting, 2017–2019).
  • Labor migrants are counted in policy even as their main market closes. As Russia tightened controls after the March 2024 Crocus City Hall attack — entry bans, a register of controlled persons from February 2025, and roughly 96,000 Uzbek citizens returning in a single ten-day window in late summer 2026 — Tashkent raised migrant treatment directly with Russia's Interior Ministry, Prosecutor General's Office, and Foreign Ministry in August 2026, and ran organized recruitment to South Korea (about 102,200 Uzbek workers by July 2026), Turkey, Germany, the UK, Japan, and the EU, while remittances reached $15.17 billion in the first nine months of 2026 (Migration Agency via Times of Central Asia, 2026; Central Bank of Uzbekistan, 2026). Russia still supplied 72.4% of first-quarter 2026 remittances — the diversification is real and, as yet, small beside the exposure.

Evidence against:

  • Karakalpakstan is counted out, at the point of a gun and then in the courts. The July 2022 protests against his own draft amendments were dispersed with what Human Rights Watch, after analyzing dozens of videos and photographs, called unjustifiable lethal force: small arms and grenades used against mainly peaceful demonstrators. The official toll is 21 dead — 18 announced on 4 July, rising to 21, including 4 law enforcement officers — with 243–270+ injured and 516 detained; Karakalpak survivors, families, and defenders believe the true toll is substantially higher, possibly far higher, and no independent investigation has ever verified the count, because access was restricted and none was permitted (HRW, 7 November 2022 and 1 July 2024; US State Department, 2022 Human Rights Report; Tom Lantos Human Rights Commission briefing, 2025 — the higher figures are the families' and activists' claims, unverified, and are labeled as such).
  • The counting-out continued in process. Two trials prosecuted 61 people, including lawyers, bloggers, and journalists; Dauletmurat Tazhimuratov, the lawyer who had called for a peaceful, permitted protest, was convicted in Bukhara on 31 January 2023 and sentenced to 16 years for conspiracy to seize power and related charges — charges the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found rested on a detention that was arbitrary and violated his freedom of expression, and in which he testified he had been beaten with a stun gun and stamped on the head in custody, allegations the authorities denied (UNWGAD Opinion; trial reporting, January 2023). The Supreme Court upheld his sentence in June 2023. On 1 May 2026 a Navoi court added a further five-year sentence — partially merged, raising his total to 18.5 years — for "actions disrupting the work of a penal institution," designated him an especially dangerous repeat offender, and ordered transfer to a stricter regime, in proceedings his family and lawyer were barred from until the final day; he has repeatedly alleged torture and ill-treatment in custody, including beatings and hazardous forced labor, which six international organizations took up in a joint statement on 8 October 2026 demanding his release (Fergana News, May 2026; Civil Rights Defenders and HRW statements, October 2026; IPHR/CIVICUS Monitor report, June 2025–May 2026). Lolagul Kallykhanova, founder of the Karakalpak outlet Makan.uz, was sentenced to eight years' restricted liberty over the same events (European Parliament, 2023). Karakalpak activists in the diaspora have been detained in Kazakhstan amid allegations of Uzbek law-enforcement involvement, and in February 2024 the Supreme Court designated the Alga Karakalpakstan movement extremist (HRW, July 2024).
  • His opponents' voters are counted out of politics altogether. With no opposition party registered since 2003 and no candidate permitted to criticize him (Tests 1–2), roughly the whole electorate that might vote against him has no lawful instrument; ODIHR's verdict — no genuine choice — is the formal record of that exclusion (ODIHR, 2023 and 2024).
  • The labor migrants' exposure is itself partly his count's debit: two decades into the reform era, well over a million citizens still work in Russia — Uzbek citizens recorded about 1.18 million work-purpose entries in the first half of 2026 alone — because domestic job creation, though real, has not absorbed a workforce growing by hundreds of thousands a year; the World Bank notes 22% of the population remains vulnerable to falling back into poverty (Central Bank of Uzbekistan; World Bank country page, 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since December 2016, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Economic opening — kept. The September 2017 currency unification ended the dual exchange rate at a stroke; trade barriers were lifted, foreign investment courted, and borders with Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan — sporadically closed under Karimov — reopened under border-delimitation agreements and a regional thaw that included softening the Rogun dam dispute. GDP rose from roughly $70 billion in 2017 to about $147 billion in 2025 (World Bank data; presidential address, August 2026).
  • Forced labor — kept, verifiably. He pledged to end state-imposed forced and child labor in the cotton harvest; the ILO verified its elimination as a system in the 2021 harvest (Test 4). This is the series' clearest example of a word kept to an external referee's standard.
  • Poverty reduction — substantially kept on the referee's numbers. The World Bank's series (17.0% in 2021 → 5.8% in 2025) documents delivery on the central material promise of his "New Uzbekistan" strategies, with growth that the Bank assesses as pro-poor (World Bank, April 2026). His own larger claim (35% → 4%) is printed in Test 4 with its methodology caveat.
  • The reform rationale for the 2023 constitution, stated at full strength: the new text enshrined habeas corpus, a ban on the death penalty, property rights, data protection, and social guarantees, and his government's position was that a substantially new constitution restarted the term count as a matter of law — a position the Constitutional Court endorsed before the referendum. The rights provisions are real text; the term consequence is graded below as a matter of his word, not of the text's other contents (referendum record, April 2023; Britannica summary).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or recast):

  • The term limit — recast in his own favor. Mirziyoyev was elected in 2016 and 2021 under a constitution limiting presidents to two consecutive five-year terms; on that text his eligibility expired in 2026. The April 2023 referendum — approved by a reported 90.2% on 84.5% turnout, in a process ODIHR found lacked genuine political pluralism, with the campaign "entirely one-sided" per CIVICUS — extended terms to seven years and reset his count to zero, making him eligible to govern until 2037 (referendum record; ODIHR referendum report, December 2023; Freedom House, 2024). He then called a snap election and took the first of the new terms in July 2023. A limit that binds only until the holder can amend it has not been kept in any sense the word-test recognizes; his predecessor used the same device, as Freedom House noted.
  • The open word on media — reversed by his own signature. In the opening years he publicly defended bloggers and rebuked officials who pressured them (2019), and his administration's stated aim was a freer press; in March 2021 he signed the criminalization of online insult of the president and of online calls to protest, under which bloggers were then imprisoned (Test 1; HRW, World Report 2022).
  • Accountability for Karakalpakstan — promised, undelivered. After July 2022 he announced the events would be investigated and the guilty held responsible; an Ombudsperson-led commission was created. Four years on, its findings have not been transparently published, no senior official has been held accountable for the lethal force his own prosecutors acknowledged investigating, and the state's energy has gone instead into further prosecuting the region's most prominent prisoner (Test 4; HRW, July 2024; US State Department, 2022).
  • The succession question — answered against the opening's spirit. The 2016 transition was sold as a break with the Karimov pattern of personalized, family-centered rule. By November 2026 his daughter heads a constitutionally entrenched Administration ranked above the prime minister in oversight of the Cabinet, sits as deputy chair of the Security Council, addressed the UN General Assembly in September 2026, and holds — with her staff and family — lifetime immunity from prosecution (Tests 1–2). Analysts dispute whether this is succession preparation or elite stabilization (Jamestown Foundation, September 2025, argued the latter); either way, the structure built is dynastic in form whatever its intent.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Shavkat Mirziyoyev's first years delivered the most consequential liberalization in Uzbekistan's post-Soviet history, and the receipts are external: the ILO verified the end of systemic forced labor, the World Bank verifies a poverty rate cut by two-thirds on its series, prisoners of the old regime walked free, and a closed economy opened. Those gains stand in this record at full strength. But the tests measure the seat, and the seat has since been rebuilt around one household: elections without opponents or criticism, a prosecution that tries the sensitive cases in closed military courts, a criminal article for insulting the president, and — in August 2026 — a constitutional law placing his daughter's office above the Cabinet with lifetime immunity for its staff and their families. The single open adjustment of his tenure, the withdrawal of the Karakalpakstan amendments within a day, was answered to the same people with lethal force, 21 officially counted dead, no senior accountability, and a protest leader whose sentence grew to 18.5 years in 2026. He kept his word where the promise enriched the country; he recast it where the promise limited him — and the limit, in the end, was the one promise the rest depended on.
