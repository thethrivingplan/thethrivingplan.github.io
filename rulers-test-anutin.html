The Ruler's Test — Anutin Charnvirakul, Prime Minister of Thailand

Subject: Anutin Charnvirakul — Prime Minister of Thailand, in his second consecutive term at grading. Leader of the Bhumjaithai Party (whose de facto leader is Newin Chidchob, who holds no formal party position). Previously Minister of Public Health (2019–2023), where he authored the June 2022 removal of cannabis from the narcotics list, and Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Interior (2023–June 2025). He withdrew Bhumjaithai from Paetongtarn Shinawatra's coalition on 18 June 2025, after the leak of her telephone call with Cambodia's Hun Sen; after the Constitutional Court removed Paetongtarn on 29 August 2025, the People's Party — the largest party in the House — backed him as prime minister under a five-point memorandum of agreement (MOA) signed 3 September 2025. The House elected him on 5 September 2025 and he took office on 7 September 2025, also taking the Interior portfolio himself. He dissolved the House on 12 December 2025, won the general election of 8 February 2026 (Bhumjaithai 192 of 500 seats), was re-elected prime minister by the House on 19 March 2026 by 293 votes, and his second cabinet — a 16-party coalition holding about 292 seats — was sworn in on 6 April 2026. He remains prime minister and, concurrently, Minister of Interior, and faces his first censure debate on 7–10 December 2026, with votes on 11 December. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The machinery of the state has moved against his own camp's edges, in public, during his premiership. On 14 September 2026 the Election Commission (EC) resolved to petition the Supreme Court for criminal proceedings against 77 people over alleged collusion in the 2024 Senate selection, including 26 serving senators of the "blue" bloc aligned with his party; if the Court accepts the petitions, those senators' duties are suspended (Election Commission resolution, 14 September 2026; ISEAS Perspective 2026/80, 11 November 2026).
  • A state agency sued his own party's founding family while he was prime minister. On 14 October 2025 the State Railway of Thailand (SRT) filed suit in Buriram against Karuna Chidchob — wife of Newin Chidchob — and Silachai Buriram Co., Ltd., seeking revocation of two title deeds at Khao Kradong (deeds No. 3466 and No. 8564) as unlawfully issued over SRT land (SRT filing, 14 October 2025, as reported by Khaosod and Matichon). The SRT governor who signed the filing resigned in the same week; no documented act of Anutin's government stopped the suit.
  • No prosecution of an opponent by his government is documented in the record reviewed. The most prominent legal case against an opposition figure of his term — People's Party list-MP Rukchanok Srinork's lèse-majesté sentence, reduced on appeal in 2026 — arises from posts made in 2021 and a first-instance verdict of December 2023, before his premiership, through courts his government does not direct (Criminal Court and Court of Appeal records, 2023–2026).
  • He submitted to a written constraint no Thai prime minister had accepted before: the five-point MOA of 3 September 2025, under which the People's Party's votes installed him on conditions — dissolution within four months, a minority government, a constitutional referendum path — that limited his own room to maneuver, and he dissolved the House inside the deadline (see Test 5).

Evidence against:

  • The Senate collusion case stops, so far, one ring below his own leadership. The EC's 14 September 2026 decision referred 77 people to court but voted — mostly by 5–2 margins — not to pursue the Section 76 allegations against 21 political figures, among them Anutin himself and Newin Chidchob, on the stated ground that the three witnesses against them were not credible (EC ruling of 14 September 2026, reasoning published October–November 2026, via Matichon). The collusion itself remains ALLEGATION — no court has ruled — but the asymmetry is documented: the case proceeds against senators and operatives and stops at the party leadership named in the petitions. ISEAS records that the exclusion "has prompted public questions about its judgement" and "leaves the dominant network largely intact"; protesters rallied on 13 September 2026 demanding charges against 229 people (ISEAS Perspective 2026/80, 11 November 2026; AFP, 13 September 2026).
  • The Khao Kradong land file stopped inside a ministry he personally headed — twice. Thailand's Supreme Court ruled that the 5,083 rai at Khao Kradong, Buriram, belong to the SRT, and the Central Administrative Court ruled on 30 March 2023 that improperly issued titles be revoked after a boundary inspection. While Anutin was Minister of Interior (2023–2025), the Department of Lands' Section 61 committee — whose composition a senator publicly questioned, alleging one member was a local mayor from the "Newin Friends" network (Senator Nantana Nanthawaropas, Senate sitting, 6 January 2025; the allegation is hers) — resolved unanimously on 22 October 2024 not to revoke or amend the titles. At least 12 plots totalling 288 rai in the area are held by the Chidchob family and its network, including the plot housing the residence of Newin Chidchob's brother (Interior Ministry findings published 1 August 2025). Anutin answered the Senate on 6 January 2025 that his ministry acted "in full accordance with the law" and that revocation "must not be due to political pressure" — a defense of the inaction, on the record.
  • The revocation was ordered only after his party left the government: Interior Minister Phumtham Wechayachai announced the revocation order on 1–2 August 2025, under a Pheu Thai-led ministry, and a former Senate candidate then filed a DSI complaint naming Anutin among officials alleged to have neglected their duty over the non-revocation (DSI complaint, August 2025 — ALLEGATION; no finding against him is documented). Anutin returned as prime minister in September 2025, again holding the Interior portfolio and with it the Department of Lands; as of grading, no completed revocation under his government is documented in the record reviewed, and the SRT's remedy has proceeded by lawsuit (above) rather than by his department's order.
  • The opposition's censure motion of 8 October 2026 — signed by 145 MPs and directed at Anutin and 17 ministers — alleges, among other things, misuse of his authority as interior minister to benefit political associates and favouritism in appointments (motion under Section 151, 8 October 2026). These are ALLEGATIONs to be debated on 7–10 December 2026, not findings; they are recorded here because they name the same pattern as the two documented files above, and the debate is the forum the constitution provides for testing them.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the Election Commission, media, the central bank, provincial governors, and coalition partners keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The central bank's autonomy is intact and publicly defended by his own government. Bank of Thailand Governor Vitai Ratanakorn — appointed in July 2025, before Anutin took office — has served undisturbed; the Monetary Policy Committee has set rates (cut from 1.50% to 1.00% over the past year, then held unanimously) without documented government interference. On 16 November 2026 Finance Minister Ekniti Nitithanprapas stated on the record that the government "will not interfere with the central bank's policy tools" and reaffirmed support for the Bank's independence; when the Bank publicly pushed back the same day against proposals to tie a governor's performance review to dismissal, the finance minister's stated position was that any change to the Bank of Thailand Act must first be agreed inside the Bank itself (Bank of Thailand statement, 16 November 2026; The Nation and Bangkok Post reporting, 16–17 November 2026).
  • His first cabinet deliberately seated technocrats in the commanding economic posts — Ekniti Nitithanprapas at Finance, Suphajee Suthumpun at Commerce, Sihasak Phuangketkeow at Foreign Affairs — figures with no prior political careers, several of whom he retained after the 2026 election (cabinet records, September 2025 and April 2026; Bloomberg/Reuters reporting).
  • The Constitutional Court — the institution that dissolved the Move Forward Party on 7 August 2024 and removed Prime Ministers Srettha Thavisin (14 August 2024) and Paetongtarn Shinawatra (29 August 2025) — has neither been defied nor packed by him; its rulings frame his own path to office and he has operated inside them, including its September 2025 ruling that a referendum must precede the drafting of a new constitution (Constitutional Court records, 2024–2025). These are facts of the system he governs in; they are graded here only where his own conduct engages them, and his conduct toward the Court is compliance.
  • The Election Commission certified the February 2026 results on its own timetable — 499 seats in early March, the final contested seat (Suphan Buri constituency 2) only on 8 April 2026 — and the People's Party took its seats and the formal leadership of the opposition (Natthaphong Ruengpanyawut became Leader of the Opposition on 14 May 2026). No media outlet closure, prosecution of journalists, or dismissal of an election commissioner by his government is documented in the record reviewed.

Evidence against:

  • His party's network dominates the Senate, the body that confirms the membership of the independent agencies — including the Election Commission and the National Anti-Corruption Commission — that are supposed to check him. Of the 200 senators selected in 2024, 153 are described as "blue" senators affiliated with the Bhumjaithai network (2024 Thai Senate election records; ISEAS Perspective 2026/80). The collusion alleged to have produced that dominance remains ALLEGATION, unadjudicated; but the structural fact is not alleged: bloc voting in the Senate since 2024 points, in ISEAS's assessment, to "an organised political network," and through its provincial networks the party "has secured dominance in the upper chamber and extended its influence into institutions long associated with military and bureaucratic guardianship." The challenge to this consolidation now comes from within the establishment itself, led by former Constitutional Court judges and former election commissioners (ISEAS, 11 November 2026).
  • He holds the Interior Ministry himself, in both his cabinets — the ministry that commands every provincial governor and the Department of Lands — while a land case touching his party's founding family sits inside that department (Test 1) and while the opposition's censure motion alleges he used the ministry's appointment powers for associates (motion of 8 October 2026, ALLEGATION). The dual portfolio is lawful and not unusual in Thai politics; its effect here is that the state's provincial machinery and the premiership answer to one man whose own network is the subject of the two files above.
  • The presiding officer of the legislature is his party's man, and the opposition leader has accused him, on the record, of using the chair to slow scrutiny: on 9 November 2026 Natthaphong Ruengpanyawut charged House Speaker and Parliament President Sophon Saram (Bhumjaithai) with "monopolizing power," obstructing a petition for an investigation into the NACC, and delaying the joint session needed to move the constitutional amendment (Thai News Agency reporting, 9 November 2026). The characterization is the opposition leader's; the underlying delay in the charter process is documented fact (Test 5).
  • His government's December 2025 conduct in the charter-amendment vote showed where the grip is exercised: in the joint sitting of 11 December 2025, the rule requiring at least one-third of senators to concur in constitutional amendments (Section 256/28) was retained with Bhumjaithai support, against the People's Party's position — preserving the veto of a Senate his network dominates over any future charter (parliamentary record, 11 December 2025; AP and Reuters reporting). Anutin's position, stated 12 December 2025, is that the MOA never mentioned the Senate's power and that Bhumjaithai "cannot force or persuade Senators" — the senators in question being, on the documented bloc-voting record, substantially his own network's.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, coalition partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He corrected himself in public on the most sensitive subject in Thai politics — the border. In November 2025 he apologised to the public for suggesting that Thailand had encroached on Cambodian territory, pledging that "there is no possibility that Thailand will lose any territory, sovereignty, honour or dignity" (International Crisis Group, Asia Report N°359, 9 October 2026, citing the apology).
  • On the December 2025 war, he rejected a false account and then accepted a real settlement. When President Trump announced on 12 December 2025 that both sides had agreed to restore the ceasefire, Thailand's Foreign Ministry contested the claim — Foreign Minister Sihasak said some of Trump's statements did not "accurately depict the situation" — and fighting continued. Fifteen days later his government signed the 27 December 2025 Joint Statement ceasefire, negotiated bilaterally through the General Border Committee after an ASEAN foreign ministers' meeting, freezing troop movements and weapons use; Thailand released the 18 Cambodian soldiers it had held since July on 31 December 2025, and the ceasefire remained in effect as of May 2026 (Joint Statement, 27 December 2025; ICRC-facilitated release records, 31 December 2025).
  • He let the wobble of December 2025 end his first government rather than cling to it: when the People's Party withdrew support and prepared a no-confidence motion, he dissolved the House the same week (see Test 5 for the contested circumstances) and submitted himself to the voters, who returned him with a larger mandate — the adjustment was made at the ballot box, in the open.
  • His government acknowledges bad numbers in its own voice. Finance Minister Ekniti publicly described the 2026 farm-debt and price shock as a "cost-of-living crisis," and the government answered with documented measures — per-rai subsidies to rice farmers and a 176-billion-baht consumer subsidy programme within a 400-billion-baht borrowing decree (Reuters, 10 June 2026). The borrowing decree faces a legal challenge from the opposition — a wobble still being answered in court, not suppressed.
  • Facing his first censure motion, he agreed a timetable rather than evading one: after an earlier late-October schedule fell through amid a timetable dispute, government and opposition whips agreed debate on 7–10 December 2026 and individual votes on 11 December, a schedule the cabinet acknowledged on 3 November 2026 (Reuters, 3 November 2026). (Since 8 October 2026 the constitution has in any case barred him from dissolving the House to escape the motion — Section 151 — a constraint he has not attempted to circumvent.)

Evidence against:

  • The December 2025 dissolution was also a refusal to face the vote that was coming. The People's Party announced its no-confidence motion on 11 December 2025 because, in its leader's words, Bhumjaithai "did not follow the terms of their agreement" in that day's charter vote; the dissolution decree followed within hours, before the motion could be filed (AP, 12 December 2025; Reuters via government spokesman Siripong Angkasakulkiat: "This happened because we can't go forward in parliament"). Anutin's own account — "They said they wouldn't support me anymore. They asked me to dissolve the house. I just followed their request" (12 December 2025) — is on the record beside the sequence. Both readings are documented; the timing is not in dispute.
  • He dissolved the House during an active war, and said so plainly: the dissolution of 12 December 2025 came on the fourth day of renewed fighting with Cambodia that ultimately killed at least 101 people and displaced more than half a million across both sides (contemporaneous tallies; AFP, December 2025). He had assured reporters the dissolution would not affect military operations. Whether a caretaker government should be chosen mid-war was contested at the time by critics; his answer was the assurance, not a postponement.
  • The charter wobble he has not answered: nine months after 21.6 million voters approved drafting a new constitution (referendum of 8 February 2026, 58.64% in favour), parliament had not held even a first-reading vote on the amendment to open the drafting process (parliamentary record as of 10 October 2026, TP Channel; public criticism through October–November 2026). Government-side figures blame the reform camp's "hidden agendas" (Thanakorn Wangboonkongchana, 15 November 2026) — the pushback is answered with counter-accusation, not with a timetable.
  • On Cambodia's maritime file, his answer to a legal process is leverage: after his cabinet cancelled MOU 44 on 5 May 2026 and Cambodia invoked UNCLOS compulsory conciliation (2 June 2026), Anutin told Hun Manet at the APEC summit and repeated on his weekly programme on 21 November 2026 that "as long as Cambodia insists on compulsory conciliation, other negotiations will have to stop by default" (Thai PBS/Asia Daily reporting, November 2026). Thailand participates in the conciliation meetings (Singapore, September 2026; The Hague, October 2026; Kuala Lumpur, 24–27 November 2026) while publicly pricing the smaller state's use of the forum against the bilateral talks it needs — the documented posture of the larger party, stated in his own words.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — the People's Party's voters, the south, cannabis users and patients, border villagers, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The People's Party's voters are counted in the only way that finally matters: their party is seated, at full strength, as the largest opposition bloc, its leader formally installed as Leader of the Opposition (14 May 2026), its MPs chairing scrutiny and its censure motion scheduled with 51 of the debate's 72 hours allocated to the opposition (House records, 2026). The February 2026 election itself was held on the date the constitution required after his dissolution, its results were certified by the EC without his interference, and every party — including the one that installed and then abandoned him — took the seats it won (EC certification, March–April 2026).
  • Border villagers displaced by the December 2025 fighting — around 600,000 people, mostly in Thailand, at the displacement peak (AFP, December 2025) — were evacuated and sheltered by his government's provincial machinery, and the ceasefire his government signed on 27 December 2025 was the instrument that let them go home; the 18 Cambodian soldiers held since July were released under it on 31 December 2025.
  • Cannabis patients remain counted in: the framework his government is building keeps cannabis lawful for medical use — the plant has not been returned to the narcotics list — with access by prescription (PT33) from licensed shops, and the draft Cannabis Control Bill approved by his cabinet on 22 September 2026 writes the medical channel into statute rather than abolishing it (cabinet resolution, 22 September 2026; Ministry of Public Health, 2026). His stated position since 2022 has been that decriminalization was for medical and economic purposes; the medical promise is being kept in the form he always described.
  • His coalition arithmetic counts broadly by design: the 16-party government of April 2026 includes Pheu Thai — the party he abandoned in June 2025 and defeated in February 2026 — whose voters are thereby inside the government their party joined on 13 February 2026 (Reuters, 13 February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The 21.6 million referendum voters are, so far, counted in the asking and not in the doing. The February 2026 referendum mandated the drafting of a new constitution; nine months later the process has not passed a first reading in the parliament his coalition controls and his party's Speaker chairs (Test 3). The People's Party — whose voters supplied the largest single bloc of the reform vote — reads the delay as its mandate being shelved; the documented record is a mandate received and not yet acted on.
  • Cannabis users and the industry his 2022 policy created are counted out by his own government's reversal. Decriminalization on 9 June 2022 — his signature act as health minister — was followed, in the absence of the regulating act that never passed, by more than 10,000 shops and an openly recreational market. Under his premiership the state has moved against that market in stages: enforcement from October 2025 (84 dispensary licences suspended and more than 109 million baht of product seized by August 2026, Ministry of Public Health figures); a ministerial regulation of 30 April 2026 restricting flower licences to credentialed medical premises; and the cabinet-approved draft bill of 22 September 2026 making cannabis medical-only, with dispensary licences expiring in stages through 2028 and renewal only for medical facilities (Ministry of Public Health; cabinet resolution, 22 September 2026). Whatever the public-health merits — and the documented smuggling outflows through Thai airports are real (Global Initiative, Mekong Risk Monitor, August 2026) — the people who built lawful businesses under his 2022 policy, and adult users who were told the plant was no longer a narcotic, are being counted out by the same man, in the opposite direction.
  • He first reached national office through a count that excluded the winners. In 2023 the Move Forward Party won the general election (151 seats) and was blocked from government by the then-appointed Senate and dissolved by the Constitutional Court on 7 August 2024; Anutin served as Deputy Prime Minister and Interior Minister in the Pheu Thai-led government formed instead (August 2023–June 2025). That exclusion was the system's act before it was his — the Court and the old Senate did it — but he took and held senior office inside the arrangement for two years, and his own path to the premiership ran through the same chamber geometry: a Senate his network now dominates (Test 2) and a charter whose amendment rule that Senate can veto (Test 2). The People's Party's voters were counted out in 2023 by institutions he benefited from and did not challenge; they are counted in under him now, with their charter mandate stalled.
  • The deep south and the border's other side sit at the edge of his count. His coalition's strength is the provincial machine — ISEAS attributes Bhumjaithai's 2026 victory substantially to "the strength of its candidates and their networks, supported by incumbency advantages" and the consolidation of local "baan yai" houses (ISEAS Trends 2026/28) — a politics that counts where networks run. On the Cambodian side of the line his government holds, Cambodia states that some 20,000 displaced Cambodians cannot return home; Thailand's Foreign Ministry rejects the claim that Thailand prevents their return (both positions as documented in the Kuala Lumpur Joint Declaration one-year review, November 2026). The dispute is unresolved and the people in it are, for now, counted by neither side's machinery.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record, with dates — the dissolution pledge first, as the central word-file of this seat.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The dissolution pledge — kept, early. The MOA of 3 September 2025 required dissolution within four months of his policy statement to parliament (delivered 29 September 2025), i.e. by the end of January 2026. He dissolved the House on 12 December 2025 — some six weeks early — and the election followed on 8 February 2026, within the constitutional 45–60 days (Royal Gazette dissolution decree, 12 December 2025). The circumstances were self-serving (Test 3) and the People's Party disputes that the MOA was honoured in spirit; the letter of the central promise was performed ahead of time, and no Thai prime minister in the modern record had previously accepted such a deadline in writing at all.
  • The referendum pledge — kept. MOA points 2–3 required, following the Constitutional Court's ruling, a referendum on drafting a new constitution before or alongside the next election. The referendum was held on election day, 8 February 2026; 21,621,638 voters (58.64%) approved drafting a new constitution (Election Commission referendum results, February 2026).
  • The minority pledge — kept for the term it covered. MOA point 4 barred Bhumjaithai from building a majority government during the interim term; his first government held 146 MPs and never added coalition partners to reach a majority (House records, September–December 2025). The majority coalition he formed in April 2026 was built after a general election the MOA itself had mandated, on the voters' arithmetic, not against it.
  • MOU 44 — the substitute promise delivered. In the February 2026 campaign he proposed a referendum on cancelling the Cambodia memoranda (MOU 43 on the land border, MOU 44 on the maritime area); he dropped the referendum after the Council of State advised it might be unconstitutional, but pledged withdrawal from MOU 44 regardless. His cabinet cancelled MOU 44 on 5 May 2026, on his stated ground that it had produced no progress in 25 years (cabinet decision, 5 May 2026; Thai PBS World, 25 September 2025, for the original referendum plan; International Crisis Group, October 2026, for the sequence). The MOU 43 option he left open remains open.
  • Continuity promises to his own voters: the technocrat economic team of September 2025 was substantially retained after the election (Ekniti reappointed Finance Minister, March–April 2026), and the consumer co-payment subsidy model he campaigned on was delivered at scale in the 2026 borrowing programme (Reuters, June 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The charter itself — the MOA's purpose, undelivered. The agreement that made him prime minister existed to start Thailand writing a new constitution. Fourteen months after he signed it, and nine months after the referendum he correctly held approved the drafting, the amendment needed to convene an elected drafting assembly has not passed a first reading; the joint session the opposition demanded had not been convened as of November 2026, and his party's Speaker stands accused by the opposition leader of sitting on the process (parliamentary record; Thai News Agency, 9 November 2026). His defense — that the December 2025 amendment vote's Senate clause was never in the MOA — is literally arguable and is recorded in Test 2; but the MOA's object, a drafting process begun, has not begun, and the delay is occurring under his majority, in his second term, with no deadline announced.
  • Cannabis — his signature policy, reversed by his own hand. The man who took cannabis off the narcotics list on 9 June 2022 and rode the policy to national prominence now presides, as prime minister, over its restriction to medical-only use, enforced from October 2025 and legislated in draft from 22 September 2026 (Test 4). His long-stated framing — medical and economic, never recreational — is on the record and softens the reversal's character; it does not change the direction: the open market his policy created is being closed by his government, and the regulating Cannabis Act he promised in 2022 as the policy's necessary companion was never passed on his watch in either role.
  • The economic mood his word created has already been withdrawn by the public. In March 2026, 68% of Suan Dusit poll respondents expressed optimism about his returned government; by May 2026, 57% told the same pollster they had little or no expectation of its performance, as war-driven fuel and fertiliser prices met Thailand's farm-debt crisis (Suan Dusit University polling, March and May 2026, via Reuters). A poll is not a promise; it is recorded here as the measurable state of belief in his government's word on the cost of living, eight months into the term he won on competence and stability.
  • The 2025 apology's pledge — "no possibility that Thailand will lose any territory, sovereignty, honour or dignity" — is being kept on the land border at the price recorded in Test 3, while on the maritime file his government cancelled the framework agreement and now conditions all other talks on Cambodia abandoning a legal process (21 November 2026). Both halves are his stated positions; the second is a word given to his nationalist base that is paid for in the bilateral relationship, and the record shows him paying it openly rather than quietly — which is why this item sits as a tension in his file rather than a breach.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Anutin Charnvirakul is the only ruler in this series who took office on a written promise to give it up — and he kept it, dissolving the House six weeks early and winning the election that followed outright. That act is real, and it frames everything else in his file: a politician whose word, at the headline level, is unusually good, and whose machinery, at the network level, is unusually complete. The documented pattern beneath the headline is that the law in his Thailand runs until it reaches his own circle and there, so far, stops: the Senate collusion case proceeds against 77 people and halts below the leadership of the party the case is about; the Khao Kradong titles stood unrevoked through his own tenure at the ministry responsible, over land the courts had already awarded to the state, including plots held by his party founder's family. No court has found collusion by him or wrongdoing in the land file, and both matters are graded as what they are — documented asymmetries, not verdicts. Around that still centre the institutions demonstrably breathe: the central bank is independent and his finance minister says so on the record, the courts that destroyed his predecessors operate untouched, the opposition he wrong-footed in December 2025 will get its four days of censure in December 2026, and the referendum he promised was held and won. What he owes, on the evidence, is the charter: 21.6 million voters instructed the drafting of a new constitution in February 2026, and the process has not begun. His file is correctable in both directions — by the Supreme Court's handling of the Senate petitions, by the censure debate of 7–11 December 2026, and above all by whether the drafting assembly his signature made possible is ever convened.
