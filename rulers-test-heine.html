The Ruler's Test — Hilda Heine, President of the Marshall Islands

Subject: Hilda Cathy Heine — President of the Republic of the Marshall Islands since 3 January 2024, in her second, non-consecutive term. The seat graded is the presidency, and the framing is stated plainly: in the Marshall Islands the President is both head of state and head of government, elected by the Nitijela (the 33-member parliament) from among its own members, and she picks her cabinet from the Nitijela. There is no separate executive seat to grade. Heine, born 6 April 1951 in Jaluit Atoll, is an educator — the first Marshallese person to earn a doctorate and the first woman to lead an independent Pacific nation. She first held the presidency from 28 January 2016 to 13 January 2020, after the Nitijela removed Casten Nemra by no confidence and selected her; she survived a no-confidence vote in November 2018 by 16–16, one vote short of the 17 needed to remove her; and she lost the seat in January 2020 when the Nitijela selected David Kabua by 20 votes to 12. The premise of this dossier was verified before grading: some secondary mirrors of the CIA Factbook carry errors (one lists her as in office "since 3 January 2023," and the election date as "2 January 2023"), but the documented sequence is that the general election was held on 20 November 2023, the Nitijela selected her on 2 January 2024 by 17 votes to 16 over the incumbent Kabua — the absolute minimum majority — and she was sworn in on 3 January 2024 (DFAT country brief; PINA inauguration reporting, January 2024; CIA World Leaders listing, updated 2 September 2026). No premise correction to the holder is needed: she remained president through every 2026 check assembled here — opening the 5th Constitutional Convention on 28 October 2026 and scheduled for an official visit to Japan on 24–27 November 2026 (RMI Office of the President, October 2026; Japan Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 10 November 2026). No no-confidence motion, succession, or removal in her second term appears in the record assembled here; the next general election is due in November 2027. She is graded primarily on the second term, January 2024 to November 2026 — roughly two years and ten months — with her first term used only where it is the origin of a promise, a policy, or a reversal graded in the second. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test is printed here verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Every test closes with the reason for its grade in plain words and a named ledger of who was helped and who was hurt. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Her flagship domestic policy of the second term is, in its design, one law in cash form. Enra — the Individual Support Distribution, launched 26 November 2025 — pays the same quarterly amount to every eligible resident citizen, with no means test and no work requirement, dividing a fixed annual pool equally among everyone enrolled. The first payment was $201.92 to more than 33,000 people; by the fourth payment, in August 2026, $215.85 was paid to 40,714 people (Ministry of Finance programme descriptions; Marshall Islands Journal reporting via the programme administrator, 2025–2026; UNDP Pacific, 2026). Finance Minister David Paul stated the design's intent on the record: "We the government want to make sure no one is left behind" (statement reported in 2026 Enra coverage). A payment that does not ask who your family is, which atoll backed which candidate, or what you earn is the opposite of a favorites system.
  • The seat itself was taken without rigging the count, and the cabinet that followed crossed the divide. She won the presidency by a single vote, 17–16, and in response included several of Kabua's supporters when forming her cabinet on 9 January 2024 (presidential selection reporting, January 2024). The losing side was given seats at the table rather than exile from it.
  • Accountability machinery was strengthened in law, for everyone, during her term. Voters in the constitutional referendum of 25 April 2025 approved six of seven amendments, including the creation of an independent Ombudsman — independent of other authorities, holding office during good behavior to age 72, reporting at least annually to the Nitijela — approved by 76.43% of votes cast (RMI Electoral Administration results, certified 28 May 2025). The Nitijela then passed the Ombudsman's Office Act 2025 and the Ombudsman's Compensation Act 2025 (PacLII sessional legislation list, 2025). In its statement to the UN Convention Against Corruption conference (CoSP11, 2025), her government recorded that the Ombudsman carries investigatory and prosecutorial powers, that a new Procurement Regulation has been drafted, and that the Personal Data Protection Act 2025 was enacted — while also printing the review findings against itself: gaps in preventive measures, procurement oversight, beneficial-ownership transparency, and public-sector ethics identified by the UNCAC review cycles of 2015 and 2022 (RMI country statement to UNCAC CoSP11, 2025).
  • The tax relief of the 2026 fuel crisis was structured to reach the bottom first: in mid-March 2026 the Nitijela passed, and the government enacted, an exemption of the first $8,320 of income from the 8% withholding tax, which Finance Minister David Paul said would raise a worker's take-home pay by more than $600 a year — a design whose full value goes to low earners and only partially to high earners (Marianas Business Journal reporting, March–April 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The state's own books do not meet the one standard the law sets for everyone else. The independent audit of the Republic for the year ended 30 September 2024 — the first fiscal year of her second term — issued an adverse opinion on the aggregate discretely presented component units because financial statements were omitted for, among others, the College of the Marshall Islands, the Majuro Water and Sewer Company, and the RMI Ports Authority, and an adverse opinion on aggregate remaining fund information with a qualified opinion on governmental activities because statements were omitted for the Health Care Revenue Fund and other funds (Ernst & Young (RMI) report on compliance and internal control, FY2024, issued 1 September 2026). One law at the payout window; a weaker standard inside the government's own accounting.
  • The standing outside assessment of corruption enforcement predates her second term but had not been displaced by a contrary record at grading: Freedom House's 2024 country report scored safeguards against official corruption at 3 of 4 and recorded that corruption "has been a chronic problem," concentrated in the allocation of foreign aid, government procurement, and transfers, and that "high-ranking public officials, however, are rarely prosecuted for corruption" (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024). No documented second-term prosecution of a senior official for corruption appears in the record assembled here; that is recorded as an absence, not as proof that none occurred.
  • Emergency law was used to suspend the ordinary rules at the very start of the term. The 25 January 2024 state of emergency for the Marshalls Energy Company waived the Procurement Code to speed repairs and procurement for Majuro's power utility (Marianas Business Journal; RNZ Pacific, 26 January 2024). The emergency was real (Test 3), but a waived procurement code is, by definition, a second set of rules for the duration — and procurement is the exact area the UNCAC reviews and Freedom House flag as the country's corruption risk.
  • The 2025 citizenship amendment, approved by voters and in force under her government, narrowed who can become Marshallese: naturalization by marriage now requires ten years of residence, a legal marriage, and a Marshallese child (2025 constitutional amendment text; RMI Electoral Administration). It was the voters' decision in a referendum, not her decree, and it is recorded here as a tightening of the door that applies to one class of would-be citizens — spouses — and to no one born Marshallese.

Why: The grade is MIXED because the two shelves point in genuinely opposite directions at the same time. The money she designed herself — Enra — is scrupulously equal, and she built a constitutional watchdog with prosecutorial powers over her own government. But the audit of her first fiscal year is adverse precisely because parts of her government did not file the accounts the law requires, the procurement code was waived by emergency order, and the documented record contains no senior corruption prosecution to show the criminal law reaching the top. One law is visible in the design; it is not yet proven in the enforcement.

Helped: Every enrolled resident citizen, who received Enra on identical terms regardless of atoll, family, or politics (40,714 recipients by August 2026); low-wage workers, who gained the full value of the $8,320 income-tax exemption; supporters of the defeated Kabua camp, several of whom received cabinet seats in January 2024; future complainants against any official, who gained an Ombudsman with constitutional independence. Hurt: Taxpayers and Compact donors, whose money is accounted for in books that drew adverse audit opinions for FY2024; honest bidders under the Procurement Code, whose rules were suspended during the January 2024 energy emergency; foreign spouses seeking citizenship, who face a longer, narrower door under the 2025 amendment. No individual is named on the hurt shelf as a wrongdoer: the audit findings are institutional, and no court finding against a named official appears in this record.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • The referees who seated her are demonstrably not hers. The Electoral Administration ran the 20 November 2023 general election and the 25 April 2025 constitutional referendum; in that referendum the voters rejected one of the seven amendments put to them — the proposal to add a Mili Atoll member to the Council of Iroij, which won 63.62% but failed the two-thirds threshold — and the result was certified as a rejection on 28 May 2025 by Speaker Brenson Wase (RMI Electoral Administration; referendum results). A gripped electoral referee does not certify the government's side losing a question. Turnout was very low — about 10% of registered voters cast a ballot — which is a civic weakness recorded here, but it is a weakness of participation, not of presidential control.
  • The outside monitors who grade the referees give them their highest marks, across her tenure. Freedom House rates the Marshall Islands "Free" at 93 of 100, with political rights and civil liberties each at 1 of 7 (best), in the assessments covering 2024 and 2025 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024, 2025, and 2026 editions). The US State Department's 2024 Human Rights Report records that the constitution provides for freedom of expression including for the press, that the government generally respected it, and that "an independent media, an effective judiciary, and a functioning democratic political system combined to provide for freedom of expression" (US Department of State, Marshall Islands 2024 Human Rights Report). No documented act of hers — no judge removed, no editor prosecuted, no election result overturned, no central banker dismissed — appears in the record assembled here.
  • She spent her term building referees she does not control. The Ombudsman created by the 2025 amendment is constitutionally independent, nominated by the Speaker with the Nitijela's approval and only formally appointed by the President, removable only as the amendment provides (2025 constitutional amendment text). The Monetary Authority Act 2025 created an independent Monetary Authority to replace the Banking Commission: the Act was certified 23 September 2025, and the Authority formally commenced operations on 23 February 2026 when the Nitijela adopted the resolution appointing its Governor and board, with the Authority describing its own mandate as "independent oversight of all financial institutions licensed in the RMI" (Monetary Authority of the Marshall Islands, institutional history and board records, 2025–2026; IMF 2025 Article IV concluding statement, 10 September 2025, commending the Act). The IMF also recorded the Act's repeal of the 2018 Sovereign Currency Act — her own first-term project — as a governance achievement (Test 3).
  • The press that covers her most closely is privately owned and prints the failures. The Marshall Islands Journal — the country's one independent weekly — reported the absence of a working fire truck in downtown Majuro during the Nitijela fire, the near-doubling of the power utility's diesel costs, and taxi fare shocks hitting lower-income residents, without any documented retaliation (Marshall Islands Journal reporting via Marianas Business Journal and RNZ Pacific, 2025–2026).
  • When the legislature's own house burned, she rebuilt the referee rather than ruling around its absence: the Nitijela was sitting in a temporary home at the International Conference Center within a day of the 26 August 2025 fire, and her cabinet's fast-track plan prioritized a new parliament building over the government's own planned new capital complex (PINA, 27 October 2025; Marianas Business Journal, August 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The constitution itself fuses the executive and the legislature — the President and every cabinet minister are Nitijela members — so the separation this test prizes is structurally thin in the Marshall Islands. That is the design she inherited, not a grip she tightened, and it is recorded as the standing caveat on this grade, not as an act of hers.
  • Emergency powers were used three times in the term on the documented record: the January 2024 energy emergency (with the Procurement Code waived), the state of disaster declared two days after the August 2025 Nitijela fire, and the 90-day state of economic emergency of 26 March 2026, which ordered a moratorium on government hiring, salary increments, and new vehicle purchases across the whole public service (RNZ Pacific, January 2024; PINA, October 2025; PACNEWS, 31 March 2026). Each was time-limited, cabinet-endorsed, and issued under the Disaster Risk Management Act 2023 — and each concentrated decision-making in the presidency for its duration.
  • There is still no freedom-of-information law: the public and the press have no statutory right to compel government information, a gap noted in media-law assessments of the RMI. The referees are independent; the public's window into the executive remains a courtesy rather than a right.

Why: The grade is HOLDS because this test grades acts of gripping, and the documented record of the second term contains none — while containing repeated acts in the opposite direction: a referendum loss certified against the government's package, an Ombudsman and a Monetary Authority built to be independent of her, a free press printing her government's failures unpunished, and a parliament rehoused within a day of its destruction. The against shelf is real but structural and inherited — a fused executive-legislature and broad emergency powers — and the honest grade records that she held those powers without, on this evidence, closing her hand on the referees.

Helped: The Nitijela as an institution, rehoused in a day and rebuilt ahead of the government's own offices; voters, whose rejection of one constitutional amendment was certified and honored; the independent press and judiciary, whose autonomy the monitors record as intact; future holders of complaints and depositors, who gained an Ombudsman and an independent Monetary Authority. Hurt: No group is documented as hurt by an act of institutional gripping in this term. Public servants bore the cost of the March 2026 emergency moratorium — frozen hiring and salary increments for 90 days — imposed by executive order rather than by legislation; that cost is recorded here, under the emergency that imposed it, and it is the only entry the evidence supports on this shelf.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, the ruler at least considered it openly. Considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clearest wobble of the term was a building burning down mid-session, and the answer was open, fast, and institutional. The Nitijela fire of 26 August 2025 destroyed the chamber, library, and archives. The cabinet met in emergency session while the fire was still burning and approved a fast-track rebuild; the legislature resumed within a week at the International Conference Center; a state of disaster was declared two days after the fire; groundbreaking for the new building took place on 8 October 2025, with US$13 million from the United States and US$30 million from Taiwan acknowledged publicly by Heine at the ceremony, and she stated the loss and the plan to the nation the same afternoon as the fire (Marianas Business Journal, August 2025; PINA, 27 October 2025). Nothing was minimized: the irreparable loss of historical archives was stated on the record by her own government's reporting.
  • She reversed her own signature policy when the evidence and the referees turned against it. Her first government enacted the Sovereign Currency Act 2018, creating the SOV digital currency — a project that contributed to the November 2018 no-confidence motion against her, which she survived 16–16. In her second term, the Monetary Authority Act 2025 repealed that Act "in its entirety," a step the IMF's 2025 Article IV mission listed among the government's notable achievements (Nitijela legislation, Monetary Authority Act 2025, Schedule 1; IMF concluding statement, 10 September 2025). If another option becomes available, this is what considering it looks like in law: her own earlier answer was taken off the books by her own later government.
  • The same pattern appears on the nuclear file. The Marshall Islands had hesitated for decades to sign the Treaty of Rarotonga — the South Pacific Nuclear Free Zone Treaty — because of its defence ties to the United States under the Compact. On 3 March 2025, at the Nuclear Victims Remembrance Day commemoration, her government signed it, with Heine stating on the record that the country had yet to join several nuclear treaties "largely due to our unique historical and geopolitical circumstances" — the constraint named, the step taken anyway (Pacific Islands Forum Secretariat, March and September 2025; Arms Control Association, May 2025). Forum leaders formally welcomed and commended the signing in their September 2025 communiqué.
  • The March 2026 fuel shock was answered in the open, with the constraint named. After the 28 February 2026 US–Israeli attack on Iran, diesel prices in Majuro jumped nearly 40% (from $6.60 to $9.10 a gallon) and gasoline rose 17%; the power utility announced a near-25% rate increase. Heine's executive order of 26 March 2026 declared a 90-day state of economic emergency, established a Recovery Coordination Committee, ordered energy conservation across government (offices later closed at 3pm daily), and was paired with the $8,320 income-tax exemption and the on-schedule second Enra payment to over 40,610 citizens on 27 March 2026 (Marianas Business Journal, March–April 2026; PACNEWS, 31 March 2026; RNZ Pacific, March 2026). The no-good-choice clause applies here, by its mechanics. The options that actually existed were: (1) absorb the fuel shock with open-ended subsidies, draining Compact funds held for the future — real harm to the trust that funds Enra and future budgets; (2) pass the full cost through to households in a country where taxis are the de facto public transport and many live paycheck to paycheck — real harm, immediately, to the poorest; or (3) share the pain — conservation imposed on the government itself first, tax relief targeted at take-home pay, cash support continued, and a response plan adopted in April 2026. She took the third door, the least-harm door actually open. She did not build this trap: the country's import dependence is structural and decades old, and the price shock came from a war on the other side of the world. Hindsight options — a strategic fuel reserve that did not exist, a renewable grid not yet built — do not count as options refused. The residual wobble she does own is graded in Test 5: energy was her declared first priority from January 2024, and the country still entered 2026 this exposed.
  • Expert pushback on fiscal policy was engaged, not ignored. The IMF's 2025 Article IV consultation recommended replacing Enra with a targeted scheme "at the earliest feasible opportunity" and reprioritizing its cost toward the most vulnerable (IMF Executive Board, 4 December 2025). The government did not adopt the recommendation — and the addendum's rule governs how that is graded: the recommendation was received through a full Article IV process the government participated in, the authorities' own statement was published with the IMF report, and the Finance Ministry stated its reasons for the universal design publicly, including that no one be left behind and that targeting machinery in a nation of scattered atolls carries its own exclusion costs (IMF Country Report 25/321, including the Statement by the Authorities; UNDP Pacific, 2026). Considering and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence.

Evidence against:

  • On the digital sovereign bond, the expert warning was sharper and the adjustment is harder to see. The IMF's September 2025 concluding statement "recommends against proceeding with the planned global launch of the 'digital sovereign bond' USDM1 as planned, given significant risks and lack of prerequisite capacity," and the Executive Board repeated the warning in December 2025 (IMF, 10 September and 4 December 2025). The government proceeded with USDM1 as the digital-wallet channel of Enra — the Finance Ministry describing it as a US-dollar sovereign bond backed one-to-one by short-dated US Treasuries, and "not a cryptocurrency, central bank digital currency or stablecoin" (Ministry of Finance descriptions, 2025–2026; Stellar Development Foundation announcement, January 2026). The ministry's characterization is its own and is recorded as such; it is not independently verified in the record assembled here. Keeping the token as one of three payment channels — alongside paper checks and bank deposits — is a partial adjustment; launching the instrument at all, against a written IMF recommendation, in a country with a single remaining US correspondent banking relationship whose loss the IMF calls "a key vulnerability," is the doubling-down entry on this shelf.
  • The wobble she acknowledged latest was her own hospitals. In her August 2026 address to the Nitijela, Heine "acknowledged persistent problems in hospital management" and said the government was examining reforms to administration, patient safety, and medical services (PINA, 7 August 2026) — an admission that came two and a half years into the term, in the same season the government was advertising a $160 million rebuild of the two hospitals (Test 4). The buildings were answered before the management was.
  • The IMO shipping-levy campaign — her government's multi-year push for a universal greenhouse-gas levy on international shipping — met its sharpest defeat when adoption of the IMO framework was postponed in late 2025. Her response was to keep pressing the same demand into 2026, telling the UN General Assembly in September 2026 that the opportunity remained to adopt binding net-zero shipping regulations (UN General Assembly address, 25 September 2026). Persistence on an existential file is not denial; but the record shows no public reconsideration of the strategy after the postponement — the demand was repeated, not re-examined on the record assembled here.

Why: The grade is MIXED because both patterns are documented in the same term. Fire, fuel shock, and a failed referendum question were answered openly and institutionally; her own first-term currency law was repealed when the referees' verdict came in; a forty-year hesitation on the Rarotonga Treaty was ended with its reasons stated aloud. Against that, USDM1 went ahead against an explicit written IMF warning in the country's most fragile financial area, and the hospital-management failure was owned only in the term's third year. She answers the wobble when events force the question; she is slower when the pushback arrives as expert advice about her own projects.

Helped: The Nitijela and the public record, by a rebuild begun within weeks rather than a legislature left homeless; Majuro households in March–April 2026, who received tax relief and an on-time Enra payment during the fuel shock instead of the full pass-through alone; the financial system's stability, by the repeal of the SOV Act; nuclear-affected communities, whose cause gained the Rarotonga signature on Remembrance Day itself. Hurt: Holders of the country's financial future bear the USDM1 risk the IMF named — if the instrument damaged the last correspondent banking relationship, the harm would fall on every Marshallese household that receives remittances or imports goods, a risk recorded here as a risk, not as a realized loss; hospital patients, whose management problems were acknowledged in August 2026 after years of known difficulty; the archives and library collections destroyed in the fire — a loss her response could not undo, recorded here because the shelf records who bore the cost, not because the fire was her act.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the outer islands, Ebeye, the nuclear-affected communities, women, children, people with disabilities, the diaspora, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The count starts with the cash: Enra's design counts every resident citizen at the same value, children included, and the count demonstrably reached the whole country — 40,714 recipients by the August 2026 payment, in a nation whose 2021 census counted 42,418 people (a separate figure, printed separately: the two numbers come from different counts in different years). Heine told the Nitijela in August 2026 that more than 40,000 residents — over 95 percent of the population — received assistance in the first distribution (PINA, 7 August 2026; Marshall Islands Journal via programme administrator, 2026). Three payment channels — check, bank deposit, and phone wallet — were maintained precisely because bank branches are concentrated in Majuro and Ebeye while checks can take weeks to reach outer islands.
  • Her term opened by counting the most displaced community in the country. One of her two first states of emergency, in January 2024, was for Kili Island — home to the Bikini community, displaced by nuclear testing and then stranded when the Bikini Resettlement Trust Fund went bankrupt — where food and fuel had been depleted. The Marshalls Energy Company published a relief plan under the emergency covering generator repairs, fuel storage and delivery, and meters for Kili (MEC relief plan, May 2024; RNZ Pacific, January 2024). The nuclear-affected were also counted institutionally: she appointed a Presidential Envoy for Nuclear Justice and Human Rights, David Anitok, in January 2024; toured nuclear-affected populations in her first months; called at the UN General Assembly in September 2024 for a formal UN apology for the 67 nuclear tests of 1946–1958; and by August 2026 reported initial work completed on a national nuclear museum and research facility (RMI Office of the President, 2024; UNGA address, 25 September 2024; PINA, 7 August 2026).
  • The outer islands appear in the delivery record, not only in speeches. The "Connecting Our Islands for Learning" project, launched June 2025, will for the first time power and connect every public elementary school, including the most remote islands, with solar power and internet (UNICEF Pacific, 20 June 2025). The World Bank's Early Childhood Development Project II, implemented by her government, reaches Majuro, Ebeye, and outer-island communities: more than 2,400 families receive conditional cash transfers, 72 percent of children under two receive regular health checkups, and 95 percent of the bank accounts receiving payments are owned by women (World Bank feature, 17 June 2026). Her August 2026 Nitijela address reported 161 climate-resilient water storage units installed or in preparation — about 2.7 million gallons of capacity, reaching nearly 1,000 households and about 4,700 people — the relocation of Lukwon Elementary School on Mili Atoll as the country's first school moved because of climate change, and the purchase of two Cessna 408 aircraft to improve emergency medical transport to the outer islands, the first already operating in Wotje (PINA, 7 August 2026).
  • Ebeye — the densely populated island beside the US Kwajalein base, long the standing example of the counted-out — is in the capital programme: a 1.8-kilometre seawall and footpath on Ebeye was completed with World Bank, Green Climate Fund, and national support (Heine, UNGA address reporting, September 2026), and in July 2026 Finance Minister David Paul confirmed $160 million secured for new hospitals in both urban centres — $100 million for Majuro and $60 million for Ebeye (RNZ Pacific reporting, 6 July 2026). Social assistance under her government provided three months of support to 1,239 older people and people with disabilities (PINA, 7 August 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Enra's universality stops at the water's edge and at the citizenship line. Eligible recipients are citizens living in the Marshall Islands; the large Marshallese diaspora in the United States — a community built by Compact migration, in a nation whose population fell from 53,158 in 2011 to 42,418 in 2021, figures printed separately from the UNDP's account — receives nothing, and non-citizen residents receive nothing (UNDP Pacific, 2026; programme eligibility descriptions, 2025–2026). The finance minister's stated aim for the scheme included encouraging people to stay; a payment that counts only those who stay is also, by design, a line drawn around those who left — many of whom left because the services graded elsewhere in this test were not there.
  • The IMF's distributional objection stands on the record unanswered in substance: a flat payment spends the same Compact Trust Fund dollar on a cabinet minister's household as on an Ebeye household in poverty, and the Fund's Board-level recommendation was to replace the scheme with targeted support "to the most vulnerable" (IMF, 4 December 2025). The government's stated reasons for universality are recorded in Test 3; the arithmetic objection — at roughly $27 million planned for FY2026, 8.1% of GDP, drawn from a trust fund built substantially from nuclear-era compensation (IMF 2025 Article IV; UNDP Pacific, 2026) — is that universality spends the counted-in future generation's buffer on everyone equally, including those who need it least. Both figures and both arguments are printed; the dossier does not blend them into a verdict the evidence does not carry.
  • Women are counted as recipients — 95 percent of early-childhood payment accounts — but the violence ledger is the worst page in this file, and it has not moved on the documented record. The counts conflict and are printed separately, never blended: the UNDP/MLSC 2024 study reports that over 60% of Marshallese women have experienced some form of gender-based violence in their lifetime, with rural women facing greater barriers to justice and support; UN Women's country material, drawing on the earlier national family health study, reports about 36% of women experienced physical or sexual violence by a partner, with 22% reporting physical violence in the previous 12 months (UNDP Pacific, Rethinking GBV study, 2024–2025; UN Women Asia-Pacific, RMI country page). The 2011 Domestic Violence Prevention and Protection Act exists, but the UNDP study records that gaps in funding and resources hinder its full implementation, especially in rural areas. No Heine-specific, documented second-term initiative on gender-based violence at the scale of her energy or Enra programmes appears in the record assembled here; it is recorded as an absence. Political counting is likewise thin: the 2023 election returned a record four women to the 33-seat Nitijela — a record that is itself the measure of the exclusion (election reporting, November 2023).
  • The service gap the programmes are closing is documented as still open. UNICEF's June 2025 account states plainly that until now remote-island schools generally lacked the power and internet Majuro and Ebeye schools have, that teachers in those communities have limited training access, and that these challenges "contribute to significant and lasting inequalities in access to quality learning" (UNICEF Pacific, 20 June 2025). Her own August 2026 address acknowledged hospital management problems (Test 3). Counting everyone in the payment is documented; counting everyone in the service is, on this record, still in progress.

Why: The grade is MIXED because the counting is genuinely universal in the one place she designed from scratch — the cash — and genuinely incomplete where the state was already failing people before she arrived. Kili, Ebeye, the outer-island schools, and the nuclear-affected communities all appear in the delivery record with money and machinery attached. But the diaspora is outside the line by design, women facing violence have laws without funded reach, and the IMF's point that equal payments to all spend the vulnerable's buffer on the comfortable stands unanswered in arithmetic. Everyone is counted in the distribution; not everyone is yet counted in the protection.

Helped: Resident citizens across all atolls, who received four equal Enra payments in the programme's first year; the Bikini community on Kili, whose emergency opened her term; outer-island children, whose schools are being powered and connected for the first time; mothers, who hold 95 percent of the early-childhood payment accounts; Ebeye residents, who gained a completed seawall and a funded hospital; older people and people with disabilities, 1,239 of whom received three months of social assistance. Hurt: Marshallese citizens in the United States diaspora, excluded from Enra by the residence rule; non-citizen residents, excluded by the citizenship rule; women in rural and outer-island communities, for whom the domestic-violence law's protection remains underfunded and hard to reach; future generations of Marshallese, whose Compact Trust Fund is drawn down at 8.1% of GDP a year for payments that include households in no need — a hurt the IMF names and the government disputes on stated grounds, recorded here as contested, with both disputants named: the IMF Executive Board and the RMI authorities.

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 3 January 2024, with dates. The two axes are graded independently: word-keeping is graded here; who was helped and who was hurt is graded in the ledgers, and neither axis launders the other.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Energy was declared the first priority, and the machinery moved. On taking office in January 2024 Heine declared energy her first priority for the term (inauguration reporting, January 2024). The documented sequence that followed: the 25 January 2024 emergency declaration allocating emergency funding to the Marshalls Energy Company for generators, transmission, and distribution; the Kili relief plan of May 2024; a modernization programme the utility totals at over $130 million in grant funding, within which energy losses were cut from 26.5% to 13.4% in under two years under an ADB-supported programme; and, on 30 October 2025, US approval of $18.7 million through the Compact for three new Majuro generators, with a performance period through 30 September 2028 (RNZ Pacific, January 2024; MEC programme statements, 2024–2025). In her September 2026 UN address she stated that within 18 months renewable energy would cut fossil-fuel use in the remote outer atolls by 88%, giving many access to electricity for the first time — a dated claim recorded here so the record can grade it when it falls due (UN General Assembly address, 25 September 2026).
  • The Compact was carried over the line. The renewed Compact agreement — $2.3 billion over FY2024–43, including $700 million allocated to a trust fund for uncompensated nuclear damage — was signed by the previous government on 16 October 2023, but its funding depended on the US Congress, and it was her government that held the relationship and the joint lobbying through the delay: the three freely-associated-state presidents' letter to Senate leaders of 6 February 2024 stated the approval's importance could not be overstated, and the Compact of Free Association Amendments Act was signed into US law on 9 March 2024 (American Journal of International Law contemporary practice, 2024; US Department of the Interior, 11 March 2024; DFAT country brief). Credit is shared with her predecessor's negotiators and is recorded as shared; the word she gave on taking office — that the Compact would be secured — was kept within ten weeks.
  • The ocean pledges were enacted, not repeated. She pledged ocean protection in her government's programme; on 28 January 2025 the government designated Bikar and Bokak atolls and some 48,000 square kilometres around them as the country's first national marine sanctuary, fully protected from fishing, under the community-led Reimaanlok framework (Oceanographic Magazine; National Geographic Pristine Seas reporting, January 2025). At the UN Ocean Conference in June 2025 she championed a moratorium on deep-sea mining, and at the General Assembly that September she stated the enacted fact: "We have enacted our own national ban on deep seabed mining permits" (UNOC3 national statement, 9 June 2025; UNGA80 statement, 24 September 2025).
  • Enra was announced, then paid, four times. Whatever its origins as policy rather than campaign pledge (the Marshall Islands has no party platforms in the usual sense; the Nitijela elects the president from its members, and her mandate is the 17–16 selection of 2 January 2024), the programme her government announced in 2025 was delivered on its announced schedule: first payment 26 November 2025 ($201.92, over 33,000 people), second payment released 27 March 2026 to over 40,610 citizens, four payments completed by August 2026 ($215.85 to 40,714 people), and $32.5 million approved for the second year (programme records and Marshall Islands Journal reporting, 2025–2026). Under the mandate rule, carrying out what her government announced is evidence on this shelf, and the delivery is documented.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The Rarotonga ratification — "timely" has stretched past a year. Signing the treaty in March 2025, her government committed to ratification, and at the General Assembly on 24 September 2025 she said: "we also look forward to completing timely ratification" (UNGA80 statement). As of June 2026 the treaty record still listed the Marshall Islands as signed but not yet ratified (Treaty of Rarotonga status records, June 2026), and no completed ratification appears in the record assembled here through November 2026. The signature was the historic step; the word attached to it has not yet been completed.
  • The parliament rebuild's completion date has passed without a documented completion. At the 8 October 2025 groundbreaking, Heine promised the reconstruction "would be completed on time," with the contractor targeting completion in August 2026 (PINA, 27 October 2025). The start-date promise — work begun in 2025 — was kept. But her own August 2026 address to the Nitijela listed "a new parliamentary chamber" among infrastructure plans, and no documented completion or opening of the new building appears in the record assembled here through November 2026 (PINA, 7 August 2026). Recorded precisely: the completion is not documented, which is not the same as documented failure — the file will be corrected in the open when the building opens.
  • The first priority is not yet the delivered fact. Energy was priority one in January 2024; in March 2026 the country still depended on imported diesel so completely that a distant war forced a 90-day economic emergency, government offices onto shortened hours, and a near-25% utility rate increase on households (Marianas Business Journal, March 2026). The $18.7 million generators carry a performance period to September 2028 — after the next election. The direction is documented and the spending is real; the promise's substance — reliable, affordable power — remains, at grading, a programme in motion rather than a kept word. This is the same record graded in Test 3 under the no-good-choice clause for the 2026 shock itself; the two gradings are distinct — Test 3 graded her answer to the trap, this test grades the distance between the 2024 word and the 2026 fact.
  • The nuclear-justice word is kept in effort, unkept in outcome — and the outcome was never hers alone to deliver. She has called for a formal UN apology for the 67 tests at the General Assembly in 2024, 2025, and again in September 2026, and for fuller US compensation (RMI Office of the President, September 2024; PINA, 28 September 2026). No apology has been given. A promise to demand is kept by demanding, and she has; a reader grading the outcome rather than the effort should note the distinction is printed here deliberately.

The two axes, named separately: Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded independently, and all four crossings are named for this record. (1) Kept her word AND it helped people: the Compact's enactment in March 2024 and Enra's four delivered payments — the word was given, the money arrived, and the ledgers in Tests 1 and 4 record who it helped. (2) Kept her word AND it still hurt people: not documented as a clean case in this record — the nearest entry, the deep-seabed-mining ban, kept an ocean pledge and forecloses a possible future revenue source, but no documented person or community is shown harmed by it, and the dossier records "not documented" rather than inventing the harm. (3) Went against her word AND it helped people: the repeal of her own Sovereign Currency Act — the SOV was her first government's enacted policy, and its repeal by her second government reversed her own earlier position; the IMF records the repeal among the term's governance achievements, and the help is recorded in Test 3's ledger in full, as is the break recorded here. (4) Broke her word AND it hurt people: not documented as a clean case in this record — the receding items above (Rarotonga ratification, the parliament completion date) are words not yet completed whose hurt is institutional and diffuse rather than documented against named people, and they are graded as receding words in the shelf above, not promoted into a crossing the evidence does not carry.

Why: The grade is MIXED because the dated, checkable promises split. The Compact was secured in ten weeks, the marine sanctuary and the seabed-mining ban were enacted as pledged, Enra paid four times on schedule, and the energy programme she named her first priority has real money and measurable loss-reduction behind it. Against that, the Rarotonga ratification she called "timely" is over a year late and counting, the parliament she promised "on time" for August 2026 has no documented opening, and Majuro entered 2026 still one fuel shock away from emergency. Her word is good on the things wholly in her hands; it stretches on the things that take longer than a speech.

Helped: All resident citizens, by the Compact's $2.3 billion secured and Enra's four payments delivered as announced; Majuro electricity customers, by the funded generator and loss-reduction programme; the ocean estate and the fishing communities who depend on it, by a sanctuary and a mining ban enacted rather than promised; nuclear-affected communities, by an envoy, a museum begun, and an apology demanded on the record every year. Hurt: The Nitijela as an institution and the public whose house it is, sitting in a temporary conference centre past the promised completion season; Pacific partners who took the Rarotonga signature as a ratification in progress; households that paid the March 2026 fuel and electricity increases while the energy promise remained in motion. No crossing in which a broken word demonstrably hurt named people is documented in this record, and none is invented here.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: HOLDS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED.

Hilda Heine holds the narrowest mandate in this series — seventeen votes to sixteen in a 33-seat parliament, in a country with no party platforms, chosen by the Nitijela rather than by direct popular vote — and the record of her second term is that of a ruler whose instincts run toward building referees and paying everyone the same, and whose weaknesses run toward time and follow-through. On one law, the grade is MIXED because Enra pays every resident citizen identically and she gave the country a constitutional Ombudsman with prosecutorial powers, while her own government's FY2024 books drew adverse audit opinions and the procurement code was waived by emergency order: helped, every enrolled citizen and every low-wage worker; hurt, the taxpayers whose accounts those were and the spouses facing a narrowed citizenship door. On gripping, she HOLDS — the only clean grade in the file — because the electoral referee certified her side's referendum loss, the press prints her failures unpunished, Freedom House scores the country 93 of 100 Free across her tenure, and she spent the term creating an Ombudsman and an independent Monetary Authority rather than capturing any: hurt, on this evidence, no one by an act of gripping, with public servants bearing only the 90-day emergency freeze. On the wobble, the grade is MIXED because the Nitijela fire and the March 2026 fuel shock were answered openly and institutionally — the fuel shock graded under the no-good-choice clause, the least-harm door taken in a trap she did not build — and her own SOV currency law was repealed when the referees turned, while USDM1 proceeded against a written IMF warning and the hospital failure was owned only in the third year: helped, households shielded from the full fuel pass-through; hurt, the bearers of the financial risk the IMF named. On counting everyone, the grade is MIXED because the cash counts all resident citizens, Kili's displaced community opened her term, Ebeye gained a seawall and a funded hospital, and outer-island schools are being connected for the first time — while the diaspora sits outside Enra's line by design, the domestic-violence law remains underfunded beyond Majuro on counts printed separately above (over 60% lifetime prevalence in the 2024 UNDP study; about 36% partner violence in the earlier national study), and the trust fund that pays everyone is drawn at 8.1% of GDP against the IMF's stated objection. On her word, the grade is MIXED because the Compact was secured in ten weeks, the sanctuary and the mining ban were enacted, and Enra paid four times as announced — while the Rarotonga ratification she called timely is still incomplete, the parliament's promised completion has no documented opening, and the energy priority of January 2024 still met a diesel emergency in March 2026. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the Ombudsman is funded and used, whether ratification is completed, whether the new Nitijela building opens, whether USDM1's risk stays theoretical, and whether the outer-atoll renewable programme meets its own 18-month, 88-percent claim when it falls due in 2028.
