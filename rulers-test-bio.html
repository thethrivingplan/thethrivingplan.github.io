The Ruler's Test — Julius Maada Bio, President of Sierra Leone

Subject: Julius Maada Bio — President of the Republic of Sierra Leone since 4 April 2018. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: Sierra Leone's 1991 Constitution makes the president both head of state and head of government — chief executive, commander-in-chief, and the appointing authority for the electoral commission, the judiciary's senior ranks, and the Anti-Corruption Commission. There is no separate executive seat to grade. Bio is a retired brigadier who first held power as a soldier: a member of the NPRC junta from 1992, he took over as head of state in January 1996 and handed power to an elected civilian government that March — the precedent he himself cites for his democratic word. He won the presidency at the ballot box in 2018 (51.8% in a run-off against Samura Kamara of the All People's Congress) and was declared re-elected on 27 June 2023 with 56.17% of the vote in the first round, just above the 55% threshold that avoided a run-off — a result the APC rejected and international observers faulted for tabulation opacity, examined in Tests 2 and 3. He is graded on the full tenure, April 2018 to November 2026, weighted to the second term, with the first term's record admitted where a promise or a pattern runs across both. His second term is constitutionally his last (Section 46), and he has repeatedly and publicly ruled out a third term, most recently on 26 October 2026 — a word recorded in Test 5 and gradeable in full only when the 2028 handover happens or doesn't. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Casualty and toll figures are kept in separate sourced counts and never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Under the mandate rule, governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure: Bio ran in 2018 on a "New Direction" of free education, anti-corruption, and discipline, and in 2023 on the "Big Five" — Feed Salone, human capital, youth employment, technology and infrastructure, and public-service reform. Carrying that platform out is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how it is done — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as two independent axes, and all four crossings are named in Test 5. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machine he built runs, measurably, against the whole administrative state and not only against his enemies. The Anti-Corruption Commission under Francis Ben Kaifala — his 2018 appointee — reported for 2025 that 12 of the 13 persons it brought to trial were convicted, that it sanctioned 1,541 public officers for defaulting on their 2024 asset declarations and drove compliance to 99.54%, and that it recovered NLe 23.56 million in that year alone (ACC 2025 Annual Report, presented to the President 16 September 2026). The 2019 amendment to the Anti-Corruption Act raised penalties and added restitution and trial in absentia; the Commission's non-conviction asset-recovery model has returned tens of billions of old leones to the treasury across the tenure (ACC reporting, 2019–2026).
  • The outcome measures moved the right way for eight consecutive years, on outside instruments rather than his own say-so: Sierra Leone has passed the MCC's Control of Corruption indicator every year since 2018 (from a failing 49% in 2017 to scores around 80%), and its Transparency International CPI ranking improved from 129th of 180 in 2018 to 109th in 2025 (ACC 2025 Annual Report, citing Transparency International and MCC scorecards).
  • The accountability process against the predecessor government ran through courts, and its findings survived appeal: the Commissions of Inquiry into the Koroma era (2007–2018) were judicially chaired, and when Samura Kamara appealed the findings against him over the sale of the state's Sierra Rutile shares, the Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal on 12 December 2023 and ordered him to refund USD 727,364 (Court of Appeal judgment, December 2023, reported in The Calabash Newspaper).

Evidence against:

  • The flagship criminal files of the tenure map onto the political map. The ACC indicted Samura Kamara — by then the APC's presumptive 2023 candidate — in November 2021 over the New York chancery renovation (six officials charged, USD 4.2 million; Kamara charged with misappropriation of USD 2.56 million, an ALLEGATION he has always denied). The trial opened in December 2021 and, five years on, has produced no verdict; under cross-examination the ACC's own chief investigator was reported to have conceded that Kamara neither approved nor signed the payments in question (trial reporting, 2024; the concession is recorded here as reported testimony in partisan-aligned outlets, not as a court finding). A prosecution that hangs over the opposing candidate through an entire election cycle and never lands is a second law in effect whatever its intent.
  • The treason file against former president Ernest Bai Koroma followed the same arc at higher stakes: charged in January 2024 with treason, misprision of treason, and harbouring suspects after the 26 November 2023 attacks, allowed to leave for Nigeria on medical grounds within the month, and then — on 14 July 2026 — the Attorney-General discontinued the proceedings entirely under Section 45 of the Criminal Procedure Act 2024, giving no reasons beyond the exercise of his legal powers (Information Minister Chernor Bah cited health grounds). Koroma denied involvement throughout. A treason charge against a former head of state that is neither tried nor explained, but simply switched off after two and a half years, is the one-law principle failing at its most visible point: the law reached him when he was an opponent in exile and released him when the politics changed, and the record contains no judicial word on whether it should have reached him at all.
  • The August 2022 accountability ran in one direction only. After the 10 August 2022 protests, 515 people were arrested and prosecuted for offences from unlawful procession to murder (Amnesty International, March 2023). The government's own Special Investigation Committee reported in March 2023 recommending police training in avoiding heavy-handedness but did not recommend investigating the use of force, and as of Amnesty's October 2023 review none of the civilian deaths had been investigated and no member of the security forces faced any process for them. Protesters faced the law; the shooters faced a training recommendation.
  • The law's most conspicuous non-application concerns Jos Leijdekkers, the Dutch trafficker convicted in absentia in Rotterdam in June 2024 (24 years, for cocaine smuggling and ordering a murder). Dutch authorities state he has lived in Sierra Leone since at least 2023 and filed a formal extradition request in early 2025; Reuters reported, citing photographs and video, that he attended a church service on 1 January 2025 seated near President Bio, and Dutch reporting has alleged a personal connection to the president's family — an ALLEGATION the government has not confirmed and his lawyer's denials do not address directly. The government's position is that the president was unaware of the man's identity, that no extradition treaty exists with the Netherlands, and that, in Chief Minister David Sengeh's words, "our security intelligence does not know where Leijdekkers is." As of November 2026 he has not been arrested or extradited (Spear News editorial review, November 2026). A state that can find 515 protesters in a week states it cannot find Europe's most wanted man inside its own borders; whether the explanation is incapacity or protection is contested — the Netherlands and OCCRP reporting say sheltering, the government says lawful process — but the asymmetry is a documented fact, and Test 1 grades asymmetry.

Why the grade lands here: there are not two laws in Sierra Leone's administration — the ACC's machine genuinely bites across the public service, and the external scorekeepers record eight years of it. But at the political summit the law behaves differently: it moves slowly or not at all against the seat's own exposure (Leijdekkers), it hangs verdictless over the seat's challenger (Kamara), it is switched on and off by executive notice against the seat's predecessor (Koroma), and it investigates only one side of a day on which both sides died (August 2022). One law in the machine, a discretionary law at the top: that is MIXED, and the open files named below are the ones that would move it. Helped: the treasury and the public, who got a functioning asset-declaration regime, sustained recoveries, and eight years of improving corruption scores; the roughly 1,500 sanctioned officers' institutions, whose compliance the sanctions produced; Sierra Leone's standing with the MCC and donors, which the corruption record underwrote. Hurt: Samura Kamara, who contested a presidency under an unresolved indictment; Ernest Bai Koroma, exiled for two and a half years under a treason charge that was never tried and never explained; the families of the August 2022 civilian dead, for whom the law prosecuted 515 people and investigated none of the deaths; the Dutch and European public, whose convicted trafficker remains, on the documented record, beyond the law's reach in Sierra Leone.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The single most durable loosening of the grip in modern Sierra Leonean history is his: in October 2020 he signed the repeal of Part V of the Public Order Act 1965, ending 55 years of criminal and seditious libel — a law every predecessor had kept and used. Parliament passed it unanimously, and no journalist has since faced a libel charge. "For more than half a century," Bio said at the signing, "we had a legislative and governance regime that criminalised journalism."
  • When his own party's Constitutional Amendment Bill reached his desk in September 2026 in contested circumstances (below), he did not sign it. On 14 September 2026 he invoked Section 122(1) of the Constitution — an unprecedented use — and referred the petition against the Bill to the Supreme Court for a judicial opinion before taking any decision on assent, telling the Clerk of Parliament his final decision would follow the Court's opinion (State House release, 14 September 2026). A president who pauses his own majority's constitutional bill and asks the referee is the test's FOR shelf in its purest form.
  • The referee then bit his own government, in public. On 20 October 2026 the Supreme Court, under Chief Justice Komba Kamanda, struck out a motion by Bio's Attorney-General to dismiss the constitutional reference itself, calling it an attempt to forestall proceedings; the substantive hearing — broadcast live, a first in Sierra Leone's history — was rescheduled to 27 November 2026 and is pending at grading (Sierra Leone Monitor, 20 October 2026; The Afroscope, November 2026). Whatever the judgment, the court is visibly not taking instructions from the executive in the most sensitive case of the decade.
  • The electoral referee he inherited was allowed to finish: ECSL chairman Mohamed Konneh, whose 2023 tabulation the opposition and observers had condemned, served out his full five-year term to 27 July 2025 and left on schedule, neither removed in anger nor retained past his mandate (Sierra Leone Monitor, July 2025). Bio has also left the term-limit referee untouched — Section 46 stands unamended, and his own departure is scheduled by it (Test 5).

Evidence against:

  • The speech offence repealed in 2020 was substantially rebuilt in 2021 under another name. The Cyber Security and Crime Act 2021 — passed over the Sierra Leone Association of Journalists' objection that it would return criminal libel by the back door — criminalizes, in Section 44(2)(b), sending a message known to be false in order to cause "insult, injury… enmity, hatred, ill will or needless anxiety." Its documented uses run against speakers: social-media influencer Hawa Hunt, arrested in December 2024 over a 2023 video insulting the president and the First Lady, detained until her release in March 2025 following a public apology, the case never reaching a verdict; Thomas Dixon, chairman of the Guild of Newspaper Editors, charged with cyberstalking and bullying in August 2025 over reporting on a mining company's legal dispute, released on bail after the company instructed police to suspend the investigation on condition he sign an undertaking not to publish further on the case; Standard Times reporter Shar Maturi detained the same day; radio journalist Sorie Saio Sesay detained six days in 2022 for forwarding a WhatsApp comment about police misconduct (This Day, 2025; IFEX/MFWA reporting; The Afroscope, October 2026). The police and judiciary have repeatedly declined to state how many people have been arrested or prosecuted under the Act at all (Awoko, via The Afroscope). A repeal whose replacement is used this way, with its usage uncounted, is a grip re-fitted, not released. Zainab Sheriff, a musician and civic-movement leader, is serving a four-year prison sentence imposed over remarks made at an opposition rally (The Afroscope, October 2026).
  • The electoral commission's next chair was appointed through the old grip, ahead of the agreed reform. The Tripartite Committee's Recommendation 36 — part of the settlement Bio signed after the 2023 election (Test 3) — calls for amending Section 32(3) to create an independent Search and Nomination Committee to vet electoral commissioners. That amendment had not been made when, on 20 February 2026, Bio appointed Edmond Sylvester Alpha — a commissioner who had served on the 2023 ECSL — as substantive chairman, citing the existing Section 32(3). The APC rejected the appointment in writing (letter of 11 February 2026), citing unimplemented Recommendations 20 and 36; the Lawyers' Society condemned the "hasty appointment" and called for its reversal; the presidency answered that 12 of 14 registered parties had approved (Awoko; MyEpic Online; Pan-African Visions, February 2026). The appointment followed the constitution as it stands; it broke the sequence the reform settlement promised — referee first reformed, then appointed — and the institution that will run the 2028 election Bio has pledged to lose gracefully is now headed by a man the main opposition formally distrusts.
  • The August 2026 amendment episode showed the grip inside his own party's legislature: on 10 August 2026, after APC MPs walked out over the Section 108(2)(b) question — whether a constitutional amendment needs two-thirds of all 149 members (100 votes) or of those present — Speaker Segepoh Solomon Thomas declared the Bill passed on a simple-majority basis (the Clerk certified 97 votes in favour), then publicly declared his ruling "final and cannot be interpreted or enquired into by any court of law" (Sierra Leone Monitor, 12 August 2026). Bio had urged MPs the day before to pass the reforms. The Bill's contents are themselves referee-rules: lowering the presidential first-round threshold from 55% to 50%+1 with a geographic-spread condition, making proportional representation permanent, and entrenching a 30% women's quota — passed, if the critics are right, without the constitutionally required majority, twelve days of national controversy, and petitions from lawyers, the Labour Congress, religious leaders, and civil society urging the president to withhold assent (The Calabash Newspaper, August 2026). That he then referred the question to the Supreme Court is on the FOR shelf; that the question existed at all — his party's Speaker asserting immunity from the courts while amending the constitution on a contested count — is on this one.
  • The chief-justice sequence invites the question the test asks. Justice Komba Kamanda presided over the July 2024 treason trial that convicted the November 2023 attackers; in late 2024 Bio appointed him Chief Justice. No act of interference by Kamanda in the executive's favour is documented — indeed, his court struck out the Attorney-General's motion in October 2026 — and the appointment of a serving senior judge is within the ordinary power; the item is recorded because the sequence (the coup-trial judge elevated within months) was publicly read as reward, and a seat that grades perception-sensitive referees must note where its appointments create that reading.

Why the grade lands here: Bio's record on the referees is genuinely double. He abolished the region's most notorious speech law and has never reinstated it in name; he let an electoral chairman he had every political reason to sack serve out his term; and in September 2026 he handed his own party's constitutional bill to the Supreme Court rather than sign it — the single strongest Test 2 act in this dossier. Against that: the speech offence returned in cyber dress and is used against journalists and critics with its statistics withheld; the electoral chair for 2028 was seated through the unreformed appointment power over formal legal-profession protest; and his Speaker claimed a ruling beyond the courts' reach. The referees still bite — October 2026 proved it — but the seat keeps a hand near several of their collars. MIXED. Helped: journalists and citizens, who have lived eight years without criminal libel; the Supreme Court, handed the defining constitutional question of the decade by the president himself; the constitution's term-limit and electoral-term rules, which have so far run on schedule. Hurt: Hawa Hunt, Thomas Dixon, Shar Maturi, Sorie Saio Sesay, and Zainab Sheriff — speakers detained, charged, or imprisoned under the replacement speech law; the APC and the legal profession, whose formal objections to the ECSL appointment were overridden; public confidence in the 2028 electoral machinery, which the appointment sequence spent down.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The defining wobble of the tenure — an election whose count his opponents, the EU, the Carter Center, and the domestic observer network all refused to certify as transparent — was answered with the largest open adjustment available to a declared winner. After the APC boycotted parliament and governance, the United States announced visa restrictions over the electoral process, and ECOWAS, the AU, and the Commonwealth mediated, Bio signed the Agreement for National Unity with the APC on 18 October 2023 and accepted a Tripartite Committee — government, APC, and moral guarantors — to review the very election that had seated him. The Committee reported in 2024 with 80 recommendations; his cabinet adopted a timed implementation plan, a fact the MCC itself cited when signing the USD 480 million compact on 27 September 2024 as progress "toward implementing the recommendations of the Tripartite Electoral Review Committee report" (MCC, 2024). When another option became available — review instead of mere incumbency — he took it, in writing, with the opposition's names on the same page.
  • The implementation, though incomplete (Tests 2 and 5), has been continuous and inspectable rather than theatrical: the ECSL reported progress item by item to the Political Parties Liaison Committee in August 2025 (consultation policy drafted, external functional review of the electoral bodies conducted, staff-recruitment policy drafted); the Attorney-General's office held a public stakeholder consultation on the constitutional and legislative reforms; and as of November 2026 the national dialogue on Recommendation 78 — the choice between district PR and first-past-the-post — was in active preparation with regional consultations planned (ECSL PPLC minutes, 27 August 2025; MyEpic Online, November 2026). The APC's second boycott, begun in early 2026 over the ECSL appointment, ended on 29 April 2026 with the party's return to parliament and councils under a moral-guarantors' communiqué — the adjustment machinery working a second time.
  • The kush catastrophe was met by naming it. After families, health workers, and the Human Rights Commission had raised the alarm for years — including the March 2024 mass burial of 32 unidentified young people — Bio declared a national emergency on drugs and substance abuse in April 2024, calling kush "a death trap" posing "an existential crisis," and stood up a National Task Force under the Vice President spanning prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and enforcement (presidential broadcast, April 2024; WHO Afro, 2024). The adjustment was late relative to the dying, and its results are graded in Test 4; as an answer to pushback it was open, institutional, and in his own voice.
  • Two late-tenure adjustments belong on this shelf. In July 2026 the Koroma treason prosecution — by then an open wound with the opposition and ECOWAS, which had brokered Koroma's medical exile — was discontinued (Test 1 records the cost to the one-law principle; here the act is recorded as de-escalation chosen over doubling down). And on 26 October 2026, at a national conference on unity and social cohesion, Bio apologized on behalf of the state to the victims of the 1991–2002 civil war and revived the reparations programme, directing that a report on implementing the Truth and Reconciliation Commission's recommendations be published by 18 January 2027 (WADR; Voice of Salone, October 2026) — a president answering a twenty-year-old grievance his own side of the war had helped create, in the same speech in which he ruled himself out of the 2028 election.
  • The September 2026 referral of the amendment bill (Test 2) is also a Test 3 act: urged by his party to sign, petitioned by civil society to refuse, he chose the third door — the court's opinion first — and stated the reason publicly: legislation altering the constitution "must inspire public trust and confidence" (State House, September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The first great wobble of the tenure was answered with force and a closed file. On 10 August 2022, protests over the cost of living — inflation was climbing toward a peak of about 47% in late 2023 (IMF reporting) — turned violent in Freetown, Makeni, and Kamakwie. The counts are kept separate, as they must be: Amnesty International records more than 20 protesters and bystanders and six police officers killed; media reports cited in Amnesty's annual reporting put the protester and bystander dead at 27, and the bodies of 27 people were released for burial on 17 October 2022, more than two months after the killings. The state's answer was a curfew, a documented internet shutdown, 515 arrests, and a presidential framing of the protests as an insurrection by the opposition; the Special Investigation Committee it appointed reported in March 2023 without recommending any investigation of the force used, and no civilian death has been investigated since (Amnesty International, March and October 2023). The wobble was not answered; it was buried — in some cases literally, two months late.
  • The response to the 26 November 2023 attacks overshot its target in the same pattern. The attacks themselves were real and grave — coordinated assaults on the Wilberforce armoury, barracks, prisons, and police stations, roughly 1,900–2,200 inmates freed, and, in separate counts, at least 21 people killed (News Central/AFP reporting) including 18 security personnel (AP). The no-good-choice clause is applied to the immediate response: with the armoury breached and thousands of prisoners loose in the capital, a curfew, a manhunt, and mass arrests of suspects were the least-harm doors actually open, and the constraint is recorded, not held against him. What the clause does not cover is what was chosen after the emergency: a treason prosecution of the former president on evidence that was never tested in court and was abandoned without explanation in July 2026, and a dragnet in which more than 80 people were arrested. The trial that did run — of Amadu Koita and ten others, convicted by a unanimous jury on 22 July 2024, Koita receiving concurrent sentences of 40–70 years (cumulative counts reported at 162–182 years) and the others 30–112 years — was a court process and is recorded as one; the twelfth accused, APC youth leader Bai Mahmoud Bangura, was separated on health grounds and no verdict in his case appears in the record assembled here.
  • On the Leijdekkers file, two years of allied pushback — a formal Dutch extradition request (early 2025), a €200,000 Dutch reward, Reuters' documented sightings, and, in October 2026, the Dutch justice minister's public claim that the EU had frozen €18 million of budget support over the case — have been answered with denial of knowledge rather than any visible process. The reasons for the EU payment's postponement are contested: the Dutch minister David van Weel attributes it to the extradition refusal; EU Commissioner Magnus Brunner said publicly it was "due to technical reasons"; the Sierra Leone government states the Commission's July 2026 letter cited the late timing of an amendment request and proposed rescheduling the 2026 and 2027 tranches to 2027 and 2028 (OCCRP, October 2026; Sierra Leone Monitor, 1 October 2026). What is not contested is the shape of the answer: no arrest, no published legal opinion on the request, no invitation to Dutch investigators — and the Chief Minister publicly counter-attacking The Hague over its refusal to extradite a Sierra Leonean fugitive ("We sent ministers for Adebayor, they refused"). Pushback answered with a counter-accusation is the doubling-down pattern, whatever the underlying merits.
  • The tripartite adjustment has been slow-walked at its decisive joints. Four years after the unity agreement, the APC's November 2026 position — that the Agreement for National Unity and the Tripartite Report have still not been laid before Parliament, that the agreed parliamentary standing committee on electoral matters (Recommendation 20) has not been created, and that the SLPP opposes both — is a matter of public record; the government disputes the characterization of delay and points to the implementation machinery above (Fritong Post, November 2026; Parliament of Sierra Leone releases, 2024). Both statements can be true at once: the process runs, and the two recommendations that would redistribute power over elections are the ones that have not landed — a sequencing choice graded again in Test 5.

Why the grade lands here: Bio has answered the two largest wobbles of his tenure — a disputed election and a constitutional amendment his own side rushed — with the rarest move in this series: he submitted his own wins to review, in writing, and let the referees keep the pen. He has answered the wobbles that came from below — the street in 2022, the drug-dead's families, the extradition file — with force, burial, or denial. The pattern is consistent enough to name: pushback that arrives through institutions gets an open answer; pushback that arrives through the street or the press gets the state. MIXED, because both halves are documented at scale. Helped: the APC and its voters, readmitted to a political process with a reform agenda attached; Sierra Leone's donors and partners, whose compact and programmes the unity agreement unlocked; the civil-war victims, who received a state apology and a dated reparations commitment in October 2026; the constitution, whose amendment question now sits with its court instead of with a signature. Hurt: the families of the August 2022 dead, whose wobble was answered with a committee designed not to investigate; Ernest Bai Koroma and the other November 2023 accused, held under capital charges for years on an untested file; Dutch and European partners, whose extradition request has met two years of denial; kush users' families, whose alarm was raised years before the emergency that finally answered it.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's regions, the protesters, the addicted, the poor, the disabled, and the girls — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The flagship counting act of the tenure is the Free Quality School Education programme, launched August 2018 and sustained for eight years at roughly a fifth of the national budget. Its access numbers are large on every instrument: more than one million additional children entered the education system between 2019 and 2023 (government statement to the UN Economic Commission for Africa, 2026); enrolment rose by over 60% within four years; gender gaps in enrolment narrowed; and the school-feeding programme now reaches, on the government's August 2026 figures, 1,016,191 pupils in 4,034 schools (FQSE desk review, World Journal of Advanced Research and Reviews, 2025; presidential address to Parliament, 7 August 2026). Fees — the mechanism by which the poor were counted out — were abolished for everyone, not for a favoured region or party.
  • Girls were counted back in by law, twice. In March 2020 his government overturned the decade-long ban on pregnant schoolgirls and teenage mothers attending school — a class of children the state had formally excluded. In January 2023 he signed the Gender Equality and Women's Empowerment Act: a 30% quota for women in parliament, cabinet, the civil service, and private firms of 25 or more employees, 14 weeks' maternity leave, and equal-pay provisions, alongside the Customary Land Rights Act 2022 giving women equal rights to own and use family land (IPU; NDI; AP, January 2023). Women's share of Parliament rose from 12.3% before the 2023 election to roughly 28% after it (IPU data) — short of the 30% the law promises, and the largest single increase in the country's history.
  • The addicted were counted as patients, at least in the response's design: the kush emergency built a treatment architecture — five rehabilitation centres (Hastings, Daru, Bo, Kono, Makeni) and, on UN reporting, 1,071 young people rehabilitated and reintegrated by 21 August 2026, alongside 52 trafficking convictions secured by the Transnational Organised Crime Unit by January 2026 (Ministry of Information, May 2026; UN in Sierra Leone, August 2026). The October 2026 apology and reparations revival extended the count to the civil war's victims, the longest-uncounted population in the country.

Evidence against:

  • The August 2022 dead were counted out of the accounting itself. The protesters and bystanders killed on and after 10 August 2022 — concentrated in Freetown and in the northern opposition strongholds of Makeni and Kamakwie — were not investigated (Test 3); their bodies were held by the state for more than two months before release for burial; and the official committee's report recommended training, not truth (Amnesty International, 2023). The six police officers killed the same days were buried with state honours on 23 August 2022. Both sets of dead were Sierra Leonean; only one set was counted.
  • The kush dead have never been counted at all. There is no official national death toll — Reuters noted the absence when the emergency was declared, and none has been published since. What exists are fragments, kept separate here as the record keeps them: the March 2024 mass burial of 32 unidentified young people (25 men, 7 women) reported by the Ministry of Health and the Human Rights Commission as alleged kush victims; a Freetown doctor's statement to the BBC that hundreds of young men had died of organ failure from kush in the capital in recent months (2024); psychiatric admissions linked to kush rising almost 4,000% between 2020 and 2023 to 1,865 at the country's only psychiatric hospital; and a 2025 clinical study of 312 poisoning patients at four treatment centres finding 9 deaths among them, with the drug shown to contain nitazene-class opioids (Journal of Interventional Epidemiology and Public Health, 2026). Against an epidemic the president himself called an existential threat, 1,071 rehabilitations in two and a half years is a real number and a small one; the uncounted dead are this test's heaviest entry.
  • Free education's quality failure falls, as such failures do, on those with no private alternative. The 2025 desk review of FQSE concludes it "largely achieved its immediate objective of expanding access but has not yet translated this into consistently improved learning achievement," with overcrowded classrooms, teacher shortages and uneven deployment, and the sharpest equity gaps for poorer households, rural communities, and learners with disabilities (WJARR, 2025). The examination record carries its own integrity questions: an unexplained seventeen-fold surge in candidates achieving five credits including English and Mathematics between 2020 and 2022 (2,144 to 37,592); in 2025, 35,020 of 148,313 entered WASSCE candidates sat no subject at all, with the Ministry blaming multiple and fraudulent registrations of the government-paid fees; in 2026 a further 47,454 results were initially withheld, and the reported English credit rate fell from 11.77% to 9.77% (independent investigation of WASSCE results 2017–2026, The Afroscope, 2026). And in November 2025 the World Bank suspended disbursements to its Free Education Project over the government's non-reporting of school-related sexual exploitation, abuse, and harassment incidents — the safety of schoolgirls, uncounted in the precise bureaucratic sense, with five conditions set for lifting the suspension by 27 February 2026 (World Bank Implementation Status Report, March 2026). The children most counted-in by the programme's promise are the ones its failures reach first.
  • The economic count remains thinnest where the 2022 protesters stood. The government's own August 2026 address reports real growth of 4.6% (2024) and 4.8% (2025) and inflation down to 4.4% by December 2025 before a rise to 10.8% in April 2026 — figures recorded here as government-reported — while rice, the staple whose price drove the 2022 street, remains import-dependent (Test 5). The counting is national in design; its benefits, on the record assembled here, arrive last in the opposition's north and in the informal settlements of the capital, and the record contains no government instrument that measures the distribution by region at all.

Why the grade lands here: Bio's counting is genuinely universal in its designs — free schooling for every child, quotas for women, land rights, readmitted teenage mothers, treatment for the addicted, an apology to the war's victims — and several of those designs are the strongest in this series. It fails in its follow-through on the unpopular dead: the protesters whose killings were never investigated, and the kush generation whose deaths the state has never even totalled. A ruler who counts everyone in the programme and declines to count the inconvenient corpses has done half of this test — the larger half, and not the half the test exists for. MIXED. Helped: more than a million additional schoolchildren and their families, girls above all; women in public life, whose parliamentary share more than doubled; teenage mothers readmitted to school; the 1,071 rehabilitated kush users and the communities of the five treatment centres; civil-war victims, formally apologized to in October 2026. Hurt: the families of the August 2022 protesters and bystanders, denied investigation and timely burial; the kush dead and their families, uncounted in any official figure; rural, poor, and disabled learners, for whom free access has not become quality; schoolgirls whose abuse reports went unfiled until the World Bank stopped the money.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 4 April 2018, with dates; the two axes printed independently.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Free education — the 2018 flagship, kept at scale for eight years. Bio's central 2018 promise was free quality schooling; the programme launched within five months of his inauguration, abolished fees at primary and secondary level, paid examination fees, and has been funded at roughly a fifth of the budget ever since, through an economic crisis, a pandemic, and an inflation spike. The access half of the promise is documented in Test 4; the word was kept in the form in which it was most expensive to keep.
  • The libel repeal — a manifesto promise his predecessors had made and broken for decades, delivered. The 2018 SLPP manifesto promised to repeal the criminal libel law; in October 2020 he signed the repeal and said, on the record, that successive governments had failed to abolish it. The subsequent Cyber Act erosion is graded where it belongs, in Test 2; the keeping itself happened, and no libel prosecution has occurred since.
  • The unity word — substantially performed. The October 2023 Agreement for National Unity committed his government to an electoral-systems review and to implementing its outcomes; the Tripartite Committee sat, reported with 80 recommendations, and a timed implementation plan was adopted at cabinet level — performance sufficient for the MCC to cite it, on the record, as part of the basis for the USD 480 million compact signed in September 2024. The compact's own timeline is the honest footnote: signed in 2024, it remains in its pre-entry-into-force phase (February 2026 – September 2027), with the five-year implementation now scheduled to run September 2027 to September 2032 — under his successor (MCA-Sierra Leone briefing to Parliament, September 2026).
  • The term-limit word — stated, restated, and so far unbroken. Section 46 bars a third term; Bio has rejected third-term speculation repeatedly — "My word is my bond… We have a time limit and that is what is going to prevail" (New York, September 2026); "In 1996, I handed over power as a soldier. In 2028, I will hand over power again as a democrat" (Harvard Kennedy School, 29 September 2026); and, on 26 October 2026, "I will not be a candidate in the 2028 presidential elections." An unverified ALLEGATION by SLPP flagbearer aspirant Musa Tarawally — that he was approached to support a plan to extend Bio's tenure to 2030 — circulates in November 2026 reporting; no independent evidence of such a plan has been produced, State House has not responded to the specific claim, and Bio's public statements run the other way (Phoenix Browser reporting, November 2026). The word is recorded here as kept-to-date; its grading completes in 2028.
  • The macroeconomic word, in part: his government promised stabilization after the 2023 crisis; on the government's own figures, inflation fell from its late-2023 peak of about 47% to 4.4% by December 2025 (before rising to 10.8% in April 2026 on global oil prices), with growth of 4.6–4.8% in 2024–2025 (presidential address, August 2026 — government-reported figures, recorded as such).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Feed Salone — the 2023 flagship, behind its own rhetoric. The Big Five's first promise was food self-sufficiency. On the government's own August 2026 figures, rice self-sufficiency rose from 68% (2023) to 73% (2025) and the rice import bill fell from USD 177 million (2022) to USD 159 million — movement, recorded as such, and movement far short of the transformation proclaimed. Bio himself set the benchmark in the same address: Feed Salone "would ultimately be judged by farmers' earnings, household food costs and Sierra Leone's ability to feed itself." On his own test, the word is not yet kept, and the import dependence that drove the 2022 protests persists.
  • 500,000 jobs — a promise without a count. The Youth Employment Scheme promised 500,000 jobs (at least 30% for women), framed in the 2024–2030 national plan as a target for 2030. Three years into the second term, no verified cumulative figure exists: in his August 2026 address the president directed that the verified cumulative employment figure, the definition of a job, and the sector breakdown be published — an instruction that records, in his own words, that the promise had never been given a checkable number (presidential address, 7 August 2026; MyEpic Online analysis, August 2026). A word that cannot be counted cannot be graded as kept.
  • "Quality" — the second word in his own programme's name. FQSE promised quality as well as access; the learning record is graded in Test 4 — stagnant foundational outcomes, an unexplained results surge in 2020–2022, tens of thousands of phantom or withheld examination entries in 2025–2026. The access word was kept; the quality word rides in the same sentence and was not.
  • The tripartite sequence — the reform word broken at the appointment gate. The unity settlement's logic was referees first, then appointments under the new rules. Recommendations 20 (parliamentary standing committee on electoral matters) and 36 (independent search committee for electoral commissioners) remained unimplemented when the ECSL chairmanship fell vacant, and the government filled it in February 2026 under the old rules (Test 2), while the SLPP opposes creating the Recommendation 20 committee outright. The word given in October 2023 was not "implement the recommendations eventually"; the MCC-cited plan was timed. On the two recommendations that redistribute power over elections, the timing has been the government's, not the plan's.

The two axes, printed independently: word-keeping and outcome are graded separately, and all four crossings occur in this record. (1) Kept his word and it helped: the libel repeal — promised, delivered, and eight years of journalism without a libel cell; free education's access promise — promised, funded, and a million more children in school. (2) Kept his word and it still hurt: the anti-corruption crusade was a 2018 promise kept in earnest — and its flagship prosecution, the Kamara chancery case, hung over his electoral challenger from November 2021 through the 2023 election and beyond without a verdict; the keeping earns credit here, and the hurt stands in Test 1 in full. The August 2022 security response likewise performed his inaugural promise to restore "discipline" while its uninvestigated dead are recorded in Tests 3 and 4. (3) Went against his word and it helped: his government's first position after June 2023 was that the election was settled and its result final; accepting a tripartite review of that election — and a unity agreement with the party that rejected his victory — reversed the stated position, and the reversal bought the country the MCC compact, the APC's return to parliament, and three years of managed rather than violent dispute. The break is recorded here; the help is recorded in full. (4) Broke his word and it hurt: the jobs promise, left uncounted while youth unemployment remained the country's most volatile grievance; and the tripartite sequence, whose breach at the ECSL appointment cost the 2028 process the trust the agreement was built to create.

Why the grade lands here: Bio's word is unusually load-bearing — his signature promises (free education, the libel repeal, the unity review, the term limit) were made in public, with dates, and the biggest of them were kept in the expensive way. The failures are of the second kind: a flagship (Feed Salone) receding behind its own benchmark, a jobs promise never given a number, a quality promise riding unkept inside a kept one, and a reform sequence broken at exactly the gate where it would have bound his own appointment power. He keeps the words that cost money and breaks or defers the ones that cost control. That is a better record than most in this series, and it is MIXED. Helped: schoolchildren and parents, who got the kept flagship; journalists, who got the kept repeal; the national treasury and energy sector, which got a USD 480 million compact on the strength of the kept unity word; voters of 2028, who hold a public, repeated, dated promise of a handover. Hurt: young jobseekers, whose promise remains uncounted three years on; rice farmers and food-buying households, for whom self-sufficiency receded; Samura Kamara, kept under indictment by a crusade whose word was honestly given; the 2028 electoral process, whose trust the broken sequence was meant to build.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Julius Maada Bio is graded on eight years and seven months: a former junta head of state who handed over power in 1996, won the presidency twice at the ballot box, and now governs under a term limit he invokes against himself in public. Test 1 is MIXED because the anti-corruption machine he built genuinely runs on one law through the administrative state — 1,541 officers sanctioned in a single year, eight straight MCC passes, a CPI climb from 129th to 109th — while at the political summit the law turns discretionary: a challenger prosecuted for five years without a verdict, a predecessor's treason charge switched off unexplained, 515 protesters prosecuted against zero investigations of the men who shot protesters, and Europe's most wanted trafficker unfindable inside his borders. Test 2 is MIXED because the tenure contains both the region's model loosening — the 2020 libel repeal, an electoral chairman allowed to serve out his term, and a president who in September 2026 sent his own party's constitutional bill to the Supreme Court instead of signing it, whereupon the court struck out his own Attorney-General's attempt to kill the case — and its opposite: a cyber law that rebuilt the speech offence and is used on journalists and critics with its statistics withheld, an electoral chair seated through the unreformed appointment power over the legal profession's protest, and a Speaker who declared a constitutional amendment beyond the courts' reach. Test 3 is MIXED because his answers split by the direction of the pushback: institutional pressure — a disputed election, a contested amendment, a twenty-year-old war debt — gets open, written, inspectable adjustment, up to apologizing for the state and ruling himself out of 2028 in the same speech; pressure from the street and the press gets curfews, shutdowns, uninvestigated dead, and, on the extradition file, two years of denial. The immediate response to the November 2023 armoury attack is recorded under the no-good-choice clause — no clean door existed that night — with the clause expressly not extended to the Koroma prosecution or the 2022 investigation design, where other doors stood open. Test 4 is MIXED because the counting designs are the strongest in this series — free schooling that put a million more children in class, pregnant girls readmitted, a 30% women's quota in law, land rights, treatment centres for the addicted, an apology to the war's victims — while the counting fails precisely over the unpopular dead: protesters uninvestigated and buried two months late, and a kush generation whose toll the state has never totalled. Test 5 is MIXED because his expensive words were kept (education, the repeal, the unity review, the term limit to date) and his control-costing words were deferred (the electoral-reform sequence), left uncounted (500,000 jobs), or left riding unkept inside kept ones ("quality"); all four crossings occur, including the rare third — he broke his own "the election is settled" position to accept its review, and the country was helped by the breaking. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: the Supreme Court's judgment on the amendment (hearing 27 November 2026) and whether he abides by it; whether Recommendations 20 and 36 are implemented before the 2028 cycle hardens; whether the Kamara case ever reaches a verdict; whether Leijdekkers is arrested, extradited, or shown to be elsewhere; whether a verified jobs figure and an official kush toll are ever published; and, above all of them, whether the 2028 handover he has promised — "I will hand over power again as a democrat" — happens on schedule. On the record as it stands, he is a ruler who built real referees and real programmes, keeps the words that cost money, and keeps a hand on the ones that would cost control.
