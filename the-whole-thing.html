How anything stays available

No server lives forever. Copies do — when there are enough of them, in enough hands.

The whole doctrine, in plain wordsCopies × permission × findability▾

Every “always available” thing in history survived the same way: copies outnumbered the failures. No book, song, or program lives on one machine somewhere. The ones that last live on everybody's machines.

Availability stands on three legs:

1 · Copies. The more hands hold it, the more doors have to close before it is gone. Every download anyone takes is a seed copy on one more hard drive, under a permission that lets it be re-posted — forever.

2 · Permission. Copyleft — explained in the next layer — so any keeper can legally stand the door back up, anywhere, the same day. Not just us. Anybody.

3 · Findability. A keeper's copy only helps if people can find it: stable addresses, public archives, and a name that points to the work wherever it happens to be standing.

Put the three together and the math flips. Closing one host is an errand. To stop a widely copied work, someone would have to make it their mission to find and destroy every copy on every hard drive — every phone, every laptop, every forgotten USB stick in every desk on Earth. That could be billions upon billions of them. Nobody has that army. Nobody ever will. The copies do not get tired, do not get scared, and do not ask permission to exist.

The honest bedrock: “always,” in the absolute sense, does not exist. What exists is cheaper to keep than to kill — copies so widespread that erasing the work everywhere stops being a practical possibility. That is the real target. The goal was never a stronger vault. The goal is more hands.

Copyleft — the permission that can't be taken back

Copyleft is a permission, not a place. Once it is given, it can never be taken back — so any keeper can bring the work back, legally, forever.

What copyleft is, and what it is notA license, not a server▾

Copyleft (a license that grants freedom and requires it to be passed on) is not a website, a host, or a storage service. It does not keep a server running or a page online. What it does is stronger in a different way: once a work is released under it, anyone who holds a copy may use it, copy it, re-post it, and build on it — forever, no asking, no fee — as long as they pass the same freedom along to the next person.

The work here travels under the standard forms of that permission: the writings under CC BY-SA 4.0 (a Creative Commons license: share and adapt freely, credit the source, share alike), any code under the GPL (the GNU General Public License, the same share-alike idea for software), and any hardware designs under CERN-OHL-S (CERN's open hardware license, strongly reciprocal). One thing is not part of the grant: the name “The Thriving Plan” itself. The work is free to copy; the name stays ours, so a copy can never pretend to be the original door.

So copyleft alone does not mean “always available.” It means always resurrectable: the right to bring the work back can never expire or be revoked. If a host dies tomorrow and the work were under ordinary copyright, whoever re-posted it could be legally stopped. Under copyleft, anybody holding a copy can stand the door back up the same day. Permission makes resurrection legal; copies make it possible. The two halves lock together — that lock is this whole page.

For when it rains

Accounts get blocked, hosts fail, companies fold. Rain is not the tragedy — rain with no umbrella is.

Why an umbrella, and why nowYou don't build an umbrella in the storm▾

You don't build an umbrella in the storm. You build it on a clear evening, fold it up, and put it by the door — because rain, eventually, is not a possibility. It is a schedule nobody gets to see.

Everything with a landlord eventually gets weather: a phone breaks, an account is blocked, a host's free tier runs out of credits and the door pauses, a company changes its mind or closes. Any one of those can take a single door down in an afternoon. None of them can take down a work that lives in many hands, under a permission nobody can revoke, in a file small enough to travel anywhere.

This bundle is that umbrella, and it is an umbrella anybody may carry — not just the people who built the work. Download it on a clear day. If the sky never changes, it cost you 120 KB in a drawer. If it does, you are holding the thing that brings the work back.
