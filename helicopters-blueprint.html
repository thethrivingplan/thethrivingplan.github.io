Why? — What it is

A helicopter blueprint that keeps everything a helicopter already does well — the rotating wing, vertical flight, autorotation — and adds one wide door beside autorotation's narrow one: a field that takes the fall.

How? — What it means

Helicopters are the VTOL craft that already exists. The Ship blueprint's field thinking for VTOL doesn't need a future craft to land on — it lands here, on the one already flying.

STANDING LAWS

Standing Laws of This Design

Three laws govern every sheet on this shelf. They are printed here the way this sheet grades them — honestly, from this design's own content, with the refusal printed at full size where a label doesn't fit.

Golden Mode

Golden — spread, never align: the field

The absorption field runs Golden. Its whole job is spread: a failure's energy taken over the whole craft and the whole descent — the fall, the ground, the roll — never concentrated at one strike point, one skid, one structural member. The law's form is biggest-gap placement at the golden angle 137.5077641°; here the form is energy with nowhere single to land.

Platinum Mode

Platinum — align exactly: the rotor

The rotor system runs Platinum, because there alignment is the function. Blades tracked to fly in one plane, balanced 1:1, hub and mast true — a rotor out of exact alignment doesn't degrade gracefully; it shakes the craft apart. Equal, on the line: no spread wanted, and none allowed.

Diamond Mode

Diamond — the weave: the design as a whole

The craft as a whole runs Diamond: align locally, spread globally. Each layer — rotor, autorotation, crashworthy structure, field — is held to its own exact standard, and the layers stand independent of one another, so no single failure takes them all. Locally aligned, globally spread: the weave is why a dead field still leaves a helicopter.

No stamp

Where no mode is stamped — printed plainly

The physics gets no label: mass × gravity × height is arithmetic, not arrangement, and the falling-energy numbers on this sheet are what they are in any mode. The field's mechanism gets none either — it is an open how, and a mode is not graded on a mechanism that doesn't exist yet. Use it when it works — don't force it.

Top Law

Adaptation — Adapt and Overcome

A top law, in the designer's words: learn your lessons and grow. This blueprint is built to adapt — it takes in new knowledge and gets better. Today's version is the floor, not the ceiling. Improvements are fitted in when verified better — through the Build Order's gates, on published numbers — never forced, and never a from-scratch rebuild. Rotorcraft already live this law: the crawl toward safety printed in The Base is a century of lessons learned the hard way. This sheet's field is the next lesson, not the last word.

Standing Law

Earned Access — the right is earned, because of the power

With great power comes great responsibility. A machine that holds a fall out of the sky is powerful, and access to it is earned — proven by actions, never entitled, never handed out. Rotorcraft already know this law in part: nobody flies one on a wish — training, checkrides, and demonstrated autorotation skill are the proof, renewed or lost on performance. This design keeps that gate and means it: the right to fly this craft is held by demonstrated competence and conduct, and it can be taken away when actions prove unworthiness. The gate is not a problem with the design. It is a must — you have to earn the right to have access to these things because of their power.

01 / PROVEN

The Base — What Already Works

This design starts from solved engineering, printed as proven — because it is. A helicopter is not a mystery waiting for a field. It is a working aircraft with one great safety answer already built in, and one narrow gap left to widen.

DOCUMENTED · PROVEN

The rotor is a rotating wing

Each rotor blade is an airfoil — a wing — spun in a circle so it makes lift without the craft moving forward. That one idea is why a helicopter can hover, rise straight up, and land on a rooftop, a ship deck, or a mountainside. Rotorcraft flight is solved engineering, flown every day, worldwide.

DOCUMENTED · PROVEN

Autorotation — the built-in answer

When the engine quits, a helicopter does not have to fall. The pilot lowers collective, air flowing up through the rotor keeps it spinning, and the stored energy in that spinning rotor is traded at the bottom for a flare and a survivable touchdown. It is a glide — on a wing that turns. Pilots train it for a reason: it works.

THIS BLUEPRINT · DESIGN

The field — the next layer

Autorotation is kept, at full strength, as the first answer. This blueprint adds a second: an absorption field around the craft that holds the fall itself — wider, earlier, and there even when autorotation's narrow door is closed. Design, not built fact.

Print that honestly: the crawl toward safety already happened here. Generations of rotorcraft engineering — crashworthy seats and fuel systems, redundant hydraulics, autorotation training — moved helicopter safety forward the same way crumple zones moved car safety forward. This blueprint is the next step on that same line, labeled as what it is: designed, not yet built.

Tap to open — How autorotation actually works, in plain words

A powered rotor is driven by the engine and pulls air down. In autorotation the flow reverses: the craft descends, air comes up through the disc, and — like wind spinning a windmill — that upward flow keeps the rotor turning on its own. The rotor becomes a parachute that is also a wing.

Descent rate stays high until the last moment. Then the pilot flares — nose up, collective raised — spending the rotor's stored rotational energy in one cushioning burst just above the ground. Done well, touchdown is gentle. Done from too low, too slow, or with no room to flare, there is nothing left to spend. That boundary is exactly what the next section is about.

02 / WHY

Why Helicopters Need This Most

Of everything on the transportation shelf, the helicopter flies the mission profile with the least margin — and its one built-in answer, for all its brilliance, is a narrow door.

The mission

Low and slow, over everything

Helicopters work where other aircraft only pass over: low over cities, wires, ridges, water, and rooftops — medevac, rescue, firefighting, news, utility work. Low and slow is the job. It is also the flight regime with the least altitude to trade and the least speed to glide on when something fails.

The physics

No glide like a plane's

A plane with a dead engine is a glider — its fixed wing carries it miles while the pilot picks a field. A helicopter's wing only works while it spins, and it spins on stored energy and upward airflow that must be managed precisely, the whole way down. There is no coasting. The margin is measured in seconds and in pilot skill under the worst surprise of their life.

The consequence

Crashes that forgive the least

When a helicopter crash is bad, it is very bad: high vertical descent rates, rollover on touchdown, rotor and mast loads through the cabin, post-crash fire. The failure and the ground are often only moments apart, with terrain — slope, water, wires, rooftops — chosen by the emergency, not the pilot.

Design

The aim answers the profile

That is why this sheet exists in the family of the Bikes and Cars line — the craft whose occupants have the thinnest margin gets the widest door. Nobody dies in a helicopter crash is not a slogan pasted on; it is sized to the gap this section just measured.

Proven — kept

The narrow door: autorotation

One door, and it asks a lot of the person walking through it: enough altitude, enough rotor speed, a pilot who recognizes the failure instantly and flies the maneuver perfectly, and a clear spot below to flare into. When all of that lines up, it saves everyone. When any of it doesn't, the door is closed.

Design — this blueprint

The wide door: the absorption field

A second door, next to the first, that does not depend on the pilot's worst second going perfectly. The field takes energy from the moment of failure — holding the fall, softening the ground, resisting the roll — whether the failure was the engine, the tail rotor, or the structure itself. Autorotation stays. The field stands beside it.

03 / DESIGN

The Design — The Field Holds the Fall

The same field thinking the Ship blueprint applies to VTOL, applied to the VTOL craft that already exists. Tap a layer to read what it does.

Design

Holding the fall

The field's first job is the fall itself: from the moment lift is lost, the field takes energy out of the descent — holding the craft the way the Ship's field thinking holds a VTOL craft — so the ground arrives slower than gravity alone would deliver it. Every metre per second removed before touchdown is energy nobody's body has to absorb.

Design

Taking the ground, and the roll

What descent remains ends in impact and, too often, rollover — skids catch, the craft tips, the rotor meets the ground. The field wraps the whole craft, so ground impact is met by absorption first, and the roll is resisted by the same shell: the field is asked to keep the cabin upright and intact while the energy spends itself outside it.

The honest number: what a fall weighs

A falling helicopter's energy is not an opinion — it is mass × gravity × height, and it arrives all at once. Drag the sliders for a typical light helicopter (about 1,500 kg loaded) and see what the field is being asked to hold — and how fast the ground is coming at the bottom of an unarrested fall.

04 / FAILURE MODES

Failure Modes the Field Is Asked to Answer

Each mode below is a real, named way helicopters are lost today. Each field answer is a design candidate — labeled, not claimed. The Build Order exists to test them one at a time.

Design candidate

Engine / drive failure

Today: autorotation — proven, narrow, pilot-dependent. The field is asked to: take descent energy from the first second, widening the altitude-and-speed envelope where a survivable touchdown exists, and cushioning the flare so an imperfect one is still survivable.

Design candidate

Ground impact

Today: crashworthy structure, stroking seats, and skids absorb what they can — over centimetres. The field is asked to: begin absorbing before contact and keep absorbing through it, stretching the stop over distance the structure alone doesn't have.

Design candidate

Rollover / dynamic rollover

Today: a skid catches on slope, deck, or soft ground and the craft rolls — often after a touchdown that was nearly fine. The field is asked to: resist the roll moment itself, holding the cabin upright while the energy spends, so “almost landed it” stops being a fatal category.

Design candidate

Tail-rotor failure

Today: lose the tail rotor and the craft spins against main-rotor torque; the trained answer — enter autorotation, cut the torque, ride it down — is one of the hardest maneuvers in the book, at the worst moment. The field is asked to: counter the yaw and hold attitude while the craft descends, turning a spin emergency into a held descent. Named here as a candidate the field must answer — its hardest attitude test.

Design candidate

Mast / structural failure

Today: a mast, hub, or blade failure can remove lift entirely and asymmetrically — the case autorotation cannot answer, because the wing itself is gone. The field is asked to: be the answer of last resort — hold what remains of the craft in a survivable attitude and take the fall whole. Labeled plainly: this is the most demanding candidate on this page, and the least engineered.

Kept from today

What is NOT replaced

Autorotation, crashworthy structure, stroking seats, crash-resistant fuel systems — all kept, all at full standard. The field is a layer added to a helicopter, never a substitute for one. If the field is dead, the craft underneath must still autorotate and still crash like the best helicopter flying today. That rule is decided; see the fallback open how.

05 / ACT

Build Order — Rigs and Uncrewed First

Nobody flight-tests an unproven absorption claim with people aboard. Low-energy stages before high-energy ones, uncrewed before crewed, and every stage gated on published absorption numbers. A stage that misses its numbers stops the line — it does not get averaged away.

  1. Bench the field cell

    One field unit, instrumented, struck and loaded by calibrated rigs. Measure energy absorbed, peak force passed through, and failure mode — the same first step as every sheet on this shelf.

    GATE: PUBLISHED ABSORPTION NUMBERS, REPEATABLE

  2. Drop rigs — dead weight, rising heights

    An instrumented mass in a helicopter-weight frame, dropped from low heights with the field live, then higher. Compare against the unarrested numbers from the calculator above — the difference is the field's measured contribution.

    GATE: MEASURED ARREST AT EACH HEIGHT BEFORE RISING

  3. Downwash rigs

    The field operating inside a working rotor's wash: thrust stands and a spinning rotor over the field cell, measuring whether the field holds, degrades, or interferes with lift. This stage answers the downwash open how before any craft carries both.

    GATE: FIELD BEHAVIOR IN ROTOR WASH, MEASURED

  4. Uncrewed airframe drops & flights

    A real, uncrewed airframe: field-on drops, field-off drops (fallback proof — it must still behave like a helicopter airframe), then uncrewed flight with induced failures at altitude: engine cut, tail-rotor cut.

    GATE: FALLBACK PROVEN + FAILURE MODES ANSWERED UNCREWED

  5. Rollover & slope rigs

    Touchdown on slopes, decks, and soft ground, crewed by instruments only: does the field resist the roll as designed? The “almost landed it” category gets its own stage because it takes its own lives.

    GATE: ROLL RESISTANCE MEASURED, NOT ASSUMED

  6. Crewed flight — last, and only on the numbers

    Only after every number above is published and replicated by an independent builder (copyleft: anyone may replicate) does a person fly in one — first at profiles today's autorotation already covers, widening only as the field's own record earns it.

    GATE: INDEPENDENT REPLICATION
06 / HONEST LABELS

The Open Hows Shelf

Printed at full size, in the open. Each of these is an unknown — and an unknown is not a no. It is work waiting for a builder.

Open How · Unknown

Field capacity for a falling rotorcraft

The energies are printed honestly above: a 1,500 kg craft falling 50 m arrives with roughly 0.74 MJ at about 113 km/h unarrested — and the numbers scale with mass and height. How much of that a field can absorb, at what rate, and how capacity scales from the suit's chip to a full airframe is not yet engineered. This is the biggest number the design owes.

Open How · Unknown

Field behavior in the rotor wash

A helicopter lives inside its own downwash — a column of fast air the field must work within, under, and against, without stealing lift or destabilizing the disc. Does the wash degrade the field? Does the field disturb the wash? Build Order stage 3 exists to find out before any craft flies with both.

Open How · Design rule set

Failure fallback — the field dead

The rule is already decided even though the engineering isn't: autorotation and structure must still work with the field dead. A dead field means an ordinary helicopter emergency flown the ordinary proven way — never a craft that traded away its narrow door for a wide one that failed to open.

Open How · Design rule set

The craft must still be a helicopter

Hover, vertical takeoff and landing, autorotation, the full mission profile — low and slow over the places the work is. A design that only survives by no longer hovering, no longer landing on rooftops and decks, has answered a different question. The field serves the helicopter; the helicopter is not redesigned around the field's convenience.
