The Ruler's Test — Robert Golob, Prime Minister of Slovenia (2022–2026)

Subject: Robert Golob — Prime Minister of Slovenia from 1 June 2022 to 4 June 2026, leading the Freedom Movement (Gibanje Svoboda, GS) in coalition with the Social Democrats (SD) and The Left (Levica). A former energy executive — long-time CEO of the state-controlled energy trader GEN-I — he won the 24 April 2022 election in a landslide (GS took 41 of 90 seats, the most any single party had won since independence) on a promise to restore "normality" after the rule-of-law conflicts of Janez Janša's 2020–2022 government. In the parliamentary election of 22 March 2026 his party again finished first — 29 seats and 28.66% of the vote, one seat ahead of Janša's SDS — but the coalition lost its majority, Golob failed to assemble a government, publicly accepted that GS would go into opposition, and handed power to Janša, who was elected prime minister on 22 May 2026; the new government was confirmed on 4 June 2026. His tenure is therefore complete, and he is graded on the completed record, June 2022 – June 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The oversight machinery reached his own cabinet, and its members fell. Justice Minister Dominika Švarc Pipan (SD) resigned in February 2024 accepting strict liability for her ministry's purchase of a court building at Litijska Street 51 in Ljubljana — approved by Golob's government on 27 December 2023 for €7.69 million and exposed within weeks by TV Slovenija's Tarča as resting on disputed valuations; a state secretary in the affair was dismissed on 19 January 2024, and the Court of Audit later found the investment documentation had omitted essential renovation costs (Litijska Affair record; Court of Audit findings). Public Administration Minister Sanja Ajanović Hovnik resigned in October 2023 amid a scandal over a ministry tender. Health Minister Danijel Bešič Loredan was dismissed by parliament at Golob's own proposal in July 2023. Interior Minister Boštjan Poklukar and Justice Minister Andreja Katič both resigned on 3 November 2025, accepting responsibility after the Novo Mesto killing (Test 4).
  • The anti-corruption commission and the prosecution were permitted to proceed against the prime minister himself, in the open, while he held office: the Commission for the Prevention of Corruption (KPK) opened its investigation of him in November 2023, he testified before it and denied the accusations, police filed a criminal complaint against him in 2024, and in March 2025 state prosecutors asked a court to open a judicial investigation (Reuters; STA/AFP reporting). No institution was dissolved, defunded, or restaffed in response.
  • The KPK's January 2026 conclusion (below) simultaneously dismissed the other allegations against him — a fact his cabinet put on the record and the commission's critics do not dispute (AFP, January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The KPK concluded — in a decision reached at the end of 2025 and reported on 13–14 January 2026 — that Golob breached the integrity rules of his office: in two text messages to Interior Minister Tatjana Bobnar he expressed "concern" and "dissatisfaction" over the "staffing situation in the police," which the commission found was acting "against the expectations and responsibilities of his office." The underlying accusation, made by Bobnar when she resigned on 14 December 2022, was that Golob had pressed her and the police leadership to remove officials appointed under the previous Janša government — that is, that staffing of the police, the state's core coercive institution, was to follow the governing side's preferences (KPK findings via AFP/STA, January 2026). The finding carries no legal penalty by itself; Golob's lawyer called the conclusions "unlawful" and announced a court challenge, and Golob denies any breach. The criminal track remains exactly that — an ALLEGATION in process: as of the January 2026 reporting, the court had not yet decided on the prosecutors' March 2025 request for a judicial investigation, and no charge has been brought.
  • A second KPK procedure concerns a personal benefit from a government appointee — ALLEGATION, unadjudicated. In May 2025 the KPK opened an investigation into whether Golob violated integrity law by holidaying in 2023, free of charge, at the Karigador (Croatia) seaside property of businessman Tomaž Subotič — a member of Golob's own party whom the government appointed to the councils of two public hospitals (the Ljubljana Psychiatric Clinic and Celje General Hospital). Subotič resigned both posts after the reporting; all involved deny wrongdoing (ANSA/STA, 28 May 2025; 24ur reporting). Opposition media have reported that the KPK's draft findings in the case established a conflict of interest; that claim is CONTESTED and no final published KPK finding in the Karigador case was located in the record consulted for this dossier.
  • The Litijska purchase (above) was approved by his government as a body, at speed, in the last days of December 2023, on valuations the seller's side had supplied; the state's own audit found the file incomplete. Whatever individual liability courts may eventually assign — a criminal report against the former minister was filed by the successor Justice Ministry in November 2026, after Golob left office — the transaction was a documented failure of even-handed stewardship of public money at the center of his government (Court of Audit; STA, November 2026).
  • The outcome measure drifted the wrong way late in the term: Slovenia's Corruption Perceptions Index score fell from 60 in 2024 to 58 in 2025 (Transparency International, CPI 2025).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the public broadcaster, the electoral authority, the prosecution, the anti-corruption commission, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His signature institutional act moved power away from the government of the day. The July 2022 amendments to the RTV Slovenia Act abolished parliament's role in appointing the broadcaster's governing bodies — the mechanism through which the Janša government had installed its loyalists, provoking a year-long journalists' strike — and replaced the 29-member Programming Council with a 17-member Council drawn from civil-society institutions and RTV employees, which appoints the management board. Voters endorsed the reform in the referendum of 27 November 2022. When the Constitutional Court suspended parts of the law in February 2023, the government waited; when the court lifted the suspension in May 2023, implementation proceeded; and the court's eventual decision on the merits, published 24 July 2026, upheld the 2022 governance model (State Media Monitor record; MFRR statement, June 2023). Media-freedom monitors classify RTV under his reform as an independent public broadcaster, and Slovenia's RSF World Press Freedom ranking improved from 50th in 2023 to 42nd in 2024, standing 36th in the 2026 index (RSF via CIVICUS Monitor; RSF 2026).
  • The Constitutional Court ruled against his government on central policies and was obeyed: in December 2025 it struck down, unanimously, the Health Services Act's ban on public-sector doctors working privately — the centerpiece of his health reform — and Golob responded that the government "completely trusted the top court's judgement" and would seek a constitutional solution (Constitutional Court decision, December 2025; CE Report). The same court held the government's mid-strike restrictions on the doctors' strike unconstitutional (FIDES report to FEMS, April 2026).
  • The KPK investigated the sitting prime minister for over two years and published a finding against him two months before an election (Test 1). The electoral authority administered the March 2026 election he went on to lose the government over; turnout was 70.25% and the transfer of power was orderly (State Election Commission results).
  • The prosecution's independence cut against him personally: it was prosecutors, not the opposition, who sought the judicial investigation of the prime minister in March 2025 (AFP).

Evidence against:

  • For over a year his government failed to implement a Constitutional Court decision. On 30 June 2023 the court (case U-I-772/21) declared judges' salaries unconstitutional — too low, in breach of judicial independence and the separation of powers — and set a six-month deadline. The deadline lapsed on 3 January 2024 with nothing enacted; the Judicial Council informed the European Networks of Councils for the Judiciary that "no concrete actions were taken," calling it a breach of rule-of-law principles; judges protested for two weeks in January 2024, handling only urgent cases. The matter was folded into a general public-sector pay reform passed on 24 October 2024 and in force from 1 January 2025 — compliance, but roughly a year after the constitution's court had ordered it (ENCJ letter, 28 December 2023; International IDEA democracy tracker).
  • The central bank was left headless for almost thirteen months by appointment politics in which his coalition was a principal actor. Governor Boštjan Vasle's term expired on 8 January 2025; the coalition had made clear its preferred candidate was a finance-ministry state secretary, and parliament — where the coalition held the majority — rejected President Pirc Musar's nominee Anton Rop in November 2024 (24 votes in favor) and declined her subsequent candidates. Slovenia sat at the ECB Governing Council without a vote while a deputy governor acted with limited powers, until parliament confirmed that deputy, Primož Dolenc, on 3 February 2026 (55–5), on the president's nomination (Reuters, 3 February 2026; CE Report). The bank's operational independence was never interfered with; its leadership was held vacant by the majority's refusal of every name but its own preference — a grip on the appointment, if not on the institution.
  • The police-staffing pressure found by the KPK (Test 1) is also a grip fact: the prime minister's dissatisfaction, conveyed to the responsible minister, concerned which people held police posts after a change of government, and the sitting police chief's tenure was not extended. Golob's stated defense — that he sought to depoliticize a police force the previous government had politicized — is on the record and is the same justification every government offers; the commission weighed it and found a breach anyway.
  • Claims that coalition politicians pressured Constitutional Court judges during the RTV case were made at the time — in a separate opinion by Judge Rok Svetlič and in a June 2023 letter by opposition MEPs to the European Commission alleging "severe political pressure." These are CONTESTED claims by interested parties, not findings; no court or EU institution adopted them. They are recorded here because the court's own 4–1 procedural path in May 2023 — lifting the suspension while conceding it could not reach a merits majority — was, on any reading, an institution under strain in a case about his government's law.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He accepted the clearest electoral rebuke of his term. His coalition's assisted-dying law — adopted in July 2025 after a 2024 consultative referendum had supported the idea — was rejected by voters in the referendum of 23 November 2025 (about 53% against, on 40.9% turnout, with the no votes clearing the constitutional quorum of 20% of the electorate), suspending the law and barring parliament from revisiting the issue for twelve months. Golob, who had campaigned for the law, acknowledged the result and said only that "the challenge we are addressing still remains" (AP/AFP, 23 November 2025).
  • He accepted the Constitutional Court's December 2025 destruction of his dual-practice ban (Test 2) without attacking the court, and his coalition committed to redrafting within the constitution.
  • When the March 2026 election stripped his coalition of its majority, he first attempted a "government of national unity" of all parties except SDS, published proposed ministry allocations on 7 April — and when the arithmetic failed, he admitted GS would go into opposition rather than contesting the result, although his party had finished first. Power transferred to Janša's coalition on schedule (election record, March–June 2026). Notably, it was Janša who publicly alleged counting irregularities on election night; Golob defended the process that was removing him.
  • In crisis he moved fast and legislatively: after the catastrophic floods of August 2023 — the worst natural disaster in Slovenia's history — his government passed emergency intervention and reconstruction legislation within months (2023 flood record).

Evidence against:

  • The NATO referendum gambit was a doubling-down that he abandoned within two weeks. At the June 2025 Hague summit Golob committed Slovenia to NATO's new 5%-of-GDP defence-spending goal; in early July the National Assembly — his own coalition partners SD and Levica voting with the opposition — backed a consultative referendum on defence spending that his party opposed. On 4 July 2025 GS announced it would propose a referendum on NATO membership itself, Golob writing that "either we remain in the alliance and pay the dues, or we leave." Legal experts questioned its feasibility, allies and opponents alike treated it as a retaliatory bluff, and on 18 July parliament annulled the spending-referendum decision, after which the membership referendum was never submitted (SEE Check fact-check, July 2025; Defense News, 24 July 2025). He had answered a parliamentary defeat by putting the country's alliance membership in play, then quietly pocketed the proposal.
  • Confronted by the doctors' strike that began on 15 January 2024, his government legislated limits on the strike while it was running (spring 2024) — limits the Constitutional Court later held unconstitutional — and, on the doctors' union's documented account, withdrew from pay arrangements its own negotiators had coordinated, leaving the dispute formally unresolved at the end of his term (FIDES report to FEMS, April 2026). The wobble — organized medicine saying the system was breaking — was answered with attrition rather than settlement.
  • He did not honor his own stated consequence for the KPK's integrity finding (Test 5): he disputed its lawfulness through counsel and reframed the commission's timing as political, rather than accepting the finding of the body his government had left free to reach it.
  • The campaign that ended his tenure was marked by the "Black Cube" affair — secretly recorded conversations of figures in his circle (including his former justice minister and the lawyer for the Litijska seller), published on an anonymous website in the weeks before the vote. Mladina's investigation of 16 March 2026 reported the operation bore the marks of the Israeli private-intelligence firm Black Cube; who commissioned it is unproven, and the affair is recorded here as CONTESTED. Golob's side alleged foreign meddling in the election. The allegation was never substantiated to the standard of a finding before the vote, and — to his credit on this test — he nonetheless accepted the election's outcome (above).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Roma communities, workers, pensioners, patients, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His social legislation was universalist in design. The abolition of voluntary supplementary health insurance and its replacement, from 1 January 2024, by a flat mandatory health contribution removed a regressive premium market from basic coverage, as pledged. The long-term care system began phasing in during 2025, financed by a new contribution, after voters in November 2022 approved his government's postponement and redesign of the inherited act. The minimum wage was raised in every year of the term. The pension reform adopted on 19 September 2025 (49 votes to 1) was negotiated with the social partners: it raises the statutory retirement age from 65 to 67 between 2028 and 2035, and the full-career retirement age from 60 to 62, while raising accrual rates — a sustainability reform whose costs fall on future retirees generally, not on a named group (OECD, Pensions at a Glance 2025).
  • His opponents' voters were counted by the same rules as his own: the November 2025 referendum that killed his assisted-dying law was honored (Test 3), the November 2022 referendums were held on laws his own majority could have simply enacted, and the March 2026 election that ended his government was administered without interference.
  • After the Novo Mesto killing (below), the two responsible ministers resigned within days, and Golob addressed the town council in person on 28 October 2025, acknowledging fear on all sides and stating the measures were "about tackling crime… not about confronting the Roma" (prime minister's address, Novo Mesto, 28 October 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The Šutar Act counted the Roma out of equal citizenship in practice, whatever its text says. On 25 October 2025 Aleš Šutar was fatally beaten outside a Novo Mesto nightclub; a 21-year-old Roma man was arrested as the suspect (the criminal case is an ALLEGATION in process; he was charged with causing particularly serious bodily harm). Mass protests followed, framed in much of the coverage as protests against "Roma criminality." Within three weeks, under urgent procedure, parliament adopted the Act on Urgent Measures to Ensure Public Security — the "Šutar Law" (17–18 November 2025) — allowing police to enter homes and vehicles without a court order in designated "security-risk areas," expanding surveillance and license-plate recognition, permitting military assistance to police, and linking welfare and municipal funding to security compliance. The law names no ethnicity; its designated zones, as the European Roma Grassroots Organisations Network, the Roma Foundation for Europe, and Slovenia's own human-rights commentary observed, map onto Roma settlements, and the rights bodies warned it institutionalizes ethnic profiling and collective punishment of a community for an individual's alleged act (ERGO Network statement, November 2025; International IDEA democracy tracker, November 2025). Golob's government drafted it, defended it as crime policy, and passed it in the emotional aftermath of a killing — the test's question is whether the accounting included the Roma as citizens; on the documented criticism of the state's own rights community, the law treated their neighborhoods as the risk to be managed.
  • The Roma file did not begin with Šutar. Roma in south-eastern settlements have lived for decades, documented by Amnesty International over many years, in informal housing with inadequate access to water, sanitation, and electricity; no Golob program closed that gap in four years, and the relationship between the state and those settlements ended his term in a security frame rather than an inclusion one.
  • Patients were counted in rhetoric and out in fact (see also Test 5): roughly 140,000 insured people had no chosen personal doctor at the end of his term — up from slightly under 122,000 at the end of 2021, on Medical Chamber figures — including some 28,000 in Ljubljana alone; the OECD recorded a median wait of 667 days for a hip replacement in 2024, among the longest in the OECD. Golob was reported — by the opposition daily Demokracija, a hostile source, recorded as such — to have told parliament that anyone without a personal doctor "simply does not want one"; whether or not the sentence is exact, the numbers it answered were official.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since June 2022, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly delivered):

  • "Normality" and depoliticization — substantially kept. The 2022 campaign's central promise was to reverse the Janša government's pressure on institutions. The RTV reform removed parliament from broadcaster appointments (Test 2); press-freedom rankings rose (50th → 42nd → 36th, RSF 2023–2026); no journalist, judge, or prosecutor was removed by his government in four years.
  • Repeal of the Janša income-tax reform — kept. Promised before the election, enacted in his first year, restoring the prior allowances schedule.
  • Supplementary health insurance — kept. Abolished and replaced by the mandatory health contribution from 1 January 2024, as pledged.
  • Pension reform — delivered. Promised as a necessity throughout the term; adopted 19 September 2025 with social-partner negotiation and a near-unanimous vote (Test 4).
  • A full term, and a clean exit. His three-party coalition — in a country accustomed to collapsing governments — served the full term (1 June 2022 – 4 June 2026), and on losing the ability to govern he left office on the constitutional schedule (Test 3).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The resignation pledge — not kept. Ahead of the 2022 election Golob stated he would "certainly resign" if the anti-corruption watchdog ever ruled he had breached integrity rules (statement circulated by conservative media and reported by AFP, January 2026). In January 2026 the KPK ruled exactly that (Test 1). He did not resign; his cabinet disputed the finding and his lawyer announced a court challenge to it. The finding is contestable and contested — but the pledge, as made, contained no exception for findings he disagreed with.
  • The property tax — announced, designed, shelved. A centerpiece of his housing and tax-shift program: unveiled on 23 December 2024 as a 1.45% levy on second and additional properties, explicitly revenue-neutral against lower labor taxes. On 4 July 2025 the finance ministry postponed it indefinitely (CE Report; STA via Connecting Region, July 2025). The promised rebalancing of taxation from labor to property never occurred.
  • Healthcare — the signature promise, not delivered. He pledged to shorten waiting lists and secure a personal doctor for everyone. Four years on: waiting lists in the OECD's longest tier (667-day median hip wait, 2024), the number without a chosen doctor higher than when he took office (~122,000 → ~140,000), his flagship dual-practice ban struck down by the Constitutional Court in December 2025, and the doctors' strike unresolved (Tests 3–4).
  • The preferential vote — a referendum result left unimplemented. In the consultative referendum of 9 June 2024 voters approved introducing a preferential vote in parliamentary elections; the electoral system remained unchanged for the March 2026 election, as the election's own documentation notes.
  • The Romanian account — his explanation not supported by the later police finding (CONTESTED). During and after the 2022 campaign, a bank account in Romania was linked to Golob in connection with GEN-I's Balkan business; Golob maintained his identity had been stolen. In November 2026 Slovenian police, working with Romanian authorities, concluded their investigation and reported finding no evidence of identity abuse — his account is not supported by the police findings. Golob responded that the Romanian prosecutor had closed the matter on limitation grounds and produced a 2023 Raiffeisen Bank clarification attributing the link to human error and similar identification data (police findings and Golob's response, November 2026). The underlying GEN-I file predates his premiership; it is graded here only as a word-versus-record item, and it remains contested between the police conclusion and his documentation.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Robert Golob governed Slovenia for a full term and left office the way the test most values: beaten in coalition arithmetic after an election his own side administered, handing power to his principal rival without contesting the count. The institutions he promised to un-grip were, in the main, un-gripped — the public broadcaster's appointments were taken out of parliament's hands and the Constitutional Court upheld the design; the anti-corruption commission investigated him for two years and found against him in print, two months before an election, and nothing happened to it. Against that stand three debits of the same kind: he pressed his interior minister over police staffing until the integrity commission called it a breach — and then declined the resignation he had pledged for exactly that event; his majority left a Constitutional Court order on judges' pay unimplemented past its deadline and a central-bank governorship vacant for a year rather than accept another institution's nominee; and in his term's last autumn, after one man's killing, his government wrote a security law whose map is the map of Roma settlements. His word was kept where it was structural — the tax repeal, the insurance reform, the pension reform, the full term — and broken where it was personal or hard: the resignation pledge, the property tax, the doctor for everyone. The seat, on this record, held Slovenia's institutions more loosely than it found them, and its occupant's promises less tightly than he made them.
