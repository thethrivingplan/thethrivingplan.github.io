The Ruler's Test — Salva Kiir Mayardit, President of South Sudan

Subject: Salva Kiir Mayardit — President of South Sudan since independence on 9 July 2011, and the only president the country has ever had. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: under South Sudan's transitional constitution the president is the executive — head of state, head of government, commander-in-chief — and in Kiir's hands the office also appoints and dismisses vice-presidents, ministers, governors, judges, generals, the central bank governor and the elections commission leadership, and rules between those appointments by republican decree. No other office in the country can check it in practice. The holder named in the brief is verified: Kiir still holds the seat in November 2026 — he dissolved the entire transitional government by decree on 22 September 2026, retained the presidency himself, registered as a voter in Juba on 20 November 2026, and will contest the coming election as the SPLM's candidate, having confirmed in July 2026: "I will be on the ballot." Two framings in the brief needed updating rather than correcting. First, there is no longer a transitional unity government to grade him inside of: the power-sharing architecture built by the 2018 Revitalized Peace Agreement (R-ARCSS) — five vice-presidents, a unity cabinet, a reconstituted parliament — was abolished by his own decree of 22 September 2026 and replaced by a caretaker administration of thirteen ministers answerable to him alone. Second, Riek Machar is a former First Vice-President: placed under house arrest on 26 March 2025, indicted on 11 September 2025 over the March 2025 Nasir violence, on trial before a special court in Juba since 22–23 September 2025 (128 sessions held by mid-November 2026, no verdict as of this grading), and formally removed from the vice-presidency by the 22 September 2026 dissolution. The charges against Machar and his seven co-accused — murder, treason, conspiracy, terrorism, destruction of public property and crimes against humanity — are ALLEGATIONs before a court that has not ruled; Machar denies ordering the Nasir attack and calls the trial political, and both positions are printed in this dossier as positions, not findings. On elections: South Sudan has never held a national election since independence. Polls were scheduled and abandoned for 2015, 2018, 2021, February 2023's roadmap date of December 2024, and then 22 December 2026 under the consensual September 2024 extension — and on 14 November 2026 the National Elections Commission moved the date again, to 11 February 2027, a 51-day shift announced on the day voter registration opened. Kiir is graded on his full tenure since 2011, weighted to the period this series grades hardest: the life and death of the 2018 settlement, 2020–2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The Test's ethic, printed on its card, governs the grading: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies here in a particular form: Kiir has never been elected president in a national vote — there has never been one — so there is no voter platform to protect or to grade. He is therefore graded on the undertakings he actually gave: the 2018 agreement he signed, the roadmaps he assented to, and his own public pledges. Governing on that announced program is never itself a failure; marks turn only on how the seat is held. The no-good-choice clause is applied where it genuinely fits (Test 3 and Test 5, the 2024 pipeline collapse), by its mechanics: the options that existed are named, the constraint is recorded rather than the demerit, and what remains gradable — whether he built the trap, and his honesty inside it — is graded around it.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The law, in its most visible current case, is being applied to the second-highest figure of the settlement rather than around him. Machar's prosecution runs through a court, in public: televised proceedings at Freedom Hall in Juba, defence counsel heard across 128 sessions by mid-November 2026, defence applications ruled on in the open, and the accused himself testifying in his own defence in October–November 2026 and denying the charges to the bench (Radio Tamazuj and Eye Radio session reporting, September–November 2026; African Human Rights Law Journal, 2026). Whatever its origins (Test 2), the state chose indictment and trial over the region's older methods — disappearance or a battlefield settlement — and the file is being built where cameras can see it.
  • His own camp is not formally untouchable, and the falls reach the very top of it. On 12 November 2025 Kiir dismissed Vice-President Benjamin Bol Mel — his protégé, SPLM first deputy chairman, and the man widely read as his chosen successor — stripped him of the four-star general's rank he had conferred on him only in September 2025, demoted him to private, and expelled him from the National Security Service, in the same night's decrees that removed the central bank governor and the revenue authority chief (presidential decrees via state television, 12–13 November 2025; Radio Tamazuj, November 2025). In October 2024 he had likewise dismissed the long-serving and widely feared director-general of the National Security Service's internal bureau. No reason was published for any of these — which cuts both ways, and is graded as such below — but the record shows the inner circle's members can and do fall.
  • The electoral framework now being built formally applies to his own side: the SPLM conducted internal primaries through 2026, and on 20 November 2026 Kiir registered as an ordinary voter at a public registration centre in Juba and urged citizens to collect their cards (South Sudan government statement, 20 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The incidence of prosecution runs one way. The figures detained, charged or driven out since March 2025 are the peace partner's: Machar and seven SPLM/A-IO co-accused; the petroleum minister and the deputy chief of staff of the SPLA-IO detained in the March 2025 arrests; opposition figures the UN Commission records as arbitrarily detained by the National Security Service through 2025, accused of inciting the Nasir conflict (UN Commission on Human Rights in South Sudan, report to the General Assembly, October 2025). Against that shelf stands the other side's column: the same Commission documents SSPDF aerial bombardments of civilian areas, a state policy of extrajudicial killings in communal-conflict zones, and systematic obstruction of accountability — and no comparable indictment of any government commander appears in the record assembled here. One law that only ever lands on one side of the old front line is not one law.
  • The corruption file shows the same asymmetry at national scale. The UN Commission's September 2025 report Plundering a Nation, built on two years of investigation, finds that more than USD 25.2 billion in oil inflows since 2011 produced almost no public services; that the off-budget "Oil for Roads" programme channelled an estimated USD 2.2 billion into patronage networks through companies the report links to Bol Mel, with most promised roads undelivered; and that a politically connected private firm, Crawford Capital, retained the great bulk of the non-oil revenues it collected on the state's behalf while little reached the treasury (UN Commission, Plundering a Nation, 16 September 2025; UN News, 16 September 2025). No prosecution of any figure implicated in those schemes appears in the record. The government's answer to the report was dismissal of its findings, not a single opened case.
  • Even the strongest for-shelf item proves the against case on inspection. Bol Mel was not convicted by a court, audited by a commission, or removed after any published finding: he was unmade by signature — appointed by decree, promoted by decree, and demoted to private by decree, with no reason stated then or since. The same is true of the eight finance ministers rotated through the office since 2020, the central bank governors, and the chairperson of the National Elections Commission, whom Kiir replaced in September 2026 weeks before dissolving the government, again without a public explanation (Radio Tamazuj, November 2025; Al Jazeera reporting, September 2026). In this seat, outcomes for friend and foe alike arrive from one hand rather than from one law — and a law whose application depends entirely on one man's signature is the thing Test 1 exists to detect.
  • A specific 2024 undertaking on the law's reach was given and not kept: the government promised in 2024 to revoke the National Security Service's powers of arrest and detention without warrant; the UN Commission records that the promise was broken and that the NSS used those powers in 2025 against the detained opposition figures above (UN Commission, October 2025).

Why the grade lands here: The for-shelf is real — a televised trial instead of a disappearance, and a successor-designate destroyed rather than protected. But both items run through the same channel as everything else in this state: the president's discretion. Where an independent process has documented wrongdoing by the seat's own networks (the UN Commission's two-year corruption investigation), the law has not moved at all; where it has moved, it has moved against the detained peace partner. The grade is FAILS because the pattern across fifteen years is consistent: the law's incidence tracks alignment, and its supreme instrument is a decree, not a code.

Helped: The prosecution service and the presidency itself, whose authority the Machar trial performs; SPLM factions that gained room when Bol Mel fell; citizens in the narrow sense that the country's most visible case is at least proceeding in a courtroom they can watch. Hurt: Machar and his seven co-accused, detained since March 2025 under charges still unproven (named here as accused, not as guilty); SPLM/A-IO officials and supporters detained or excluded since March 2025; taxpayers and service-users across the country, on the UN Commission's finding that the revenues collected in their name were diverted while schools, hospitals and payrolls went unfunded; whistleblowers and journalists working under unrevoked NSS detention powers.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Some referees still stand and publish. The ceasefire monitors (CTSAMVM) and the peace-implementation monitor (RJMEC) continued issuing critical reports through the settlement's collapse without being dissolved by Juba. UNMISS remains deployed. The National Elections Commission formally issued the November 2026 postponement itself, published a full revised calendar, and opened registration at 3,161 centres across the ten states and three administrative areas (NEC announcements, 14 November 2026). The special court trying Machar has entertained defence applications and heard the accused at length rather than proceeding summarily (session reporting, September–November 2026).
  • The 2018 settlement's collegial structure did operate for five years: from February 2020 to September 2026 South Sudan was formally governed with five vice-presidents drawn from the signatory parties, a unity cabinet and a reconstituted parliament — institutions Kiir headed but did not, in that period, abolish.

Evidence against:

  • On 22 September 2026 Kiir dissolved the referees' entire building by republican order: the transitional government, the Transitional National Legislative Assembly, the Council of States, all ten state governments and assemblies, and the administrations of the three administrative areas; the First Vice-President and all four vice-presidents were relieved, and every national minister dismissed. In their place he appointed thirteen caretaker ministers and caretaker governors of his own choosing — caretakers expressly barred from entering long-term agreements, incurring substantial debt or disposing of public assets without presidential authorisation, so that, in the words of the order's own structure, every decision of consequence runs through one office (republican order via SSBC, 22 September 2026; Atlantic Digest, September 2026). The legal basis was assembled the same day: amendments to the elections law passed by the parliament he dissolved that afternoon, following September 2026 constitutional amendments that removed the census and permanent-constitution prerequisites the peace agreement had set before elections (UN Commission statement, October 2026; TVN World, November 2026). The UN Commission's chair named the sequence in terms this dossier records as a named critic's characterization, printed because of who speaks it: "a constitutional coup through legislative reforms: the unilateral dismantling of the inclusive power-sharing settlement and concentration of all authority in the President" (Yasmin Sooka, UN Commission, October 2026).
  • The election's own referees serve at his pleasure in fact: the NEC chairperson was replaced in September 2026 without explanation, weeks before the dissolution; the president who will oversee the vote is also its expected ruling-party candidate, while his principal rival remains detained and on trial and the rival's party is excluded from the process (UN Commission, October 2026). The UN Security Council's April 2026 reduction of its electoral-support role narrowed the outside check at the same moment.
  • The accountability referee designed to be independent of him was never allowed to exist. The Hybrid Court for South Sudan — the African Union-backed court written into Chapter V of the agreement he signed in 2018, meant to try the war's crimes on all sides — remains unestablished in November 2026, eight years on; the truth commission and reparations authority advanced only to legislation and selection processes by 2026 and are not operational (OHCHR reporting, 2026). A referee that could have judged his own side has been kept unborn for the whole life of the settlement.
  • The pattern below the summit is continuous rotation by decree: eight finance ministers since 2020, central bank governors dismissed without stated reasons (most recently in the 12 November 2025 decrees), an army chief dismissed in July 2025, his replacement dismissed in October 2025, and the first man reinstated — a security and economic referee corps whose tenure is measured in weeks and whose survival rule is the president's confidence (Radio Tamazuj, November 2025). The judiciary that tries Machar sits inside the same structure: a special court constituted for the case, under a chief justice and judicial leadership appointed by the presidency, in a system whose ordinary courts the UN Commission describes as starved of funds while rule-of-law institutions decay (UN Commission, October 2025).

Why the grade lands here: Test 2 is decided by September 2026. Whatever autonomy South Sudan's institutions had under the settlement existed inside a structure the president could — and finally did — abolish in a single afternoon, replacing it with caretakers forbidden to act without him, on the eve of an election he will contest and oversee. The surviving for-shelf items (monitors still publishing, a commission still calendaring, a trial still hearing the defence) are real, but they operate on sufferance inside a grip that has now been demonstrated, not merely threatened. FAILS.

Helped: The presidency, which enters the electoral period holding every lever the settlement had distributed; the caretaker officeholders appointed 22 September 2026; the SPLM apparatus around the president's candidacy. Hurt: The dissolved officeholders and assemblies — Machar and the four vice-presidents, parliamentarians, state governments — removed without any vote; the NEC and judiciary as institutions, whose independence the sequence repriced in public; voters, whose election will be administered under a framework one contestant controls; and the signatory parties of 2018, whose guaranteed shares of power were cancelled by one party's signature.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The largest adjustment of the tenure is the settlement itself. After the 2018 signature, Kiir did the thing the wobble of war-weariness and regional pressure demanded: in February 2020 he formed the unity government and brought Machar back to Juba as First Vice-President, and for five years the arrangement — however strained — held the country out of full-scale civil war. When the parties jointly judged the transition unready in September 2024, the extension to December 2026 was assented to by the signatories collectively, not imposed by him alone (R-ARCSS parties' decision, September 2024).
  • He has repeatedly opened doors the hard line of his own camp opposed. In May 2024 his government launched the Tumaini Initiative in Nairobi to bring holdout armed groups who never signed the 2018 agreement into the settlement — another option, created by his own side and pursued through initialled protocols before it stalled (UNMISS reporting, 2024–2025). In the economy's worst moment he dealt pragmatically with the Sudanese authorities controlling his only export route, and oil production and exports restarted on 8 January 2025 after the pipeline shutdown (Petroleum Ministry statements, January 2025).
  • Inside his own house, pushback has sometimes moved him against his own prior choices: Bol Mel's removal in November 2025 followed months of sanctions pressure, corruption reporting and internal SPLM friction over oil-revenue management, and it destroyed the succession project Kiir himself had built that year (Radio Tamazuj and regional reporting, November 2025). And in November 2026, faced with an electoral calendar that plainly was not ready — registration materials reached parts of the country only in early November, and the Civil Society Forum had stated on 28 October 2026 that conditions did not support credible elections — the process he oversees accepted the NEC's 51-day postponement rather than forcing a hollow vote on 22 December (NEC, 14 November 2026; South Sudan Civil Society Forum statement, 28 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The defining pushback of 2025 was answered with escalation. In early March 2025 the White Army — a Nuer community militia the government links to the SPLA-IO, a link Machar denies — overran the SSPDF garrison at Nasir, killing its commander and soldiers; on 7 March a UN helicopter evacuating soldiers was fired on and a crew member killed (African Human Rights Law Journal, 2026; UN reporting, March 2025). Those were crimes demanding an answer. The answer Kiir's government chose went far beyond the garrison's attackers: SPLM/A-IO officials were arrested in Juba, Machar was placed under house arrest on 26 March, Ugandan troops were invited into the capital, and government aircraft bombed Nasir town in mid-March 2025 — strikes residents and monitors described as hitting civilian areas, an account the government disputes, saying it targeted armed positions (contemporaneous Reuters and local reporting, March 2025; the characterisation of the strikes is contested and is printed as such). The ceasefire monitors' proposed option — a neutral unified force to replace the exhausted garrison, recommended before the overrun — had not been taken up (UN Commission reporting, 2025).
  • The regional and international answer to the crisis was delivered to him repeatedly and in writing: the African Union's C5 and IGAD, after his September 2026 dissolution, reaffirmed the 2018 agreement as the framework, called for an immediate cessation of hostilities and the release of political detainees, and urged parties to refrain from unilateral actions (AU C5 Plus statement, 29 September 2026). The UN Commission through January and October 2026 urged de-escalation, an end to airstrikes in civilian areas, and a return to the agreement's pathways. The documented record shows no release, no ceasefire initiative and no re-engagement with the agreement's framework in response: the Jonglei offensives continued through 2026, with SSPDF evacuation orders in January–March 2026 emptying counties ahead of operations (UN Commission, January 2026; UN Protection Cluster, October 2026). Under the other-option rule this is the file's clearest debit: another option — detainee release and a return to the settlement's table, proposed by the agreement's own guarantors — became available, repeatedly and publicly, and there is no record of it being considered. It was not declined with stated reasons. It was not looked at.
  • His own movement's internal wobble is answered with the decree book rather than with process: no reshuffle in the record — not Bol Mel's, not the army chiefs', not the finance ministers' — was accompanied by a stated reason, an inquiry, or a charge (Radio Tamazuj, November 2025). Adjustment happens, but silently and personally, which teaches every other actor in the system that the only appeal is to the man, not to any rule.
  • The no-good-choice clause, applied where it fits: the 2024 fiscal collapse. In February 2024 the export pipeline through Sudan ruptured inside Sudan's own civil war; Sudan declared force majeure and South Sudan's oil — over 90 percent of state revenue — stopped for nearly a year. The options actually open to Kiir in 2024 were all harmful: slash the payroll and the security wage bill in a country held together by both, borrow against future cargoes at punitive terms, or run arrears and let inflation tax everyone. He largely ran arrears — civil-service salary arrears reached 8 months by June 2024 and 12–13 months by February 2025 (IMF, 2024; UN Security Council reporting, 2025) — and the dossier records the shock itself as a constraint, not a demerit: no choice available to him in 2024 was good, and the restart he negotiated for January 2025 counts in his favour above. What remains gradable, and is graded on Tests 4 and 5, is the trap's construction: the single-pipeline dependence and the absence of any reserve were built across thirteen years of his presidency in which the oil flowed and neither an alternative route nor a stabilisation fund was delivered.

Why the grade lands here: The adjustments are real and some are large — a unity government formed and held for five years, a peace door opened in Nairobi, a successor destroyed when the pressure mounted, an unready election date allowed to move. But the pattern has a consistent boundary: Kiir adjusts where the pushback does not touch the seat's grip, and escalates or refuses to look where it does — Nasir, the guarantors' detainee-release call, the Jonglei warnings. A ruler who answers some wobbles openly and meets the largest ones with dissolution and airstrikes is the definition of MIXED on this test, and the grade is stated with its boundary named rather than averaged away.

Helped: South Sudanese generally, in the five years (2020–2024) when the settlement he maintained kept national war switched off; holdout groups offered a door at Tumaini; public employees, when the January 2025 oil restart partially restored the payroll's funding; voters, in the narrow sense that an unready December vote was not forced. Hurt: The people of Nasir town, bombed in March 2025 after their county's garrison fell (responsibility for the garrison attack itself lies with its attackers; the town's bombardment was the government's chosen answer); SPLM/A-IO's detained leadership, held while guarantors' release calls went unanswered; the communities of Jonglei and Upper Nile whose counties were emptied by evacuation orders through 2026; and every official in the system taught by silent decrees that tenure is personal, not institutional.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition's communities, the protesters, the displaced, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The state's doors stayed open to strangers at scale. Since April 2023, 1,424,507 people fleeing Sudan's war had crossed into South Sudan by 31 August 2026 — returnees and refugees absorbed by one of the world's poorest states without a policy of refusal (UN Protection Cluster, October 2026). Whatever its capacity failures, the border did not close.
  • The electoral count now being built is formally universal: registration opened on 14 November 2026 at 3,161 centres across all ten states and three administrative areas, including opposition-leaning regions, and Kiir publicly urged every eligible citizen to register and collect a card (NEC, November 2026; government statement, 20 November 2026). The 2018 framework he headed also carried the agreement's 35-percent women's representation rule in its legislatures while it stood.

Evidence against:

  • The humanitarian ledger is the largest counting failure in this series. Ten million people — three-quarters of the population — need humanitarian assistance in 2026 (OCHA Humanitarian Needs and Response Plan, February 2026). The IPC projects 7.8 million people, 56 percent of the population, in acute food insecurity at Phase 3 or above for April–July 2026, including 73,300 in Catastrophe (Phase 5), with a credible risk of famine in four counties — Luakpiny/Nasir and Ulang in Upper Nile, Nyirol and Akobo in Jonglei (IPC analysis via FAO/WFP/UNICEF, April 2026). Two million children under five are acutely malnourished. These are not weather statistics alone: the UN agencies name escalating conflict and displacement as the crisis's drivers, and the four famine-risk counties are the same counties at the centre of the government's 2025–2026 military campaigns — Nasir above all, whose people were bombed in March 2025 and whose county now faces famine risk with its markets and services destroyed (FAO GIEWS country brief, September 2026).
  • The 2026 campaigns fell, in their geography, on the counted-out. Between January and May 2026, fighting between the SSPDF and SPLA-IO displaced more than 420,800 people, over 324,100 of them in Jonglei; more than 240,000 have been displaced in Jonglei alone since December 2025 (ACAPS, June 2026; UN Protection Cluster, October 2026). The UN Commission documents repeated SSPDF airstrikes in Uror, Ayod and Nyirol counties killing and injuring civilians and destroying homes, markets and medical facilities, and 72-hour evacuation orders — in March 2026 ordering all civilians, humanitarian workers and even peacekeeping staff out of operational areas — that emptied whole counties ahead of offensives, driving some 100,000 people across the Ethiopian border (UN Commission, January 2026; Protection Cluster reporting, March 2026). The communities bearing this are overwhelmingly the Nuer and other opposition-associated populations of the greater Upper Nile — the same constituency whose political leadership sits detained in Juba. A contested item is recorded in the same register: on 19 October 2026 drone strikes on Amiet Market in the Abyei area killed over 220 people by the caretaker administration's count; the perpetrator is not established in the record assembled here and no attribution is made (ACAPS, October 2026).
  • The state's own servants are counted late or never: salary arrears stood at 8–13 months across agencies in January 2026 and 13–14 months by July 2026 on World Bank figures — teachers, nurses and soldiers unpaid for over a year while, on the UN Commission's findings, off-budget schemes moved billions (World Bank figures, 2026; UN Commission, September 2025). Cholera since September 2024 has passed 98,000 cases and 1,624 deaths in a country whose health budget the Commission finds starved (UN OCHA, February 2026).
  • Politically, the count is being taken with the opposition's voters' champion in a cell: the February 2027 vote will proceed while Machar is detained and on trial, his party excluded from the process, and other opposition actors outside it — a structure the UN Commission warned in October 2026 eschews inclusion and promotes exclusion, in a country whose short history shows where exclusionary counting ends (UN Commission, October 2026).

Why the grade lands here: Test 4 asks whether everyone is in the accounting. On the documented record, the costs of this seat's choices — the campaigns, the arrears, the diverted revenues, the exclusionary election — land with grim consistency on the same populations: the opposition's home counties, the displaced, the unpaid, and the hungry, while the for-shelf (an open border, a universal registration) shows the state counting everyone only where counting costs the seat nothing. When 56 percent of the population is in acute food insecurity and the famine-risk map is the campaign map, the grade is FAILS.

Helped: Arrivals from Sudan's war, admitted rather than refused; communities in government-held areas where payrolls and services, however thin, continued; registered voters in all states, formally included in the coming count. Hurt: The people of Jonglei and Upper Nile — over 420,800 displaced in five months of 2026, the bombed counties of Uror, Ayod, Nyirol and Nasir, and the 73,300 people in Phase 5 catastrophe; 2.2 million acutely malnourished children; public employees owed over a year of wages; cholera's 1,624 dead in a starved health system; and opposition-supporting citizens, whose political weight is being counted in an election their detained leader cannot contest.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 2011, with dates; the signed settlement of 2018 is the spine of the file.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The founding signature was performed for five years. Kiir signed the R-ARCSS on 12 September 2018 and did its central act: the unity government was formed in February 2020 with Machar as First Vice-President, the five-vice-president structure and unity cabinet functioned, and Kiir's repeated public pledge that he would not take the country back to war held at national scale from 2020 through 2024 — the longest stretch without full-scale civil war in the country's existence. A kept word of that size belongs on this shelf at full weight.
  • The September 2024 roadmap was his word too, and its process is running. The extension the parties jointly assented to in September 2024 promised elections in December 2026 with the prerequisites decoupled if necessary; through 2026 Kiir publicly and repeatedly committed to the vote, confirmed his own candidacy in July 2026, assented to the decoupling amendments, and registered to vote in public on 20 November 2026 (Citizen TV interview, July 2026; government statement, November 2026). The machinery of the promise — commission, calendar, registration — exists and is moving.
  • The oil restart was delivered as stated: production and exports resumed 8 January 2025 after the shutdown, and output has since recovered to roughly 170,000–175,000 barrels per day (Petroleum Ministry, January 2025; African Development Bank outlook, November 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Elections — the date has been given and moved for a decade. Kiir's governments scheduled and then abandoned votes for 2015, 2018, 2021 and December 2024 before the current cycle; the December 2026 date he championed through the summer of 2026 was moved by the NEC on 14 November 2026 to 11 February 2027, five weeks before polling, on the day registration opened (NEC, 14 November 2026). The extra 51 days may prove wise (see the crossing named in the summary), but the word as given — December 2026, the date on which he dissolved a government in September expressly to prepare — was not kept, and the transitional period his signature was to end now extends past its term with him still in the seat.
  • One army — never delivered. The agreement's core security promise, a single unified force, produced a partial first phase and then stalled: Phase II training and deployment were still being urged on the parties by UNMISS as unmet benchmarks years later (UNMISS, November 2024), and in 2025–2026 the two armies the agreement was meant to merge fought open war across Jonglei and Upper Nile. The promise's failure is no longer administrative; it is the war itself.
  • Justice — the Hybrid Court was promised in 2018 and does not exist. Chapter V's three mechanisms were the agreement's answer to the war's crimes: by 2026 the truth commission and reparations authority had reached legislation and selection stages without operating, and the Hybrid Court — the one body that could have tried Kiir's own side — remains unestablished (OHCHR, 2026). Alongside it, the 2024 promise to revoke NSS warrantless detention powers was broken on the UN Commission's record (October 2025), and the permanent constitution and census he signed up to as election prerequisites were not delivered but deleted as prerequisites by the September 2026 amendments.
  • The payroll word: through the oil recovery of 2025–2026, with production back near 175,000 barrels a day, salary arrears grew — to 13–14 months by July 2026 (World Bank figures, 2026). The state's first charge on its restored revenue was not the wages it owed.

Why the grade lands here: Kiir's word divides cleanly, and the summary below names the division rather than hiding it in the average. The promises that protected the seat and the settlement's shell — stay, sign, form the unity government, hold the vote eventually, keep the oil flowing — were substantially kept, one of them (five years without national war) at historic weight. The promises that would have limited the seat or judged it — elections on a date, one army, a court for all sides, detention powers surrendered, wages paid first — were broken, stalled past recognition, or amended away. That is a MIXED file with the seam showing, and the seam is the finding.

Helped: The signatory elites and the public alike, in the kept half: five years without full-scale war is the single largest delivered good in this dossier; oil-sector workers and the treasury, by the 2025 restart; voters, by a process that now at least has dates, centres and cards. Hurt: Citizens promised a vote in 2015, 2018, 2021, 2024 and December 2026, still waiting in November 2026; the war's victims on all sides, promised a Hybrid Court in 2018 and given none; soldiers of both armies, promised one uniform and kept in two; public employees, promised wages that fell 13–14 months behind while the oil recovered; and detainees held under detention powers the government promised in 2024 to give up.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Salva Kiir is the only president South Sudan has had, and this dossier grades fifteen years of one seat: the man who signed the 2018 settlement, performed its central act for five years, and then — between March 2025 and September 2026 — dismantled it piece by piece, ending with a single decree that dissolved every institution the agreement had built and left his own office as the only one standing. One law fails because the law's incidence tracks the old front line: a televised trial for the detained peace partner and documented impunity for his own side's bombardments and diversions, with outcomes for friend and foe alike delivered by decree rather than by process — Bol Mel's destruction by signature is the emblem, not the exception. Without gripping fails on the afternoon of 22 September 2026, when the grip was demonstrated in full: legislature, vice-presidents and state governments abolished, caretakers forbidden to decide without him, the election's referees his appointees, and the one court that could have judged his side kept unestablished for eight years. The wobble is mixed because the adjustments are genuine — a unity government held for five years, the Tumaini door opened, a successor sacrificed, an unready date allowed to move — but they stop at a consistent boundary: where pushback touched the seat itself (Nasir, the guarantors' call to release detainees and return to the table), the answer was escalation or a refusal to look, while the one true no-good-choice in the file, the 2024 pipeline collapse, is recorded as a constraint rather than a demerit, with its trap's construction — thirteen years of single-route dependence — graded where it belongs. Counts everyone fails on the arithmetic of the hurt: ten million in need, 7.8 million acutely food insecure, famine risk in exactly the counties his forces campaigned through, 420,800 displaced in five months, and an election being counted with the opposition's leader in detention. His word is mixed, and the two axes are printed separately because they cross in all four directions on this record: he kept his word and it helped people — the 2020 unity government and five years without national war; he kept his word and it still hurt people — the Nasir accountability he promised his own side was delivered as a prosecution, inside a campaign of airstrikes and county-emptying offensives that fell on the accused's home populations; he went against his word and it helped people — the December 2026 date he had championed was abandoned five weeks out, and the 51 days bought a registration process that was visibly not ready; and he broke his word and it hurt people — one army, the Hybrid Court, the surrendered detention powers and the paid payroll, each promised, each undelivered, each feeding the war and the hunger above. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the 11 February 2027 vote happens and whom it counts, whether the Machar trial reaches a verdict a neutral observer can respect, and whether the February 2027 seat is filled by an electorate or by another decree.
