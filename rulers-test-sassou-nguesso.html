The Ruler's Test — Denis Sassou Nguesso, President of the Republic of Congo

Subject: Denis Sassou Nguesso (born 23 November 1943) — President of the Republic of Congo. PREMISE VERIFIED, November 2026: he still holds the executive seat, and the seat has not changed hands. He first held the presidency from 8 February 1979 to 31 August 1992, lost power in the democratic transition that elected Pascal Lissouba, and returned to power on 25 October 1997 after the 1997 civil war, in which his Cobra militia, supported by Angolan forces, defeated the elected government's forces (contemporaneous and reference record; CRCA timeline, 2026). He has held the seat continuously since 25 October 1997 — 29 years at grading — and is graded on that continuous holding, weighted to the recent terms (2016, 2021, and 2026), as instructed. THE MARCH 2026 ELECTION HAPPENED: presidential elections were held on 15 March 2026 (security forces voted on 12 March), with seven candidates validated by the Constitutional Court on 20 February 2026. Provisional results announced by Interior Minister Raymond Zéphirin Mboulou on 17 March 2026 gave Sassou Nguesso 94.82% (2,507,038 votes in the provisional count) on a reported turnout of 84.65% (Reuters, 17 March 2026; Congo Sentinel provisional tally, March 2026). The Constitutional Court confirmed the result on 28 March 2026 as 94.90% (2,509,456 votes), rejected an annulment appeal by candidate Uphrem Dave Mafoula, and he was sworn in on 16 April 2026 at Kintélé for a fifth consecutive term — a five-year term running to 2031 (Constitutional Court final results via World Bank country page, 2026; Diplomat.so court report, March 2026). Conflicting turnout figures are printed separately and never blended: the Interior Ministry's provisional turnout was 84.65% and the Constitutional Court table reproduced in reference sources gives 84.99%, while a separate report of the Court's public hearing stated turnout of 65.9% (Diplomat.so, March 2026) — the discrepancy is recorded here as unresolved in the sources assembled. Reuters witnesses on election day reported short or non-existent lines at many Brazzaville polling stations (Reuters, 15–17 March 2026). His closest challenger, Mavoungou Zinga Mabio of the Alliance for Democratic Alternation in 2026, received 1.48% in the provisional count and 1.40% (37,141 votes) in the final table — both figures are printed as sourced, not averaged. The 2015 constitution, as amended by referendum, permits three five-year terms; reporting on the current provisions states he is barred from standing again in 2031 (Diplomat.so, March 2026; Atlas factbook, 2026). Prime Minister Anatole Collinet Makosso, head of government since 12 May 2021, resigned with his government on 17 April 2026 and was reappointed by Sassou Nguesso on 23 April 2026 — the presidency, not the premiership, is the executive seat graded, because the president appoints and reappoints the prime minister, commands the state, and personally authorised the signature security operation of the recent term (presidential record, April 2026; International IDEA tracker, October 2025). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The Ethic: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and those who chose the public eye are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing — allegation alone never strips anonymity. The mandate rule applies: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one; carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The formal law, on its face, applies to the powerful as well as the weak, and foreign courts applying their own law have been allowed to proceed against his circle without Congo blocking them by force: France's Court of Cassation ruled in November 2010 that the "biens mal acquis" (ill-gotten gains) complaint by Transparency International France and SHERPA against Sassou Nguesso and relatives could proceed, and French seizures of family real estate (2018), luxury vehicles (2018), and bank-account sums (2019) are recorded in the World Bank / UNODC Stolen Asset Recovery (StAR) database, sourced to France, October 2024. The case exists and has run for over a decade — the record does not show him abolishing the foreign process, because he cannot.
  • Inside his own majority, criticism was at first voiced and the critic was eventually allowed to leave rather than disappeared: National Assembly member Aimé Hydevert Mouagni, aligned with the governing majority, was detained in March 2024 after criticising widespread official corruption and security failures; parliament lifted his immunity in April 2024 and he was charged — but in October 2024 he was allowed to leave the country for medical treatment in Turkey (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025). The release is on this shelf; the detention and charges are on the other.
  • The state has submitted, at least in form, to one external fiscal law for everyone in government: after the IMF found in 2017 that Congo's debt had been understated — the government had reported about 77% of GDP while the real figure was about 117%, with oil-trader loans repaid in future oil not fully disclosed (IMF findings as summarised in the documented record) — Congo entered an IMF programme (approved 2019) whose disclosure and governance conditions applied to the state's own oil finances, and the World Bank records non-oil revenue mobilisation and tighter customs and VAT enforcement rising in 2023–2024, with total revenues reaching 25.1% of GDP in 2024 (World Bank Economic Update, September 2025). A rule that binds the ruler's own treasury is the shape Test 1 looks for, even where the binding came from outside.

Evidence against:

  • The criminal law at home runs one way. The two best-known opposition figures in the country, General Jean-Marie Michel Mokoko and André Okombi Salissa — both candidates against him in 2016 — have been in prison for nearly a decade after convictions for undermining state security, convictions the Africa Center for Strategic Studies describes as "widely viewed as politically motivated," upheld by courts whose judges he appoints (Africa Center, 2026; Reuters, 13 March 2026). UN bodies have declared Mokoko's detention arbitrary; both men remained imprisoned through the March 2026 election (documented rights record, 2026). No comparable prosecution of a ruling-party (PCT) power-holder of similar rank appears in the domestic record assembled here.
  • The law was rewritten in 2025 to remove opponents from the field before the vote. In June 2025 Interior Minister Mboulou decreed that all political parties must have founding members from at least four regions and representation in at least six; the decree dissolved or eliminated many leading opposition parties, including Clément Mierassa's Parti Social-Démocrate Congolais (PSDC), Jean-Jacques Yhombi Opango's Rassemblement pour la Démocratie et le Développement (RDD), and Jean-Pierre Agnangoye's Parti du Peuple (PAPE) (Brill, Congo Vol. 22, 2025). A rule announced by the incumbent's own interior minister, in the run-up to his re-election, that dissolves the parties organising against him is a second law at the source, whatever its administrative wording.
  • The family shelf is long, documented, and entirely foreign-enforced. In France's ill-gotten-gains investigation: his nephew Wilfrid Nguesso was indicted in March 2017 for laundering embezzled public funds; his daughter Julienne Sassou Nguesso and her husband were indicted in June 2017; his nephew Edgar Nguesso, director of the Presidential Estate, and Edgar's mother were indicted a month later (Amnesty International, 2021, summarising the French file). These are indictments — ALLEGATION in law, not convictions — and they are printed as such. Global Witness investigations alleged that his son Denis-Christel Sassou Nguesso, a PCT member of the National Assembly and former deputy director-general of the state oil company SNPC, siphoned over USD 50 million from the treasury through a sham public-works contract structure, and that his daughter Claudia Sassou Nguesso, also a member of the National Assembly and presidential head of communications, received almost USD 20 million of apparently stolen state funds used to buy a luxury apartment in New York (Global Witness, 2019). Both are ALLEGATION — Denis-Christel Sassou Nguesso denies wrongdoing — and no domestic Congolese proceeding against either appears in the record. In March 2024 the US Justice Department moved to seize the New York apartment purchased in 2014 for the president's daughter, saying it was part of a scheme to launder embezzled Congolese public funds (US DOJ action via Freedom House, 2025) — again a US allegation in a forfeiture filing, not a verdict, and again foreign.
  • The outcome measure has not moved in a generation: Congo scored 23 of 100 on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025 (153rd in the trading-economics rendering of the index), against a 2003–2025 average of about 21 and an all-time high of 26 in 2012 (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released 2026). Twenty-nine years of continuous rule have left the perceived-corruption score essentially where it began.

Why this grade: In plain words: the law that imprisons his opponents for a decade does not touch his family, his party, or his treasury's insiders at home; every serious proceeding against his circle has had to be brought by a foreign court, and the one domestic fiscal discipline on record was imposed by the IMF after the state's own debt figures proved false. That is two laws — one for the house, one for everyone else — sustained over the whole graded tenure and renewed in 2025 by the party-dissolution decree.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the presidential family and the PCT insider network, who have held office, state-oil posts, and parliamentary seats without domestic legal consequence (named above, on documented indictments and investigations, all labeled ALLEGATION where unproven); foreign creditors and the IMF programme, which gained disclosure the domestic public had been denied. Hurt — opposition candidates and parties (Mokoko and Okombi Salissa imprisoned; PSDC, RDD, and PAPE dissolved by decree in 2025); Congolese taxpayers and service users, whose oil revenue is the alleged source of the foreign-held assets; and the deputy Aimé Hydevert Mouagni, detained and charged after criticising corruption from inside the majority itself.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The forms of independent institutions exist and sometimes speak. The Constitutional Court ran a formal validation process in 2026, validated seven candidates including six challengers, heard and ruled on candidate Mafoula's annulment appeal rather than ignoring it, and confirmed the result on 28 March 2026 (Constitutional Court record, March 2026). Domestic rights monitors — the Congolese Observatory for Human Rights (OCDH) and the Centre d'Action pour le Développement (CAD) — publish detailed, named, sharply critical reports inside Congo, including OCDH's 27 February 2026 report titled Human Rights in Grave Difficulty in Congo-Brazzaville: the illusion of democracy and the rule of law to perpetuate tyranny, and CAD's 9 December 2025 annual report cataloguing 4,182 alleged civil and political rights violations in 2025 (OCDH, February 2026; CAD, December 2025). That such reports can be presented at press conferences in Brazzaville at all is real, and it is recorded here.
  • He left office once when an election removed him: in 1992 he departed after Pascal Lissouba won the democratic transition election, and spent five years in opposition and exile rather than annulling the result (reference timeline, 2026). It is the strongest single item on this shelf — and it is 34 years old, predating the graded continuous tenure.
  • The regional central bank (BEAC) and CEMAC framework sit outside his personal appointment, and Congo's IMF programme reporting, World Bank economic updates, and debt figures are published by outside institutions whose numbers the government does not control — the World Bank's 2025–2026 updates openly record poverty "stubbornly high," debt at about 95% of GDP in 2026, and growth that has "not yet translated into a meaningful reduction in the poverty rate" (World Bank, 2025–2026).

Evidence against:

  • The judiciary is his appointment. The Africa Center for Strategic Studies states it plainly: "Sassou-Nguesso appoints all national judges in the country, and the courts, in turn, have been supportive of the President's interests, including upholding the convictions of leading opposition figures" Mokoko and Okombi Salissa (Africa Center, 2026). Freedom House's earlier assessment recorded the Constitutional Court's confirmation of the 2015 referendum as "viewed as a rubber stamp approval of Sassou Nguesso's efforts to remain in power" (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2018).
  • The constitution itself was changed to keep the seat. The October 2015 referendum — adopted, officially, by 94%, with the opposition and international observers questioning the credibility of the process and results (US State Department Human Rights Report, 2016) — replaced the 2002 constitution's two seven-year terms and age limit of 70, under which he could not have run again, with a new text that removed the age limit and reset the term count, and granted former presidents complete immunity (US State Department, 2016; Freedom House, 2018). A referee's rulebook rewritten by one player, mid-game, to extend that player's eligibility is the definition of gripping this Test grades.
  • Parliament is held. After the July 2017 legislative elections — boycotted by the opposition and marked by credible fraud reports — the PCT claimed 96 of 151 seats and its allies 12 more, and elections were indefinitely postponed in nine Pool districts because of the conflict there (Freedom House, 2018). By the 2026 cycle the Africa Center records the PCT and its allies controlling all but 29 seats in the 151-seat National Assembly (Africa Center, 2026).
  • The media and the vote itself were gripped in the most recent term decision. On election day, 15 March 2026, a nation-scale internet blackout took connectivity to about 3% of ordinary levels, confirmed by NetBlocks, whose director Alp Toker called it "a measure likely to limit transparency during today's election"; communications were partially restored only after provisional results were announced, and the authorities gave no explanation (NetBlocks via Reuters, 15 March 2026; Eu SEE / Hivos alert, March 2026). The same shutdown pattern had been used in 2016 and 2021. In January 2026 the director-general of presidential security made statements suggesting military force would be used against protesters during the elections (Eu SEE alert, March 2026). Freedom House scores Congo 15 of 100, "Not Free," in the 2025 index cycle (Freedom House via Africa Center summary, 2026).
  • Law enforcement itself was taken into the presidential household. In September 2025 he assigned "Operation Zero Kuluna," the campaign against urban gangs, not to the police, gendarmerie, or courts, but to the Direction Générale de la Sécurité Présidentielle (DGSP) — the unit whose job is guarding the president and his family — and on 24 October 2025 he confirmed he had personally authorised it and made it a permanent mechanism (International IDEA Global State of Democracy tracker, September–October 2025; OCDH, September 2026). OCDH alleged the DGSP lacked legal authority to conduct law-enforcement operations at all (IDEA tracker, September 2025) — an ALLEGATION about legality, printed as such; the assignment itself is documented fact.

Why this grade: In plain words: every referee that could remove him — the courts he appoints, the court that validates his elections, the parliament his party dominates, the parties register his interior minister can dissolve, the internet his authorities switch off on election day, and now the police power he handed to his own guard — answers, directly or structurally, to the seat. The surviving counterweights (monitors who may publish, foreign institutions who may count) are ones he cannot grip, not ones he has left alone.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the PCT and the presidential security apparatus, whose institutional reach grew with each cycle (a dissolved-opposition field in 2025, a permanent DGSP police role from 2025); the incumbent himself, re-elected in 2016, 2021, and 2026 under rules and referees aligned with the seat. Hurt — voters, whose election-day information was cut to 3% connectivity in 2026; opposition parties dissolved or boycotting; Pool voters, whose nine districts lost legislative elections indefinitely after 2017; and judges, journalists, and monitors who work inside institutions the record shows to be structurally subordinate.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, the ruler at least considered it openly. Considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Pool war was ended by negotiation, not only by force. After the April 2016 – 2017 military campaign in the Pool region (below), the government signed a ceasefire agreement with Pastor Ntumi's Ninja/Nsiloulou forces in late December 2017, and a subsequent peace arrangement has largely held — the most recent acute phase of the Pool insurgency is dated 2016–2019 in the reference record, with no return to full war since (Freedom House, 2018; CRCA timeline, 2026). When a negotiated door opened, he eventually took it.
  • The debt wobble was answered with disclosure and a programme, eventually. When oil prices collapsed and the IMF established that reported debt (about 77% of GDP) was really about 117% (IMF, 2017), the government entered the IMF programme rather than defaulting in silence, and by 2024–2025 the World Bank records debt falling from 103.6% of GDP in 2020 to 93.6% in 2024, non-oil revenue rising, and per-capita income rising in 2024 for the first time since 2015/2016 (World Bank Economic Update, September 2025). The no-good-choice mechanics of that fiscal trap are graded separately below; the adjustment itself, once made, was real.
  • The 1992 precedent stands on this shelf too: pushed back by voters at the ballot box in the transition election, he left. It predates the graded tenure, and the shelf records it as precedent, not as recent practice.

Evidence against:

  • The defining wobble of the tenure — the voters' refusal, in 2015, to extend his eligibility — was answered by changing the rule and then by force. The 2015 referendum passed amid opposition protests; in March 2016 he was proclaimed winner with 60% on almost 69% turnout (Constitutional Court proclamation via US State Department, 2016), and on 4 April 2016 gunfire and explosions in southern Brazzaville killed 17 people by the government's count (three police officers, two civilians, 12 attackers) and displaced more than 17,000 people; the government blamed the Ninja/Nsiloulou, whose leader Pastor Ntumi denied responsibility, and many observers suggested the operation was coordinated to instill fear — a contested attribution, printed with its disputants (US State Department Human Rights Report, 2016). The government then launched security operations across the Pool; by 2017 the campaign had displaced at least 80,000 citizens (Freedom House, 2018). Pushback at the ballot box was answered with a military campaign in the region most associated with the opposition.
  • The 2026 wobble — a boycott by major opposition parties, empty Brazzaville polling stations witnessed by Reuters, and monitors' warnings — was answered with the blackout and the count, not with any adjustment: no postponement, no dialogue, no explanation of the shutdown was offered by the authorities (Reuters, March 2026; Eu SEE alert, March 2026). Prime Minister Makosso rejected accusations of repression and bias, telling Reuters "no election is won in advance" and pointing to oversight bodies (Reuters, 13 March 2026) — denial in the government's own words, recorded as its stated position.
  • The other-option rule, applied to the clearest recent case: if another option becomes available, the Test asks whether he at least considered it openly. In 2025–2026 that option arrived in writing, twice. OCDH and CAD both proposed the same one: judicialise the anti-gang campaign — bring detainees before courts, return law enforcement to the police and gendarmerie, investigate deaths in custody (OCDH, February and September 2026; CAD, December 2025). The documented response was the opposite of consideration: on 24 October 2025 he confirmed the DGSP operation, declared it a permanent mechanism, and extended it to more cities and more security branches (IDEA tracker, October 2025). No stated reasons declining the judicial option appear in the record assembled here. Refusing to look at it at all is what Test 3 grades against.
  • The same refusal pattern met the opposition's 2025 pushback on the electoral roll: after the interior minister announced a two-month revision of the rolls in September 2025, long-standing opposition leaders including Mathias Dzon and Clément Mierassa called for a halt to the process, warning of "a new electoral hold-up, like in 2016 and 2021" (Brill, Congo Vol. 22, 2025). The process continued unchanged; the parties that had organised a single-candidate coalition were, by then, largely dissolved under the June 2025 decree (Test 1).

The no-good-choice clause, applied where it genuinely fits — the debt trap: The fiscal crisis of 2015–2019 is the one file in this dossier where the clause's mechanics genuinely apply. The options that actually existed once oil prices collapsed were: (1) disclose the full debt and accept an IMF programme with austerity and governance conditions — real harm to public services and to the patronage economy; (2) continue concealment and risk disorderly default — real harm to salaries, imports, and the currency bloc; (3) deeper oil-backed borrowing from traders — the instrument that had built the trap, at worse terms. He chose option (1), the least-harm door actually open, and the World Bank's 2024–2025 figures (debt ratio falling, revenue rising) record that it worked as fiscal triage. That choice is graded as the best available in the moment and is not held against him. Graded separately, as the clause requires: he built the trap — the undisclosed oil-collateralised debt accumulated under his presidency, and the IMF's 77%-versus-117% finding is a finding about his government's own reporting; and honesty inside the trap was partial — disclosure came to the IMF under programme pressure, years after the borrowing, not to Congolese voters when the loans were taken.

Why this grade: In plain words: across the graded tenure, every pushback that touched his hold on the seat — the 2015 protests, the 2016 result, the Pool, the 2025 roll dispute, the 2026 boycott — was answered with rule changes, force, blackout, or denial, and the one documented alternative offered to him in the recent term (judicialise Zero Kuluna) was not considered but entrenched against. The genuine adjustments on the FOR shelf (the 2017 ceasefire, the IMF triage) came after force and concealment had already run their course.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — residents of the Pool who got a ceasefire in December 2017 after displacement, and a peace that has largely held since; the state's creditors and salary earners, served by the IMF triage over disorderly default. Hurt — the Pool's displaced: more than 17,000 from Brazzaville's southern neighbourhoods in April 2016 by the government's own account of that episode, and at least 80,000 displaced across the Pool campaign by 2017 (separate sourced figures for separate counts, not a total); the 17 killed in Brazzaville on 4 April 2016 by the government count; voters in 2016, 2021, and 2026 who voted, protested, or boycotted under shutdown and threat; and urban youth in 2025–2026, for whom the offered judicial option was refused (Test 4 ledger).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the southern and Pool communities, the indigenous peoples, the urban poor and youth, the protesters, and the regions that did not elect him — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The peace, where it was made, counted the Pool back in: the December 2017 ceasefire and the arrangements that followed ended the displacement emergency, and the reference record dates the last acute Pool insurgency to 2016–2019, with structural tensions remaining but no renewed war (CRCA timeline, 2026). A region that had been a war zone under his campaign has been at peace for most of the last decade — that peace is a real good delivered to real people, and it is on this shelf.
  • National programmes and the growth record are not nothing. The World Bank records real GDP growth of 2.6% in 2024 and 3.1% in 2025, per-capita income rising in 2024 for the first time since 2015/2016, agriculture expanding under local-content policies, manufacturing exports rising, and state social projects including a social-protection and youth productive-inclusion project and the USD 132 million ProClimat livelihoods project (World Bank, 2025–2026). His 16 April 2026 inauguration pledge was framed universally — he vowed to govern as "the president of all Congolese" and not to "betray voters' trust" (inaugural reporting, April 2026). The pledge is recorded here; Tests grade the accounting, not the sentence.
  • Infrastructure built under the tenure — the roads, the Kintélé complex, the corridors named in his 2026 address, including corridor 13 toward Ouesso, Impfondo, and the Central African Republic border, and the planned Brazzaville–Kinshasa road-rail bridge — is national in stated scope, reaching northern and river regions, not only the capital (inaugural address summary, April 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Pool was counted out by force within the graded tenure: at least 80,000 citizens displaced by the 2016–2017 campaign (Freedom House, 2018), nine Pool districts denied legislative elections indefinitely after 2017 (Freedom House, 2018), and a region whose political identity — Lari, southern, associated with the opposition and with Ntumi's Ninjas — was treated as a security problem rather than a constituency. The ceasefire repaired the war; it did not restore the missing elections on any date in the record assembled here.
  • Urban poor youth are the most recent counted-out group, on named monitors' counts — printed separately, never blended. CAD documented at least 200 extrajudicial killings under Operation Zero Kuluna in a June 2026 report covering September 2025 to April 2026 (CAD via African Quarters, 2026). International IDEA's tracker recorded at least 10 people extrajudicially killed in late September 2025 alone, with CAD stating those killings were carried out without trial or prior investigation and the true toll was likely higher (IDEA, September 2025). OCDH, in September 2026, counted 561 young people disappeared since the operation began a year earlier, gathered from family testimony across Brazzaville, Pointe-Noire, Dolisie, and Nkayi, describing night-time arrests, some of school-enrolled youth, without clearly established judicial procedure (OCDH via Congo Sentinel and RFI, September 2026). CAD's separate annual count put alleged civil and political rights violations at 4,182 in 2025, up 131% from 1,814 in 2024, with arbitrary detentions up 266% and torture and ill-treatment up 205% on its methodology (CAD, December 2025). The government has not, in the record assembled here, published its own count of the operation's dead or detained, and neither parliament, the judiciary, nor the National Human Rights Commission formally condemned the alleged excesses (CAD, 2025). The gang violence the operation answered was real — the monitors themselves do not dispute that insecurity required a response (OCDH, September 2026) — what is counted out is the due process of the young men taken.
  • Opposition supporters were counted out before they could be counted at all in 2026: the declared candidate Lassy Mbouity, president of Les Socialistes Congolais, was abducted from his home by armed masked men on 11 May 2025 and found nine to ten days later in critical condition showing signs of torture; his party accused the regime of orchestrating it, the government denied involvement and had not publicly responded as of 22 May 2025, some observers suspected the account was fabricated as an asylum basis, and he subsequently withdrew his candidacy and relocated to France, having filed at least five complaints to the High Court "to no avail" (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025; IDEA tracker, May 2025; Africa Center, 2026; Brill, 2025 — the abduction is documented, its authorship is contested and is printed as ALLEGATION against the government, denied by the government). A candidate who exits the race after an abduction, whoever committed it, is a voter-choice removed without investigation resolving the file.
  • The oil accounting does not reach the population. Oil is about half of GDP and 80% of exports (World Bank, 2026), yet the Bank records poverty "elevated" and not meaningfully reduced by the 2024 recovery, youth unemployment around 42%, electricity access at 67% urban against 12.4% rural, clean-water access at 74% overall against 46% rural, a human capital index of 0.42 against a 0.48 lower-middle-income peer average, and only 30% of children proficient in reading by the end of primary school (World Bank country page and Economic Update, 2025–2026). CAD's 2025 report also recorded the reported deportation and forced labour of approximately fifty indigenous inhabitants from the Enyellé district to Imese, across the border in the Democratic Republic of Congo (CAD via Congo Sentinel, 2025) — an ALLEGATION in a monitor's report, printed as such, concerning the community with the least power in the country to answer back. Amnesty's earlier file adds the health ledger: governance failures, corruption, and concealed debt contributed to a health-rights crisis for Congo's five million citizens (Amnesty International, 2021).

Why this grade: In plain words: a president of all Congolese does not leave one region's elections indefinitely postponed, another generation of urban youth subject to a presidential-guard sweep whose own monitors count the disappeared in the hundreds, and an oil state's rural majority at 12.4% electricity after 29 years. The FOR shelf — peace in the Pool since 2017, growth returning, universal language — is real but it is the exception pattern; the accounting, group by group, counts out the Pool, the opposition, the indigenous, and the poor young.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Pool residents since the 2017 ceasefire, who live without war; urban residents frightened by gang violence, whom the state visibly answered (the monitors concede the insecurity was real); beneficiaries of the social-protection, youth-inclusion, and ProClimat projects on the World Bank's record; northern and corridor regions named for infrastructure investment. Hurt — the Pool's displaced (at least 80,000, Freedom House count) and its nine districts' voters; the families of the 561 disappeared youth on OCDH's count and of the at-least-200 killed on CAD's count (separate counts, separate shelves, both monitors', neither confirmed by any government count); indigenous inhabitants of Enyellé on CAD's allegation; rural Congolese at 12.4% electricity and 46% clean water; and the roughly 42% of youth unemployed on the World Bank's figure.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 1997, weighted to the 2016, 2021, and 2026 terms, with dates. The two axes — word-keeping and helped/hurt — are graded independently; all four crossings are named.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Stability — the core promise of every campaign since 2002, substantially kept. He has run, each cycle, as the guarantor of peace and continuity after the 1997–2003 wars, and the record since the December 2017 Pool ceasefire is of a country without a renewed civil war, holding regular (if gripped) elections, growing at 2.6% in 2024 and 3.1% in 2025 (World Bank, 2025). Under the mandate rule, carrying out the stability platform voters were offered is FOR-shelf evidence here, whatever Tests 1–4 grade about how it was held.
  • The 2026 programme — stated, dated, and now on the record to be graded. His campaign, "Let's Accelerate the March Toward Development," and his 16 April 2026 inaugural address set ten priority actions: mobilise more public financial resources; invest in human capital; strengthen discipline and governance and combat "deviant behavior" by public officials; revitalise the economy; make agriculture and industry priority sectors; create more jobs; deploy basic and developmental infrastructure; promote research and innovation; deepen social rights; preserve a healthy environment (presidential biography and inaugural summaries, April 2026). Seven months into the term these are direction, not record; they are printed here so the file can grade them later, and no credit beyond "stated" is taken for them now.
  • The fiscal word, once given to the IMF, was substantially performed: debt-to-GDP fell from 103.6% (2020) to 93.6% (2024), non-oil revenue rose, and the programme's disclosure conditions were met sufficiently for the World Bank to record improving revenue mobilisation and debt management in 2024–2025 (World Bank Economic Update, September 2025). A promise kept to a creditor is still a promise kept, and it is on this shelf — with its origin graded in Test 3.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Diversification — promised every cycle, unkept across all of them. Successive programmes since the 2000s have pledged to free Congo from oil dependence; in 2026 the World Bank still records oil at about half of GDP and 80% of exports, growth "once again being driven primarily by the oil sector," and diversification whose employment effect is weak — non-oil firm revenues up 11% but jobs up only 3% (World Bank, 2025–2026). His own 2026 address re-promised agriculture, industry, and jobs — the same words, a new term. A pledge repeated for two decades while its measure does not move is a broken word on an instalment plan.

In 2015, facing ineligibility, he sponsored the referendum that removed both, amid protests and a credibility dispute recorded by the US State Department (2016). Whether framed as a new constitution or a broken settlement, the effect graded here is plain: the limit that applied to him was changed by him, for him, and the 2016, 2021, and 2026 terms all stand on that change. The current text now bars a further term after 2031 (Diplomat.so, 2026) — a limit whose credibility the 2015 precedent itself undermines, recorded as a risk, not a prediction.

Not yet a completed term's broken promise; already contradicted in its first year by the machinery that produced the term.

The two axes, named independently (word × outcome): 1. Kept their word AND it helped people: the stability pledge — kept since the 2017 ceasefire in the narrow sense that no civil war has resumed, and the Pool's residents are the helped. The keeping earns its Test 5 entry here; it does not launder the hurt, which stands. 3. Went against their word AND it helped people: not documented in the record assembled here. No clear case was found of a pledge of his being broken in a way that benefited the public; the dossier records the crossing as absent rather than inventing one. 4. Broke their word AND it hurt people: the diversification and anti-corruption pledges — broken across successive terms (oil still ~50% of GDP and 80% of exports; CPI 23), with the hurt falling on the unemployed young (about 42% youth unemployment), the rural unconnected (12.4% electricity), and every service user of an oil state whose revenues the monitors allege leak abroad (World Bank, 2025–2026; Global Witness ALLEGATION, 2019).

Why this grade: In plain words: the word he has kept most reliably is the word about himself — that he would remain, and that order would remain with him. The words owed to everyone else — diversify the economy, clean the government, rotate the seat, include all Congolese — have been re-issued each cycle and met in none, and the 2026 term opened by re-promising the same list under a new title. Twenty-nine years is long enough that "not yet" is a verdict.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — those whose first need was the absence of war: the general population since 2017, and the state's creditors, whose programme was performed. Hurt — the young, promised jobs in cycle after cycle into 42% unemployment; savers and service users of the hidden-debt years; voters promised a rotating seat in 2002 who have now voted in four elections (2016, 2021, 2026, and the 2015 referendum) structured by its removal; and the families in crossing 2, hurt by a word that was kept.
