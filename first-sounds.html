First Sounds

Soft music you make together — you, and your baby in your arms. Tap a pad and a gentle note blooms into a little melody. No beeps, no flashing frenzy, no claims it will make anyone smarter. Just sound, faces, and time together.

Built for the youngest listeners: slow tempos, soft music-box and marimba voices, a volume cap that cannot be turned up loud, and drifting high-contrast shapes for young eyes — there to glance at, never to stare at instead of you.

🔊 First, keep it quiet — babies' ears are delicate

This player starts low and is capped low on purpose. Even at its highest setting it stays well under ordinary phone volume — and you should still keep it softer than that.

About conversation level If you can talk over it in a normal voice without raising yours, it's about right. If you have to raise your voice, it's too loud — turn it down.
Never headphones on a baby No headphones, no earbuds, and no phone speaker pressed near a baby's ear. Play it out loud, across the room or in your hand at arm's length.
Short and sweet A few minutes of music-making together is plenty. Watch your baby: turning away, fussing, or going quiet-and-still can mean “enough for now.”

Slow shapes for young eyes — they breathe with the music

Why black and white? Newborns see strong contrast long before they see colour clearly, so the shapes start bold and simple. They move slowly on purpose — fast motion is for older eyes.

What the science actually says

Graded plainly, at full size — what is documented, and what is not. This page would rather lose a good headline than print a claim that doesn't hold.

Supported · documented

What research does support

  • Babies track sound patterns from birth. Newborns respond to rhythm, melody contour, and changes in pitch — hearing is one of the senses working earliest, and babies notice when a pattern changes. Developmental hearing research, replicated across many labs.
  • Babies prefer infant-directed singing. When adults sing slowly, higher, and with exaggerated warmth — the way people naturally sing to babies — babies attend longer and are soothed more than by ordinary adult singing. Your singing counts more than any speaker. Infant music-perception studies comparing infant-directed vs. adult-directed song.
  • First-year sounds shape the map for language. Across the first year, babies tune their hearing to the sounds of the language(s) around them — the sound environment literally helps organise how speech will be heard. Rich, varied, human sound — talking, singing, everyday music — is the real input. Speech-perception research on perceptual narrowing in the first year.
  • Shared music is shared attention. Making sounds together — tapping, bouncing, singing back and forth — is a form of early turn-taking and bonding. That togetherness is the benefit with the best support, and it's why this page is built for two, not for a baby alone with a screen.
Not supported · named plainly

What research does not support

  • The “Mozart effect” does not stand up. The famous 1990s claim that listening to Mozart raises intelligence came from a small, short-lived effect in adults on one paper-folding task. Larger follow-ups and reviews did not find a lasting IQ gain — in babies or anyone else. It is named here because you will meet it in marketing for baby products. It should not sell you anything.
  • No page makes a baby smarter. Not this one. There is no evidence that an app, a playlist, or a particular composer boosts a baby's IQ or guarantees faster development — and any product that promises that is promising past its evidence.
  • “More stimulation” is not “better.” Flooding a baby with sound and images is not a documented shortcut. Babies learn from responsive people — faces that answer them, voices that pause for them — far more than from volume of input.
  • Background screens are not a teacher. For under-2s, health guidance consistently favours sound, faces, and hands-on play over screen time. See “Together, not a babysitter” below.

So why this page at all? Because music together is enjoyable, soothing, and a lovely way to spend attention on each other — reasons that need no inflated claim to be worth your time.

Together, not a babysitter

Sound and faces over screens

Guidance for children under 2 consistently points the same way: keep screen time minimal, and put the sound, your face, and your hands first. Use this page as a sound toy you hold — phone in your hand or playing across the room, baby looking at you, the shapes an occasional glance rather than a show to watch.

Ways to play together

  • Echo: tap a pad, then sing its note back. Pause. Babies love the pause — it's where their turn lives.
  • Bounce gently in time with a lullaby — rhythm felt in the body is music too.
  • Name what you hear as you go — “high… low… soft…” — ordinary talk around sound is language food.
  • Stop when they say so, in their way: looking away or fussing is a complete sentence.
