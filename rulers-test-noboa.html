The Ruler's Test — Daniel Noboa, President of Ecuador

Subject: Daniel Noboa Azín — President of Ecuador since 23 November 2023, when he won the snap election called after President Guillermo Lasso dissolved the National Assembly and completed the remaining 17 months of Lasso's term; re-elected on 13 April 2025 with 55.63% of the vote for a full four-year term running to May 2029, and in office at the time of grading. He is the heir of the Noboa business group built by his father Álvaro Noboa — among Ecuador's largest fortunes, centered on Exportadora Bananera Noboa — and the family group's long-running tax dispute with the state is part of his file (Test 1). His elected vice president for 2023–2025 was Verónica Abad, with whom he fell into open conflict (Tests 1 and 2); since May 2025 his vice president has been María José Pinto. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The gravest crime committed by his own security forces was prosecuted to conviction. After a military patrol detained four Afro-Ecuadorian boys aged 11 to 15 in the Las Malvinas neighborhood of Guayaquil on 8 December 2024 — the boys' burned remains were found near the Taura Air Force base on Christmas Eve — prosecutors charged 17 soldiers, and on 22 December 2025 a court sentenced eleven of them to 34 years and eight months for enforced disappearance, gave five cooperating soldiers 30-month sentences, and acquitted one officer; the sentence ordered a public apology and reparations, and in February 2026 the Constitutional Court required the Air Force commander to apologize publicly to the families (court ruling, 22 December 2025; Amnesty International and UN Working Group on Enforced Disappearances statements, December 2025–February 2026). The prosecution proceeded under his government, against his own armed forces, in the middle of the security campaign those forces were waging for him.
  • He submitted his program to voters and accepted the answers, including the answers he did not want (see Test 3): in April 2024 voters rejected two of his eleven referendum questions, and in November 2025 they rejected all four — the rejections stood.
  • The extradition machinery he created was used first against the country's most powerful gang leader, not against a political figure: José Adolfo Macías, "Fito," leader of Los Choneros, was recaptured on 25 June 2025 and extradited to the United States on 20 July 2025 — the first Ecuadorian extradited from Ecuador — after accepting extradition before a judge (Reuters and AP, June–July 2025).

Evidence against:

  • His family's company was the emblematic beneficiary of a tax remission written into a law his own government promoted. Exportadora Bananera Noboa's debt to the SRI tax authority — arising from a transfer-pricing dispute dating to 2005 and long among the largest tax debts in the country — stood at roughly US$94.6 million. Under the tax-remission mechanism of the Organic Law of Public Integrity, passed by his government's Assembly majority in June 2025, the company paid approximately US$25.7 million of principal and had 100% of interest, fines, and surcharges forgiven; by early October 2025 the debt stood at zero. In September 2025 the Constitutional Court declared the law unconstitutional, but without retroactive effect, so the remission stood (Bloomberg Línea's reconstruction of the debt's path to zero, 2025; GK and Expreso reporting on the conflict-of-interest question). The remission was a general measure open to all taxpayers, and the company's position — that it paid its capital like any other taxpayer after favorable court rulings reduced the assessed amount — is on the record. What is also on the record is the sequence: the country's most famous tax debtor was the president's family firm, the forgiveness was enacted under his government, and the law that carried it was later held unconstitutional.
  • He exempted himself from the campaign law that binds every other official. Ecuadorian electoral law requires an incumbent seeking re-election to take leave of absence so the state is not used as a campaign instrument. Noboa refused the leave for the 2025 elections, instead declaring himself in "temporary absence" for brief campaign periods and designating interim vice presidents by decree; the EU Election Observation Mission recorded that he "repeatedly switched between his roles of president and candidate," that state media showed a pro-incumbent bias, and that no institution would clearly state a position on the failure (EU EOM Final Report, Ecuador 2025). The law's burden fell on everyone else; its beneficiary wrote himself an exception.
  • The one law reached his elected vice president through a ministry. When Verónica Abad — elected on his own ticket — became an obstacle to the leave-of-absence problem (she, not a designee, was constitutionally next in line), the Labor Ministry suspended her from office for 150 days on 8 November 2024 for "unjustified abandonment of post," based on her late arrival at a reassignment to Turkey that his own government had ordered. A judge declared the suspension unconstitutional on 23 December 2024 and ordered a public apology; the executive nonetheless maintained the sidelining, and UN independent experts reported that the military blocked her access to the Vice Presidency and described "the misuse of administrative sanctions and the judicial persecution of her family" — her eldest son was detained for 46 days in a corruption case (an ALLEGATION he contests) and her lawyer was arrested in January 2025 (OHCHR, UN experts' statement, 18 February 2025; Reuters, December 2024). On 27 February 2025 an electoral-court judge suspended Abad's political rights for two years in a gender-political-violence case brought by the government's own foreign minister (Tribunal Contencioso Electoral ruling, February 2025).
  • Prosecution has run downhill toward his opponents. Aquiles Álvarez, the Revolución Ciudadana mayor of Guayaquil and one of Noboa's most prominent critics, was investigated from 2024 in the "Triple A" fuel case, arrested in a dawn raid on 10 February 2026 in a second case ("Goleada" — organized crime, money laundering, and tax fraud, all ALLEGATIONs he denies), held in preventive detention, and on 29 August 2026 convicted in the Triple A case and sentenced to six years and eight months, with some US$61.5 million in joint restitution ordered against nine convicted persons and six companies (Attorney General's Office bulletins; AP, February 2026; Fiscalía-reported verdict, August 2026). The conviction is a court's documented outcome and is recorded as such; Álvarez and his party maintain the cases are political persecution designed to remove him, and that position is recorded beside it. During the September–October 2025 Indigenous strike, the Attorney General's Office opened an "unjustified private enrichment" investigation into more than 50 people including the president of CONAIE, the strike's leading organization (Human Rights Watch, October 2025) — financial investigation opened against protest leadership mid-protest.
  • He made a fraud claim of his own, without evidence, when a result displeased him. After the first round of the 2025 election finished in a virtual tie, Noboa said on 11 February 2025 that he had evidence of fraud confirmed by an OAS report; OAS officials publicly clarified that no abnormalities had been detected, and the EU mission recorded that he "did not present evidence or lodge objections with the electoral authorities" (EU EOM Final Report, 2025). The same mission found no evidence supporting his opponent's later fraud claims either — the standard this test applies is that the claim-maker with state power behind him is held to it first.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, the prosecution, and media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Constitutional Court has ruled against him repeatedly, in public, and its rulings have taken effect. It provisionally suspended 16 articles of his three flagship June 2025 laws (National Solidarity, Intelligence, Public Integrity) on 4 August 2025; struck down the National Solidarity Law outright on 26 September 2025; suspended his decree attempting to bypass its review of the constituent-assembly question (September 2025); and in February 2026 ordered the Air Force's public apology in the Las Malvinas case (Constitutional Court rulings, 2025–2026; Human Rights Watch, July 2026). He has not dissolved, packed, or stripped the Court of its funding, and the channel he ultimately used for his constitution project was the constitutional one — a referendum, which he lost.
  • The electoral authority ran elections that international observers — invited by the state — called transparent and well organized, and those observers' reports criticizing the incumbent were published freely (EU EOM and OAS reports, 2024–2025). Independent and opposition media continue to operate and to publish investigations of his family and government, including the tax-remission reporting cited in Test 1.

Evidence against:

  • When the Constitutional Court suspended his security laws, the president of the republic personally led a street march against it. On 12 August 2025 Noboa marched on the Court wearing a bulletproof vest, along a route lined with billboards showing the nine justices' names and photographs under the text "These are the judges who are stealing our peace"; the Court reported that its security perimeter fence had been removed and military personnel displayed outside its premises, and condemned "a stigmatization that increases the security risk" of its justices and "directly affects the independence of the body" (Reuters, 12 August 2025; Constitutional Court statement). Before the march, his government minister had called the Court an "enemy of the citizenry" in national broadcasts, and the president of the National Assembly accused it of siding with "enemies of the people"; the UN Special Rapporteur on the independence of judges warned that such labeling by high officials places judicial independence at risk (OHCHR, 15 August 2025). The same day as the march, Noboa announced a referendum proposal to allow impeachment of Constitutional Court judges.
  • He attempted to route around the Court's review power by decree. On 19 September 2025, after the Court had rejected seven of his proposed referendum questions, he issued Decree 148 calling the constituent-assembly consultation without the prior constitutional review the law requires; the Court provisionally suspended it the same day, and he repealed it — then issued Decree 153 the next day with the same call, leaving the electoral council to forward it to the Court itself (Decrees 148 and 153, September 2025; Foro por la Democracia challenge; CNE action, 20 September 2025). The question eventually went through review and to the voters — after the bypass had been tried and blocked.
  • The retaliation is documented as a pattern, not an episode. A September 2026 report by NYU School of Law's Rule of Law Lab, Executive Retaliation Against Ecuador's Constitutional Court, documents the executive (i) publicly delegitimizing the Court, (ii) intimidating and publicly exposing its justices, (iii) weaponizing criminal proceedings against justices who rule against the government, (iv) curbing the Court's institutional powers, and (v) imposing administrative restrictions on its operations — concluding that the Court "has emerged as the country's principal check on the abuse of executive power" and that the campaign against it threatens that check (NYU Rule of Law Lab, 22 September 2026).
  • Government by emergency decree is the standing condition, not the exception. Between January 2024 and June 2026 Noboa issued 28 executive decrees declaring, renewing, or modifying states of emergency; on 18 June 2026 he decreed an "internal armed conflict" anew and authorized the armed forces and police to "neutralize" threatening structures (Human Rights Watch, July 2026). The Constitutional Court found in multiple rulings that the government had not provided sufficient information to justify that the constitutional threshold for such decrees was met (HRW, citing the Court's rulings).
  • The vice-presidential maneuvers belong here too: an elected vice president suspended by a labor ministry, interim replacements named by decree, and — per UN experts — the military physically blocking the elected officeholder's access to her office (OHCHR, 18 February 2025). Whatever the personal conflict, the institutional fact is that the executive rearranged a constitutionally elected office by administrative means when the officeholder became inconvenient.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Twice, voters said no to him at scale and he took the answer. On 21 April 2024 his referendum won nine of eleven questions; the two rejections — international arbitration (65% no) and hourly labor contracts (70% no) — were accepted without challenge. On 16 November 2025 voters rejected all four of his questions: foreign military bases (about 61% no), a constituent assembly to rewrite the 2008 constitution (about 62% no), cutting public financing for parties, and shrinking the National Assembly from 151 to 73 seats (about 53% no) — a sweeping defeat delivered a year into his full term, against polls and against his own heavy campaign spending. Noboa acknowledged the result on social media, pledged to respect the will of the Ecuadorian people, and announced cabinet changes (CNE results; presidential statements, 16 November 2025).
  • Blocked by the Court on Decree 148, he repealed it within a day rather than defying the suspension order, and the constituent question proceeded through the review channel (September 2025).
  • Under pressure during the 2025 Indigenous strike, his government did eventually sit at dialogue tables with Imbabura Indigenous organizations from 15 October 2025 (strike coverage, October 2025) — late, and without conceding the core demand, but the channel was opened.

Evidence against:

  • His characteristic first answer to a judicial check is pressure, not adjustment: the march on the Court, the justices' faces on billboards, the "enemy of the citizenry" label, and a proposed impeachment mechanism for the judges — all in response to a provisional suspension pending constitutional review (August 2025, Test 2).
  • When the Constitutional Court limited his September 2025 emergency decree during the Indigenous strike to two provinces, he answered on 4 October 2025 with a new decree extending the emergency to ten additional provinces (Human Rights Watch, October 2025) — the ruling was not disobeyed; it was routed around.
  • On the strike's substance he did not adjust at all. The diesel-subsidy repeal that triggered the September–October 2025 CONAIE strike was never conceded; he labeled the protesters terrorists and claimed the Venezuelan gang Tren de Aragua was behind the demonstrations, and after 31 days CONAIE ended the strike on 22 October 2025 "without achieving its objectives" (strike records; CIVICUS and HRW reporting, October 2025). In previous decades the same movement's strikes forced presidents to retreat on the same policy; his answer was to hold the policy and outlast the street.
  • The November 2025 referendum defeat was accepted formally and answered substantively by escalation: within months he had re-decreed the "internal armed conflict" (18 June 2026) and deepened joint military operations with the United States (from March 2026), while analysts noted he spent much of the post-referendum period abroad (HRW, July 2026; Ojalá/CIEES reporting, January 2026). The voters' no closed the constitutional door he had asked for; it did not move the strategy the no was widely read as repudiating.
  • His first response to an unfavorable electoral signal — the tied first round of February 2025 — was an evidence-free fraud claim (Test 1), withdrawn only in the sense that it was never substantiated or pursued.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Indigenous movements, prisoners, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • On the broadest material measure, the poor were counted in during his second year. Income poverty fell from 28.0% in December 2024 to 21.4% in December 2025 — roughly 1.2 million people — and extreme poverty from 13.0% to 8.0%, driven mainly by labor income, with the World Bank noting gains concentrated in lower-income households (World Bank Poverty & Equity Brief, October 2026, on INEC data). Multidimensional poverty remained high at 41.7%, and the Bank warned in 2026 that real earnings were contracting again — both facts are on the record beside the headline.
  • The diesel-subsidy removal carried a stated compensation design: of the roughly US$1.1 billion in projected annual savings, US$300 million was designated for bus companies, freight operators, and poor families affected by the price rise (government statements, September 2025).
  • His opponents' voters were counted at the ballot box even when they voted against him: the 2024 and 2025 referendum results against his proposals were certified and honored, and the 2025 presidential count that his opponent disputed was verified by EU and OAS observers as reflecting the vote (EU EOM Final Report, 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Prisoners are counted out, in body counts. The prison system has been under military control since January 2024 as part of the "internal armed conflict," and the massacres continued inside it: 14 dead at Machala prison on 22 September 2025 (13 inmates and a guard), 17 dead in Esmeraldas days later, 31 dead at Machala on 9 November 2025 (27 by asphyxiation/hanging, per the SNAI prison agency), and 13 more at Machala on 7 December 2025 (SNAI statements; Reuters and CNN, September–December 2025). More than 600 inmates have been killed in Ecuador's prisons since 2020 (IACHR), and at the Litoral prison alone, the Guayaquil Human Rights Committee documented at least 600 prisoner deaths from health and nutrition causes in 2025; the IACHR issued precautionary measures over the prison health crisis (CDH Guayaquil; IACHR, November–December 2025). His answer — a new maximum-security prison, El Encuentro, opened in November 2025 to isolate gang leaders, with Jorge Glas among the first 300 transferred — addresses control; the dying in the existing prisons is the count that did not change.
  • The disappeared are counted out. Amnesty International documented enforced disappearances by the armed forces in at least five 2024 operations, ten people including six children; by March 2026 the UN Committee on Enforced Disappearances had received 51 reports of alleged enforced disappearance by law-enforcement agencies during 2024–2025 operations, and the IACHR issued precautionary measures on 30 December 2025 for 26 disappeared individuals (Amnesty International, 2025; HRW, July 2026; IACHR via CEPR, 2026). The Las Malvinas convictions (Test 1) show the system can answer; the accumulating case files show the practice the convictions answered was not singular.
  • Indigenous Ecuadorians were answered as a security problem. The September–October 2025 strike against the diesel decree was met with states of emergency, mass deployment (some 12,000 soldiers in over 2,000 operations), and — on the Alliance for Human Rights' count — 391 reported violations, 473 injured, and 206 detained; three protesters died: Efraín Fuerez, shot during a military convoy operation in Cotacachi on 28 September 2025 (the Attorney General's Office opened an investigation into illegitimate use of force); José Guamán, shot in Otavalo on 15 October; and Rosa Paqui, who died after tear-gas exposure in Loja on 14 October (HRW video-verified reporting; Alliance for Human Rights; AFP, September–October 2025). The president publicly associated the movement with terrorism and a foreign gang (Test 3), and its president was placed under financial investigation mid-strike (Test 1).
  • The flock as a whole is less safe than when he took office. The homicide rate he inherited at its then-record level (about 46–47 per 100,000 in 2023) fell in 2024 (about 38.8) and then rose past it: 2025 closed with 9,216 intentional homicides, a rate of about 51 per 100,000 — the deadliest year in Ecuador's recorded history and the highest rate in Latin America (Ministry of the Interior figures via InSight Crime; CEPR, 2026). The government points to early-2026 improvements — March 2026 homicides down 28% year-on-year per the Interior Ministry, and declines in border zones it attributes to joint operations with the United States — and those claims are on the record; they follow, and do not erase, the record year.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since November 2023, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The security-first mandate itself — kept as method. He campaigned in 2023 and 2025 on an iron fist against the gangs ("Plan Fénix"), and governed exactly that way from his seventh week in office: the "internal armed conflict" declaration of 9 January 2024 (Decree 111, naming 22 criminal groups as terrorist organizations), military deployment to streets and prisons, and successive emergency decrees. No voter who chose the method was deceived about the method; its results are graded under Test 4, not here.
  • Extradition — delivered. A 2023 promise, approved by voters in April 2024, and executed: Fito's extradition on 20 July 2025 was the first of an Ecuadorian national from Ecuadorian soil (Reuters/AP, July 2025).
  • The maximum-security prison — built. He promised a new high-security prison to break gang control of the existing ones; El Encuentro opened in Santa Elena province in November 2025 and received its first 300 high-risk inmates, Glas among them (SNAI, November 2025).
  • Ask the people — done, twice. He promised consultations and held them (April 2024, November 2025), accepting defeats in both (Test 3).
  • Blackouts — the dated promise was kept. After rationing of up to 14 hours a day in September–December 2024 (drought-driven, with losses estimated around US$2 billion), he announced on 10 December 2024 that outages would end on 20 December; household rationing ended on that schedule, and nationwide rationing did not recur in 2025 (2024 blackout records; energy-sector reporting). Earlier interim reduction promises in October 2024 had slipped within days — the record contains both.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The core promise — a safer Ecuador — stands inverted by the outcome measure. He promised to defeat the gangs and restore safety; 2024 improved on 2023, and then 2025 became the most violent year in the country's history (about 51 homicides per 100,000, Test 4). A president may keep his method and still break his word on the result he sold with it; the word was results.
  • Diesel — reversed. Through the 2023 campaign and into his government he assured voters the fuel subsidies would not be eliminated outright — the assurances were a staple of strike coverage precisely because they existed — and on 12 September 2025 he eliminated the diesel subsidy by Decree 126, raising the price from US$1.80 to US$2.80 a gallon overnight, the single act that triggered the 2025 national strike (Decree 126; strike reporting, September 2025).
  • Taxes — reversed. He campaigned in 2023 on lowering the tax burden; within five months of taking office he raised VAT from 12% to 15% (effective 1 April 2024) to fund the internal conflict — a defensible fiscal act and a broken campaign commitment, both (2024 tax law records).
  • A new constitution — pursued past the constitutional path, then refused by the voters. A signature 2025 campaign promise; he pursued it through the August 2025 pressure campaign against the Court and the September 2025 bypass decrees (Test 2), and when it finally reached the ballot on 16 November 2025 the voters refused it by about 62%. The promise to seek it was kept; the accompanying claim that the existing constitution was the obstacle to safety was put to the country, and the country answered otherwise.
  • The long arc, factually: Noboa entered politics as a 35-year-old assemblyman and banana-group heir, won a snap election in October 2023 as a fresh face against a security collapse, and governed from the start as the hardliner he campaigned as — the embassy raid on Mexico (5 April 2024, to seize former vice president Jorge Glas hours after Mexico granted him asylum; Mexico broke relations and sued at the ICJ, which on 23 May 2024 declined provisional measures on the strength of Ecuador's assurances while emphasizing the "fundamental importance" of embassy inviolability; Ecuador countersued over the asylum; both cases remain pending on the merits) fixed his international signature early: rules, including the oldest rule of diplomacy, yield when they shelter an opponent. The domestic record through 2026 — the sidelined vice president, the marched-on court, the jailed opposition mayor, the record homicide year, and, on the other side, the honored referendum defeats, the convicted soldiers, and a poverty rate cut by nearly seven points in a year — is the same signature written larger: forceful and genuinely willing to be voted down, and repeatedly willing to bend the referee when the referee, rather than the voters, says no.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Daniel Noboa has governed Ecuador for three years as the man he campaigned as — and the tests catch the two halves of that sentence. The half that holds: he asks the voters real questions and takes their answers, including a four-question sweep against him in November 2025; his own soldiers were convicted for the Las Malvinas disappearances; his signature extradition and prison were delivered; and poverty fell by nearly seven points in 2025. The half that fails: his family's firm saw the country's most famous tax debt extinguished under a remission in his own government's law; he wrote himself out of the campaign-leave rule and had his elected vice president suspended by a ministry and kept from her office; when the Constitutional Court checked him he marched on it beneath billboards of the justices' faces and tried to decree his way around its review; prisoners died in massacres and of neglect inside prisons his military runs; protesters were investigated, and three died; and the security promise at the center of his word produced, in 2025, the deadliest year in Ecuador's history. The voters remain free to dismiss him, and the Court still sits — but on the evidence assembled here, the seat gives by exception for its own house, grips the referees who rule against it, and counts prisoners, the disappeared, and the Indigenous movement as acceptable losses in a war whose own scoreboard moved the wrong way.
