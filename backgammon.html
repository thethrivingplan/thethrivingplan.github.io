Pip count

The pip count is the total number of single steps all your checkers still need to reach home and off. Lower is better. Pros count pips to judge the race: ahead by a lot with no contact left? Run. Behind? Stay back and hope for a shot.

The cube, in plain words

The doubling cube asks one question: “Still think you can win at least one time in four?” If your honest chance is better than 1 in 4, taking usually pays, because the games you win are now worth double. If it is worse, pass and give up just the current value. Doubling is strongest when you are winning but not yet so far ahead that the opponent passes at once — roughly when you win about 7 times in 10. When a cube decision is live and Teaching is on Coach, this page shows its own reckoning of your winning chances, and how it got them.

Race or contact?

Every position is a blend of two games. The race: pure speed, judged by pip count once no checkers can still hit each other. Contact: while checkers can still be hit, position matters more than speed — made points (a “prime”) wall the opponent in, an anchor in their home board keeps you safe from being shut out, and a blot left where many dice rolls can hit it is a real cost. Pros hit loose blots in your home board almost always, run their back checkers when the opponent's board is strong, and hold an anchor when behind in the race.

An earlier run of this same check caught the top two levels running level — Pro was retuned with the shot-counting described below and re-verified before this table was printed.

How the levels differ, in plain words: Learning judges a move roughly and often picks a plainly worse one, the way a new player does. Steady judges properly but still blurs its pick. Sharp judges properly and blurs only a little. Pro never blurs, and before it leaves a checker loose it counts every dice roll that could hit it next turn — all 36, one by one. Cube manners differ too: Learning takes almost any double and rarely offers one; Pro offers near the true numbers and passes a double it wins less than about one time in four.

How to play — tap to open
  • The game this is: standard backgammon, the documented classic — the same rules used in clubs and tournaments worldwide. Each player has 15 checkers on 24 points (the triangles). White moves from the 24-point down toward the 1-point and off; Black moves the other way.
  • Roll and move: move one checker by each die, or one checker by both. Doubles are played four times. You must use as much of the roll as the rules allow; if only one die can be played and either could, the higher die must be played.
  • Points and blots: two or more of your checkers on a point make it yours — the opponent cannot land there. A single checker (a blot) can be hit: the opponent lands on it and it goes to the bar.
  • The bar: a checker on the bar must re-enter into the opponent's home board (by a die roll, onto an open point) before any other checker moves. If no entry point is open, the turn is lost.
  • Bearing off: once all 15 of your checkers are in your home board (points 1–6), you may bear them off with exact rolls, or with a higher roll from your highest occupied point. First to bear off all 15 wins.
  • Scoring: a normal win scores the cube value. A gammon — the loser bore off no checkers — scores double. A backgammon — the loser bore off none and still has a checker on the bar or in the winner's home board — scores triple.
  • The cube: before rolling, the player who doesn't own the cube may offer to double the stakes. The opponent takes (play on at double, and the cube passes to them) or passes (lose the current value at once). The cube runs 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64.
  • First roll: each game opens with a roll-off — one die each; the higher die opens, playing both numbers shown. Ties roll again.
