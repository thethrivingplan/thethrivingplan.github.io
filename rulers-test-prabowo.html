The Ruler's Test — Prabowo Subianto, President of Indonesia

Subject: Prabowo Subianto — President of Indonesia since 20 October 2024; previously Minister of Defense (2019–2024) and, before that, commander of the Army Strategic Reserve (Kostrad) and of the Special Forces (Kopassus) under President Suharto. He won the February 2024 election with 58.6% of the vote, with Gibran Rakabuming Raka — the eldest son of his predecessor, Joko Widodo — as his vice president. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption commission (KPK) was permitted to strike inside his own cabinet: on 20–21 August 2025 the KPK arrested his Deputy Minister of Manpower, Immanuel Ebenezer, in a sting operation over an alleged extortion scheme in safety-certification fees, and named him and ten others suspects. Prabowo dismissed him the same day he was named a suspect, and his party moved to expel him (KPK statements and contemporaneous reporting, August 2025). The extortion itself is an ALLEGATION; the suspect has proclaimed his innocence and, notably, publicly asked the president for amnesty.
  • Enforcement continued to reach the edges of his own administration in his second year: in October 2026 the KPK named five suspects in a disaster-agency corruption case, including the younger brother of the sitting disaster-management chief, and Prabowo dismissed that chief the following day (Kompas TV reporting, October 2026).
  • His stated anti-corruption posture is on the record and emphatic: anti-corruption is written into his "Asta Cita" mission, and his early administration emphasized asset recovery and strengthening law-enforcement institutions (Pukat UGM assessment, November 2024; comparative legal-policy study, Eksekusi Journal of Law, December 2025).
  • The July 2025 clemency package was not confined to political figures: the amnesty approved by parliament covered 1,116 convicted persons (Antara, 1–2 August 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Clemency was used as a coalition instrument in corruption cases. On 30–31 July 2025, with parliament's approval, Prabowo granted abolition to former trade minister Tom Lembong — terminating his corruption case while his appeal was still pending, days after he had been sentenced to 4.5 years — and amnesty to Hasto Kristiyanto, secretary-general of the PDIP, the one major party outside his coalition, who had been sentenced to 3.5 years for bribery. The government's stated reason was national unity ahead of Independence Day; legal scholars and commentators across the spectrum described the moves as political transactions that weaken law enforcement and judicial independence when applied to corruption cases before the judicial process is complete (presidential letters R42 and R43/Pres, 30 July 2025; Lex Renaissance, 2026; Jakarta Post legal-expert reporting, 31 July 2025). Fairness requires noting that Lembong's conviction was itself contested by legal experts who argued a policy decision had been criminalized without proven state loss; the abolition can be defended as correcting a contested conviction — but it corrected it by presidential prerogative extended to a politically useful defendant, not by a rule applied to all such defendants.
  • The structural condition of one-law enforcement deteriorated on his watch and he did not repair it: the KPK has operated under the weakened 2019 revision of its law — which subordinated it to the executive and stripped investigative powers — and his government has neither restored the pre-2019 law nor advanced the long-stalled Asset Confiscation Bill, the very bill whose passage protesters demanded (Pukat UGM, November 2024; former Judicial Commission chairman Suparman Marzuki, December 2025; Indonesia Corruption Watch statement, 10 February 2026).
  • The outcome measure moved the wrong way: Indonesia's Corruption Perceptions Index score fell from 37 in 2024 to 34 in 2025, and its rank fell ten places to 109th of 180, with Transparency International Indonesia and Indonesia Corruption Watch attributing the fall to weakening oversight, normalized conflicts of interest, and patronage appointments (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released 10 February 2026; Reuters, 10 February 2026).
  • The coalition-of-everyone cabinet removed the institutional check that makes one law possible. His Red and White Cabinet — 48 ministers and more than 100 officials once deputy ministers and agency heads are counted, the largest of the post-1998 era — seats nearly every party in parliament; the governing coalition controls roughly 81% of the DPR, and the one party left outside, PDIP, styles itself a "balancing" party rather than an opposition. Analysts across two years of assessments describe the predictable result: a legislature that does not scrutinize, and strategic posts allocated to allies and party chairpersons as the price of support (cabinet inauguration records, October 2024; University of Melbourne, Indonesia at Melbourne, November 2026; ICW, February 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the anti-corruption commission, regional governments, media, and the military's civilian counterparts keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts still rule against the security establishment, in public: on 15 October 2026 the Constitutional Court ruled unanimously that the 1997 Military Justice Law is conditionally unconstitutional and ordered parliament and the government to reform it within two years so that soldiers accused of ordinary crimes face civilian courts — a direct judicial limit on the military's institutional autonomy, delivered in a case brought by two civilian petitioners (Constitutional Court, Case No. 260/PUU-XXIII/2025, 15 October 2026). The same court ruled in May 2025 that government agencies and officials may not file defamation complaints under the Electronic Information and Transactions (ITE) Law (Constitutional Court ruling, 2 May 2025).
  • The 2025 TNI Law, as passed, formally preserves the rule that officers must resign from active service before taking most civilian posts; the government's stated position is that the listed posts are exceptions requiring military expertise, not a general license (Reuters, 20 March 2025).
  • PDIP remained outside the cabinet and free to criticize; civil-society organizations openly publish annual report cards grading his administration a failure, and a coalition of them formed a public "shadow cabinet" in November 2026 — dissent exists in the open (Indonesia at Melbourne, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The signature act of his first year was a law expanding the military into civilian life. The revision of the 2004 Armed Forces (TNI) Law, passed unanimously on 20 March 2025, widened the list of civilian posts open to active-duty officers from 10 to 16 institutions — including the Attorney General's Office — and expanded the military's "operations other than war" into areas such as narcotics interdiction and cyberspace. It was drafted with minimal public participation, with key deliberations held in a closed-door hotel meeting on 14–15 March 2025, and passed over mass student protests and a joint warning from Human Rights Watch and six Indonesian rights organizations that it revived dwifungsi, the dual-function doctrine of the Suharto era (Human Rights Watch, 19 March 2025; Reuters, 20 March 2025; Global Change, Peace & Security, January 2026). The companion reform that would have subjected soldiers to civilian courts — revision of the Military Tribunal Law — was not taken up.
  • In practice the military's footprint in civilian programs expanded with the law: the armed forces are embedded in the logistics of his flagship free-meals program and in food-estate projects, and his administration has pursued the largest peacetime military expansion in Indonesia's modern history, including plans for up to 500 new battalions, many of them "territorial development" units tasked with securing and implementing development projects, above all in Papua (Reuters, March 2025; Human Rights Monitor, Annual Report 2025).
  • The KPK's subordination (Test 1) is also a grip fact: the anti-corruption body remains under executive influence, its independence unrestored, and no move has been made to return it to its pre-2019 standing (Pukat UGM, November 2024; ICW, February 2026).
  • Protest itself was met with criminal process: after the August–September 2025 demonstrations, police charged 959 people, including activists and human-rights defenders accused of "incitement" for protesting or posting on social media, under provisions of the Criminal Code and ITE Law that Amnesty International identifies as the standard instruments for silencing critics (Amnesty International, December 2025 investigation; Urgent Action ASA 21/0391/2025, October 2025).

The subject's own military past, stated exactly as documented. The brief for this dossier requires the 1998 record to be set out with findings, allegations, and denials kept distinct. It belongs to this test because it concerns the same institution whose autonomy is at issue.

  • Documented findings: In 1997–98, a Kopassus unit known as Tim Mawar (the Rose Team) abducted 23 pro-democracy activists. Eleven of its members were tried by a military court (December 1998–April 1999), convicted of kidnapping and detaining nine activists, and sentenced to 12–22 months; the team's commander and four captains were dismissed (military court record, as compiled by ICTJ–KontraS, 2011). Thirteen of the abducted activists remain missing to this day. A National Commission on Human Rights (Komnas HAM) inquiry, completed in November 2006, found evidence pointing to crimes against humanity and concluded that at least 27 persons might bear criminal responsibility; it recommended prosecution and an ad hoc human rights court. The Attorney General's Office declared the file incomplete, and no such prosecution or court has ever followed (Komnas HAM inquiry summary, 2006; ICTJ–KontraS, 2011).
  • The military's own process regarding Prabowo: An Officers Honorary Council (DKP) examined then-Lt. Gen. Prabowo in August 1998 and recommended his dismissal; President Habibie then discharged him from the armed forces — his campaign later characterized the discharge as honorable and the council's decision as a recommendation only, not a verdict (DKP decision KEP/03/VIII/1998/DKT, as cited by his campaign team, January 2024). Prabowo has never been tried in any court over the abductions.
  • What he has admitted, and what he denies — CONTESTED between the principals: Prabowo has acknowledged ordering the detention of the nine activists who were later released, maintaining he acted on orders from superiors and that the detentions were lawful; he denies responsibility for the activists who disappeared. Gen. Wiranto, the armed-forces commander at the time and a member of the DKP, stated publicly in 2014 that Prabowo told the council the abductions were his own initiative, not ordered by the command — a direct contradiction of Prabowo's account that neither man has withdrawn (Jakarta Post, 20 June 2014; campaign statements, 2023–2024). Whether he ordered, and what he knew of the fate of the missing thirteen, remains ALLEGATION — unadjudicated in any court, unresolved by the state, and denied by him.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, students, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Confronted with the largest unrest of the post-1998 era, he conceded the trigger. After protests over a Rp 50 million monthly housing allowance for lawmakers erupted on 25 August 2025 and exploded following the death of Affan Kurniawan, a 21-year-old motorcycle-taxi driver run over by a police tactical vehicle on 28 August, Prabowo announced on 31 August — flanked by party leaders — that parliament would revoke the allowance and other perks and impose a moratorium on overseas work trips (presidential press statement, 31 August 2025; DBS Group Research summary, 1 September 2025).
  • Individual accountability followed in the Kurniawan case: the police officer who drove the vehicle was dishonorably discharged, and the government proposed a Police Reform Commission (protest outcome records; DBS, September 2025). Prabowo expressed condolences and his government promised an investigation.
  • He reshuffled his cabinet in the crisis's wake, including replacing the widely respected finance minister, Sri Mulyani Indrawati, in September 2025 — a political cost absorbed rather than denied (protest outcome records, September 2025).
  • On the free-meals program, he eventually owned the failure in public: on 15 October 2025 he acknowledged the program's flaws (while citing a 0.0007% incident rate), and through 2026 his government closed kitchens, replaced the National Nutrition Agency's management in July 2026 with reforms to supervision and beneficiary checks, prioritized remote areas in a revised target, and in August 2026 ordered that meals be consumed within four hours of cooking (presidential statements, October 2025; USDA GAIN report ID2026-0029, July 2026; Asia Daily, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The concession came paired with criminalization. In the same 31 August address in which he revoked the perks, Prabowo said there were indications of acts "leading to treason and terrorism" and instructed the police and military to take firm action (presidential press statement, 31 August 2025; Soufan Center IntelBrief, 5 September 2025).
  • The force used was, on the documented record, unlawful in pattern: Amnesty International's Evidence Lab verified 36 videos showing beatings, close-range water cannon, and dangerous tear-gas grenades; at least 4,194 people were arrested between 25 August and 1 September (a figure police confirmed to Amnesty), 959 were charged — 295 of them children — and at least 1,036 people were victims of violence in 69 documented incidents; the National Commission on Human Rights reported 10 dead (Amnesty International investigation, December 2025; Komnas HAM, September 2025). Protesters' structural demands — passage of the Asset Confiscation Bill, the police chief's resignation, systemic police reform — were not met.
  • His first wobble of the term set the pattern. When students protested his Rp 306 trillion in budget cuts in February 2025 under the banner "Dark Indonesia" (Indonesia Gelap), he did not engage the grievance; he mocked the premise — Indonesia, he said, faces a bright future, citing a projection that it would be the world's fourth-largest economy by 2050 — and analysts across the coverage described the response as tone-deaf (CNA/Jakarta Globe reporting, 25–26 February 2025).
  • On the meals program, the adjustment lagged the harm by a year and never included the pause critics asked for: in May 2025, with poisoning cases already recurring, he defended the program and speculated the illnesses might be caused by students eating with their bare hands; health and education NGOs (CISDI, JPPI) urged a temporary suspension of the program and were refused, even as cases climbed into the tens of thousands (presidential remarks, 5 May 2025; program critics' statements, September 2025).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Papua, the poor, students and workers, religious minorities, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • His flagship program is universalist in design and genuinely large in reach. The Free Nutritious Meals program (MBG), launched 6 January 2025, provides free meals to schoolchildren, toddlers, and pregnant and breastfeeding women without partisan or regional test. It reached 22.7 million beneficiaries by September 2025, passed 41 million by November 2025, and stood at 58.2 million people through 27,485 kitchens by 31 October 2026, against a target of 82.9 million — with the 2026 target revision explicitly prioritizing people in remote and secluded areas (National Nutrition Agency figures via Asia Daily, November 2026; USDA GAIN report, July 2026).
  • His opponents' voters were courted rather than excluded: PDIP's secretary-general received amnesty (Test 1) and PDIP figures were offered places in his orbit; whatever its other character, the coalition-building was inclusionary toward the 2024 opposition's electorate.

Evidence against:

  • Papua is counted out, at scale, and the count worsened under him. Indigenous Papuans faced an expanded military campaign in 2025: more than 105,000 people were internally displaced by December 2025, up from roughly 85,000 in 2024; documented cases of extrajudicial killings, torture, and enforced disappearances rose; and military operations increasingly served to secure extractive and agribusiness projects — including the Merauke food estate on indigenous land — with new territorial battalions established across the region. The drivers documented by monitors are no longer only responses to armed resistance but territorial control for resource extraction, with humanitarian access to the displaced impeded (Human Rights Monitor, Annual Report 2025 and Q4 2025 report). Prabowo publicly dismissed deforestation concerns about palm-oil expansion in Papua, arguing plantations "absorb carbon dioxide" (Pusaka Foundation year-end report, January 2026).
  • The children his flagship program counts in were harmed by it in documented mass. Food-poisoning cases began ten days after launch (Sukoharjo, 16 January 2025) and recurred nationwide: 6,452 children affected by late September 2025; 16,109 pupils by 31 October 2025; and Health Ministry surveillance data counted 50,059 people affected in 560 incidents by early September 2026, with no deaths reported in that outbreak series (program records compiled from National Nutrition Agency and Health Ministry data; Associated Press reporting, September 2026). The Health Ministry attributed the incidents to bacterial and viral contamination from poor kitchen sanitation.
  • The austerity that funded his priorities was taken from the flock's services: the January–February 2025 budget cuts of roughly Rp 306 trillion fell on education, health, and regional governments — the grievance that launched the "Dark Indonesia" protests — while the cabinet itself grew to the largest in decades and, in the 2026 budget, some 44% of the education budget was allocated to the meals program rather than to schools (budget reporting, February 2025; APSN, August 2025).
  • Students and workers who protested were answered as a security problem: 959 charged after August 2025, including 295 children and at least a dozen activists and human-rights defenders charged with incitement (Amnesty International, December 2025).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since October 2024, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Free meals — kept, at scale. The signature promise of the 2024 campaign was launched on 6 January 2025, less than three months after inauguration, and expanded from an initial ~15–20 million target to 58.2 million beneficiaries by October 2026 (Test 4). Whatever its failures of execution, the promise was not abandoned or quietly shrunk; its budget grew from Rp 71 trillion in 2025 to Rp 335 trillion allocated for 2026.
  • Downstreaming — kept. The resource-processing policy inherited from his predecessor (export restrictions pushing smelting and processing onshore, beginning with nickel) has been continued and extended under his administration, as pledged (2025 budget and policy records; ISEAS Perspective 2024/85).
  • Defense modernization — continued. Major procurement set in motion while he was defense minister — including the 42-aircraft Rafale program contracted with France in 2022 — has proceeded under his presidency, alongside an expanded defense posture and the battalion build-up described in Test 2 (Reuters, March 2025; Human Rights Monitor, 2025).
  • His stated theory of coalition government was also kept, for what it is worth: he promised an inclusive government of national unity and built exactly that — the cost of which is graded under Tests 1 and 2, not here.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • 8% growth — not on any documented path. He pledged to lift growth from ~5% to 8% during his term (variously framed as by his third year or by 2029). Growth remained at about 5% through 2025 — 5.03% in 2024, roughly 5% in 2025 — and economists across the coverage regard 8% as unreachable on current policy; the vehicle built to deliver it, the Danantara sovereign wealth fund launched 24 February 2025, itself drew governance criticism for reporting directly to the president with state-enterprise assets moved outside ordinary budget oversight (BPS figures via ISEAS and press reporting, 2025; AFP, 24 February 2025).
  • Eradicating corruption — the record runs the other way. He promised relentless anti-corruption enforcement; two years in, the KPK remains structurally weakened, the Asset Confiscation Bill remains stalled, clemency has been extended to convicted corruption defendants as coalition currency, and the country's CPI score fell three points (Tests 1 and 2; Transparency International, February 2026). Former Judicial Commission chairman Suparman Marzuki's one-year verdict — "this year hasn't been effective; it's just been idle chatter" — is an interested critic's characterization, recorded here because the institutional record above independently supports its direction (UMY, December 2025).
  • The long arc, factually: the man who in 1998 was discharged from the army after the DKP process (Test 2) spent two decades contesting that record's meaning, ran for president in 2014 and 2019 on a strongman platform — disputing both election results, with his 2019 loss followed by deadly riots by supporters, after which he joined the winner's cabinet as defense minister — and won in 2024 on a softened, continuity platform ("gemoy" rebranding, running as Joko Widodo's heir with Widodo's son as his deputy). In office he has governed as neither the 1998 figure his critics feared in full nor the cuddly grandfather of the 2024 campaign: the militarization of civilian governance and the Papua campaign are continuous with the earlier record; the meals program and the coalition-of-everyone are the new instruments. His word on the two largest material promises splits exactly along that line.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Prabowo Subianto has built the most complete grip on Indonesian power since the fall of Suharto — a cabinet containing nearly every party, a legislature with no functioning opposition, a military law that returns soldiers to civilian office, an anti-corruption commission left in its weakened state, and clemency used to settle accounts with the political class — while the country's corruption index fell and its one structurally distinct people, the Papuans, were displaced in greater numbers under an expanding military campaign. Against that record stand real counterweights: he kept his signature promise at genuine scale, feeding 58 million people; he conceded the trigger of the 2025 unrest within a week and let the KPK take a member of his own cabinet; and the Constitutional Court still rules against the military establishment in his capital. But the tests measure the seat, not the intentions, and on the evidence assembled here the seat gives by favor more than by law, grips more than it holds, and counts the flock with a named exception in the east.
