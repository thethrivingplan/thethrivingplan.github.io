The Ruler's Test — Haitham bin Tariq Al Said, Sultan of Oman

Subject: Haitham bin Tariq Al Said — Sultan of Oman since 11 January 2020. The seat graded is the sultanate, and the reason is stated plainly: the Sultan holds executive power in Oman. He is head of state and prime minister, supreme commander of the armed forces, sole legislator by royal decree, appointer of the government, and chairman of the Supreme Judicial Council (Basic Statute of the State, Royal Decree 6/2021; Congressional Research Service, Oman: Politics, Security, and U.S. Policy, updated 4 November 2026). There is no separate prime minister to grade: the office is his. He succeeded his first cousin Sultan Qaboos bin Said, who died on 10 January 2020 after a reign of almost fifty years, leaving no children and no publicly named heir. Under the succession rule then in force, the Royal Family Council had three days to choose; it decided instead to open Qaboos's sealed will, which named Haitham — a former foreign-ministry undersecretary and, from 2002, Minister of Heritage and Culture, who had also chaired the Oman Vision 2040 committee (CRS, 2026; succession record, January 2020). His mandate is hereditary, not electoral: the seat was never voted on, which is recorded here as a fact about its mandate, not as a grade in itself — the Test grades the holding. Haitham, born in 1955, is graded on just under seven years in office, from the succession and the COVID-era fiscal crisis, through the unemployment protests of 2021, the fiscal and social-protection reforms of 2021–2025, to the Iran war of 2026, in which Oman was struck on its own soil and threatened by its own ally. In January 2021 he rewrote the succession itself, creating the post of Crown Prince and male primogeniture; his eldest son Theyazin bin Haitham is Crown Prince and, since January 2026, Deputy Prime Minister for Economic Affairs (Royal Decree 6/2021; CRS, 2026). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Each test closes with the grade's reason in plain words and a ledger naming who his actions on that test helped and who they hurt. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is never itself a failure — and this seat was never elected at all: for a hereditary seat there is no platform to grade, only the holding. Marks are lost only for how power is held — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), refusing to consider another option when one becomes available (Test 3), counting out part of the population (Test 4). Word-keeping and outcomes are graded as separate axes and printed independently; neither launders the other. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier says so and grades the best possible choice available in the moment; that is not held against him. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The fiscal laws of this reign apply, on their face, to everyone in the jurisdiction alike, including the well-off and including non-citizens. Value-added tax at 5% has applied since 16 April 2021 without a citizen exemption (tax record, 2021). The Personal Income Tax Law, Royal Decree 56/2025 of 22 June 2025 — the first personal income tax in any GCC state — taxes residents, Omani and expatriate alike, at a flat 5% only on income above OMR 42,000 a year, from 1 January 2028; the Tax Authority's impact study found roughly 99% of the population would not be subject to it (Royal Decree 56/2025; PwC Middle East Tax News, 2025; CRS, 2026). The one new tax of the reign falls, by design, on the richest one percent first.
  • The ordinary courts have, on the documented record of this reign, ruled against the prosecution in a speech case against a named critic: on 8 November 2026 the Court of First Instance in Seeb acquitted human-rights lawyer Majid bin Abdullah bin Saeed Al-Ruhaili of charges arising from opinions published on his X account in 2025, though a second case against him (for "insulting a sister state," Kuwait) was still pending at grading, and he was released on bail in it (Gulf Centre for Human Rights, November 2026).
  • The Social Protection Law, Royal Decree 52/2023, replaced a patchwork of separate pension and welfare schemes — which had produced, in the ILO's words, "gaps in coverage and comprehensiveness" — with a single unified system and a government-funded universal floor for Omani citizens across the lifecycle: children, the elderly, persons with disabilities, widows and orphans (ILO, 2023; Royal Decree 52/2023). One scheme, one set of rules, in place of eleven repealed predecessor laws and systems listed in the decree itself.
  • Oman Transparency standing has improved modestly on the standard measure: Oman ranked 52nd of 182 in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025 (Transparency International, CPI 2025).

Evidence against:

  • At the source, one person is placed outside the law's reach by the constitution itself. The Basic Statute declares the Sultan's person inviolable, respect for him a duty, and his command to be obeyed (Basic Statute, Royal Decree 6/2021; Oman's report to the UN Committee Against Torture, 2025). No equivalent immunity attaches to any other Omani. Criticism of that one person is then criminalised separately from criticism of anyone else: Penal Code Articles 97 and 115 are used to criminalise peaceful expression, and prosecutions for "undermining the prestige of the state" run through the record (GCHR and Oman Center for Human Rights and Democracy, joint UPR submission, November 2025; Amnesty International, annual report on Oman, 2026).
  • In February 2025 the seat wrote the inequality into the most basic status of all. The new Omani Nationality Law, ratified by Royal Decree 17/2025 of 2 February 2025, provides that citizenship "shall be revoked" from an Omani who "verbally or physically offends the Sultanate of Oman or the Sultan himself," or who belongs to a group whose principles "harm the interests" of the Sultanate — with, on the rights organisations' reading, no mechanism of judicial appeal against the revocation (Royal Decree 17/2025; International IDEA, February 2025; GCHR, 11 February 2025; Amnesty International, 2026). A supporter who praises the Sultan keeps his citizenship unconditionally; a critic who offends him holds his at discretion. That is a second law at the source, signed by the seat being graded.
  • The documented prosecutions fall on one side of the map only. In 2025 alone, on Amnesty International's record: activist Talal Al-Salmani was arrested on 8 April 2025 after a peaceful gathering at which he criticised the government, sentenced within two days to a year in prison for "spreading news likely to undermine the prestige of the state" (reduced on appeal in May to three months, released 6 August); writer Mohammed Ali Al-Bar'ami was arrested on 18 June 2025, two days after posting that the government system was "corrupt," and sentenced on 28 July to four years in prison and a OMR 5,000 fine for publishing "what may harm public order"; Salem bin Salam Al-Salti was summoned on 8 August 2025 over social-media comments criticising unemployment policy and subjected to enforced disappearance, its duration unconfirmed at year's end (Amnesty International, The State of the World's Human Rights, Oman chapter, April 2026). No pro-government speaker is documented facing any of these instruments. The guilt or innocence of each man on the underlying facts is not this dossier's to state; the one-sidedness of the instrument's use is documented.
  • The ruling family's place inside the state has deepened, not thinned, under a Sultan who — unlike Qaboos — delegated the foreign and finance portfolios. His full brother Shihab bin Tariq is Deputy Prime Minister for Defence Affairs; his eldest son Theyazin is Crown Prince and, since the Council of Ministers was reconstituted by royal decree on 12 January 2026, Deputy Prime Minister for Economic Affairs; his second son Bilarab bin Haitham was appointed in the same January 2026 decree Minister of State and Governor of Muscat (BTI 2024 country report; State Media Monitor, Oman cycle 2025/26, citing Royal Decree 17/2026). No member of the ruling family is documented as prosecuted for any offence in this reign; the accountability record runs the other way (against-shelf above).

Why: The fiscal and social laws of this reign are genuinely general — the income tax lands on the richest first and applies to expatriates and Omanis alike, and the social-protection floor unified eleven schemes into one. But Test 1 grades the source, and at the source the Sultan is constitutionally inviolable, criticism of him personally is a crime and, since February 2025, a ground for losing citizenship itself without a court in between, while his brother and both sons hold the defence, economic and capital-city portfolios. One law for taxes; a different law for the throne and those who question it. Helped: High-income residents, who face a clear, single, announced-in-advance tax rule from 2028 rather than discretionary levies; Omani citizens as a class, whose welfare entitlements now run under one unified scheme; Majid Al-Ruhaili, acquitted in November 2026 in the first of his two cases. Hurt: Critics and activists, who hold their citizenship and liberty under speech provisions no supporter ever meets — Talal Al-Salmani, Mohammed Ali Al-Bar'ami and Salem bin Salam Al-Salti named on Amnesty International's 2025 record; every Omani whose status, since February 2025, depends in law on not offending one named person.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The elected chamber exists, is elected on a regular cycle, and is not a pure decoration. The 90 members of the Consultative Council (Majlis al-Shura) are directly elected by Omani citizens, male and female, over 21; members may question ministers, select their own leadership, and review government-drafted legislation (CRS, 2026). The October 2023 election was conducted entirely through the Intikhab mobile application, with turnout of 65.88% of registered voters on the Inter-Parliamentary Union's figures — 496,279 of 753,690 registered voters — up from 49.02% in 2019 (IPU; Oman Observer, 2023).
  • The judiciary's habits have not wholly followed its structure: the Seeb court's November 2026 acquittal of Al-Ruhaili (Test 1) was a ruling against the Public Prosecution in a political-speech case, delivered in this reign and on this record.
  • Unlike his predecessor, Haitham did give up personal possession of some referees' chairs Qaboos had kept: he relinquished the foreign-affairs and finance portfolios and is no longer head of the central bank (BTI 2024 country report). The Central Bank of Oman has operated through the fiscal consolidation and the 2026 war without any documented act of the Sultan directing a monetary decision.

Evidence against:

  • The referees are, by constitutional design, the Sultan's own. He is prime minister as well as head of state; he holds "sole authority to enact laws through royal decree" (CRS, 2026); he appoints all members of the upper house, the State Council (Majlis al-Dawla) — appointees usually former senior officials, military officers and tribal notables (CRS, 2026); political parties are banned, so no organised opposition can form at all (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025; IPU, 2023). The Shura Council may review and question; "law-making powers rest firmly in the hands of the sultan" (CRS, 2026). Freedom House scores Oman's judiciary 0/4 for independence and government transparency 0/4, and rates the country "Not Free" at 24/100 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025/2026).
  • In 2022 the grip on the courts was formalised, not loosened. Royal Decree 35/2022 reconstituted the Supreme Judicial Council with the Sultan himself as its chairman, a deputy chairman who is not a judge appointed by royal decree, and a secretary-general likewise appointed by royal decree; it abolished the separate Administrative Court — the court whose whole job was suits against the government — folding its work into circuits of the general courts, and brought the Public Prosecution under the unified council's supervision (Royal Decree 35/2022; Supreme Judicial Council official site; Decree Blog analysis, 2022). The Basic Statute's Article 77 declares judicial authority independent; the council that governs judges' careers is chaired by the head of the executive they are to check.
  • The media referee was re-legislated twice in two years, each time toward the centre. The Media Law enacted in November 2024 granted the Ministry of Information broad powers over content and licensing, including suspension of activities without judicial approval and surveillance of journalists and media institutions; its Executive Regulations followed in September 2025 (BTI 2026 country report; Amnesty International, 2026). The 2026 Law on Combating Information Technology Crimes then criminalised producing or re-publishing anything that "undermines public order or public morality" or constitutes "rumors or misleading information" — one month to three years, rising to three to ten years where intent to incite "strife, hatred, racism, sectarianism, or division" is deemed, and five to fifteen years for "false or misleading information" during emergencies or wartime (Law on Combating Information Technology Crimes, 2026, Articles 20, 23, 30; IFEX/SMEX analysis, 17 July 2026; Law Library of Congress, Global Legal Monitor, 23 September 2026). Reporters Without Borders ranks Oman 127th of 180 in its 2026 index and characterises self-censorship as the rule, with criticism of the Sultan unacceptable (RSF, 2026).
  • The human-rights referee is the government's own: the Oman Human Rights Commission is a government entity (CRS, 2026), and the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights' letter of 20 October 2026 on Oman's fourth-cycle Universal Periodic Review invites Oman — citing Royal Decree 47/2026 of 14 April 2026 — to enable the Commission to achieve Paris-Principles "A status," which is to say it does not hold it (OHCHR, UPR letter on Oman, 20 October 2026).

Why: A seat holds without gripping when the referees can still say no to it. In Oman the Sultan chairs the judicial council, appoints the upper house and the prosecution's governing body, legislates alone, banned parties before this reign and kept the ban, and in 2024–2026 signed a media law and a cybercrime law that widen state control over the two channels — press and internet — through which any referee could be criticised. The surviving for-shelf (an elected advisory chamber with rising turnout, one acquittal, a central bank left alone) shows institutions functioning inside the grip; it does not show the seat holding off. Helped: The executive itself, which legislates, appoints and prosecutes without any institution able to block it; state and state-aligned media, operating under a licensing regime the ministry controls. Hurt: Voters, whose elected chamber cannot make law; judges and prosecutors, whose governing council is chaired by the head of government; journalists and online speakers, governed since 2024–2026 by suspension-without-a-judge and by speech offences carrying up to fifteen years in wartime; defendants in administrative cases, whose dedicated court against the government was abolished in 2022.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? And when another option became available, did he at least consider it openly?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The first great wobble of the reign — the unemployment protests of May–June 2021 — was answered with a material adjustment announced within days, not only with force (the force is on the against-shelf). Protests by unemployed and laid-off young Omanis began in Sohar on 23 May 2021 and spread to Salalah, Ibri, Sur, Ibra and Rustaq, demanding jobs, better living conditions, reform and an end to corruption. On 25 May 2021 the Sultan issued directives for a plan to create more than 32,000 job opportunities during that year, including 12,000 in the civil service and military, with subsidised private-sector placements and expanded military recruitment (Amnesty International Report 2021/22; GCHR/OAHR documentation, 2021). The demand was named by state media as "among the most important priorities" of the Sultan — the grievance was acknowledged in public, in substance.
  • The zero-women result of the October 2023 Shura election — 32 women among 843 candidates, none elected, though women cast 47.84% of the ballots — was answered, after the Oman Human Rights Commission itself flagged the gap in its July 2025 UPR submission, with another option considered and taken: on 15 June 2026 the Sultan directed the allocation of 11 additional Shura seats for women, one per governorate, and Royal Decree 98/2026 of 26 October 2026 wrote the seats into the election law, with preparations reported on 11 November 2026 for the next term (Foreign Ministry announcement, 15 June 2026; Royal Decree 98/2026; Oman Observer, 11 November 2026). A documented failure of the existing arrangement produced a structural change, openly decreed.
  • The 2026 war was answered in the open, against pressure from both belligerents. Oman had hosted US–Iran talks in Muscat on 6 February 2026 in a last attempt to prevent the war; after US and Israeli strikes began on 28 February 2026, Oman's Foreign Ministry publicly expressed regret over the US and Israeli operations — a rare open disagreement with its US partner (CRS, 2026). When Iranian drones then struck Omani ports (against the mediation partner, too), Oman distanced itself from GCC positions and refrained from attributing the attacks to Iran, condemned attacks when they hit Musandam and summoned Iran's ambassador after the July 2026 Duqm strike, yet kept the diplomatic channel open, built a "southern route" for shipping along its own coast in coordination with the International Maritime Organization, stated that no tolls would be imposed, and proposed — openly, on an UNCLOS Article 26 basis — voluntary service fees for specific services rendered, a proposal Iran rejected in late July 2026 and the United States opposed (International Crisis Group, CrisisWatch, March 2026; CRS, 2026). Twice in 2026 President Trump threatened to bomb Oman if it obstructed US efforts on the strait (CRS, 2026). Oman neither joined the war, nor retaliated, nor closed the channel: the adjustment was continuous, public, and made under threat from both sides.
  • Pushback through the Universal Periodic Review produced a real, dated concession: after years of recommendations, Oman acceded to the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights — Royal Decree 89/2025 of 16 October 2025, instrument deposited 10 November 2025, in force for Oman 10 February 2026 (UN depositary notification C.N.645.2025; OHCHR, 2026). The reservations attached are on the against-shelf; the accession itself, urged on Oman across successive review cycles, happened.

Evidence against:

  • The 2021 protests were also answered with the other hand: security forces fired tear gas, arrested scores of protesters, confiscated phones, and released many only after they signed pledges not to participate in future demonstrations (Amnesty International Report 2021/22; GCHR/OAHR, 2021). The jobs directive answered the demand; the pledges answered the demanding. The pattern repeated later in the reign: BTI records arrests and imprisonment of individuals protesting corruption and calling for reform in 2023 and 2024 (BTI 2026 country report).
  • On expression, every documented pushback in 2024–2026 was answered by widening the instrument criticised. The UN and rights organisations criticised the speech provisions of the Penal Code; the answer was the Media Law of November 2024, the citizenship-revocation law of February 2025, and the cybercrime law of 2026 (Tests 1–2). If another option became available — the ICCPR Oman had just joined supplies one, in its Articles 19, 21 and 22 — there is no documented instance of the new speech laws being reconsidered against it; instead, Austria and Finland formally objected in November 2026 that Oman's ICCPR declarations on Articles 18, 22, 23 and 27 subordinate the Covenant to national law and Sharia in terms "incompatible with the object and purpose" of the treaty — objections Oman has not, on the record assembled here, answered by narrowing either the declarations or the laws (UN Treaty Database objections, November 2026).
  • The domestic-worker and migrant-labour pushback was answered late and partially: only on 13 October 2025 — after a BBC investigation into Malawian domestic workers trafficked to Oman, a Do Bold survey finding almost all of 469 interviewed Sierra Leonean women in Oman to be victims of forced labour, and a US Trafficking in Persons record of no labour-trafficking conviction in seven consecutive years — did Ministerial Decision 574/2025 set minimum standards for domestic workers (weekly and annual leave, regulated hours, a ban on passport retention) (Amnesty International, 2026; BBC Africa Eye; US State Department TIP Report 2025 via MRRORS analysis, 2025). Adjustment, yes — after years of documented harm, and by ministerial decision rather than by bringing domestic workers inside the Labour Law (Test 4).

Why: The pattern splits cleanly by direction. Economic and representational wobbles — jobs in 2021, the zero-women election in 2023, the UPR process — were answered with real, dated adjustments: 32,000 jobs directed within two days, 11 women's seats decreed, the ICCPR joined. The existential wobble of 2026 was answered openly and proportionately under threats from both belligerents. But pushback on expression itself — the one wobble that is about his own power — was answered every time by widening the law, and the street in 2021 got tear gas and signed pledges alongside its jobs plan. Open on everything except the grip: MIXED. Helped: Young Omani jobseekers of 2021, whose central demand was met with a 32,000-job directive in days; Omani women as future legislators, guaranteed 11 seats from the next term; shipping, insurers and coastal residents in 2026, served by the southern route, Omani search-and-rescue, and a state that refused escalation; domestic workers, who gained written minimum standards in October 2025. Hurt: The 2021 protesters who were tear-gassed, arrested, and made to sign away future protest as the price of release; speakers and writers of 2024–2026, for whom each round of criticism produced a broader law (Tests 1–2); the cause of the ICCPR itself in Oman, joined with declarations two European states have formally objected subordinate it to the very laws it was meant to test.

The no-good-choice clause, applied by its mechanics: The Strait of Hormuz in 2026 was a genuine no-good-choice situation, and it is recorded as one. Oman is, with Iran, one of the two states whose territorial waters form the strait. The options actually open to its government from March 2026 were: align openly with the United States — whose forces use Duqm and Salalah under a 2019 framework agreement, and whose president twice threatened to bomb Oman for obstructing him — and invite the Iranian retaliation that in fact killed people in Sohar, Duqm and Salalah anyway (International Crisis Group counts the March 2026 toll as at least two civilians killed in the deadliest single attack, two expatriate workers killed and at least ten injured at Sohar on 13 March; CRS, citing INSS, counts an estimated 66 Iranian strikes on Oman between February and October 2026 with 14 fatalities — printed as separate sourced figures, never blended); or align with Iran and lose the US security relationship and invite the threatened US strikes; or hold the middle — condemn, mediate, keep shipping moving along its own coast, retaliate against no one. Each door carried real harm, and the trap was not of the Sultan's making: he had hosted the talks meant to prevent the war three weeks before it began. He took the least-harm door actually open, honestly and in public, and the dilemma is not held against him. What remains gradable — and is graded on the shelves — is the domestic use of the wartime moment: the 2026 cybercrime law's five-to-fifteen-year wartime speech penalties were a choice inside the trap, not a requirement of it.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the women, the migrant workers who are nearly half the population, the religious communities, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Citizens, as economic persons, are counted with unusual completeness for the region. The Social Protection Law (Royal Decree 52/2023, in force 1 January 2024) built what the ILO calls a benchmark for the region: a unified contributory pension system plus a government-funded universal floor of lifecycle benefits for children, the elderly, persons with disabilities, widows and orphans — explicitly designed, in the Sultan's own National Day formulation quoted by the ILO, to catch citizens as the universal fuel and electricity subsidies were withdrawn and VAT introduced (ILO, 2023; OHCHR UPR letter, 20 October 2026, noting the law's "comprehensive benefits for women, children, the elderly, and persons with disabilities"). The fiscal consolidation's costs were, by design, partly recycled to the people bearing them.
  • Workers in the formal economy gained enforceable protections in the Labour Law, Royal Decree 53/2023: forced labour prohibited (Article 5), passport retention without written consent prohibited (Article 6), an eight-hour day, a Wage Protection System run with the Central Bank, and exemption of workers from judicial fees in labour suits (Royal Decree 53/2023, via NCCHT statements, 2024). The anti-trafficking law, Royal Decree 78/2025, then expanded the definitions of slavery, domestic servitude and forced labour and strengthened victim protections, including fee waivers and shelter provision (Royal Decree 78/2025; MRRORS analysis, 2025). Domestic workers gained written minimum standards in October 2025 (Test 3).
  • Religious communities are, in Oman's documented practice, counted in rather than policed as blocs: Oman is the only country where Ibadi Islam is the main faith, the state presents itself — and is widely reported — as permitting churches, a synagogue's successor congregations, and Hindu temples to operate openly, and no sectarian exclusion from state employment of the kind documented elsewhere in this series appears in the record assembled here (CRS, 2026; State Department religious-freedom reporting). Women, after the 2023 shut-out, were counted back in by the 11 reserved seats of 2026 and by 18 appointments to the 83-member State Council made a month after the 2023 election (CRS, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Nearly half the people who live in Oman are counted out of its politics entirely and most of the way out of its labour law. As of 30 June 2025 there were 2,296,984 foreign nationals in Oman — 43% of a population of 5,306,976 — mostly from South and South-East Asia and East Africa, including a large, predominantly female domestic workforce (official figures via Global Detention Project UPR submission, 2026). They hold no vote at any level. Domestic workers remain outside the Labour Law's main protections, covered instead by the October 2025 ministerial standards; the kafala sponsorship system ties a worker's residence to her employer, and the documented consequences in this reign include the BBC's Malawi investigation (54 women repatriated by their own government at a cost of over USD 160,000, most released only after USD 1,000–2,000 was paid to the employer) and the Do Bold survey of Sierra Leonean women (BBC Africa Eye; US TIP Report 2023/2025). The enforcement count runs against the workers, not the employers: the Ministry of Labour reported arresting 12,319 people for "illegal labour practices" and deporting 7,615 foreign workers between January and May 2025 alone, after 23,566 arrests and 18,053 deportations in 2024 — figures printed as the ministry's own, via the Global Detention Project's UPR submission; the US TIP Report records no labour-trafficking conviction secured by the government in seven consecutive years (Global Detention Project, 2026; US State Department TIP Report 2025).
  • Women are counted as citizens with lesser rights in the laws that govern family and status. Although the Basic Statute states that all citizens are equal and bans gender-based discrimination, women face discrimination in law in divorce, inheritance, child custody and legal guardianship (Human Rights Watch, Oman country page, 2026). The February 2025 nationality law widened the gap: Omani mothers may pass citizenship to their children only in restricted cases — on International IDEA's summary, effectively widows or divorcees — while Omani fathers pass it as of right, and the residence period for foreign wives of Omani men rose from five to eight years; Amnesty records the law as putting children of Omani women at risk of statelessness (Royal Decree 17/2025; International IDEA, February 2025; Amnesty International, 2026). Oman still lacks comprehensive legal protection against domestic violence (Amnesty International, 2026).
  • Two further groups are counted out by criminal law rather than by omission. Consensual same-sex conduct is criminalised under the Penal Code, and in July 2024 the deadly attack near a Shia mosque in Muscat, claimed by the Islamic State, marked the Shia minority as a target the state could not protect in that instance — recorded here as an attack on the counted, not an act of the seat (International IDEA country record, July 2024). Protesters, finally, were counted as a security file in 2021 and after (Test 3): the demand was answered, the demanders were made to sign pledges.
  • The death penalty is retained for a range of offences and Oman remains classified retentionist; the UN High Commissioner in October 2026 urged Oman to establish a moratorium — a recommendation not taken up on the record assembled here (Amnesty International, Oman page, 2026; OHCHR UPR letter, 20 October 2026). No execution figure is printed here because the sources assembled do not agree on a current count; the retention itself is not in dispute.

Why: Inside the citizen body, this reign's counting is genuinely strong — a universal social-protection floor, a unified pension system, workers' protections in the formal economy, religious communities left in peace, and women legislatively re-included after a shut-out. But "everyone" in Oman includes the 43% who are foreign residents, and they are counted out of the vote, largely out of the Labour Law if they work in homes, and into the deportation statistics by the tens of thousands; it includes Omani women, who cannot pass their own citizenship to their children on equal terms; and it includes anyone whose private life or protest the Penal Code criminalises. A count that is complete for male citizens and partial for everyone else is not everyone. Helped: Omani children, pensioners, persons with disabilities, widows and orphans, covered by the 2024 social-protection floor as subsidies were withdrawn; formal-sector workers, Omani and expatriate, under the 2023 Labour Law and Wage Protection System; trafficking victims, given expanded definitions and fee waivers in 2025; Omani women as voters and future Shura members. Hurt: The 2.3 million foreign residents — domestic workers above all, on the BBC and Do Bold records — who live under kafala without a vote and, if in homes, outside the Labour Law; the 7,615 people deported in the first five months of 2025 on the ministry's own figures; Omani women married to foreign men, and their children, whose citizenship the 2025 law restricted; protesters made to sign pledges; LGBT Omanis, criminalised by the Penal Code.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Public statements and program pledges vs. the record since 11 January 2020, with dates. For a hereditary seat there is no election platform; the words graded are the Sultan's own accession speeches, his National Day addresses, and the Vision 2040 programme he chaired before he reigned and adopted as his own.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The foreign-policy word — kept, under fire, for seven years. In his first speech, 11 January 2020, he pledged to trace Qaboos's course: peaceful coexistence, good neighbourliness, non-interference, and contributing "to resolving disputes by peaceful means" (accession speech, 11 January 2020). The record since is the region's most consistent mediation file: Oman hosted the US–Iran talks of April 2025 and February 2026, brokered the US–Houthi ceasefire of May 2025, kept channels to Tehran, Riyadh and Washington simultaneously, and in the 2026 war refused both belligerents' demands to abandon the middle (Test 3; CRS, 2026). Whatever its costs, the word given on day one is the policy of year seven.
  • The fiscal word — kept, with dates and figures. He inherited a state whose public debt peaked at about 68% of GDP in 2020 and whose credit stood at junk with all three agencies, and pledged fiscal balance and diversification under Vision 2040 — a programme he had chaired as minister. The record: VAT introduced April 2021; the medium-term fiscal plan executed through subsidy reform; debt down to 36% of GDP in 2025 on the Ominvest/Oxford Business Group account, and to 30.4% of GDP by June 2026 on the IMF's latest assessment, with the fiscal breakeven oil price fallen from about USD 100 a barrel in 2016 to the mid-USD 50s; investment-grade ratings restored by all three major agencies (Moody's Baa3 and Fitch BBB− in 2025); non-oil growth of 3.5% year-on-year in the first half of 2025; the IMF concluding in January 2026 that Oman "entered 2026 from a position of strength" (IMF, 2025 Article IV Consultation, January 2026; Jefferies, Vision 2040 at Five, 2026; Oxford Business Group/Ominvest, September 2026).
  • The social-protection word — spoken, then built. At the fiftieth National Day in November 2020 he committed the state to "providing the necessary protection and care for citizens" and to directing part of fiscal-policy revenues to make social protection "a comprehensive national umbrella" (National Day address, November 2020, via ILO, 2023). Royal Decree 52/2023 built exactly that, in force 1 January 2024 (Test 4).
  • The succession word — the uncertainty he inherited, closed. Having been chosen by a sealed letter, he regularised what Qaboos had left ambiguous: Royal Decree 6/2021 of 11 January 2021 established primogeniture and the Crown Prince post, and Theyazin's position has since been exercised in the open — Minister of Culture, Sports and Youth, then Deputy Prime Minister for Economic Affairs in January 2026 (Royal Decree 6/2021; CRS, 2026). The next succession, unlike his own, is a published rule.
  • The income tax — announced with its date, and the date stands. Royal Decree 56/2025 set the start at 1 January 2028 with two and a half years' notice; as of grading the start date has not been moved (Royal Decree 56/2025; Decree Blog, October 2026). The missed implementing deadline is on the against-shelf; the headline commitment itself is unbroken.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The freedom word — given in February 2020, contradicted by his own later laws. In his first speech after the mourning period, on 23 February 2020, he said Omanis and residents live in "a country of law that respects the principles of equality and freedom," where "dignity and freedom of expression is protected," and promised to listen to the youth and understand their needs (speech of 23 February 2020). The subsequent record is Tests 1–2: the 2021 protesters tear-gassed and pledged into silence, the Media Law of 2024, citizenship revocable for offending the Sultan in 2025, speech offences widened again in 2026, and the ICCPR joined with declarations subordinating expression-adjacent articles to national law. The word was plain; the record runs the other way.
  • The youth-employment word — answered in crisis, not yet kept in structure. The 23 February 2020 speech promised a complete national employment scheme; the May 2021 directive created 32,000 opportunities for that year. But unemployment among young Omanis remained the grievance that produced protests again in 2023–2024 (BTI 2026), and the state's structural answer — Omanisation quotas widened by Ministerial Resolution 501/2024, reserving more professions to Omanis — works in part by counting foreign workers out (Test 4) rather than by growing the private employment the Vision promises. The IMF's January 2026 assessment still lists labour-market bottlenecks among the unfinished reforms (IMF, 2026).
  • The income-tax fine print — a deadline in his own decree, missed. Royal Decree 56/2025 required the Tax Authority chairman to issue the executive regulations within one year of publication (30 June 2025). By 7 October 2026 — over three months past the deadline — the regulations had not appeared in the Official Gazette, and the Tax Authority's own page listed only the decree (Official Gazette record to No. 1668, 4 October 2026, via Decree Blog tracking, October 2026). Small against the fiscal record; exactly the kind of word Test 5 exists to catch.
  • Vision 2040's own targets — direction kept, destination receding. The Vision targets oil at 16% of GDP by 2030 and 8.4% by 2040; the actual figure was about 31% in 2024, and the bulk of foreign investment through 2025 remained in oil and gas, with manufacturing flat (Jefferies, 2026; CRS, 2026, citing EIU, October 2025). The trajectory is real; on the programme's own arithmetic the transformation is behind its schedule, and the 2026 oil-price windfall — a projected surplus of as much as USD 3 billion if spending holds — rewards the old economy, not the new one (Bloomberg, 3 November 2026, via CRS, 2026).

Why: Divide his words by kind and the pattern is exact. Words about the state's direction — neutrality and mediation, fiscal balance, a social-protection umbrella, a settled succession — were kept, on dates, with figures this dossier can cite, through a pandemic, a debt crisis and a war. The word about his own power's limits — a country where freedom of expression is protected — was given in February 2020 and has been contradicted by every speech law signed since, and the small print of his own tax decree missed its own deadline. A ruler who keeps the programme and breaks the liberty: MIXED. Helped: Creditors, investors and everyone paid from the Omani treasury, by a debt ratio halved and investment grade restored; citizens under the social-protection umbrella he promised in 2020 and built in 2023; the region's disputants, who kept a working channel in Muscat for seven years. Hurt: Speakers, writers and protesters, who were promised protected expression in February 2020 and received the laws of 2024, 2025 and 2026 instead; high-income taxpayers and their employers, left without the implementing regulations his decree promised by June 2026; young Omanis still queueing for the structural jobs answer the 2020 speech promised.

The two axes, stated plainly (word × outcome): Crossing 1 — kept his word and it helped people: the November 2020 social-protection commitment, delivered as Royal Decree 52/2023's universal floor, which caught citizens as subsidies were withdrawn. Crossing 2 — kept his word and it still hurt people: the fiscal-balance word was kept faithfully — VAT at 5% from April 2021 and the phased removal of universal fuel and electricity subsidies were the keeping of it — and the ILO records those very measures as the cost-of-living pressure the protection floor was then built to answer; the keeping earns its credit here, and the household burden stands in the ledger in full. Crossing 3 — went against his word and it helped people: not documented. His largest departure from his predecessor's practice — naming a crown prince and delegating the finance and foreign portfolios — was framed by the seat itself as building on Qaboos's legacy rather than breaking the continuity pledge, and no instance was found in the record assembled here of a clear pledge broken to documented benefit; recorded as not documented rather than invented. Crossing 4 — broke his word and it hurt people: the 23 February 2020 assurance that freedom of expression is protected in Oman, followed by the arrests of 2021 and the speech laws of 2024–2026, whose costs are ledgered in Tests 1 and 2.

Overall Summary

Haitham bin Tariq came to the throne by a dead man's sealed letter in January 2020, inheriting a junk-rated treasury, a youth-employment grievance, and the region's most valuable diplomatic asset — a reputation for talking to everyone. On one law, he fails at the source: his fiscal laws are genuinely general and his income tax lands on the richest first, but his person is constitutionally inviolable, criticism of him is a crime, since February 2025 it can cost an Omani his citizenship without a court, and his brother and both sons now hold the defence, economic and Muscat portfolios — helped being taxpayers under clear rules and citizens under one welfare scheme, hurt being every critic whose status depends on not offending one man. On holding without gripping, he fails structurally: he chairs the Supreme Judicial Council, appoints the upper house, legislates alone, abolished the court that heard suits against the government, and signed the media and cybercrime laws of 2024 and 2026 — helped being only the executive itself, hurt being voters, judges, journalists and online speakers. On the wobble, he is genuinely mixed: the 2021 jobs directive in two days, 11 women's seats after the 2023 shut-out, ICCPR accession, and a 2026 war answered openly and without escalation under Iranian strikes (14 dead on CRS's INSS-based count) and American threats to bomb his country — a trap the no-good-choice clause records as not of his making and not held against him — set against tear gas and signed pledges for the 2021 street and a wider speech law after every round of criticism; helped being jobseekers, future women legislators and everyone who ships through the strait, hurt being protesters and speakers. On counting everyone, he fails on the denominator: the social-protection floor counts citizens comprehensively, but 43% of the people who live in Oman — 2.3 million foreign residents, domestic workers above all — are counted out of the vote and largely out of the Labour Law, Omani women cannot pass citizenship to their children as men can, and the ministry's own figures record 7,615 deportations in five months of 2025; helped being citizens under the floor, hurt being the resident near-half of the country. On his word, he is mixed with a clean split: the directional promises — mediation, fiscal balance (debt from 68% of GDP to 30.4% by June 2026 on the IMF's figures), the protection umbrella, a published succession — were kept with dates and numbers, while the February 2020 promise of protected expression was contradicted by his own laws and his tax decree's regulation deadline passed unmet in June 2026. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the income tax starts on 1 January 2028 with its regulations issued; whether the 11 women's seats are filled in the 2027 Shura election; whether the ICCPR declarations are narrowed or the speech laws tested against the Covenant Oman joined in February 2026; whether any citizenship is ever revoked for offending the Sultan under the 2025 law, and what a court is allowed to say about it; and whether domestic workers are brought inside the Labour Law or left under a ministerial decision.
