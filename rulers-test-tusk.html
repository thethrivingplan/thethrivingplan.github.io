The Ruler's Test — Donald Tusk, Prime Minister of Poland

Subject: Donald Tusk — Prime Minister of Poland since 13 December 2023; previously Prime Minister 2007–2014 and President of the European Council 2014–2019; leader of Civic Platform and the Civic Coalition (KO). His four-party coalition (KO, Poland 2050, the Polish People's Party PSL, and the Left) won a Sejm majority in the 15 October 2023 election, in which Law and Justice (PiS) placed first in votes but could not form a government. He has governed throughout in cohabitation with PiS-backed presidents: Andrzej Duda until August 2025, then Karol Nawrocki. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The accountability drive against the previous government has run through courts that repeatedly ruled against the prosecution and the government, in public. Former deputy justice minister Marcin Romanowski, arrested in July 2024 on Justice Fund charges (ALLEGATIONs, denied), was released within days after a court found he held immunity as a member of the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe. The "Hajnówka Five" border-aid activists — charged under the previous government's prosecution service in 2022 and tried under Tusk's — were acquitted on 8 September 2025, and in March 2026 the prosecution withdrew its appeal after the Prosecutor General's intervention, making the acquittal final (court record; Amnesty International reporting, 2025–2026). A one-law system is visible precisely where the state's cases fail in open court.
  • The enforcement machinery has also touched his own camp. In November 2024 the Central Anti-Corruption Bureau detained the mayor of Wrocław, a politician of Tusk's own Civic Coalition camp, in the Collegium Humanum diploma scandal; he was charged (ALLEGATIONs) and released, and the case proceeded through the courts rather than being quietly closed (CBA statements and press reporting, November 2024).
  • The two most prominent convictions of PiS figures — former interior minister Mariusz Kamiński and his deputy Maciej Wąsik — rest on a court judgment, not executive action: convicted of abuse of power in December 2023 in a retrial of a 2015-era case, after the Supreme Court ruled in June 2023 that President Duda's 2015 pardon, issued before the verdict was final, could not end the proceedings. The December 2023 verdict predates any Tusk control of the prosecution's handling of that case (court record, 2015–2024).
  • Poland joined the European Public Prosecutor's Office in February 2024, submitting fraud and corruption affecting EU funds to a prosecutor outside national control (European Commission Rule of Law Report, 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The opposition's public money was withheld on a contested legal theory. After the National Electoral Commission (PKW) — reconstituted by the new Sejm majority — rejected PiS's 2023 campaign financial report on 31 August 2024 and cut its subsidy and reimbursement by millions of euros, a Supreme Court chamber overturned the PKW decision in December 2024. The finance minister nevertheless declined to restore the full payments, on the stated ground that the chamber issuing the ruling (the Chamber of Extraordinary Control, staffed by judges appointed under PiS) is not a lawful court — a position the government maintains and PiS and its allies dispute. The practical effect is documented and undisputed: the largest opposition party fought the 2025 presidential election with a substantial part of its statutory funding withheld, despite a court ruling in its favor (PKW decisions, 2024; Supreme Court ruling, December 2024; finance ministry statements, 2025; the dispute was serious enough to draw a May 2025 letter of concern from U.S. House Foreign Affairs Committee members to the European Commission).
  • The prosecution's change of leadership was executed by interpretation rather than by the statutory procedure (see Test 2): on 12 January 2024 the Justice Minister declared National Prosecutor Dariusz Barski's 2022 reinstatement from retirement ineffective and removed him without the written presidential consent the statute requires for dismissal, then installed a successor — first an "acting" appointee, then Dariusz Korneluk in March 2024 — without the presidential opinion the statute requires for appointment. The Constitutional Tribunal and a Supreme Court chamber later held the removal unlawful; the government, disputing those bodies' composition, did not reverse it (Justice Ministry statements, January–March 2024; tribunal and Supreme Court rulings, 2024; the legal dispute is documented in detail by both government critics and independent legal commentators, and its procedural facts are not in dispute — only their legality is, which remains CONTESTED between the institutions).
  • The flagship case against former justice minister Zbigniew Ziobro — 26 charges centered on the Justice Fund, including the purchase of Pegasus spyware allegedly used against domestic opponents (ALLEGATIONs; Ziobro denies wrongdoing and calls the case political retaliation) — has been accompanied by ministerial rhetoric that blurs the line between prosecution and politics: after a Warsaw judge lifted the European Arrest Warrant for Romanowski in late 2025, Justice Minister Waldemar Żurek publicly raised the possibility of removing that judge, remarks Ziobro cited in his January 2026 asylum statement and which rule-of-law monitors recorded with concern (parliamentary immunity vote, November 2025; Reuters, 12 January 2026; Polish press reporting, December 2025–January 2026). Ziobro himself fled the jurisdiction — Hungary granted him asylum in January 2026 (revoked by Hungary in July 2026, after which a Warsaw court finalized a pre-trial detention order; he remained abroad) — a fact pattern each side reads as proof of its own account, and which this dossier records without adopting either.
  • Accountability has been visibly slower and softer where it approaches the governing camp's own interests: the promise to depoliticize state-company boards (Test 5) was followed by appointments that the daily Rzeczpospolita counted in 2026 as 219 board members linked to the four governing parties — a newspaper's count, CONTESTED by the government, but unrebutted in its particulars.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the Constitutional Tribunal, public media, the central bank, and prosecutors keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The institutions he inherited in captured form still bite him daily, and he has not replaced their personnel with his own. The PiS-appointed central bank governor, Adam Glapiński, remains in office setting monetary policy and publicly defying the government; the government has pursued him only through the constitutional route of a State Tribunal motion (preliminary motion March 2024; a Sejm committee recommended referral on 9 October 2026 after hearing over 50 witnesses; the Sejm vote was scheduled for late November 2026, still pending at grading). The charges against Glapiński — including deficit financing in 2020–21 and violating the apoliticality requirement — are ALLEGATIONs he denies; the procedural point stands on its own shelf: the government chose the slow legal road over any administrative shortcut (Sejm committee record, 2024–2026; NBP statements).
  • Private media operate freely and the opposition's media ecosystem grew on his watch: PiS-aligned broadcasters TV Republika and wPolsce24 expanded their reach, opposition newspapers and broadcasters attack the government daily, and no private outlet has been closed. Courts have repeatedly ruled against his government — releasing Romanowski, acquitting the Hajnówka Five, blocking elements of the judicial overhaul — without documented retaliation against the deciding judges in those cases (court records, 2024–2026).
  • His government restored Poland's compliance posture toward the European courts: it recognized the primacy of EU law, committed to implementing CJEU and ECtHR judgments, and presented a rule-of-law Action Plan (February 2024) on the basis of which the European Commission closed the Article 7 procedure on 29 May 2024, ending a six-year proceeding, and unblocked EU funds (von der Leyen announced up to €137 billion in February 2024; the first recovery-fund payment followed in April 2024) (European Commission, February–May 2024).
  • The presidency — held by his opponents throughout — has functioned as a real check, which he has absorbed rather than circumvented: President Duda referred his Constitutional Tribunal and judicial-council statutes to the Tribunal (which invalidated them in 2025) and vetoed bills including the December 2023 budget-related act and the March 2024 morning-after-pill law; President Nawrocki has vetoed 48 government bills since August 2025, a record, including the February 2026 judicial-council (KRS) reform. The government's reforms died by those vetoes; it did not enact them by decree (presidential and Sejm records, 2023–2026).

Evidence against:

  • Public media were taken by force of office in his first week. On 19 December 2023 the Sejm passed a resolution "on restoring legal order" in public media; the same day Culture Minister Bartłomiej Sienkiewicz dismissed the boards of TVP, Polish Radio and the PAP news agency under commercial-code powers, disregarding a Constitutional Tribunal interim order of 14 December 2023 (which the ministry declared non-binding because of the panel's composition). TVP Info was taken off air for about a week amid physical confrontations at the broadcaster's headquarters. When President Duda vetoed the budget-related act carrying media funding, the government placed the broadcasters into liquidation (27 December 2023) — a status in which they remain, governed by liquidators, because the statutory route to reform them is veto-blocked. The Helsinki Foundation for Human Rights and other groups that had condemned PiS's TVP criticized the takeover's legality and the government's refusal to publish its legal opinions; a Warsaw court later upheld the liquidation registrations. TVP under PiS had been documented by international monitors as a government propaganda outlet — that is the context, and it is on the record — but the method used to seize it was the executive's own, and fact-checkers (Demagog) have since documented pro-government tilt in the new TVP's flagship news as well (Sejm resolution and ministry decisions, December 2023; CIVICUS Monitor; Columbia Journalism Review, 2024; Demagog monitoring, 2024).
  • The Constitutional Tribunal's rulings are not published, by his government's choice. Following PiS's own 2016 precedent — which Tusk's camp had condemned as a constitutional violation at the time — the government has withheld publication in the official journal of Tribunal rulings it deems issued by improperly composed panels; by November 2026 the number of unpublished rulings stood at 85, some affecting ordinary litigants' pension and benefit claims (Supreme Court resolution of 18 November 2026, which held Tribunal judgments binding regardless of publication; the government's non-publication practice is admitted and defended by the government itself). The CJEU held in Case C-448/23 (2025) that the Tribunal, because of the irregular 2015–2016 appointments, does not meet the requirements of an independent tribunal established by law — the government's core justification. Both facts stand: the Tribunal's composition is internationally adjudged defective, and the executive's remedy of non-publication is the same instrument whose use by PiS began the crisis.
  • The 2026 Tribunal endgame involved pressure on the institution's own leadership. After the Sejm elected eight judges to vacant Tribunal seats in 2026 (March, June, September) and President Nawrocki swore in three while refusing the oath to five, the Venice Commission stated on 13 October 2026 that all eight are full members, and the ECtHR had already issued interim measures (5 May 2026) requiring Poland not to hinder four of the March-elected judges. On 30 October 2026 one of the new judges suspended the Tribunal's PiS-appointed president and vice-president for a month, in proceedings the International Commission of Jurists criticized as lacking a fair procedure established by law and carried out with police involvement; the ICJ in the same statement condemned the president's refusal to swear the judges as violating international standards. On 18 November 2026 a 43-judge Supreme Court resolution held that only president-sworn judges can issue recognized Tribunal judgments; Justice Minister Żurek dismissed it as a "pseudo-resolution" by "neo-judges." The crisis is live and unresolved at grading; what is documented is that by late 2026 both camps were acting on the Tribunal outside ordinary legal procedure, and the government's camp was one of them (ICJ statement, November 2026; Venice Commission, October 2026; ECtHR interim measures, May 2026; Supreme Court resolution, 18 November 2026 — the status of the chambers involved is itself CONTESTED between the institutions).
  • The prosecution service's leadership change (Test 1) subordinated the office's formal independence to a ministerial legal theory, and the offices of Justice Minister and Prosecutor General remain fused in one person — a PiS-era arrangement Tusk's Action Plan promised to separate and, per the December 2025 GMF/ReThink.Democracy assessment, has not (GMF paper, December 2025).
  • Pressure points on courts and the central bank short of capture: in July 2025 Justice Minister Żurek initiated dismissal proceedings against 46 presidents and vice-presidents of courts, described by him as a mandate from Tusk to "clean up" the judiciary; the legal basis and individual grounds are CONTESTED and challenged by judicial associations (Human Rights Watch World Report 2026). In the central-bank board dispute of 2026, Tusk declined to countersign President Nawrocki's four nominees to the NBP management board (submitted February 2026, returned unsigned in May, resubmitted in October), leaving the board at risk of falling below its statutory minimum after a member's term expired on 4 November 2026 — the mirror image of the grip he protests: each side holding an appointment hostage (NBP and presidential-chancellery statements, 2026; financial press reporting, November 2026). The Sejm majority also voted in 2025 to bring the PiS-appointed National Broadcasting Council chairman before the State Tribunal, and litigation over TV Republika's terrestrial licence — granted by that council in 2024 — continued through the courts; the council and the broadcaster characterize the pressure as political, the government as accountability (Sejm and court records, 2024–2026; the licence litigation's merits remain undecided).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, markets, the EU, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The 2025 presidential defeat was absorbed, not overturned. His candidate Rafał Trzaskowski lost the 1 June 2025 runoff to Karol Nawrocki, 49.11% to 50.89% — the closest result in post-1989 Poland. Trzaskowski conceded; Tusk submitted his government to a Sejm confidence vote on 11 June 2025 and won it (243–210); Nawrocki was inaugurated on 6 August 2025 without obstruction, and the government has since legislated in cohabitation with him, absorbing 48 vetoes. The result stood because his camp let it stand (electoral commission and Sejm records, June–August 2025).
  • He corrected course on his own government's pace, in public. After the presidential loss was widely attributed in part to the slow pace of rule-of-law repair and PiS-era accountability, Tusk reshuffled a third of his cabinet on 23–24 July 2025, replacing Justice Minister Adam Bodnar — who conceded on leaving that the restoration "dreams have faded" — with the more confrontational Waldemar Żurek, and framed the change as a reset against "defeatism" (reshuffle announcements, July 2025; AP reporting, November 2025).
  • Farmers moved him, and he moved Brussels. Facing the February–March 2024 farmers' blockades (260 roads blocked; clashes outside the Sejm on 6 March), Tusk convened an agricultural summit on 29 February 2024, publicly sided with the farmers' core complaints, pressed the European Commission to suspend or withdraw Green Deal provisions burdensome to Polish agriculture and to cap Ukrainian imports, and pursued an embargo on Russian and Belarusian food products; the Commission subsequently withdrew its pesticide-reduction proposal and relaxed fallow-land requirements, and EU-level caps on sensitive Ukrainian imports were adopted. The adjustment was open and negotiated rather than repressive (Chancellery statements, February–March 2024; Reuters, February 2024; protest record).
  • When the president vetoed, he worked around lawfully or accepted the loss. After Duda vetoed the morning-after-pill law in March 2024, the health ministry achieved partial availability through a pharmacy-consultation regulation within its existing powers (May 2024) rather than defying the veto. After Nawrocki's first veto (August 2025) killed a combined wind-farm/energy-price-freeze bill, the government split the measures and passed the price freeze separately, which the president signed. The KRS and Tribunal statutes, by contrast, were simply lost to referral and veto — accepted as lost (presidential and legislative records, 2024–2026).
  • On abortion, he stated the arithmetic honestly. After the Sejm rejected decriminalization on 12 July 2024, Tusk said in August 2024 that no majority for liberalization exists in this parliament and none will until the next election — an open admission against his own promise, recorded here as candor and in Test 5 as non-delivery (statements, August 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The 2025 election result was questioned by his camp for weeks before it was fully accepted. Government figures including the Justice Minister promoted scrutiny of alleged counting irregularities (some 50,000+ electoral protests were filed; court-ordered recounts shifted roughly 18,000 votes, far short of the margin); the Supreme Court validated the election on 1 July 2025, with the government simultaneously disputing the legitimacy of the validating chamber. On 25 July 2025 Sejm Speaker Szymon Hołownia — a coalition partner — stated publicly that persons associated with the coalition had suggested to him that the president-elect should not be inaugurated; Tusk continued to voice doubts about the process through July before the matter closed with the 6 August inauguration. The outcome was respected; the weeks of doubt are also the record (Supreme Court validation, 1 July 2025; Hołownia's Polsat statement, 25 July 2025; the irregularity ALLEGATIONs were examined and dismissed as outcome-irrelevant by the Court — whose chamber status the government contests, a circularity the dossier notes without resolving).
  • On the Constitutional Tribunal, every pushback has been met by doubling down: the December 2023 interim order disregarded, rulings left unpublished (85 by November 2026), the November 2026 Supreme Court resolution publicly dismissed by his justice minister as a "pseudo-resolution," and the October–November 2026 escalation inside the Tribunal (Test 2) met with government support rather than de-escalation — while a cross-party "constitutional reset" negotiation convened by the presidential chancellery in November 2026 proceeded, per reporting, without his coalition at the table (ICJ, November 2026; Polish press, November 2026).
  • On the border, documented criticism has changed nothing (Test 4): UNHCR's February 2025 warning that the asylum-suspension law breaches non-refoulement, the Helsinki Foundation's constitutional objections, and Amnesty's May 2025 statement were answered by repeated extension of the suspension through 2025 and into 2026, not by amendment (Amnesty International, 1 May 2025; HRW World Report 2026).
  • Rhetoric toward referees: Tusk and his ministers have repeatedly framed adverse rulings as the acts of "neo-judges" and illegitimate bodies — accurate as to the appointments' documented irregularity (CJEU C-448/23; ECtHR case law), and simultaneously a standing refusal to absorb any adverse decision from those bodies as binding, which is the definitional opposite of answering a wobble (government statements, 2024–2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — migrants at the Belarus border, PiS voters, farmers, women, minorities — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Ukrainians are counted in, at scale, and stayed in. Poland continues to host roughly a million Ukrainian refugees under temporary protection with access to work, healthcare and benefits, maintained by Tusk's government through successive extensions, even as he simultaneously defended Polish farmers against Ukrainian imports — holding both populations in the account at once, at real political cost with his own electorate (temporary-protection extensions, 2024–2026; UNHCR registration data).
  • Farmers were treated as constituents, not enemies: summit access within weeks of the first blockades, a prime minister publicly adopting parts of their program against his own EU camp's Green Deal, and negotiated EU-level concessions (Test 3). Their protests were policed — the 6 March 2024 Warsaw protest saw clashes and tear gas — but not criminalized as a movement, and their organizations remain in formal dialogue with the agriculture ministry (protest and summit records, 2024).
  • Women received the deliverable parts of his program that could pass: state funding for IVF restored by law in late 2023 and operating from June 2024; the "Active Parent" benefit (PLN 1,500/month) from October 2024; the widow's pension enacted and in force from January 2025; and August 2024 health-ministry guidelines clarifying that pregnancy endangering a woman's health — including mental health, on a psychiatrist's certificate — is a lawful ground for termination, an administrative widening of access within the existing statute (statutes and ministry guidelines, 2023–2025).
  • His opponents' voters kept their voice and used it to beat him: PiS's media, party structures and electorate operated freely enough to win the 2025 presidential election against his candidate — the single strongest evidence that the out-group is inside the count politically (election record, June 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Migrants at the Belarus border are counted out, by statute. The law adopted 21 February 2025 empowers the government to suspend the right to apply for international protection at the Belarus border in cases of "instrumentalization"; Tusk signed the first 60-day suspension order on 27 March 2025, and it was repeatedly extended, remaining in force through 2025 and into 2026. Amnesty International (1 May 2025) called the law a flagrant violation of international law that "effectively codifies" pushbacks; UNHCR warned it breaches non-refoulement; the Helsinki Foundation found it unconstitutional. The monitoring group We Are Monitoring recorded 1,790 pushbacks between January and July 2025 alone (HRW World Report 2026), and rights groups state the law's exemptions (children, pregnant women, medical emergencies) are not respected in practice — CONTESTED by the Border Guard, which maintains returns are lawful. Deaths in the border forests continued to be documented by humanitarian monitors during his term, and the exclusion ("buffer") zone barring journalists and civil-society monitors from the border area was maintained. The context is real and belongs on the record: the crossings are organized by the Belarusian and Russian states as a documented hybrid operation, and attempted crossings fell sharply (from ~15,500 in the first half of 2025 to a few hundred in the first half of 2026, per the Interior Ministry). The security rationale is documented; so is the exclusion.
  • The criminal process continued to reach the people who aid migrants. The Hajnówka Five prosecution — begun under PiS — was carried to trial under Tusk's prosecution service in January 2025 before ending in acquittal (September 2025; appeal withdrawn March 2026); of 41 people whose aid-related proceedings ended in 2025, 38 were acquitted or had charges dropped, per monitoring by Just Security and partner organizations — a pattern in which the process itself, years under charge, is the burden, whoever began it. One of the acquitted five faced a separate investigation opened while her trial was still running (Just Security, 2025; case records).
  • Women's central claim remains unmet: abortion law stands where the 2020 Tribunal ruling left it — lawful only for rape, incest, or threat to life or health — because the decriminalization bill failed in his own coalition's Sejm (12 July 2024, 218–215, with 24 of 29 PSL deputies and his own party's absentees supplying the margin) and the promised legalization to 12 weeks was never brought to a vote it could win. The women whose record 2023 turnout (74% of eligible women voted) powered his victory are, on this item, counted as promised and not delivered (Sejm record, 12 July 2024; Amnesty International, October 2024).
  • PiS voters' party was financially penalized on a contested ruling (Test 1): whatever the merits of the campaign-finance irregularities the PKW found (documented findings of unlawful in-kind contributions, ~$835,000, disputed by PiS), the finance ministry's refusal to restore funding after the December 2024 Supreme Court ruling meant the state's money rules bound the opposition more tightly than they bound the government's interpretation — a counting-out measured in the opposition's reduced capacity during a presidential campaign (PKW and ministry records, 2024–2025).
  • LGBT citizens' recognition remains undelivered: partnership legislation was diluted to cohabitation contracts, passed the Sejm, and was vetoed by President Nawrocki on 17 July 2026 — the veto is the president's act; the three-year delay in reaching it is the government's (legislative record, 2023–2026). The March 2025 Supreme Court ruling easing trans gender recognition was a court's act, not the government's, and is recorded as context only.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record, with dates; his own explanations on the shelf.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The EU promise was kept in full and fast. He campaigned on ending the rule-of-law war with Brussels and unblocking Poland's funds. The Action Plan was presented in February 2024; von der Leyen announced up to €137 billion unblocked on 23 February 2024; the first recovery payment arrived in April 2024; the Article 7 procedure — open since 2017 — was closed on 29 May 2024. Whatever the state of the domestic statutes (below), the external half of the signature promise was delivered on schedule (European Commission, 2024).
  • A visible share of the "100 specifics" (100 konkretów) was enacted: IVF funding restored (law late 2023, operating June 2024); the forest-logging moratorium over ~100,000 hectares of valuable forest (January 2024); the children's helpline restored; the PiS-appointed children's ombudsman replaced; an industry ministry seated in Silesia; the Active Parent benefit (October 2024); the widow's pension (in force January 2025); successive minimum-wage increases; indictments filed against foreign-ministry officials in the visa affair, as promised. Independent promise-trackers (Demagog and others) counted only a minority of the 100 fully implemented in the first hundred days — the promise's own timeframe — with the count rising thereafter; the delivered items above are documented fact, and so is the shortfall against the 100-day pledge (statute book, 2023–2025; Demagog's 100-specifics tracker).
  • On the presidency he kept the constitutional word: having promised to govern lawfully in cohabitation if voters chose otherwise, he did — accepting Duda's referrals and vetoes and Nawrocki's 48 vetoes as binding facts rather than treating the office as an obstacle to be nullified (Tests 2–3).

Evidence against:

  • The flagship tax promise was not kept. The pledge to raise the tax-free allowance to PLN 60,000 — a centerpiece of the 100 specifics — has not been enacted in three years; the finance ministry has cited affordability, and the promise has been successively deferred toward the end of the term and beyond, without formal renunciation (budget records, 2024–2026; the pledge's non-delivery is a staple of opposition and independent promise-tracking alike).
  • Abortion and partnerships, the mobilizing promises of 2023, were not delivered: legalization to 12 weeks "within 100 days" became, by Tusk's own August 2024 statement, impossible until after the next election; decriminalization failed in July 2024; partnership rights reached the president's desk only in diluted cohabitation form in 2026 and were vetoed. The coalition arithmetic and the presidential veto are documented causes; the promise was nonetheless made in unconditional terms and is graded as made (campaign program, September 2023; Sejm record; statements, August 2024).
  • Rule-of-law restoration — the promise that named his government — is half-kept at best. The statutes that would restore the judicial council, the Tribunal and the Supreme Court were passed but killed by presidential referral and veto; the separation of Justice Minister and Prosecutor General, promised in his own Action Plan, remains unimplemented (GMF, December 2025); the status of the ~2,500 judges appointed under the PiS-era council ("neo-judges") was still unresolved in law as of late 2026, with the Justice Ministry's April 2025 outline still not enacted; and the restoration's executed parts were carried out by the contested methods of Tests 1–2. His first justice minister's exit line — "all those dreams have faded away by now" (Bodnar to AP, 2025) — is the government's own shelf-marking.
  • The depoliticization promise inverted in practice: point 68 of the 100 specifics promised to dismiss all state-company board members and re-select by transparent competition on competence, "not family and party connections." Competitions were held in many companies, but Rzeczpospolita's 2026 count found 219 governing-party-linked appointees on state-company boards, and the promised statutory ban on party-linked appointments was never enacted (the count is a newspaper's and CONTESTED; the absence of the promised law is not). The related promise to slim the state also stalled: the government numbers ~109 ministers and deputy ministers, comparable to the PiS-era figure the program criticized (independent analyses, 2025–2026).
  • The accountability promise was kept in volume and missed in closure: investigations and charges against PiS-era figures are numerous and documented (the Justice Fund cases, the visa affair, the Pegasus inquiry, charges against a former prime minister over the 2020 postal-election decision — Mateusz Morawiecki, charged February 2025, ALLEGATION, denied), but three years in, almost none of the flagship cases has reached a final judgment; the two most senior targets are abroad (Romanowski in Hungary; Ziobro, whose Hungarian asylum was revoked in July 2026, outside Poland with a detention order outstanding). Tusk's own July 2025 reshuffle — sacking his justice minister for insufficient results — is the documented admission that delivery lagged the promise (Sejm and prosecution records, 2024–2026; reshuffle statements, July 2025).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Donald Tusk's file is the mirror image of the captured-state cases elsewhere in this series: he inherited institutions that had been seized by his opponents — a Constitutional Tribunal internationally adjudged not to be a lawful court, a prosecution service fused to the justice ministry, a public broadcaster that monitors had documented as a party propaganda organ, a judicial council whose appointments Europe's courts do not recognize — and he was asked by his voters to un-seize them while the presidency, the Tribunal and the council remained in the other camp's hands. The documented record of his answer splits cleanly. Where the legal road was open, he took it and it worked: the EU breach closed and €137 billion unblocked, funds and IVF and benefits delivered, a lost presidential election absorbed and its winner inaugurated, farmers negotiated with rather than dispersed, a central-bank governor pursued only through the constitution's slowest procedure. Where the legal road was blocked, he repeatedly chose the other camp's own instruments — a broadcaster seized by ministerial signature in his first week, a prosecutor removed by legal theory, a court's rulings left unpublished by the dozen, a finance ministry declining to pay the opposition what a court had awarded it — each defended, with documented basis, as the only available answer to a prior illegality, and each recorded here as what it is: the grip answered with a grip. The count is likewise double: a million Ukrainians held inside it and his opponents free enough to beat him, against a border where asylum itself now stands suspended by his signature and the women whose turnout elected him still waiting on the promise that mobilized them. He is the only ruler in this series whose failures of restraint are mostly failures to refrain from his predecessors' methods rather than inventions of his own — the tests grade the methods, not the inheritance, and on the evidence they land, all five, in the middle.
