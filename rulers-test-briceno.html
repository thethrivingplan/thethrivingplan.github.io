The Ruler's Test — John Briceño, Prime Minister of Belize

Subject: John Briceño (Johnny Briceño) — Prime Minister of Belize since 12 November 2020. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Belize's Governor-General (Dame Froyla Tzalam since May 2021) is a ceremonial head of state, while executive power — command of the government, the budget, and the security forces — sits with the prime minister, who in Briceño's case also holds the finance portfolio personally. He is graded on the seat as he has actually held it across six years and two terms. His People's United Party (PUP) won the 11 November 2020 election in a landslide (26 of 31 House seats, 59.6% of the vote) on the Plan Belize manifesto, inheriting a pandemic-collapsed economy and public debt above 100 percent of GDP. He was re-elected on 12 March 2025 — the PUP holding all 26 seats with 67.91% of the vote (85,096 votes; turnout 64.97%) against a United Democratic Party (UDP) split into two rival factions (Tracy Panton's, three seats; Moses "Shyne" Barrow's, two — Barrow lost his own seat, and Panton became Belize's first female Leader of the Opposition) — and sworn in for a second term on 13 March 2025. Where the record of the seat's first term bears directly on a test, it is graded as part of the tenure; the second term's own manifesto, Plan Belize 2.0 (launched February 2025), is graded in Test 5. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The accountability machinery of his government has been aimed, publicly and on the record, at the other side's tenure through ordinary legal process. In August 2023 his government sued former UDP Lands Minister Hugo Patt, his former chief executive, the former Lands Commissioner, and three private companies, alleging that on the eve of the November 2020 election the companies had paid some $59,000 for lands the government had acquired for $6.5 million (court filing as reported, August 2023). The Senate Special Select Committee inquired in public session (December 2023 – 2024) into the Portico "Definitive Agreement" — a cruise-port deal signed on 1 October 2020 by then-Minister Erwin Contreras, which former Barrow Cabinet members testified had never received Cabinet approval; Briceño had already repudiated the agreement theatrically, tearing a copy in Parliament on 16 June 2023. The inquiry aired, under oath, the sale of 50.1-acre coastal parcels near the project site at $5,300 each against a Lands Department valuation of some $4,000 per acre (Senate hearing, 28 February 2024).
  • In 2026 the machinery turned inward, at least at the level of audit. When leaked SmartStream records purported to show hundreds of Ministry of Defence payments — many structured just under the $10,000 oversight threshold — flowing to businesses connected to five siblings of Oscar Mira, a PUP minister who had served in that ministry, Briceño ordered the Auditor General in: "when we saw these continuous under ten thousand dollar payments, it raises questions and raises an alarm and that is why I sent the Financial Secretary to send the auditor general to take a look at what is going on" (Briceño, 23 June 2026). Mira stepped back from Cabinet, as did Defence Minister Florencio Marin Jr., who requested a 90-day leave so the audit could proceed "without any perceived interference" (Greater Belize reporting, June–July 2026). The payments themselves, and any wrongdoing by Mira or anyone else, remain ALLEGATIONs: Mira denies wrongdoing, says he is confident the audit will clear his name, and no finding had been published at grading.
  • His own camp has not been immune from process at the ministerial level. In August 2023 Briceño accepted a leave of absence from Minister Andre Perez after a sexual-harassment ALLEGATION by a female attorney (the allegation is recorded as an allegation; Perez counter-alleged extortion and a consensual relationship, and no criminal charge is documented). The file was investigated under the Attorney General's ministry, and Perez stayed out of Cabinet for over seven months (Government Press Office, 21 August 2023; Greater Belize, March 2024).
  • The statutory toolkit was strengthened on his watch: the Civil Asset Recovery and Unexplained Wealth Act (July 2023) made the Financial Intelligence Unit a civil recovery authority, and of 17 cases the FIU investigated in 2025 it secured one conviction with asset forfeiture in a theft and money-laundering case involving over $500,000 (US State Department, 2026 Investment Climate Statement on Belize). Belize completed the second cycle of its UNCAC implementation review in August 2025.

Evidence against:

  • Six years in, the one-law record has audits, inquiries, and civil suits — but no completed prosecution of a senior political figure of either party. The US State Department's 2026 assessment is blunt: the law "provides criminal penalties for corruption by public officials, but the government did not always implement the law effectively," adherence to asset-declaration law "remains negligible and enforcement inadequate," and law enforcement "often failed to adequately investigate or prosecute corruption-related crimes." Belize's Corruption Perceptions Index score in the 2025 index is 36 of 100 (104th of 182) (Transparency International, CPI 2025).
  • The Perez file closed without a published accounting. Perez was reinstated in March 2024 as Minister of Blue Economy and Disaster Risk Management (he did not recover the Civil Aviation portfolio), and requests for the investigation's outcome were referred by the Attorney General to the Office of the Prime Minister; no findings were published in the record assembled here. The opposition had objected from the start that the investigator — the Attorney General, a Cabinet colleague — made the process "a farce" and demanded a criminal investigation instead (Opposition Leader Shyne Barrow, August 2023). Whether or not the investigation was sound, a process whose result is never shown cannot demonstrate one law.
  • The BTL–Speednet transaction is the second term's central one-law problem, because the benefit ran toward insiders and the process ran over every referee until the street reversed it. Belize Telemedia (BTL) — roughly 92 percent owned by the state, the Social Security Board, and the Central Bank — agreed in January 2026 to buy Speednet ("Smart"), its only full-licence competitor, for BZ$80 million (BZ$10 million cash plus BZ$70 million in BTL loan notes). The sellers were the Waterloo Group Charitable Trust (77.5 percent, associated with Lord Michael Ashcroft) and, for the remaining shares, Jaime and Renan Briceño — relatives of the Prime Minister (deal terms disclosed January 2026; regulator's files as compiled by Belize Pulse, September 2026). The National Trade Union Congress voted against the deal in January; the Chamber of Commerce demanded full disclosure and an independent valuation; the UDP called it "one of the most blatantly corrupt and reckless deals ever foisted upon the Belizean people" — an opposition characterization, recorded as an ALLEGATION-level claim, not a finding. BTL's government-influenced board voted 8–2 to proceed on 4 August 2026 anyway. Only then did Cabinet reject the acquisition (13 August 2026), and BTL withdrew on 24 August 2026. The regulator, the Public Utilities Commission, never ruled. A law that reaches the insiders' transaction only after unions, the chamber, independent senators, and protesters have all pushed is not operating at the source.
  • The same pattern — formal compliance, withheld substance — marks the transparency file. In 2025 the Attorney General's Ministry refused a freedom-of-information request for government-paid legal fees (fees incurred, in part, in the redistricting litigation, Test 2); the Ombudsman directed partial release; the Attorney General appealed (National Perspective, February 2026 — an opposition-leaning outlet, cited for the procedural facts, which are not disputed). Belize acceded to UNCAC in 2016, under the previous government; a decade on, implementation remains incomplete, the Protected Disclosures (whistleblower) Bill is still not law, and the government's own Director of Good Governance attributes the gap to drafting capacity and funding while conceding the country "could still be years away" (Greater Belize, 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral bodies, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The judiciary he inherited has ruled against his government at the highest level, and the rulings stood. On 23 March 2026 the Caribbean Court of Justice (CCJ) unanimously reinstated two of activist Jeremy Enriquez's appeals in the redistricting case, which the Belize Court of Appeal had struck out on procedural technicalities advanced by the Attorney General and the Elections and Boundaries Commission; the CCJ held that procedural issues must not block access to justice and overturned a wasted-costs order made personally against Enriquez's counsel, finding it unjustified (CCJ, March 2026; reasons July 2026). The government did not defy the ruling: Briceño publicly committed to completing redistricting by the end of 2026 (Amandala, 25 March 2026). In the Maya land case the CCJ ended its decade of monitoring on 30 October 2025 satisfied that "the core elements of implementation have taken root," and in July 2026 it dismissed the Maya Leaders Alliance's clarification application — a ruling for the government's position — through the same open process (CCJ media release, October 2026).
  • The chief justice he appointed was chosen by an open, advertised process: the post was advertised regionally, eight applications were received, a Bar Association panel interviewed and vetted, and the recommended candidate — Justice Louise Blenman, an Eastern Caribbean appellate judge and Belize's first female chief justice — was appointed after consultation with the Leader of the Opposition, under a restructured Senior Courts Act (Government Press Office, June 2022; sworn in 2 September 2022). Under her tenure the Senior Courts report measurable institutional gains: criminal-case backlog cut from roughly ten years to one-to-two years, civil backlog's oldest matters from nine years to three (Senior Courts of Belize, 2025 annual summary). The US State Department records that "the judiciary remains independent," while noting delays (2026 Investment Climate Statement).
  • The media operate against him daily and unpunished. Belize stood 47th of 180 in RSF's 2025 World Press Freedom Index (68.32), and its main outlets — Channel 5, Channel 7, Amandala, Love FM — cover his government's scandals, including the Mira audit and the Speednet deal, in depth and in the open. The 2025 general election was administered by the Elections and Boundaries Commission, its result accepted across the map, and the opposition — including a first female Leader of the Opposition — seated without dispute.
  • The Central Bank's normalization proceeded on institutional rails: the IMF records the central bank reducing its large stock of government securities and rebuilding international reserves toward four months of imports, alongside primary fiscal surpluses — the fiscal-monetary grip of the 2020 crisis loosening rather than tightening (IMF, 2025 Article IV concluding statement).

Evidence against:

  • The electoral map — the master referee of the whole system — has now been kept in a state the state's own side conceded was unconstitutional through two of his elections. In the 2019 Belize Peace Movement case the Attorney General conceded the boundaries in Schedule 1 of the Representation of the People Act breach section 90 of the Constitution, which requires divisions "as nearly equal as may be"; the largest divisions carry several times the voters of the smallest. The Elections and Boundaries Commission delivered redistricting proposals in July 2023. The governing majority introduced the implementing bill in May 2024, sent it to committee, and never brought it back — then held the March 2025 election on the unreformed map, with Briceño saying redistricting was by then "too late" (Amandala and Greater Belize, February–March 2025). In court, the state's posture was procedural suffocation: the claim was dismissed at first instance and the appeals struck out on technicalities the CCJ later held were wrong. The referee proposed; the player holding the majority declined the new lines and defended the old ones on procedure. That is a grip on the one institution that decides all the others.
  • The conduct of the redistricting litigation generated a judicial-integrity file that remains contested and unresolved. Enriquez applied for Justice Tawanda Hondora's recusal, alleging bias — his counsel Peter Knox KC told the CCJ that a hearing transcript records the judge saying, in substance, "I have not dismissed it as yet. I am just going through the motion, but I will clamp down on him when I get back." That is the claimant's ALLEGATION, sourced to a transcript passage his side cites; a complaint was filed with the Judicial and Legal Services Commission. What is not contested is the CCJ's own finding that the judge failed to follow proper procedure before making the personal costs order against counsel (CCJ reasons, July 2026). The dossier records the episode as an open integrity question hanging over a case the government had every institutional reason to slow.
  • Oversight visibility was narrowed in the middle of a live audit of his own government. During the Mira investigation, the Public Service Union reported that finance officers across the service had their SmartStream access restricted to their own ministry's transactions — removing exactly the cross-ministry visibility needed to detect a pattern of split payments — "introduced without notice, without consultation, and without any legal basis being disclosed, in the middle of a national procurement scandal." The PSU's characterization of motive ("It is a curtain") is the union's; the restriction itself and its timing are the union's documented complaint, and no public government rebuttal appears in the record assembled here (Greater Belize, 2026).
  • The telecom regulator's independence is contested from two directions. The PUC refused Starlink an internet licence in 2025 after objections from both incumbent carriers, a ministry, BTL's union, and the Social Security Board — whose letter stated that a Starlink licence "would lead to a reduction in investment income to the SSB" (PUC record, as compiled by Belize Pulse, September 2026) — and then never ruled on the BTL–Speednet merger at all, leaving the decision to Cabinet politics. After the deal collapsed, the union movement's reform demands included restructuring the PUC so that four of seven commissioners are nominated by the social partners — a demand that exists because the commission's independence is not currently credited by the institutions it regulates (NTUCB, August 2026). RSF also records structural pressure on media: advertising revenue dependence on the government of the day, and intimidation of critical journalists through threats of costly defamation suits; RSF condemned a police assault on journalist Louis "Ljay" Wade in March 2025 and called for an independent investigation. Belize slipped to 66th in the 2026 index (61.66).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Speednet reversal, however late (Test 1), was executed openly and against his own side's momentum: Briceño ordered a pause in the acquisition on 6 February 2026, in the House, "to ensure that the proper regulatory process is followed"; when BTL's board voted to proceed regardless, Cabinet publicly rejected the deal on 13 August 2026 on the stated ground that the government, the SSB, and the Belizean people are BTL's majority owners, and the withdrawal followed within eleven days (Amandala, February 2026; Government Press Office, 13 August 2026). The wobble was answered with a full stop, in public, at political cost.
  • The 2025 public-sector wage dispute was settled by negotiation under real pressure — marches from 9 May, sick-outs, road blockades in June — through three successive government counterproposals, ending in a deal all three unions voted to accept (BNTU: 75.04 percent in favor): an 8.5 percent increase phased 4.5 percent (October 2025) and 4 percent (October 2026), with the two frozen increments restored in April 2026 and April 2027 (Amandala and Greater Belize, June–July 2025). The agreed increases were then budgeted and paid as agreed — Briceño's March 2026 budget reports the 4.5 percent delivered and the 4 percent provided for (Budget Presentation, 10 March 2026).
  • In the sugar industry he intervened personally and produced agreement: after months of government involvement, Briceño locked BSI and the Belize Sugar Cane Farmers Association in a room in January 2024 — "I sanctioned both parties and their lawyers to sit in a room and not to leave until we have an agreement" — and a two-year agreement ended the impasse and the blockades (Caribbean Today/CMC, 10 January 2024). His government subsequently appointed a Commission of Inquiry into the industry's fairness claims, whose report was delivered in February 2025.
  • His fiscal wobble-answering has a track record of early concession when the numbers moved: the 10 percent public-sector salary cut of June 2021 was legislated for three years and restored in full on 1 July 2022 — two years early — when fiscal targets were met ahead of schedule (Public Sector Emoluments and Allowances Reduction (Amendment) Act, No. 13 of 2022; Amandala, July 2022). In November 2026, facing the "Enough is Enough" cost-of-living protests, his government announced a relief package in the Assembly — the residential electricity GST-free threshold doubled to $200, continued fuel-tax cuts, some $50 million in spending cuts including a 50 percent cut to ministerial allowances — and Briceño defended it on the record while affirming the right to protest (Greater Belize, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the two files where the pushback came from courts and from Indigenous Belizeans rather than from unions and markets, the pattern is delay until compulsion. Redistricting: a conceded constitutional breach (2019), a commission report (2023), a shelved bill (2024), an election held on the old map (2025), a first-instance dismissal, appeals struck out on the state's technicalities — and only after the CCJ reversed those strike-outs in March 2026 did completion acquire a date (end of 2026), with Briceño simultaneously calling the ruling "moot"/"academic" because the government was "already working on it" (Amandala, March 2026). Adjustment arrived; it arrived after six years and two courts.
  • The Maya land file shows the same shape over a longer arc. Eleven years after the 2015 CCJ Consent Order, the implementing legislation existed only as a draft before a review panel (October 2025), five Toledo villages had rejected the government's December 2023 draft policy, and at the CCJ's final monitoring hearing the Maya side's counsel, Leslie Mendez, told the court the Maya had acted in "the highest degree of good faith" and met "a recalcitrant government who dragged us through this long process, consistently acting in bad faith." That is the Maya Leaders Alliance's characterization, contested by the government's counsel, who argued the delay reflects "the fundamental difficulties" of balancing competing land interests and called the draft "a good faith effort" (Greater Belize, 31 October 2025). The shelf records both; the eleven years are not contested.
  • His first instinct under criticism is sometimes dismissal rather than adjustment. When the BSCFA wrote in March 2025 questioning his removal of the agriculture minister from the sugar portfolio, Briceño answered publicly: "they are not the Prime Minister. If Fred Ortega or anyone of them wants to be prime minister, put their name on a ballot" — before a meeting was arranged the following week (Amandala, April 2025). Of the November 2026 protesters he said the movement is "obviously... politically driven," while conceding the right to demonstrate (Greater Belize, November 2026).
  • On crime, the chosen answer to each surge has been the same instrument — a state of emergency with detention powers — while the outcome measure has reversed. Emergencies were declared repeatedly across the tenure, including in 2024 and twice in 2026, the latest on 31 October 2026 covering parts of Belize City, Ladyville, Cotton Tree, and Unitedville, with police files prepared on approximately 60 people (Greater Belize and Government of Canada travel advisory, November 2026). Murders, after years of decline (91 in 2025, ~21.8 per 100,000, Belize Crime Observatory), rose in 2026: 58 in the first half against 41 in the same period of 2025 (Crime Observatory), and 7News' independent count reached 94 by the end of October — a media count, kept separate from the official series. Families of some October 2026 detainees allege innocent people were swept up and have threatened court action — an ALLEGATION the police answer by pointing to intelligence-led files (Greater Belize, November 2026). Repeating the instrument while the count climbs is doubling down, whatever the instrument's constitutional basis.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Maya, the Garifuna, the opposition's voters, the poor of the Southside, and those who share the streets — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The headline social accounting moved toward everyone, measurably. Multidimensional poverty fell from 36 percent in 2021 to the low-to-mid twenties by 2024 — 22.1 percent on the Statistical Institute of Belize measure reported by the IMF, 26.4 percent in the World Bank's outlook, the difference being measure and vintage (IMF, 2025 Article IV; World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, 2025). Unemployment fell from 14 percent in 2020 to 3.0 percent in April 2024 and 2.1 percent in 2025, with the government reporting 2.0 percent in 2026 (IMF; Budget Presentation, March 2026). The homicide rate fell from an average 37.2 per 100,000 in 2016–19 to 21.2 in 2023 (IMF, 2024 Article IV).
  • The flagship programs were built universal rather than clientelist in design. National Health Insurance — a pilot confined to Belize City's Southside since 2001 — was rolled out district by district until the final region (Cayo) signed on in February 2026, completing nationwide coverage with a projected 325,000 users and a $57 million allocation (Greater Belize, February–March 2026). The Education Upliftment Project grew from 947 students at four schools in 2022 to more than 14,000 students at 27 secondary schools in 2026/27, with all students at government secondary schools attending free and 83 percent of secondary students receiving some assistance; the National Healthy Start Feeding Program serves some 20,000 students daily (Budget Presentation, March 2026; Ministry of Education, April 2026). The minimum wage rose from $3.30 to $5.00 (January 2023), and the income-tax threshold was raised to $29,000 in the 2026 relief package.
  • Representation's symbols moved too: Briceño advised the appointment of Dame Froyla Tzalam, a Mopan Maya woman, as the first Indigenous Governor-General (May 2021), and his second-term House seated the country's first female Leader of the Opposition.

Evidence against:

  • The Maya — the community with the strongest legal paper in the country — remain the worst counted. Multidimensional poverty among Belize's Maya population reached 60.2 percent in 2024, and Toledo district, the Maya heartland, recorded the country's highest rate at 57.5 percent (World Bank, 2025). Eleven years after the CCJ's Consent Order recognized Maya customary land tenure as constitutionally protected property, no implementing law has been passed; the government's draft proposes measuring customary land by population — a maximum of five acres per person, per the Maya Leaders Alliance's account of the government's position (spokesperson Cristina Coc, 2026) — which the Alliance rejects as the state "imagining" what customary tenure should be rather than recognizing the tenure that exists. The CCJ's July 2026 dismissal of the clarification application turned on the bill still being a draft; if it passes in a form the Maya say violates their rights, a fresh constitutional challenge is the remedy the court left open (CCJ, [2026] CCJ 13 (AJ) BZ).
  • The Garifuna have no equivalent paper and are asking not to need it. In 2025–26 a government-appointed commission moved to delineate village boundaries on the Placencia Peninsula (Hopkins/Sittee River; Placencia/Seine Bight). The National Garifuna Council insists the boundary question cannot be separated from traditional Garifuna territory at a moment when, in its president Alex Nolberto's words, traditional lands are slipping into foreign hands: "they are trying to separate the issues of the territorial, traditional Garifuna territory and this village boundary conversation, and I don't see how you can separate the two" (Greater Belize, 2026). No Garifuna land-protection framework comparable even to the Maya's unfinished one exists in the record.
  • The security burden is distributed onto the poorest streets. The states of emergency fall overwhelmingly on Belize City's Southside and its satellite villages — the same communities the NHI pilot was invented for — with detention powers exercised over dozens of mostly young men at a time, and families alleging in October 2026 that some detainees are innocent (an ALLEGATION, contested by the police's intelligence claim). Canada's travel advisory singles out Southside Belize City for avoidance. A count that includes the Southside in the health registry and the emergency proclamation in the same year is counting it two different ways.
  • The cost-of-living file is where the counted say they are not counted. The November 2026 "Enough is Enough" movement — union-led, built around eleven good-governance demands submitted to the government in August 2026 and a cost-of-living case — put it in the words of union senator Glenfield Dennison: "the working-class people of Belize feel like they are one paycheck away from poverty" (Greater Belize, November 2026). The PSU president's example — a household electricity bill doubling from $76 to $164, untouched by a GST threshold — is an anecdote, recorded as the protesters' evidence, not a statistic; the statistic that frames it is that poverty, even on the government's preferred measure, still covers more than a fifth of the country after six years of growth.
  • Women's presence in the counted chamber remains thin: four of the 31 elected members of the House are women (IPU Parline, 2025).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Manifesto pledges and public statements vs. the record since 12 November 2020, with dates; Plan Belize (2020) first, then Plan Belize 2.0 (2025).

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The $5 minimum wage — kept, on a date. Plan Belize promised a $5.00 minimum wage; by Statutory Instruments No. 169 and 170 of 2022 it took effect for all categories of workers on 1 January 2023, up from $3.30 (Government Press Office, 22 December 2022).
  • The salary restoration — kept early. The 10 percent public-sector salary cut (June 2021) was presented as a three-year sacrifice to be restored when fiscal targets were met; it was restored in full on 1 July 2022, two years ahead of schedule, and increments were unfrozen from 1 April 2023 (Amendment Act No. 13 of 2022; Cabinet Brief, 9 March 2023). The 2025 negotiated settlement — 8.5 percent plus the two lost increments on stated dates — was paid and budgeted as agreed (Test 3).
  • NHI nationwide — kept, completing in the second term. Plan Belize promised to revitalize and expand National Health Insurance; the rollout finished with the Cayo contracts of 19 February 2026 — later than first-term rhetoric implied, and delivered (Greater Belize, February 2026).
  • The debt word — kept in the numbers. The 2020 government inherited public debt of 103.3 percent of GDP; through the 2021 Blue Bond debt-for-marine-protection swap, a negotiated Petrocaribe discount, primary surpluses, and growth, it fell to 61.1 percent by end-2024 (IMF, 2025 Article IV). Real GDP grew a cumulative 27.6 percent in 2021–23 and 8.1 percent in 2024 (IMF).
  • Free education — expanding as pledged. The Upliftment Project's trajectory (947 students in 2022 to 14,000-plus in 2026/27, all government secondary schools free) is the Plan Belize education commitment in visible motion (Budget Presentation, March 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Redistricting — the clearest broken word of the tenure, because it was owed to the Constitution and not only to voters. The PUP in opposition supported the Peace Movement's case; in government it accepted the 2019 concession that the boundaries are unconstitutional and received the Commission's proposals in July 2023. The implementing bill was tabled in May 2024 and never debated; the March 2025 election was held on the unreformed map; the promise is now re-dated to "before the end of 2026" (Briceño, March 2026) and was unfulfilled at grading, eleven months after the election it was meant to precede and seven years after the concession. Every element of this paragraph is documented in Test 2; it is recorded here because Test 5 is where a re-dated constitutional promise lands.
  • The anti-corruption word — framework delivered, implementation deferred. Plan Belize's good-governance chapter promised to give the anti-corruption framework effect; Belize's UNCAC accession (2016) predates him, but full implementation was his pledge to complete. Six years on: the second review cycle is done (August 2025), the unexplained-wealth act exists (2023), and the whistleblower law, campaign-finance transparency, and verified asset declarations do not; his own good-governance director says full implementation could still be "years away" (Greater Belize, 2026). The NTUCB's eleven good-governance demands of August 2026 — the unions' list of the undelivered items — were still unanswered at grading, and the government's mid-audit SmartStream restriction (Test 2) moved the transparency record backward in the same season.
  • Plan Belize 2.0's dated items are mostly still ahead of the record. The $6.00 minimum wage, promised in February 2025, remained a proposal at grading, with the wage still $5.00 (wage reviews, 2026). The 500 starter homes on a $10 million budget and the permanent twice-yearly GST-free weekends were announced as commitments; the documented record at grading shows GST-free weekends granted episodically (including in the November 2026 relief package) rather than enacted as the promised permanent law. These are eighteen months old, not six years; they are recorded as unkept-to-date, with the term left to run.
  • The top of the service was paid first. While increments for the service at large stayed frozen, chief executive officers' allowances were doubled ($750 to $1,500 monthly) from 1 July 2022 as a "reinstatement," and during the 2025 wage dispute it emerged that CEOs and the Cabinet Secretary had received a 15 percent salary increase ($76,000 to $88,000) while the unions' 8.5 percent was still being negotiated (Amandala, October 2022; Greater Belize, 2025). The amounts are small against the wage bill; the sequence is the evidence — the word about shared sacrifice was kept for the many on schedule and for the few ahead of it.
  • The long target recorded, not graded: Plan Belize 2.0 pledges to "defend our territory and we will win at the I.C.J." Guatemala's territorial claim, submitted to the International Court of Justice in 2019, remained undecided at grading; the pledge is noted here so the record can grade it when the Court rules.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

John Briceño is the first prime minister in this series to be graded across two full electoral mandates, and the file is genuinely two-sided at every test — which is itself the finding. The economic word was kept at a scale few in the series match: debt cut from 103 to 61 percent of GDP, a minimum wage delivered on its date, a salary cut restored two years early, poverty down by a third, unemployment at historic lows, health insurance and free secondary education universalized in fact and not only in speeches. The same tenure holds the system's master referee — the electoral map — in a condition his own Attorney General conceded was unconstitutional, through two elections, defended in court on procedural technicalities until the Caribbean Court of Justice reversed them; and it produced, in the BTL–Speednet deal, a transaction whose benefits ran toward the Prime Minister's own relatives and a foreign magnate, advanced by a state company's board over the objections of unions, the chamber, and the opposition, and stopped only when Cabinet yielded to the street. The wobble record splits the same way: unions, cane farmers, and bondholders pushed and were answered openly; courts and Maya villages pushed and were answered in years. The counts split too — the poor are counted in the poverty statistics and the NHI registry, and the Maya, whose poverty rate is 60 percent and whose eleven-year-old consent order still has no law behind it, are counted last. No institution was captured on this watch: the courts rule against him and are obeyed, the press is free and hostile, the central bank normalized. But the grades are all MIXED because the distance between the framework and its enforcement — UNCAC acceded but not implemented, an audit ordered but not yet reported, a bill tabled but never debated, an investigation concluded but never published — is the most consistent quantity in the file. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether redistricting is actually completed by his end-of-2026 date, whether the Auditor General's Mira report is published and what follows it, whether the Maya land bill reaches the Assembly in a form its beneficiaries recognize, and whether Plan Belize 2.0's $6 wage and 500 homes exist by the next election.
