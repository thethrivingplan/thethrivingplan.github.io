The Ruler's Test — Min Aung Hlaing, Military Seat of Myanmar (President since 10 April 2026)

Subject: Senior General (ret.) Min Aung Hlaing — and the military leadership he heads — graded as the seat that has held executive power in fact in Myanmar continuously since the coup of 1 February 2021. This dossier grades power as held, not recognition: who actually commands the government, the budget, and the armed forces, whatever formal dress that power currently wears. The formal dress has changed three times on his watch, and each change is recorded exactly. (1) From 2 February 2021 to 31 July 2025 he ruled as Chairman of the State Administration Council (SAC), and from 1 August 2021 also as Prime Minister of a provisional government; sovereign power had been formally transferred to him as Commander-in-Chief by Acting President Myint Swe on the day of the coup, when a one-year state of emergency — later extended seven times — was declared (Wikipedia, State Administration Council; ISP-Myanmar Stakeholder Tracker No. 2, August 2025). (2) On 31 July 2025 the state of emergency was ended, the SAC was dissolved, and its powers passed to the National Defence and Security Council (NDSC) and to a new State Security and Peace Commission (SSPC), chaired by Min Aung Hlaing himself, with a new Union Government headed by former general Nyo Saw as prime minister (NDSC Order No. 4/2025; US-ASEAN Business Council election milestones, 2025; The Sentry / Justice For Myanmar, December 2025). Myint Swe, who had taken medical leave on 22 July 2024 and transferred his presidential duties on an acting basis to Min Aung Hlaing, died on 7 August 2025, after which Min Aung Hlaing held the acting presidency in his own right (state media via Madhyamam, 7 August 2025). (3) After the phased general election of 28 December 2025, 11 January 2026, and 25 January 2026, Min Aung Hlaing stepped down as Commander-in-Chief on 30 March 2026 — handing the post to his former intelligence chief and loyalist, General Ye Win Oo — was elected President by the Presidential Electoral College on 3 April 2026 with 429 of 584 votes cast, and was sworn in on 10 April 2026 at the head of a 34-member Union Government, with Nyo Saw as First Vice President and Nan Ni Ni Aye as Second Vice President (Reuters, 3 April 2026; Amnesty International, 3 April 2026; President's Office Order 1/2026). As of November 2026 that is the current formal position: President Min Aung Hlaing, simultaneously chairman of the reconstituted NDSC, head of the Union Government, and appointer of the Union Consultative Council created by law on 3 February 2026 (ISEAS Perspective 2026/75, 22 October 2026). The parallel National Unity Government (NUG) — formed in April 2021 by lawmakers elected in 2020, aligned with People's Defence Forces and, since 30 March 2026, joined with the Kachin Independence Organisation, the Karen National Union, the Chin National Front, and the Karenni National Progressive Party in the Steering Council for the Emergence of a Federal Democratic Union (East Asia Forum, 12 November 2026) — is noted where it bears on the tests but is NOT graded as the seat: it does not hold the capital, the central state apparatus, or the seat this dossier grades. Its existence, and the territory the resistance holds, are themselves evidence on several tests. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record since 1 February 2021. Method: The ethic of the Test is printed here as it stands on the Test card: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. The mandate rule is applied: governing on a platform voters elected is never itself a failure, and carrying out such a platform is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5 — in this case the seat holds no electoral mandate from any election the record shows to have been free, a fact graded under Tests 1, 2, and 5 rather than presumed. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Some instruments of the seat are written as general laws and have, at points, been applied against its own apparatus. The People's Military Service Law activated on 10 February 2024 is on its face universal — two years' service for men aged 18–35 and women aged 18–27 (junta announcement via Zaw Min Tun, February 2024). In November 2026 the Ministry of Defence announced legal action against nearly 200 people inside the conscription system itself — 102 offenders in financial-extortion cases and 97 accused of unauthorised forcible conscription, as of end-October 2026 — and the Central Committee for Conscription recorded 182 extortion complaints with action completed in 91 (Ministry of Defence statement in state media, 22 November 2026, via Asia Daily). Vice Senior General Soe Win publicly acknowledged corruption and failures by recruitment committees (Ministry of Information statement, January 2026).
  • Prisoner amnesties have been issued in general form, not only for allies: on 26 November 2025, 8,865 people were pardoned or had sentences commuted, 3,085 of them convicted under Section 505A — the incitement provision used against the coup's critics (UEC/junta announcements, November 2025); on 4 December 2025 six jailed journalists were among more than 3,800 political prisoners released in a pre-election amnesty (Committee to Protect Journalists, 4 December 2025); on 17 April 2026 former President Win Myint was pardoned and released; on 30 April 2026 a one-sixth sentence reduction applied to all remaining prisoners, including Aung San Suu Kyi (Reuters, 17 April 2026; CNN, 30 April 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The seat's founding act was itself a second law. The military annulled the result of the November 2020 general election — an election the National League for Democracy (NLD) won — detained President Win Myint and State Counsellor Aung San Suu Kyi on 1 February 2021, and ruled by emergency decree; no court finding of the alleged election fraud at a scale that would void the result was produced before the seizure, and the fraud claim remains the military's own assertion, disputed by the NLD, by the NUG, and by the election observers whose 2020 assessments are on the public record (contemporaneous reporting, February 2021; FIDH/ALTSEAN-Burma briefing, 6 February 2026). One law for the winner of an election, another for the general who lost it, is the definition this test grades.
  • The legal order since the coup runs one way. Aung San Suu Kyi was convicted in closed proceedings on charges including incitement, corruption, election fraud, and breaching state secrets, reaching a cumulative 33-year sentence by late 2022 — reduced to 27 years, then in April 2026 to a remaining 18 years, served from 30 April 2026 as house arrest at an undisclosed residence; her allies and rights groups describe the charges as politically motivated, a characterization recorded here as their position, while the verdicts themselves, delivered by courts operating under the seat (Test 2), are documented fact (CNN, 30 April 2026; Reuters, April 2026). In March 2023 the Union Election Commission dissolved the NLD for declining to re-register under the junta's January 2023 party law, which also barred persons convicted of a crime — a category the seat's own courts had just manufactured for the elected leadership (UEC, March 2023; election-law reporting, January 2023).
  • The constitution the seat invokes reserves power to itself by design: 25% of seats in both houses of parliament are appointed by the Commander-in-Chief without any vote, and the ministries of Defence, Home Affairs, and Border Affairs are military-nominated under the 2008 Constitution — so in the 2025–26 parliament the military-backed Union Solidarity and Development Party's 231 of 263 contested lower-house seats and 108 upper-house seats (UEC results announced 3 February 2026, via Burma News International) sit on top of 166 unelected military seats, a combined bloc of roughly 86% (Burma Insight analysis, March 2026; Global Asia Forum, 2026).
  • Impunity at the source is documented at the highest level. On 27 November 2024 the Prosecutor of the International Criminal Court applied for an arrest warrant for Min Aung Hlaing, stating there are reasonable grounds to believe he bears criminal responsibility for the crimes against humanity of deportation and persecution of the Rohingya, committed August–December 2017 in Myanmar and in part in Bangladesh (ICC Prosecutor Karim Khan, statement, 27 November 2024; UN News, 27 November 2024). This is an application, not a warrant and not a verdict — recorded as ALLEGATION at the highest evidentiary grade the international system issues short of trial — and as of grading no issuance decision appears in the record assembled here. The junta's response was jurisdictional, not evidentiary: "Myanmar is not a member state of the ICC and therefore does not recognize its statement" (junta statement via Radio Free Asia, 27 November 2024). No domestic proceeding against any senior officer for the 2017 operations, or for any post-coup operation, appears in the record. Domestically, the asymmetry is statistical: the Assistance Association for Political Prisoners' figures, as carried by OHCHR, record a minimum of 8,075 civilians killed by the military and 31,101 arrested on political grounds between February 2021 and 31 May 2026 (OHCHR factsheet, September 2026) — against zero documented prosecutions of the forces that did the killing.
  • The conscription law's universality fails in its operation, on the seat's own admissions and on monitors' records: families with money pay administrators to spare their sons and substitutes are supplied in their place, with substitute prices reported at 10–30 million kyat — a burden falling by design on households too poor to pay (residents' accounts, Laputta Township, November 2026, via Asia Daily; Ministry of Defence's own extortion figures, November 2026). Rohingya men and boys — denied citizenship under the 1982 law and therefore, as Human Rights Watch noted, outside the conscription law's own citizen-only terms — were forcibly recruited from villages and displacement camps from early 2024, more than 1,000 within two months, some under threat of losing food rations (HRW and Fortify Rights reporting, 2024, via the forced-conscription record). A law that conscripts the stateless it elsewhere refuses to count, and that the rich can buy out of, is two laws.

Why this grade: In plain words — the seat did not merely bend one law; it was founded by setting aside an election result, and every general instrument it has issued since (party law, election law, conscription law, amnesty) has operated with a built-in exception for the military and a built-in exposure for its opponents, down to its own November 2026 admission that its draft could be bought out of. The FOR shelf is real but narrow: general-form amnesties and action against low-level conscription corruption are what a one-law record would look like at the edges, and they do not reach the source.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: prisoners released under the general amnesties, including six journalists freed in December 2025, Win Myint (released 17 April 2026, though under strict surveillance — Reuters / Asia Daily, October 2026), and the families of the 8,865 pardoned in November 2025; households wealthy enough to purchase conscription exemptions — helped by the very mechanism that fails this test.
  • Hurt: Aung San Suu Kyi and the detained elected leadership; the NLD's voters, whose 2020 ballots were annulled; the 31,101 arrested on political grounds (AAPP via OHCHR, to 31 May 2026); Rohingya conscripts taken outside the law's own terms; poor households whose sons filled quotas that richer households paid to escape.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Two formal separations did occur in 2026, and they are recorded. Min Aung Hlaing resigned as Commander-in-Chief on 30 March 2026 before taking the presidency — the presidency and the military command are now held by two different men (Reuters, April 2026). A parliament was convened from 16 March 2026, sat, voted in an electoral college whose count was published (429 votes for Min Aung Hlaing, 126 for Nyo Saw — Reuters, 3 April 2026), and in July 2026 that parliament passed a resolution critical of the ASEAN Five-Point Consensus as undermining Myanmar's equality in ASEAN — a position at odds with the executive's then-stated hope of regaining full ASEAN participation, showing the chamber is not scripted on every line (Frontier Myanmar, Political Insider, 26 July 2026).
  • ASEAN's special envoy was admitted, within limits: Philippine Foreign Secretary Ma. Theresa P. Lazaro, as the ASEAN Chair's Special Envoy, visited Naypyidaw in January and October 2026 and a displaced-persons camp in Taunggyi on 28 October 2026, meeting the junta-appointed foreign minister — access the seat granted, though only to select junta-controlled areas (ASEAN summit reporting, Manila, November 2026; BusinessMirror, 15 November 2026).
  • Exiled and independent media continue to publish detailed, sourced criticism of the seat from outside its reach — the very reporting cited throughout this dossier — and six journalists were released in December 2025 (CPJ, December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The electoral referee is the seat's own appointee operating the seat's own rules. The Union Election Commission dissolved the NLD in March 2023, administered a 2025–26 election from which voting was absent in 67 of 330 townships, and operated under a July 2025 "Law on the Protection of Multi-Party Democratic General Elections from Obstruction, Disruption, and Destruction" that criminalized criticism of the election itself (UEC records; FIDH/ALTSEAN-Burma, February 2026). An estimated 10.5 million voters were excluded and another 11 million boycotted, on FIDH/ALTSEAN's count; official turnout was announced at 52% (phase 1), 55% (phase 2), and 56% (phase 3) — the two pictures are printed separately, as sourced, and not blended (UEC figures via the 2025–26 election record; FIDH/ALTSEAN-Burma, 6 February 2026).
  • The courts that convicted the elected leadership sat in closed proceedings under a judiciary whose senior appointments the seat controls; the pattern is quantified in the media file: between February 2021 and December 2025 the military detained 226 journalists from over 100 outlets, charged 178, and its courts sentenced 93 to a combined 521 years, with 38 still imprisoned at December 2025 (International Center for Not-for-Profit Law, Assessing Detentions of Journalists in Myanmar, January 2026). CPJ's separate count put at least 27 journalists still behind bars in December 2025 — printed separately, not blended (CPJ, 4 December 2025). Independent domestic media licences were revoked from the coup's first weeks; what remains inside the country operates under incitement, "false news," and counter-terrorism provisions (ICNL, January 2026; Athan, Annual Report 2025).
  • Centralization was deepened, not loosened, at the moment of supposed civilianization. On 3–4 February 2026 — after the election, before parliament convened — the NDSC enacted the Union Consultative Council Law, creating a council appointed by the president, with a remit covering national security, international relations, peace, and legislation; Reuters reported expert assessment that it would let Min Aung Hlaing become president "without loosening his grip on the powerful armed forces," and the Institute for Strategy and Policy — Myanmar described a body with "no oversight" and a "total lack of accountability" (Reuters, 4 February 2026). The council was formed on 10 April 2026 with 11 members chaired by Soe Win, the former deputy commander-in-chief (Union Consultative Council formation order, 10 April 2026). The post-election NDSC was reconstituted the same day with 10 of its 11 members active or retired military officers (USANAS Foundation analysis, 2026), and of the 30 union ministries, 26 are held by military officers or USDP figures, with some 30% of ministers under international sanctions for their roles in the coup and in human-rights violations — Burma Campaign UK's count, recorded as that organization's documented tally (Burma Campaign UK briefing, 2026).
  • His successor at the head of the military is his own former intelligence chief, General Ye Win Oo — the officer who, on the New York Times' reporting, led the team that arrested Aung San Suu Kyi on the morning of the coup — chosen over the deputy commander and the chief of general staff in what analysts describe as a loyalty-first succession (Reuters, April 2026; Asia Daily analysis, November 2026). The referee was not released; he was replaced by the seat's own hand.
  • The prosecution's independence fails at the top of the file: the only arrest-warrant application against the seat's holder comes from an international prosecutor the seat refuses to recognize (Test 1), and no domestic prosecutor has opened a case on any documented post-coup killing of civilians.

Why this grade: In plain words — every referee named in this test is appointed by, staffed by, or answerable to the seat: the election commission dissolved the winning party and criminalized criticism of its own election; the courts jailed the elected government and 93 journalists; the central institutions of the 2026 "civilian" order — NDSC, Union Consultative Council, the military command itself — were filled by the president's own nominees, his predecessor-in-uniform chairing the oversight council and his intelligence chief taking the army. Stepping down as Commander-in-Chief while appointing the successor, the overseers, and the referees is a change of chair, not a loosening of grip.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: the officers and USDP figures who received the ministries, council seats, and commands the grip distributes — including Ye Win Oo (Commander-in-Chief), Soe Win (chair, Union Consultative Council), and Nyo Saw (First Vice President); state media, which operate unimpeded.
  • Hurt: independent journalists — 226 detained, 93 sentenced, 38 still imprisoned at December 2025 (ICNL); voters in the 67 townships with no voting at all; the NLD as an institution, dissolved by the referee; any future litigant or candidate whose case reaches a court or commission the seat appoints.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, the ruler at least considered it openly.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Twice, when a China-brokered option became available, he took it. Under Chinese mediation the Myanmar National Democratic Alliance Army withdrew from Lashio in April 2025, returning the city it had captured in 2024, and the Ta'ang National Liberation Army later returned Mogok and Momeik in late 2025 (WarWatch analysis, 2026; LearnOrbis conflict record, 2026). Accepting a mediated hand-back of lost territory is an adjustment, openly arrived at, and it is recorded as one.
  • After the 28 March 2025 earthquake he made the request this seat almost never makes: he appealed publicly for international assistance, admitted foreign rescue teams, and allowed the Associated Press to report from Naypyidaw and Mandalay (AP reporting, September 2025). His government announced a unilateral ceasefire on 2 April 2025 and extended it, in form, to the end of June 2025 (Hitotsubashi GGR briefing, August 2025).
  • In 2026 he showed two small, documented flexibilities toward the deposed leadership: Win Myint was released on 17 April 2026, Aung San Suu Kyi was moved from prison to house arrest on 30 April 2026, a Red Cross official was permitted to meet her in August 2026, and on 22 October 2026 she was allowed a 20-minute telephone call with her son Kim Aris — their first direct conversation in more than five years (Reuters, April 2026; Asia Daily, October 2026; ASEAN summit reporting, November 2026). In July 2026 he stated his government had engaged with 13 ethnic armed organisations under his presidency's 100-Day Plan (East Asia Forum, 12 November 2026) — engagement claimed by the seat, recorded as its claim.

Evidence against:

  • The street's pushback in 2021 was answered with the force that started the civil war, and the pattern never reversed. Peaceful protests against the coup were met with lethal crackdowns; the conflict that followed has, on the AAPP figures carried by OHCHR, killed a minimum of 8,075 civilians by 31 May 2026 — and on open-source counts cited by the UN Secretary-General, total civilian deaths may be as high as over 16,000 since 2021; the two figures are printed separately, as sourced (OHCHR factsheet, September 2026; UN Secretary-General's report, 3 August 2026). A separate ASEAN-summit news report in November 2026 put total conflict deaths, all sides, at more than 100,000 — a different measure, printed as its own source's figure and not blended with the civilian counts (Economic Times, November 2026).
  • The battlefield's pushback — Operation 1027, launched October 2023, in which the seat lost over 100 towns (ISP-Myanmar count: 101 towns lost, 22 retaken by end-2025) — was answered not with political adjustment but with the two escalations that define the tenure: activation of mass conscription in February 2024 and a rising air campaign. OHCHR records 2,592 air attacks in the twelve months to May 2026, 17% more than the preceding year, with airstrikes the main cause of civilian deaths — 845 of 1,241 verified civilian deaths in that period (OHCHR, September 2026). By 2026 the military had regained ground — reopening the Mandalay–China-border highway, retaking Falam, Tedim, and Tonzang in Chin State — by those means, not by settlement (WarWatch, 2026).
  • The earthquake wobble is the clearest single file. The seat announced a ceasefire effective 2 April 2025; the UN human rights office recorded at least 61 attacks across Myanmar after the earthquake, including 16 after the ceasefire was supposed to take effect, some airstrikes launched within hours of the tremors (UN News, April 2025). The IISS Myanmar Conflict Map assessed the ceasefires as "largely performative," with the Tatmadaw continuing "a ferocious air campaign" including in earthquake-affected areas, and five months on OHCHR reported continued restriction of aid to areas outside the seat's control — restrictions the authorities deny, a denial recorded here (IISS, May 2025; AP, September 2025). The earthquake itself killed more than 3,800 people, with 207,000 displaced on the AHA Centre's 23 April 2025 count — printed as that source's figure.
  • The other-option rule, applied exactly: in April 2021 ASEAN offered a standing option — the Five-Point Consensus, which Min Aung Hlaing personally agreed to in Jakarta: immediate cessation of violence, constructive dialogue among all parties, mediation by an ASEAN special envoy, humanitarian assistance, and an envoy's visit to meet all parties (ASEAN, April 2021). Considering it openly and declining parts with stated reasons would not count against him. The record shows the opposite of consideration: five years on, ASEAN leaders at Manila on 15 November 2026 again recorded "lack of substantive progress," kept his political leadership excluded from summits, and condemned continued violence against civilians (ASEAN leaders' review, 15 November 2026); in November 2026 his government rejected proposals on the special envoy and on progress reports, arguing the election had removed the need for an envoy, and his parliament had already resolved in July 2026 to review participation in the Consensus as inconsistent with non-interference (Malaysian Foreign Minister Mohamad Hasan, Manila, 14 November 2026; Frontier Myanmar, July 2026). The option was not weighed and declined; it was agreed, unimplemented, and finally argued out of existence.
  • Foreign partners' pushback met the same answer. ASEAN's exclusion since 2021, Western sanctions, and the ICC application (Test 1) produced no documented policy adjustment; instead the seat hired two US public-relations firms in August 2025 to lobby Washington (The Sentry / Justice For Myanmar, December 2025) — image adjusted, conduct not.
  • The no-good-choice clause was considered for this test and does not fit it. The clause grades a ruler leniently where the options that actually existed each carried real harm. Here an option that existed from April 2021 onward — envoy-mediated dialogue with all parties, with humanitarian access — carried no comparable harm and was the seat's own signed agreement; and the earthquake ceasefire's breach was a choice made while rescue was under way. The trap, where there is one, was also of the seat's own earlier building: the war that made every later choice hard began with the coup graded in Test 1. The clause's remaining questions are still answered on the record: he was not honest about the trap while in it (the ceasefire was announced and breached), and the least-harm door actually open — implementing the Consensus — was not taken.

Why this grade: In plain words — every major pushback of the tenure, from the street in 2021 to the battlefield in 2023 to the earthquake in 2025 to ASEAN across all five years, was answered by escalation, denial, or rebranding rather than open adjustment; the genuine adjustments on the FOR shelf — two China-brokered hand-backs, an aid appeal, and small easings for two elderly prisoners in 2026 — are real but tactical, each taken when it cost the seat nothing it valued, while the one standing offer that would have cost it control was refused a hearing for five years.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: residents of Lashio, Mogok, and Momeik, where mediated hand-backs replaced assaults; earthquake survivors reached by the foreign rescue teams admitted in April 2025; Aung San Suu Kyi and Win Myint, marginally, in 2026; the seat's own international position, served by the lobbying contracts.
  • Hurt: civilians under the air campaign — 845 of the 1,241 verified civilian deaths in the year to May 2026 were from air attacks (OHCHR); earthquake-affected communities in resistance-held areas, where aid was restricted; the young men taken under conscription to answer the battlefield losses (Test 4); ASEAN's mediation effort and the displaced people it was meant to reach.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the ethnic and religious minorities, the protesters, the conscripts, the displaced, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The seat's claimed accounting is nationwide in form: the 2025–26 election was staged across 263 townships in three phases, official results were published for both houses and for state and region parliaments — where ethnic parties including the Shan Nationalities Democratic Party (7 lower-house seats), the Pa-O National Organisation, and the Mon Unity Party (5 seats each at state/region level) won representation (UEC results via Burma News International, 3 February 2026). Ethnic-party lawmakers therefore sit in the 2026 parliaments, in small numbers, and that is recorded.
  • The April–October 2026 easings touched the two most famous counted-out people in the country: Win Myint released, Aung San Suu Kyi moved to house arrest, permitted a Red Cross meeting and a family phone call (Test 3 shelf). His July 2026 statement claims engagement with 13 ethnic armed organisations under his 100-Day Plan (East Asia Forum, November 2026).
  • Humanitarian need is at least officially acknowledged: the seat cooperated with the ASEAN envoy's October 2026 visit to a displaced-persons camp in Taunggyi and requested international earthquake assistance in 2025 (ASEAN reporting, November 2026; AP, 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The Rohingya remain counted out in law and in fact. Some 1.2 million Rohingya refugees remain in Bangladesh, almost 80% women and children, with no safe, voluntary return under way (UN Secretary-General's report, August 2026); those remaining in Rakhine State faced, on OHCHR's 2025–26 findings, "widespread and systematic violations and abuses" by both the Arakan Army and the military — both parties named, as the report names them (OHCHR, A/HRC/63/21, August 2026). The military that denies Rohingya citizenship simultaneously conscripted Rohingya men and boys to fight the Arakan Army, in some documented cases under threat to food rations and, on escapees' accounts recorded by monitors, used them in exposed positions — those accounts are recorded as ALLEGATION-level testimony, not verdicts (HRW / Fortify Rights, 2024). The ICC deportation-and-persecution application (Test 1) stands unanswered on its evidence.
  • The displacement ledger, printed as separate sourced figures and never blended: OHCHR's factsheet records 3.6 million internally displaced (September 2026); the UN Secretary-General's report records more than 3.96 million internally displaced for the period to 31 July 2026; OCHA's figure cited in October 2025 was more than 3.6 million displaced and almost 22 million people in need of humanitarian assistance; OHCHR records 15.2 million people facing acute hunger (OHCHR factsheet, September 2026; UN SG report, August 2026; UN Third Committee statement, 6 October 2025). Whichever sourced figure is used, roughly one in fifteen people in the country has been driven from home on this seat's watch, and aid to those outside its territory is restricted (Test 3).
  • Protesters and the merely suspected were counted as enemies from the first weeks: 31,101 arrested on political grounds and 14,278 still in detention in severely inadequate conditions at 31 May 2026 (AAPP via OHCHR); nearly 23,000 political prisoners were held at the time of the election, on FIDH/ALTSEAN's count — printed separately (FIDH/ALTSEAN-Burma, February 2026). Executions resumed under this seat in July 2022 for the first time in decades, with four men hanged after closed trials — the fact of the executions is documented; the fairness of those trials is contested between the seat, which called them lawful, and the UN and rights organizations, which condemned them as politically motivated.
  • The young and the poor are counted — as manpower. OHCHR records over 125,000 people reported as conscripted since the law's 2024 activation, in 25 batches of as many as 5,000 (OHCHR, September 2026); ISP-Myanmar's November 2026 study records an almost eightfold surge in annual recruitment after activation, through 29 consecutive intakes by October 2026, and a 30th intake began on 16 November 2026 (ISP-Myanmar, On Point No. 7, 9 November 2026; Asia Daily, November 2026). On 22 July 2026 a sitting MP told the Pyithu Hluttaw that conscription authorities were taking youths, including students, from checkpoints, homes, and streets beyond quota; the deputy home affairs minister replied that those detained were draft-dodgers — both statements recorded as made (Pyithu Hluttaw proceedings via the conscription record, July 2026).
  • Whole peoples were counted out of the seat's own founding ritual: no voting in 67 townships; in Chin State, participation below 5% on FIDH/ALTSEAN's count; 10.5 million excluded on the same count (FIDH/ALTSEAN-Burma, February 2026). A census of the nation that cannot be taken in the nation's periphery is an accounting of the center only.

Why this grade: In plain words — the seat's accounting includes everyone in exactly one register: as subjects of conscription, taxation of movement, and air power; and excludes them in every register that would give them standing — citizenship for the Rohingya, ballots for 67 townships, liberty for some 14,000–23,000 political prisoners on the separate counts printed above, and safe return for over a million refugees. The FOR shelf's ethnic-party seats and two prisoners' easings are real, and they are a handful of names against ledgers in the millions.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: the small ethnic parties seated in the 2026 parliaments and their voters; Win Myint and Aung San Suu Kyi, in the limited easings recorded; displaced people in the Taunggyi camp reached by the ASEAN envoy's visit.
  • Hurt: the Rohingya — refugees in Bangladesh, the remnant in Rakhine, and Rohingya conscripts; the internally displaced, 3.6 million (OHCHR) to more than 3.96 million (UN SG) on the separate counts; the 15.2 million facing acute hunger (OHCHR); conscripts and their families, above all the poor families who could not buy exemption; political prisoners still detained; the executed men of July 2022 and their families.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 1 February 2021, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • An election was, eventually, held, and state duties were, in form, handed to the winning party. The SAC's Five-Point Road Map (published 2021, restated in state media 30 June 2023) promised that "[u]pon accomplishing the provisions of the state of emergency, free and fair multiparty democratic elections will be held in line with the 2008 Constitution, and further work will be undertaken to hand over State duties to the winning party." Elections were held in three phases, 28 December 2025 – 25 January 2026; the USDP won; a parliament convened; and on 10 April 2026 state duties passed to a government formed from that parliament (Global New Light of Myanmar, 30 June 2023; UEC results, February 2026). The event promised occurred. Its defining adjectives — "free and fair" — are graded below, and the mandate rule is honored here: had this seat held an electoral mandate and governed on it, that governing would sit on this shelf; the failure recorded is in the adjectives and the timing, which were part of the promise as made.
  • The earthquake ceasefire was announced and, in form, extended — declared 2 April 2025 and prolonged on paper to end-June 2025 (GGR briefing, August 2025). Its breach is graded below; the announcement and extensions themselves are documented.
