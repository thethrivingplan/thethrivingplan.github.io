The Ruler's Test — Mohammed VI, King of Morocco

Subject: Mohammed VI — King of Morocco since 23 July 1999. The seat graded is the throne, and the reason is stated plainly: in Morocco the government governs, but the king rules. Under the 2011 Constitution the king appoints the head of government from the party that wins the most seats (Article 47) and may dismiss ministers, dissolve parliament (Article 96), chair the Council of Ministers that approves strategic decisions and organic laws (Article 48), command the armed forces, chair the Supreme Council of the Judiciary and the Supreme Council of Ulema, and exercise the right of pardon (Article 58); his person is constitutionally inviolable (Article 46) and he is not answerable to any elected body. The palace also stands at the center of the economy through the royal holding Al Mada, one of the kingdom's largest conglomerates. The brief named Aziz Akhannouch as head of government "at last record"; that record has changed, and the change is itself part of the evidence. Akhannouch — billionaire owner of the Akwa Group and its fuel distributor Afriquia, appointed in October 2021 after his National Rally of Independents (RNI) won the 2021 election — led the government for five years and is treated throughout as the king's instrument and as context: the governments a Moroccan king appoints administer his program, and the strategic files (the Sahara, social protection, water, the security services, the judiciary's apex) are set at the palace. In the parliamentary election of 23 September 2026 the RNI fell from 102 seats to 66; the Authenticity and Modernity Party (PAM) came first with 97 of 395 seats, Istiqlal took 65, and the opposition Justice and Development Party (PJD) recovered from 13 seats to 54, on turnout of 38.08%, down from about 50.4% in 2021 (Interior Ministry provisional results; International IDEA democracy tracker, September 2026). On 29 September 2026 the king appointed PAM's coordinator Fatima Ezzahra El Mansouri head of government — the first woman to hold the post — and appointed her 32-member cabinet on 16 October 2026; the handover took place on 17 October, and her coalition (PAM, Istiqlal, USFP, PPS) holds 207 of 395 seats, with the RNI in opposition. She has held office for roughly a month at grading and is not graded separately; her appointment is graded as the king's act. The king, 63, has had documented health episodes — shoulder surgery after a fall in December 2024 and a period using a cane — but walked unaided into parliament in October 2025 and was chairing working sessions in late October 2026 (Associated Press, October 2025; Royal Office statements, October 2026). He is graded on the full reign, with the weight of the evidence on the last five years, and on the completed Akhannouch tenure (2021–2026) where the government's record is the throne's record by delegation. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic of the Test, in the words of the man who set it: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Two standing rules govern the grading. First, the mandate rule: governing on a platform a ruler was elected or appointed to carry out is never itself a failure — the Test grades the holding, not the platform; marks are lost only for how power is exercised. Second, word-keeping and outcomes are separate axes and are printed independently: a ruler can keep his word and still hurt people, or break his word and help them, and neither fact launders the other. Where the record shows a situation in which there was no good choice, the dossier says so and grades the choice that was available in the moment, not the trap.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The pardon power has been used, at scale, in favor of the state's own jailed critics. On Throne Day, 29 July 2024, the king pardoned 2,476 people, among them the three imprisoned journalists whose cases had defined the press-freedom file — Taoufik Bouachrine (12 years, 2018), Soulaimane Raissouni (5 years, 2021), and Omar Radi (6 years, 2021) — together with historian Maati Monjib, YouTuber Reda Taoujni, activist Saida Alami, and five journalists in exile (Ministry of Justice statement, 29 July 2024; RSF and IFJ statements, July–August 2024). Whatever else it shows (below), the releases were real, and RSF called them "a major step forward for press freedom in Morocco."
  • The competition referee moved against the head of government's own company. In November 2023 the Competition Council closed its fuel-market investigation with a settlement under which nine distributors — including Akhannouch's Afriquia — agreed to pay a combined 1.84 billion dirhams over alleged price coordination (Competition Council, November 2023). The settlement was not an admission, and the opposition's larger claim — some 17 billion dirhams of excess fuel profits, pressed by PJD leader Abdelilah Benkirane and others — remains an ALLEGATION no court has adopted; but the fine reached the most connected firm in the kingdom, and it was paid.
  • Enforcement has reached senior figures of the governing parties themselves. In July 2025 a Casablanca court convicted senior PAM figures in the drug-trafficking file known as "Escobar of the Sahara," sentencing Saïd Naciri — former president of the Wydad football club and a Casablanca district president — to ten years, with regional council president Abdenbi Bioui also convicted (Casablanca Court of Appeal, July 2025; contemporaneous reporting). Dozens of local elected officials have been dismissed or prosecuted by administrative courts in the same period.
  • The ballot itself is administered by one rule: the 2026 election was run on schedule, the count was not overturned when the palace-aligned incumbent lost a third of his seats, and the opposition PJD's recovery from 13 to 54 seats was certified rather than blocked (Interior Ministry results; International IDEA, September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The pardon that freed the journalists is also the clearest proof of the second track: in Morocco, relief from a prosecution flows from the throne's grace, not from a rule that protects the next journalist. The convictions themselves stood; the pardon remits sentences without erasing them, and RSF's own one-year assessment concluded that after July 2024 "judicial harassment and defamation campaigns against critical journalists have not only continued but also intensified" (RSF, "One year after royal pardon, press still under attack," 30 June 2025). A law that requires a king's mercy to stop it working is not one law.
  • Speech is prosecuted under the Penal Code rather than the press code — which since 2016 carries no prison terms — so the protective rule is routed around. Hamid El Mahdaoui, director of Badil.info, was sentenced in November 2024 to 18 months and a 1.5-million-dirham fine for "dissemination of false allegations" and defamation, upheld on appeal on 30 June 2025; in 2025 he faced five separate legal proceedings, all initiated by the same complainant, the sitting Minister of Justice (RSF recommendations to the El Mansouri government, November 2026; Human Rights Watch World Report 2026). Publisher Ali Anouzla has been prosecuted since 2013 for "condoning terrorism," his trial adjourned for over a decade (RSF, November 2026). Journalist Ali Lmrabet was arrested on arrival at Tangier airport on 12 July 2026 and held three days in an investigation into alleged dissemination of false information arising from his journalism; the charges were pursued after his release (RSF, July 2026; Maghrebi.org, September 2026).
  • The former human-rights minister Mohamed Ziane, imprisoned since 2022, received a further five-year sentence in July 2024 in a corruption case brought after he criticized the king from prison; rights groups describe the charges as politically motivated — an ALLEGATION-level characterization the dossier records as their position, not a finding — and public appeals for his pardon were still circulating, unanswered, in November 2026 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World country record; petition reporting, November 2026).
  • The Gen Z prosecutions ran on the mass track. After the GenZ 212 protests of September–October 2025, the public prosecutor reported 2,480 people facing prosecution by 29 October 2025, 1,473 of them in custody (Presidency of the Public Prosecutor's Office via Associated Press, 29 October 2025); the Moroccan Association for Human Rights (AMDH) counts at least 5,700 arrests, and EFE reporting puts detentions above 6,000, with sentences running to 15 years (AMDH, September 2026; EFE/Infobae, 2026). Transparency International's 2025 index release states the asymmetry in one sentence: in Morocco, perceived corruption is "undermining investigation of embezzlement by elected officials and civil servants and was a factor in triggering mass Gen-Z anti-government protests" (Transparency International, CPI 2025 MENA release, 10 February 2026). Morocco scored 39 of 100 on that index, 91st of 182 — improved from 37, and still a score that says the source is not clean.
  • The conflict-of-interest file at the top was never adjudicated on any rule. The head of government for five years remained the reference shareholder of a group holding roughly a third of the fuel market and the largest share of national fuel storage, with no divestiture or blind trust required or made; in November 2023 a consortium including his Afriquia Gaz won the contract for Africa's largest seawater desalination plant at Casablanca (6.5 billion dirhams), a project the National Investment Commission — which he chaired — classified as strategic. Akhannouch defended the award in parliament on 16 December 2024 as the cheapest bid, transparently tendered, with no state funding; opposition parties called it a textbook conflict of interest and Benkirane demanded his resignation. Both positions are on the record; no court or ethics body ever ruled between them, because Morocco has no mechanism that can grade the head of government's own file (parliamentary record, 16 December 2024; MENAS Associates; Billionaires Africa, July 2026). The illicit-enrichment law that might have supplied such a rule has been pending, unenacted, for years.
  • Technical surveillance supplies the background track: Amnesty International's technical analysis for the 2021 Pegasus Project found Moroccan journalists including Radi, Bouachrine, and Raissouni targeted with NSO Group spyware; the Moroccan government denies the findings and has pursued legal action against the organizations that published them. The targeting is recorded here as a technically supported finding that the state contests — CONTESTED (Amnesty International Security Lab, 2021; Moroccan government denials, 2021–2026).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the two-track pattern is documented across independent monitors and across the whole period, not in a single episode: critics of the palace and the government are prosecuted under ordinary criminal law with prison attached, minors and street protesters are processed in the thousands, the conflict-of-interest question at the summit is structurally unanswerable, and the one great act of relief — the 2024 pardons — was itself an act of discretion that left the prosecuting machinery intact and, on RSF's count, busier within a year. The for-shelf is genuine: a competition fine that reached the prime minister's own firm, convictions of governing-party seniors in a drug file, and an honestly counted election. It shows the law can reach upward. Test 1 grades whether it does so by one rule, and the record says it reaches upward by exception and downward by system.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the 2,476 pardoned in July 2024 and their families, including the freed journalists; fuel consumers, marginally, by the Competition Council settlement; voters whose 2026 ballots were counted as cast. Hurt — the prosecuted journalists and their newsrooms (Mahdaoui fined 1.5 million dirhams by a minister's complaints; Anouzla twelve years under an adjourned charge); Mohamed Ziane; the roughly 2,480 prosecuted GenZ 212 defendants and the hundreds still imprisoned a year later (below, Test 3); and every Moroccan whose complaint against an official runs into the embezzlement-investigation deficit Transparency International names.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The electoral referee has repeatedly been allowed to cost the palace its preferred outcomes. The PJD governed for a decade after winning pluralities the palace disliked (2011, 2016); the RNI's 2026 defeat was accepted and executed within six days by the appointment of the winning party's leader; the PJD's partial return in 2026 was certified. No party has been banned, no result annulled, no count falsified on the documented record (Interior Ministry results, 2011–2026; International IDEA, 2026).
  • The central bank speaks in its own voice. Bank Al-Maghrib sets rates independently, and its long-serving governor Abdellatif Jouahri has publicly criticized government fiscal policy and subsidy decisions for years without removal or public rebuke from the palace (Bank Al-Maghrib statements and press conferences, 2021–2026).
  • State-adjacent referees have bitten the hand that appointed them: the Competition Council's fuel settlement (Test 1) was negotiated by a council whose president is a royal appointee; the National Human Rights Council (CNDH), also royally appointed, publicly distinguished peaceful GenZ 212 protesters from those committing violence and called for investigations into the deaths during the unrest (CNDH statements, October 2025).
  • Critical media continue to publish: outlets such as Le Desk, Lakome, Badil, and Enass carry reporting hostile to the government and survive, however precariously; Morocco rose from 120th (2025) to 105th of 180 in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index, score 50.55, in part because no new journalist had been jailed for their work in the index's reporting window (RSF World Press Freedom Index 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The constitution itself is the grip. The king chairs the Supreme Council of the Judiciary, appoints half the Constitutional Court's members and its president's nominating channel, appoints the head of government and may dismiss any minister, chairs the Council of Ministers through which all strategic legislation passes, commands the armed forces, and presides over the religious field as Commander of the Faithful, the sole authority whose fatwas bind. Freedom House scores the elected government's ability to determine policy at the bottom of its scale and rates Morocco 37 of 100, Partly Free — 13 of 40 for political rights (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026). A referee system whose apex is the graded seat cannot be described as held at arm's length; the autonomy that exists inside it (above) exists on revocable sufferance.
  • The prosecution is not independent of the executive the palace appoints, and the GenZ 212 file shows the result: thousands of prosecutions launched within weeks, in proceedings the AMDH and Amnesty International describe as mass and summary in character — their characterization, recorded as such — with sentences up to 15 years (AMDH, 2026; Amnesty International statements, October 2025). The public prosecution's presidency passed in 2025 within the same judicial apex the king chairs.
  • The media's independence is punished by process rather than by closure — the method RSF's North Africa director describes as legal and financial exhaustion plus "orchestrated defamation campaigns" that produce self-censorship (RSF, Maghrebi.org interview, 2026; RSF statement, 30 June 2025). The day after the July 2024 pardons, a coordinated defamation campaign targeted the released journalists (RSF, June 2025). State advertising and printing/distribution economics keep most private media dependent; the index rise to 105th is explicitly cautioned by RSF as partly a function of other countries' decline.
  • The electoral rules themselves were re-cut before the 2021 election in a way that foreseeably reduced the incumbent PJD's seat haul: the electoral quotient was recalculated on registered voters rather than votes cast, a change the PJD denounced as engineered against it and official commentary defended as broadening representation — CONTESTED as to motive, undisputed as to effect (the PJD fell from 125 seats to 13) (organic law debates, 2021; election results, 2021 and 2026).
  • The interior and security apparatus answers to the palace, not to the government of the day: the Interior Minister and the heads of the security services are understood across the documented record as royal appointees in the "sovereignty ministries," and during the GenZ 212 unrest it was that apparatus — gendarmerie, national security, territorial surveillance — that conducted the arrests, while the elected government fronted the response (Interior Ministry statements, September–October 2025; protest chronologies, Wikipedia and press, 2025).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the test asks whether the referees keep their autonomy, and in Morocco the referees' autonomy is a grant, not a right: the graded seat chairs the judiciary's supreme council, appoints the constitutional court's core, owns the pardon, commands the security services, and faces no mechanism that can overrule him. The for-shelf matters — an honest count, an outspoken central bank, a competition council that fined the premier's firm, a rights council that criticized a crackdown — and it is exactly what keeps this from the closed systems elsewhere in the series. But every one of those pockets of independence operates inside a structure the king heads and could lawfully tighten tomorrow; independence that exists because the holder permits it is the definition of a grip that has not needed to close.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — voters, whose choices have repeatedly seated and unseated governments; readers of the surviving independent press; litigants and firms in the ordinary run of cases the system decides without political charge. Hurt — journalists and activists whose cases are never ordinary (Test 1); defendants in the GenZ 212 mass proceedings; the PJD's 2021 voters, whose ballots were devalued by a quotient change made before the count; and parliament itself, whose 395 members legislate downstream of a Council of Ministers they do not control.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, when another option became available, he at least considered it openly: considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The founding wobble of the reign's second decade was answered with a constitution, in the open. When the February 20 Movement filled the streets in 2011, the king announced a constitutional revision by speech on 9 March 2011 — within three weeks — and the new text, whatever it retained (Test 2), transferred the choice of head of government to the election result and expanded the government's formal powers; it was put to a referendum on 1 July 2011. The adjustment was public, reasoned, and fast.
  • The GenZ 212 wobble was answered on the policy shelf within weeks. The king's address opening parliament on 10 October 2025 adopted the protesters' own framing — "social justice is not a temporary priority… it is a strategic direction" — instructed elected officials to stop wasting time, and rejected the premise that national projects and social programs compete; the Council of Ministers he chaired on 19 October 2025 then approved the orientations of a 2026 finance bill raising health and education allocations to roughly 140 billion dirhams, about a 16% increase, alongside bills subsidizing up to 75% of campaign expenses for candidates under 35 (Associated Press, 10 October 2025; 2026 finance bill reporting, October 2025). The movement had written to the king directly asking him to intervene; he answered in parliament, in public, on the substance of services and regional inequality.
  • The voters' pushback of September 2026 was executed, not managed. The palace did not protect its incumbent: within six days of an election in which the king's head of government lost a third of his seats and turnout fell to 38%, the winner was appointed, a coalition excluding the incumbent's party was permitted to form, and the transfer of power on 17 October 2026 was orderly and complete (Royal Cabinet statements, September–October 2026; handover reporting, 17 October 2026).
  • Adjustment by anticipation is also on the record: the February 2025 Eid al-Adha decision (below, no-good-choice) was announced three months before the feast, with the reasons — a herd down 38% since 2016, rainfall 53% below the thirty-year average — published in advance rather than imposed by surprise.

Evidence against:

  • The accountability half of the GenZ 212 demands was refused, and refused silently. The movement's letter asked for corrupt officials to be investigated, for the head of government's resignation, and — as the trials mounted — for the detainees' release. The king's 10 October speech did not mention the unrest, the three deaths at Leqliaa, or the arrests; no investigation into the shootings was announced by the palace (the CNDH called for investigations; the authorities maintain the gendarmes fired in self-defense against an attack on their post — the responsibility for the deaths remains CONTESTED); and a year later roughly 650 people remained in prison on the AMDH's September 2026 count, 662 on the prison administration's own July 2026 figure, with families demonstrating outside parliament for their release (Associated Press, 10 October 2025; AMDH, September 2026; DGAPR figures via EFE, July 2026). The first Unity Day pardon of 30–31 October 2026 benefited 907 people — 17 released outright, 595 sentence reductions, one life sentence commuted — and no reporting indicates GenZ 212 detainees or Hirak leaders were among them (Ministry of Justice statement, 30 October 2026; North Africa Post, October 2026).
  • The street's earlier files were answered the same way. The Hirak Rif movement of 2016–17 — whose demands were hospitals, a university, and jobs for Al Hoceima — was met with mass arrests and 20-year sentences for its leaders; royal pardons freed some 280 Hirak detainees between 2018 and 2021, but the leadership was deliberately left inside, and a decade later six prominent figures, including Nasser Zefzafi and Nabil Ahamjik, remain in prison in Tangier, nine years into sentences the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found arbitrary in Zefzafi's case in an opinion adopted 30 August 2024 calling for his release (UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention, 2024; HuMENA advocacy dossier, November 2026; Middle East Eye, October 2026). Zefzafi was granted two short humanitarian leaves in 2025 — to visit his dying father in May and to attend his funeral on 4 September — and returned to prison each time. The option of release has been available at every Throne Day for a decade; it has been considered and refused in silence, which the addendum's rule grades as the refusal, not the consideration.
  • Where adjustment would bind the palace's own timetable, the pattern is deferral rather than answer: the family-code revision the king launched in his July 2022 Throne Speech, with a six-month deadline in his September 2023 letter, produced 139 approved proposals in December 2024 and then no bill at all — never adopted by the Government Council, never filed in parliament, formally pushed to the next legislature in September 2026 (Test 5). The wobble from women's organizations and from conservatives was managed by letting the file sleep.

Why: The grade lands on MIXED because both halves are documented at full strength and neither cancels the other. On services, budgets, and electoral outcomes, this seat adjusts — publicly, with reasons, sometimes within days: a constitution in 2011, a 16% health-and-education increase and a youth-participation package in October 2025, a lost election honored in September 2026. On accountability — deaths at Leqliaa, detainees, the Hirak leadership, investigations of the powerful — it does not adjust at all: it waits, in silence, until the street goes home, and lets the courts' sentences stand as the answer. The wobble is answered where answering costs money; it is refused where answering would cost the structure.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — patients and students in line for the expanded 2026 health and education budgets; young candidates subsidized under the new participation bills; the 907 beneficiaries of the October 2026 pardon; the electorate whose 2026 verdict was honored in full. Hurt — the families of the three young men killed at Leqliaa, whose file has no public investigation; the roughly 650 GenZ 212 prisoners still held a year on, including convicted minors (76 minors among the convicted on the count cited by Amnesty International's reviewers in January 2026; EFE reported convictions extending even to children under twelve — recorded as reported, not independently verified); the six Hirak leaders in their tenth year of imprisonment; and women whose custody, guardianship, and protection cases are decided under a 2004 code the state itself declared inadequate in 2022.

The no-good-choice clause, applied: One file in this dossier is graded under the clause. By early 2025, seven years of drought had cut the national herd by 38% since the 2016 census and driven sheep prices beyond working-class reach ahead of Eid al-Adha, when some six million animals are normally sacrificed. The options actually open were these: let the rite proceed and watch the breeding herd — the country's recovery stock — be slaughtered at speculative prices, with the poor priced out of a central religious observance and herders' remaining capital destroyed; or suspend the rite, inflicting real religious pain, a lost season's income on herders and seasonal workers, and an enforcement problem in every market. On 26 February 2025, three months ahead, the king chose the second door and said why, in a message read on state television: performing the rite "in these difficult circumstances will cause real harm to large segments of our people, especially those with limited income"; he grounded the suspension in his religious authority as Commander of the Faithful, noted the rite's status as recommended rather than obligatory practice, cited his grandfather's precedent, and undertook to perform the sacrifice on behalf of the nation (royal message, 26 February 2025, via the Ministry of Islamic Affairs; AFP reporting, February–June 2025). A 6.2-billion-dirham relief plan for herders, including feed subsidies and partial debt relief, followed (government announcement, 2025). The trap was built by drought, not by the seat; the choice was announced early, reasoned in public, and aimed at the least-harm door. It is recorded as the best possible choice available in the moment and is held neither for nor against him in the grade above; the hurt it caused herders and the devout is in the ledger below because the ledger records outcomes, not blame. (Helped: poor households spared a ruinous purchase and the national herd's breeding stock. Hurt: herders, livestock traders, and seasonal workers who lost the year's main market, and families for whom the feast's center was absent.)

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Sahrawis, the Rif, the rural interior, the Amazigh, women, the young, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The social-protection generalization is the largest counting exercise in the kingdom's history, and it was designed as universal. Under the framework law the king launched in 2020–21, compulsory health coverage (AMO) expanded from about 8.6 million beneficiaries in 2021 to some 24.3 million by the end of 2024, including the AMO-Tadamon scheme carrying roughly 4 million vulnerable households — some 11 million people — with the state paying their contributions at about 9.5 billion dirhams a year; direct social assistance, launched in December 2023, pays 500 to 1,200 dirhams monthly to eligible households, with a program budget rising from 25 billion dirhams (2024) toward 29 billion (2026) (Ministry of Health and Social Protection reporting; Akhannouch statement to the House of Councillors, 27 May 2025; government and institutional data via Moroccan press, 2025). National poverty fell from 11.9% in 2014 to 6.8% in 2024 on the official statistics cited by Reuters (Reuters, September 2025).
  • The counting reached groups earlier regimes left at the margin: the Amazigh New Year (Yennayer) was made an official paid holiday from January 2024, a recognition Tamazight activists had sought for decades; two regularization campaigns (2014, 2017) gave legal status to tens of thousands of sub-Saharan migrants, a policy without regional parallel at the time; and the southern provinces have received sustained development investment under the 2015 development model, with the king's October 2025 invitation to the Tindouf camps' residents to return to "a united Morocco" with equal rights repeated after Resolution 2797 (royal speeches, 2023–2025; UN General Assembly Fourth Committee record, 2026).
  • The earthquake file, on the state's numbers, was substantially executed: of 59,675 homes damaged in the 8 September 2023 Al Haouz earthquake, the government reports 51,154 rebuilt by September 2025 and 54,425 completed by 2026, with more than 7.2 billion dirhams of assistance paid, including monthly emergency aid to over 63,000 families, under a five-year, 120-billion-dirham program (Reuters, September 2025; interministerial commission reporting, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Western Sahara is counted by a different instrument. Freedom House assesses the territory separately at 4 of 100, Not Free — among the lowest scores on earth — while Morocco itself scores 37 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026). The referendum on self-determination promised in the 1991 ceasefire has never been held; on 31 October 2026 the UN mission created for it, MINURSO, expired without renewal after Russia vetoed a United States draft that would have gone further toward Rabat's position and a Russian draft failed — an outcome Morocco claims as victory and the Secretary-General answered by stating that the right to self-determination "did not go away" (Carnegie Endowment analysis, November 2026; UN reporting, October–November 2026). Sahrawi activists in the territory face prosecution and surveillance on the documented record of UN and rights bodies, and the Gdeim Izik defendants remain imprisoned. The Polisario Front's memorandum of 17 November 2025 disputes the Moroccan reading of Resolution 2797 itself — that it recognized Moroccan sovereignty or made autonomy the sole basis — and that dispute is recorded as live (Polisario memorandum to the Security Council, 17 November 2025).
  • The Rif is counted last, a decade on. The region whose 2016–17 protests demanded a hospital and a university still holds six of the movement's leaders in prison (Test 3), its core demands are described by regional reporting as unimplemented, and in the summer of 2026 tens of thousands of young people — many from the Rif — attempted the crossing to Ceuta (Maghreb Insider, October 2026; HuMENA, November 2026).
  • The earthquake's tail is contested, and the contest is the evidence. Survivors' organizations and local reporting dispute the completion figures: families remained in plastic tents into a third winter in December 2025 reporting, a survivor died in his tent in Asni on 25 November 2025 on civil-society accounts, and survivors protested outside parliament on the disaster's second anniversary chanting that stadium spending moved faster than their housing; the government states that all tents have been dismantled and remaining cases are individual (Reuters, September 2025; IFRC operation update, May 2026; survivor-group statements, 2025). Both counts are printed because the gap between them is precisely what Test 4 measures: the state's accounting closes the file; the counted say it does not.
  • The young were counted as a security category in 2025–26: official youth unemployment stood at 37.7% when the GenZ 212 protests began, and the state's answer to the generation that will inherit the country included thousands of prosecutions and dozens of convicted minors (official statistics via protest chronologies, 2025; AMDH, 2026). Girls are counted unevenly: the state's own judicial council recorded 10,691 authorized child marriages in 2024 under the exception the unreformed code still permits (CSPJ figures via Moroccan legal reporting, 2026).

Why: The grade lands on MIXED, at the failing edge, because the two shelves are both structural. The welfare counting is real, universal in design, and delivered at a scale — 24 million insured, millions of households on direct aid, poverty nearly halved in a decade — that few seats in this series match; a FAILS grade would have to pretend it away. The political counting-out is equally real: a territory held at 4 of 100 whose promised referendum is now orphaned of its UN mission, a region whose leaders sit in prison ten years after asking for a hospital, and a youth cohort processed through the courts. The seat counts everyone as a beneficiary and several someones not at all as citizens with a claim; the king's own July 2025 phrase — no place for "a Morocco moving at two speeds" — is the standard his file is graded against, and on his own words the second speed persists.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the roughly 11 million people carried into health coverage by AMO-Tadamon and the millions more insured since 2021; households receiving direct assistance (the National Agency for Social Support records 18.2 billion dirhams of flat-rate aid paid to nearly 1.47 million households between December 2023 and December 2025, a narrower count than the government's "4 million families reached" across all aid categories — the discrepancy is recorded as contested counting, claimed by the agency's own annual report on one side and government statements on the other); the 51,000-plus households whose quake-damaged homes the state reports rebuilt; Amazigh Moroccans whose language and New Year the state now recognizes. Hurt — Sahrawis whose self-determination claim lost its UN instrument in October 2026 and whose activists face prosecution; the Rif's imprisoned leaders and their families; quake survivors still in tents on the contested tail of the official count; the GenZ 212 defendants; and girls married under judicial exception, 10,691 of them in 2024 alone.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Royal speeches, programs, and dated commitments vs. the record, 1999–2026, with the Akhannouch government's program stated as the word of the seat's own appointee.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The Sahara word — kept for eighteen years, to the letter. In 2007 the king proposed autonomy under Moroccan sovereignty as Morocco's settlement offer and held that single position through every negotiation cycle, at real diplomatic cost, until on 31 October 2025 the UN Security Council adopted Resolution 2797 — 11 votes in favor, China, Pakistan, and Russia abstaining, Algeria not participating — taking the autonomy proposal as a basis for negotiations toward a mutually acceptable solution providing for self-determination (Security Council Report, 2025; UN record). The king's speech that evening — "there is a before October 31, 2025 era, and an after" — announced Morocco would update and detail the initiative and submit it to the UN; the work is in motion at grading. Whether the resolution says what Rabat claims is contested by Polisario (Test 4); that the king's stated objective of four decades was substantially achieved is not.
  • Social protection — the reign's largest promise, delivered in substance. The king committed in 2020 to generalizing social protection to all Moroccans by 2025; the framework law followed in April 2021, AMO coverage tripled to some 24.3 million by the end of 2024, and direct assistance began flowing in December 2023 and continues (framework law 09-21, 2021; Ministry of Health and Social Protection data, 2025). His successor government has now pledged to double the minimum direct aid from 500 to 1,000 dirhams by the end of its mandate — recorded here so the record can grade it later (El Mansouri government program, 19 October 2026).
  • The 2011 constitutional word — delivered on schedule. The revision promised on 9 March 2011 was drafted, put to referendum on 1 July 2011, and promulgated; the head of government has since always been appointed from the winning party, including in 2026 when doing so removed the palace's own incumbent (Test 3). What the constitution retained for the throne is graded under Test 2; the promise to hold the referendum and appoint from the ballot was kept exactly.
  • Unity Day — a word created and immediately kept. On 4 November 2025 the king proclaimed 31 October a national holiday, Unity Day, "an occasion on which His Majesty the King grants His pardon"; on 30 October 2026 the pardon issued, benefiting 907 people (Royal Office proclamation, 4 November 2025; Ministry of Justice, 30 October 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The family code — the clearest receding word of the current reign. The revision was announced in the Throne Speech of July 2022; the king's letter of September 2023 instructed that proposals be submitted to him within a maximum of six months. The proposals arrived in December 2024 — 139 amendments, after the Supreme Council of Ulema rejected three (DNA paternity, abolition of taasib inheritance priority, inheritance between Muslims and non-Muslims) as closed by religious text. Then the file stopped: as of November 2026 no bill has been adopted by the Government Council or filed in parliament; in September 2026 the Justice Ministry confirmed it would move to the next legislature, and the incoming government's program lists it for that legislature's first year (Moroccan legal reporting, October–November 2026; Equality Now, 2026). A six-month instruction is three years old; the 2004 code — including the child-marriage exception used 10,691 times in 2024 — remains the only law.
  • The two-speeds word — named, not yet met. In his Throne Speech of 29 July 2025 the king declared there is no place for a Morocco moving at two speeds and directed integrated territorial programs covering all regions "without exception"; the 29 July 2026 speech repeated the instruction and the need for effective implementation (MAP, Throne Speeches, 29 July 2025 and 29 July 2026). The programs are launched; the territorial gaps they answer — the same gaps the GenZ 212 protests and the Rif file document — are the measure by which the word will be graded, and at grading they persist.
  • The appointee's word, which the seat owns by delegation. The Akhannouch government's 2021 program promised a million jobs, growth averaging above 4%, and a generalized middle-class expansion; it left office in October 2026 with unemployment higher than it inherited (youth unemployment 37.7%), the million-jobs target unmet on any published count, and its leader's party reduced from 102 seats to 66 — the electorate's own grading, executed by the king without interference (government program, 2021; official statistics, 2025; election results, September 2026). Under the mandate rule, none of that makes governing on the program a failure; it makes the program's unkept numbers part of this seat's Test 5 file, because the king chose the man, kept him for five years, and presented the program as the monarchy's own social project in speech after speech.
  • The pardon pattern as a word problem. Successive Throne Speeches have framed the monarchy as the guarantor of rights and freedoms; against that standing word stand the files of Tests 1–3 — Zefzafi in year ten of a sentence the UN's experts call arbitrary, Ziane's additional sentence, Mahdaoui's five proceedings — none of them touched by the pardons of July 2024 or October 2026, which together freed thousands. A guarantor's word is graded on the cases it conspicuously does not reach.

Why: The grade lands on MIXED because this seat's word is unusually reliable where the objective is the state's — territory, coverage, calendars — and unusually elastic where the objective would bind the palace or its chosen people. The Sahara position was held for eighteen years until the Security Council moved to it; the social-protection generalization, a promise most governments would have re-dated, was substantially delivered on time; elections and appointments follow the announced rule even when they cost the incumbent. Against that stand a family-code deadline that dissolved without acknowledgment, a territorial-equality pledge restated annually while the gap it names persists, and an appointee's program whose headline numbers were never met and never owned as missed. The word is kept where the king is the author of both the promise and the deadline's enforcement; it recedes where keeping it would require the palace to grade itself.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the insured and assisted millions of the social-protection file (Test 4); Moroccan diplomacy's stakeholders and the southern provinces' residents positioned under the autonomy track; every voter whose ballot has seated the government the constitution describes. Hurt — women and girls living under the unreformed 2004 code, including the 10,691 minors married by judicial exception in 2024; workers who planned around the 2021 program's jobs and growth numbers; the Hirak and press defendants whose exclusion from successive pardons is itself a communicated decision; and Sahrawi claimants, for whom the king's most faithfully kept word is the instrument of their claim's defeat — recorded on both axes, as the method requires: the keeping is credited here, the hurt stands in the ledger, and neither launders the other.

The four crossings, named where they occur in this file: 1. Kept his word AND it helped people: the social-protection generalization (promised 2020, delivered in substance by 2025) and the Unity Day pardon (proclaimed 2025, exercised 2026). 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt people: the Sahara autonomy position, held without deviation for eighteen years to its 2025–26 vindication — a kept word whose success is the Polisario claim's defeat and the Tindouf camps' continued exile; and the standing word that sentences lawfully pronounced will stand, kept in the GenZ 212 and Hirak files at the cost recorded in Tests 1 and 3. 3. Went against his word AND it helped people: the October 2025 sequence — the palace's public position, restated in the king's own speech, was that street pressure sets no agenda and national projects concede nothing to social programs; within nine days the government he chairs had moved roughly 140 billion dirhams toward the street's two demands and subsidized the young candidates the movement's generation asked for. The softening contradicted the stated posture and materially helped the protesters' substantive goals; both facts are printed. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt people: the family-code revision — a royal initiative with a royal six-month deadline, unbuilt three years later, while the unreformed code's exceptions continue to be used thousands of times a year.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Mohammed VI is graded as what he is: not a constitutional figurehead but the seat itself — head of a state whose elected government administers a program the palace sets, inside a constitution that makes him head of the judiciary's council, the armed forces, and the religious field, pardon in hand and answerable to no one. The file that results is two files. In the first, he is among the most effective holders in this series: a fifty-year territorial claim carried to a Security Council resolution that takes his 2007 autonomy proposal as the basis for settlement; a social-protection generalization that tripled health coverage to some 24 million people and put monthly cash into millions of households; elections that run on time and are honored when they remove his own appointees — most recently in September 2026, when the palace let its billionaire head of government fall, appointed the kingdom's first woman to the post within six days, and handed over power without a murmur; and, in the drought file, a no-good-choice decision announced early, reasoned in public, and aimed at the least-harm door. In the second file, the same seat runs a two-track justice: journalists prosecuted under the penal code by a sitting minister's complaints while the head of government's own conflicts were structurally ungradeable; some 2,480 young protesters prosecuted in a season, roughly 650 still imprisoned a year later with no investigation of the three who were shot; the Rif's leaders in their tenth year of sentences the UN's working group calls arbitrary, passed over by pardons that freed thousands of others; a family-code reform, promised on a six-month royal deadline, asleep for three years; and a disputed territory counted at 4 of 100 whose referendum mission expired in October 2026. The two FAILS are Tests 1 and 2, and they are the same fact seen twice: where the law and the referees would bind the palace, the palace is the law and the referees' chairman. The MIXED grades are equally specific — he answers the wobble with money and ballots but never with accountability; he counts everyone as a beneficiary and not everyone as a citizen; his word is iron on the state's objectives and elastic on his own deadlines. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the El Mansouri government is permitted to govern or is managed; whether the family-code bill is filed in the new legislature's first year as now promised; whether the GenZ 212 prisoners and the Hirak six are released; whether the updated autonomy initiative is submitted to the UN and what it offers the Sahrawis it counts; and whether the two-speeds instruction of July 2025 is ever followed by a published accounting of the gap it names.
