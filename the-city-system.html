War Strategies — campaigns are systems too

A city and a campaign run on the same hidden machinery: supply lines instead of streets, morale instead of neglect, intelligence instead of police attention. Battles get the paintings. Systems win or lose the war around them. This War Room teaches strategy at the map level — history, incentives and logistics — as abstract choices. It does not teach real-world tactics, weapons, or operations.

War Room — play a campaign in 6 turns

Pick a campaign, spend 10 effort points, choose one doctrine, then resolve the turn. The opponent adapts: repeat the same doctrine and it stops working. Starve supply or morale and the campaign collapses no matter how bold the plan was.

The one-line version

Amateurs talk battles. Systems talk supply.

A force that cannot eat, move, communicate or believe in what it is doing stops being a force — whatever its courage. That is why this page grades every doctrine by what it does to supply, morale and information first, and ground second.

Why does this belong on a city page? Because Harbor is a supply problem, gang territory is a morale-and-legitimacy problem, and police attention is an intelligence problem — at street scale. Learn the shape once, see it everywhere. That is The Flow Is One Knowledge, applied.

Read the opponent

The opponent in this War Room does three honest things real opponents do:

1. Adapts — your repeated doctrine loses power.
2. Attacks your weakest system — low supply or low morale gets punished.
3. Trades ground for time when losing — so a fast early gain can still lose a long campaign.

The War Playbook — seven principles, each with its failure mode

1. Logistics first

What: Food, fuel, transport and repair decide how far and how long any force can operate.

Why it works: effort cannot be spent twice; a unit at the end of a broken supply line has courage but no options.

When it fails: when leaders treat supply as paperwork and spend it all on the visible fight. In the War Room: watch what a frontal turn does to Supply.

2. Terrain is a multiplier

What: Rivers, hills, streets and weather multiply or divide the same force.

Why it works: ground channels movement and visibility — the defender who chose it starts ahead before anything happens.

When it fails: attacking prepared ground head-on because it is the shortest line on the map. Shortest ≠ cheapest.

3. Concentrate at the decisive point

What: Be strong where it decides, even if that means being thin elsewhere.

Why it works: spreading evenly is strong nowhere; outcomes are usually decided locally, not on average.

When it fails: when “decisive point” is guessed without intel — concentration on the wrong place is just a bigger mistake, faster.

4. Information & deception

What: Know more than the opponent, and let them confidently know something false.

Why it works: every plan is a bet on a picture of the opponent. A wrong picture makes even a strong force spend itself in the wrong place.

When it fails: when deception is used once too often — opponents learn, and a seen-through feint costs the force you split off for it.

5. Morale & legitimacy

What: People hold, advance or quit based on whether they believe the cause, the plan and each other.

Why it works: morale is not decoration — it is the system that turns supply and numbers into actual behaviour under stress.

When it fails: when victories are bought by spending trust. It does not refill at the depot. In the city above, this is the same system as community presence vs. gang recruitment.

6. Attrition vs. manoeuvre

What: Two grand shapes: wear the opponent down (attrition) or make their position useless (manoeuvre).

Why each works: attrition favours whoever has deeper supply and can afford the exchange; manoeuvre favours whoever has better intel and mobility.

When it fails: picking the shape your resources don't support — attrition on a short supply line, or manoeuvre blind. Most real disasters are this mismatch.

7. The political end — and the exit

What: Wars end politically, not just militarily: terms, exhaustion, legitimacy and outside pressure close them.

Why it matters: “Winning” ground without an end you can actually hold is how a campaign victory becomes a longer problem.

When it fails: when no one defined what “done” looks like. The best possible outcome is re-asked whenever information changes — negotiate from the position you have, not the one you wish for.

History, graded honestly

Well supported Cannae (216 BCE) is the classic encirclement taught for two millennia: a weaker centre that gave ground drew the opponent in while strength closed from the sides — manoeuvre + deception, not raw numbers.

Well supported Long campaigns repeatedly turn on supply — armies that outran their food and transport shrank without losing a headline battle.

Contested “One genius general / one decisive battle won the war” stories are usually compressed: they leave out supply, allies, money and politics that made the battle matter. Enjoy the story; grade it as a story.

Tap to open · Why the opponent adapting is the whole lesson

Static puzzles teach you to find the answer. Opponents remove that comfort: any pattern you repeat becomes information for the other side. That is true in campaigns, in markets, and in the district game above — a stall run the same way every day draws attention the same way every day. Strategy is not a move. It is a sequence played against someone who is learning you while you learn them.

Tap to open · Friction: why no plan survives contact — graded plainly

Historians and theorists call it friction: weather, fatigue, misunderstanding, delay and chance pile up until the real operation barely resembles the plan. This War Room models a small, honest slice of it — small random swings, supply burn, and morale drift. The lesson is not “planning is useless.” It is the opposite: plan for the system, keep reserves, keep an exit, and never build a plan that only works if everything goes right.

How each system works — one tap down

Tap to open · Economy: wages, prices & the gap between districts

Work pays the local wage; goods cost the local price. Heights pays $34 and charges $18; Harbor pays $20 and charges $8. Buying in Harbor and selling in Heights earns the gap — that is arbitrage, and it is also why money tends to flow out of poor districts unless someone deliberately spends it there. Your community hour is the opposite flow: time spent where it earns nothing cash, but changes the block.

Tap to open · Police attention: a system, not a score for its own sake

Attention rises when visible rule-breaking happens where patrols can see it, and falls with time and lawful behaviour. At 3+ stars, an interception becomes likely each hour: the consequence in this model is a fine and lost time — the two costs real systems actually impose most often. Notice who fines hurt most: $40 is an annoyance in Heights and a week’s margin in Harbor. Same rule, unequal weight — that asymmetry is the lesson.

Tap to open · Neglect, wealth & “broken windows” — graded honestly

Contested claim The “broken windows” idea says visible disorder invites more disorder. This simulation includes a mild version: high neglect slowly raises gang influence and lowers safety. Honest grading: the strong version of the claim — that cracking down on minor disorder causes major-crime drops — is contested in real research; cities that cleaned up also changed many other things at once. What is well supported is narrower: neglected places get fewer services, fewer customers, and less investment, and that feedback loop is real whether or not the strong claim holds.

Tap to open · Territory & gangs: incentives, not monsters

Gang influence here is a number driven by inputs: it grows where neglect is high and lawful income is scarce (recruitment is easier when the lawful path pays least and slowest), and shrinks when neglect falls and community presence rises. That framing matters: if influence is produced by conditions, changing conditions changes influence. Enforcement alone, in this model, only pauses it — patrols raise safety while they last, then the inputs reassert themselves.

Tap to open · Feedback loops: the whole game in one paragraph

Wealth → investment → lower neglect → higher safety → more customers → more wealth. And the mirror: poverty → neglect → low safety → no customers → deeper poverty. Most city policy, good and bad, is an attempt to push a district from one loop into the other. Watch your own log: nearly everything you do nudges one of those arrows.
