The Ruler's Test — Mike Johnson, Speaker of the United States House of Representatives

Subject: Mike Johnson (R-LA) — 56th Speaker of the United States House of Representatives since 25 October 2023. The seat graded is the Speakership itself, and the framing is stated plainly: the Speaker is not a president or a prime minister. The office cannot sign or veto laws, command agencies or armies, or enact anything alone. What it actually holds is agenda control — what the House votes on and what it never votes on — floor scheduling and recess, the Rules Committee lever that sets the terms of debate and which amendments, if any, are allowed, committee and Ethics-process leverage, the swearing-in of members-elect, and the majority's chief negotiating position against the Senate and the White House. He is graded on his exercise of those powers, not on his whole career as a member, except where that record bears directly on the office. Premise verified for November 2026: Johnson still holds the gavel today. He was elected on 25 October 2023 by 220–209 after the House removed Kevin McCarthy, and re-elected on 3 January 2025 by 218–215 for the 119th Congress (House records and contemporaneous reporting; Speaker's office, 2026). In the midterm elections of 3 November 2026 the voters removed his majority: the Associated Press count in mid-November gave Democrats at least 256–259 House seats against roughly 169–176 Republicans, with a small number of races still uncalled and outlets reporting slightly different running totals because they call races at different times — the conflicting counts are printed here as separate sourced figures, never blended (AP election results, November 2026; NBC News projection, November 2026; Taft congressional update, 5 November 2026). Johnson won re-election in his own Louisiana district with about 58% (Shreveport Times, 3 November 2026), announced on 4 November 2026 that he would not seek to remain House Republican leader (Associated Press via AURN and Gray, 4 November 2026), and on 10 November 2026 congratulated the Republicans' new leadership team for the next Congress, led by Rep. Jim Jordan as minority leader (Speaker Johnson statement, 10 November 2026). House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries is Speaker-designate for the 120th Congress — chosen by his caucus in November 2026 and appearing as "Speaker-Designate" on CBS's Face the Nation on 22 November 2026 — but he is not Speaker until the full House elects him when the new Congress convenes on 3 January 2027 (House Democratic Leader release, 22 November 2026; Hobbs Straus memorandum 26-049, November 2026). Johnson therefore remains Speaker through the lame-duck session, until noon on 3 January 2027 — a point he himself made to his members in his 20 November 2026 attendance letter (Johnson "Dear Colleague" letter, 20 November 2026, via USA Today reporting). Because his tenure is effectively complete — three years and one month, with only the lame-duck weeks left — this dossier grades the substantial, essentially finished record, in the series' completed-tenure handling, and states that timing plainly rather than pretending January changes the file. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is NOT inherently a failure and is never graded as one — carrying out that platform is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for HOW it is done: a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Santos expulsion was allowed to reach the floor as a vote of conscience, unwhipped. George Santos (R-NY), facing 23 federal criminal charges and a House Ethics Committee investigation, was expelled on 1 December 2023 by 311–114 — only the sixth expulsion in House history. Johnson said he was concerned about the precedent of expelling a member not yet convicted, said he would not push members either way, and in the end voted himself, with the rest of Republican leadership, against expulsion — but he did not whip the vote, and 105 Republicans voted to expel (NPR, 1 December 2023). An ally was removed under the same process that removes anyone, over the Speaker's own stated preference.
  • In his first Congress he repeatedly brought broadly bipartisan bills to the floor under suspension of the rules — government funding and the April 2024 national-security package among them — knowing they would pass with more Democratic than Republican votes, rather than holding them to a majority-of-the-majority rule that would have applied a different, partisan-only law to what could pass (contemporaneous reporting, March–April 2024; see Tests 3 and 5).
  • The FY26 appropriations process he completed in January 2026 was, on the Appropriations Committee's own account, committee-led and bipartisan-bicameral in negotiation, with the Democratic ranking appropriator's cooperation publicly credited by Chairman Tom Cole (House Appropriations Committee, January 2026; Speaker Johnson remarks, 22 January 2026). The same funding law applied to districts of both parties by formula and markup, not by loyalty list, on the documented record assembled here.

Evidence against:

  • The clearest two-laws record in the file is the seating of members-elect. Republicans Jimmy Patronis and Randy Fine, special-election winners in Florida, were sworn in by Johnson during pro forma sessions within 24 hours of their elections in April 2025 (Rep. Greg Stanton letter to Johnson, October 2025; Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee materials, October 2025). Democrat Adelita Grijalva, who won Arizona's special election on 23 September 2025, was not sworn in for 50 days — until 12 November 2025 — leaving more than 800,000 Arizonans without representation (Arizona lawsuit reporting, CNN and NBC News, 21 October 2025; Grijalva's own account, December 2025). Johnson attributed the delay to the government shutdown and said it had nothing to do with the Epstein files; critics, including Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes, who sued, alleged the delay existed because Grijalva's signature would be the 218th on the discharge petition forcing an Epstein-files vote — that motive is recorded here as an ALLEGATION by named critics, disputed by Johnson, not as a finding. The differential treatment itself — 24 hours for two Republicans, 50 days for one Democrat, under the same pro forma mechanism he had personally used — is documented fact, and no neutral rule that produces both numbers has been stated on the record.
  • The Ethics referee was asked to protect an ally. In November 2024, after former Rep. Matt Gaetz resigned upon being nominated for attorney general, Johnson said he would "strongly request" that the House Ethics Committee not release its investigation report into Gaetz — allegations of sexual misconduct, illicit drug use, and improper gifts, all ALLEGATION-level as to Gaetz — reversing the position he had taken two days earlier that "the Speaker of the House is not involved in that and can't be involved in that" (Associated Press reporting, 15 November 2024). His stated ground was a real rule — former members are beyond the committee's jurisdiction — but the intervention was for a presidential ally facing Senate confirmation, senators of both parties were asking for the report to vet the nomination, and the House then voted 206–198, and again 204–198, to refer the release resolutions back to committee, burying the report (House votes, December 2024). The report was eventually released by the committee itself later that month; the Speaker's ask, and the floor votes that backed it, stand as the record of the attempt.
  • The Epstein-files votes were blocked for months by procedural law applied to one subject. Through 2025, House Republican leadership blocked floor consideration of the bipartisan Epstein-files release effort — including a 211–210 procedural vote on 15 July 2025 — while Johnson stated, "I have always been for full transparency of the Epstein files" (House Roll Call 194, 15 July 2025; Johnson statement, November 2025). Only when the discharge petition reached 218 signatures over his opposition did the bill come to the floor, on 18 November 2025, passing 427–1; Johnson voted for it while saying he was "deeply disappointed" it had not been amended with the protections he sought, protections Senate Majority Leader John Thune then declined to add, judging the bill "sufficient" (Politico congressional reporting, 18 November 2025). A second discharge petition — for an enforcement bill, the Epstein Files Transparency Act II — reached 218 signatures on 11 November 2026, again over the Speaker's objections, with a floor vote expected in December 2026 (Massie petition reporting, November 2026). The pattern is the same in both Congresses' files: a transparency rule for subjects the leadership favors, a procedural wall for this one.

Why: The grade lands at FAILS because the differential cases are not close calls on neutral ground: the same swearing-in mechanism moved in a day for allies and in fifty days for an opponent; the Ethics process was personally lobbied for an ally after a public promise of non-involvement; and one bipartisan transparency bill needed the House's rarest end-run, twice, to get the vote every other bill gets by scheduling. The FOR shelf is real — Santos was expelled unwhipped, bipartisan bills did move — but those are cases of Johnson declining to use the lever, not cases of the lever being applied evenly. One law means the rule, not the restraint, is even.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: former Rep. Matt Gaetz, whose Ethics report was delayed at the moment of his attorney-general nomination (he later withdrew); President Donald Trump, spared an Epstein-files floor fight for months in 2025 and again in late 2026; Republican members spared recorded votes on the files and, in the Santos case, free to vote their conscience.
  • Hurt: Rep. Adelita Grijalva and the more than 800,000 residents of Arizona's 7th District, unrepresented for 50 days (Arizona lawsuit, October 2025); Epstein survivors and the public seeking the files, whose bipartisan bill waited on a discharge petition rather than a schedule; the Senate's vetting process in November 2024, which was denied the House's own investigative file on a cabinet nominee.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized? For this seat the referees are the House's own checks: the Ethics Committee, the discharge petition, the National Emergencies Act fast-track, the committee system, and the House itself as a check on the executive.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The committee system was, by the end, genuinely handed power he could have kept. Chairman Tom Cole stated on the record that leadership "pushed the work down to us and out of leadership offices down to the committee level, just as the Speaker said he would do," crediting Johnson for all 12 FY26 appropriations bills passing the House in January 2026 — "This Speaker is the reason these twelve bills happened" (House Appropriations Committee, January 2026). Whatever the earlier record (Test 5), the final appropriations cycle ran through the referees, not around them.
  • He submitted to the House's own removal mechanism and let it decide. When Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene filed and then triggered a motion to vacate in March–May 2024, Johnson did not change the rule mid-fight or refuse the vote; the House tabled the motion 359–43 on 8 May 2024, with 196 Republicans and 163 Democrats voting to keep him (NPR/WOSU reporting, 8 May 2024). He survived by the chamber's verdict, including the other party's votes, not by disabling the instrument.
  • The discharge petition — the rank-and-file's constitutional end-run around a Speaker — was never abolished or rule-changed out of existence on his watch, even though it was used against him successfully in November 2025 and again in November 2026 (see Test 1). The instrument survived its own success.
  • No documented act in the record assembled here shows Johnson defying a court order, punishing the Congressional Budget Office for adverse scores — CBO's estimates that his signature law added $3.4 trillion to deficits and 10 million to the uninsured were published and debated, not suppressed (CBO estimate as enacted, July 2025) — or retaliating against media coverage; critical coverage of his speakership ran continuously and unimpeded throughout.

Evidence against:

  • The Ethics Committee's independence was gripped directly, by his own description. The "strongly request" on the Gaetz report (Test 1) was, as the Associated Press noted at the time, a highly unusual intervention in a panel that has traditionally operated independently — made more pointed by his statement two days earlier that a Speaker "can't be involved in that" (AP, 15 November 2024). A referee who is lobbied by the Speaker, on the record, in the ally's confirmation week, is not being held without gripping, whatever the jurisdictional argument's merits.
  • The House's statutory check on the executive was switched off by redefining time. In March 2025, leadership inserted into a routine rule (H.Res. 211, adopted 216–214 on 11 March 2025) a provision that "each day for the remainder of the first session of the 119th Congress shall not constitute a calendar day" for purposes of section 202 of the National Emergencies Act — freezing the fast-track clock that guarantees the House a vote on ending the emergency behind President Trump's 1 February 2025 tariffs (Rules Committee record, 10 March 2025; New York Times, 11 March 2025). Successor rules extended and adjusted the freeze (H.Res. 313; H.Res. 707; H.Res. 722), with an eventual expiry of 31 January 2026 reached only after internal Republican dissent forced a compromise in September 2025 (KIPEC current-affairs note, 29 January 2026). When the clock finally ran, the House voted on 11 February 2026 to end that emergency, 219–211, with six Republicans crossing over — proof the check existed and had been withheld for eleven months. Separately, on 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that the emergency trade law did not authorize those tariffs at all (Supreme Court ruling, 20 February 2026, via contemporaneous legal reporting). The Speaker used the House's rules power not to check the executive but to shield the executive from the House.
  • During the 43-day government shutdown of October–November 2025 — the longest full shutdown on record — Johnson kept the House in recess with no votes for the entire period, on 24-hour notice to return, while the Senate and the White House negotiated (Johnson statement, 20 October 2025; House record, October–November 2025). The House's leverage as a negotiating referee was voluntarily surrendered: the chamber that alone can originate revenue bills sat out its own shutdown, and returned on 12 November 2025 to pass the Senate-shaped ending bill 222–209. Democrats and some commentators criticized the recess as deference to the president; Johnson's stated rationale was pressure on Senate Democrats to accept the House-passed stopgap — recorded as his stated reason, with the institutional effect, a House not in session, undisputed.
  • The Speaker lent the office's weight against a court proceeding. On 14 May 2024 Johnson appeared at the Manhattan courthouse during Donald Trump's criminal trial, called it a "sham" and a "travesty of justice," and declared the defendant — then a candidate, later president again — innocent and "a friend" (Reuters and contemporaneous reporting, 14 May 2024). POLITICO reported in June 2024 that Trump privately demanded Johnson use Congress to "overturn" the resulting conviction, and that Johnson, who already believed the House had a role, discussed legislative responses including targeting prosecutors and the special counsel through appropriations (POLITICO, 13 June 2024). A Speaker is free to hold an opinion; standing outside a courtroom in office to delegitimize a live prosecution of his party's leader, then exploring the appropriations lever against prosecutors, is the office's weight on the referee's scale.

Why: The grade lands at FAILS because the two clearest referee cases — the Ethics Committee and the National Emergencies Act clock — were both gripped by the Speaker's own hand, on the record, in the executive's or an ally's interest, and the shutdown recess removed the House itself from the board for 43 days. The FOR shelf shows a Speaker who, late and under pressure, let committees work and let the removal and discharge instruments live; it does not outweigh documented episodes of the grip itself. Under this Test, declining to abolish a check is not the same as not gripping it.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: President Trump, shielded from a House tariff-termination vote for eleven months and defended at his trial by the second official in the line of succession; the executive branch generally, which negotiated the shutdown's end with the Senate while the House sat out.
  • Hurt: the House as an institution — its members denied the emergency-termination vote the statute promises them, and its bargaining position in the shutdown; the Ethics Committee's tradition of independence; farmers and exporters in tariff-exposed districts (for example Minnesota's 7th District, with roughly $9 billion in agricultural products a year and Canada as its state's largest export market) whose representatives could not force the vote their law provided for (Rules Committee and district reporting, 2025–2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, he at least considered it openly. Considering it and declining with stated reasons is NOT against-evidence. Refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Ukraine reversal is the series' clearest case of a wobble answered in the open. Johnson entered the speakership having voted against earlier Ukraine aid as a member, and for months in early 2024 held the line that no further aid would move without border security — declaring the bipartisan Senate border-and-aid package "dead on arrival" in February 2024 and refusing a vote on the Senate's $95 billion aid bill (The Dispatch, April 2024; contemporaneous reporting, February 2024). If another option became available — separate aid bills, moved with Democratic votes, without the border measure — he then took it, openly and at known cost to himself: in April 2024 he brought the Ukraine, Israel, and Taiwan bills to the floor, saying "I believe to be the right thing… to allow the House to work its will" and "you do the right thing, and you let the chips fall where they may," and telling CNN, "We're going to stand for freedom and make sure that Vladimir Putin doesn't march through Europe" (The Dispatch, April 2024). The package passed; Greene's motion to vacate followed within weeks, and he survived it. The adjustment was public, reasoned, and personally expensive — exactly what this test asks for.
  • The voters' verdict of 3 November 2026 was accepted without denial or maneuver. Johnson took public responsibility — "real leadership… leaders have to take responsibility when things go wrong, whether it's deserved or not" — declined to seek the minority leadership, congratulated Rep. Jim Jordan's new leadership team on 10 November 2026, and stated he would remain in the fight "from a role different than as minority leader" (Johnson interview, Fox News, 16 November 2026; Speaker Johnson statement, 10 November 2026). No challenge to the results, no attempt to hold the gavel past 3 January 2027, appears in the record.
  • The tariff freeze was itself partially unwound under pushback from his own side: when Republican dissent grew in September 2025, leadership compromised on a 31 January 2026 expiry rather than the longer extension it had sought, and the termination vote then happened (KIPEC note, 29 January 2026; House vote, 11 February 2026). Adjustment inside his own conference, openly arrived at.

Evidence against:

  • The shutdown wobble was doubled down on for 43 days. The pushback was specific and bipartisan in origin: Senate Democrats and House Democrats demanded negotiation over expiring Affordable Care Act subsidies, and a bipartisan one-year extension bill existed — an option Rep. Mike Lawler publicly pressed on Leader Jeffries in October 2025, and one Johnson's House never took up, because the House was not in session to take up anything (contemporaneous reporting, 8 October 2025). Johnson's answer throughout was that the House had done its job in September and the Senate must act; the House stayed in recess, no negotiation forum was opened in his chamber, and the shutdown ended on the Senate's terms. If another option became available — a House-negotiated extension, a shorter stopgap with talks attached — the record shows it was not considered openly in the House; it was not looked at in the House at all, because the House had been sent home.
  • On the Epstein files, the adjustment came only when the mechanism left him no choice, and the reasons given shifted. Across 2025 his explanations moved between the Oversight Committee's ongoing investigation, the shutdown (for the Grijalva seating), and the need for victim-protection amendments — amendments the Senate's leader then reviewed and declined as unnecessary (Politico, 18 November 2025). When the discharge petition forced the vote, he voted yes and called himself "deeply disappointed" at the unamended bill. A wobble answered by the petition is an answer the institution gave, not one the Speaker chose; and in November 2026, faced with the second petition, his position had not adjusted — it reached 218 signatures over his objections again.
  • The lame-duck attendance collapse was met with exhortation, not redesign. In November 2026, with his majority expiring, 14 Republicans skipped a procedural vote on a Wednesday (forcing its cancellation) and 22 were absent the next day, and Johnson tabled Friday's votes and sent members home for Thanksgiving after a "Dear Colleague" letter pleading for attendance (USA Today reporting via Colorado Pols, 24 November 2026; Johnson letter, 20 November 2026). Rep. Mike Simpson warned the remaining agenda could be squandered; a departing member publicly replied that nothing scheduled outweighed leaving. The letter is an open acknowledgment of the problem — but the scheduling lever, the one tool this office wholly owns, was used to end the week's votes rather than to force the question.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because both shapes are present at full strength and neither cancels the other. On Ukraine he did the hardest thing this test measures — reversed himself in public, for stated reasons, knowing it might cost him the gavel. On the electorate's 2026 verdict he adjusted instantly and gracefully. On the shutdown and the Epstein files, the two longest wobbles of the tenure, he refused to look at the available option in his own chamber until the chamber's mechanisms, or the Senate, decided without him. The record is genuinely two-sided, and the grade says so.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: Ukraine, Israel, and Taiwan, whose April 2024 aid passed because he changed his mind in the open; the House's credibility in November 2026, where a defeated Speaker's prompt acceptance and congratulation of successors kept the transfer ordinary; Republican members in September 2025 whose dissent shortened the tariff freeze.
  • Hurt: federal workers and benefit recipients who went 43 days without pay or full services while the House sat out the negotiation (shutdown record, October–November 2025); people relying on the expiring ACA subsidies, whose bipartisan one-year extension never received a House hearing during the shutdown; Epstein survivors, whose vote waited for a petition rather than a Speaker's reconsideration.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include those who did not elect the majority — the minority party's voters, the poor, the unrepresented — or are some counted out? The platform itself is not graded here; the counting is.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The completed FY26 appropriations — all 12 bills passed by the House in January 2026, six already signed into law at the time of the announcement — funded the whole government's services for every district, through a process the committee describes as bipartisan and bicameral, replacing stopgap funding that serves no one well (House Appropriations Committee, January 2026; Speaker Johnson statement, 22 January 2026). Keeping the state funded through regular bills is the baseline form of counting everyone, and in the final cycle he delivered it.
  • The April 2024 national-security package counted people outside his electorate entirely: aid to Ukraine, Israel, and Taiwan passed his House with Democratic votes over his own conference's majority objection (The Dispatch, April 2024). Whatever its motive, the act treated allied populations under attack as within the accounting.
  • Ceremonial counting was done across the aisle and without condition: on 11 November 2026, after his party's defeat, Johnson led a bipartisan, all-member Veterans Day tribute on the House steps (Speaker Johnson release, 11 November 2026). Small, but on the record, and in the lame-duck weeks when nothing required it.

Evidence against:

  • For 50 days, 800,000-plus people were counted out of the House entirely. The Grijalva seating delay (Tests 1–2) is also a Test 4 fact: Arizona's 7th District had no vote, no casework channel, and no voice in the chamber from 23 September to 12 November 2025, during a shutdown whose costs fell on exactly such constituents, while every Republican district seated that year was represented within a day (Arizona lawsuit, October 2025; Stanton letter, October 2025). No other district in his tenure was asked to wait.
  • The signature law's own scorekeeper counts the burden downward. The Congressional Budget Office's estimate of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act as enacted projects 10 million more people without health insurance in 2034 and $3.4 trillion added to deficits over 2025–2034, with $1.06 trillion of reduced federal spending from Medicaid and marketplace changes, the largest single piece being Medicaid work requirements at $325.6 billion (CBO, July 2025, via American Hospital Association summary, 21 July 2025). CBO's distributional analysis, counting tax and benefit changes together, put the bottom tenth of households at roughly $1,200 a year worse off on average over 2026–2034 and the top tenth roughly $13,600 a year better off (CBO, Distributional Effects of Public Law 119-21, 11 August 2025). An earlier House-passed version was scored at $2.4 trillion added deficits and 10.9 million more uninsured (CBO, June 2025) — the figures are printed separately because they score different versions, and both point the same way. The mandate rule is honored here: extending the 2017 tax cuts and tightening Medicaid eligibility were the platform, and the platform is not the failure. What Test 4 grades is that the law's net accounting, on the neutral scorekeeper's numbers, takes most from the households with least — including millions who never voted for the majority — while the Speaker's public framing described it as delivering for "all hard-working Americans" (House Republican leadership statement, 3 July 2025). The counted-out were described as counted in.
  • The shutdown's costs were distributed to people with no seat at his table, while his chamber was absent: federal employees worked unpaid or were furloughed for 43 days, and the ACA subsidy cliff — the substantive dispute — fell on marketplace enrollees, disproportionately lower-income, whose extension his House never voted on during the closure (shutdown record, October–November 2025; see Test 3).
  • The District of Columbia's residents — some 700,000 people with no voting member in his chamber — were governed over, repeatedly: through 2025 Johnson's House passed bills codifying the president's executive order on D.C. crime and reversing D.C.'s own enacted policies, with Johnson framing it as Congress "confronting the crime crisis in our nation's capital" (Speaker Johnson statements, November 2025). Congress's constitutional authority over the District is not in dispute; Test 4 asks only whether the people who live there were in the accounting, and the documented record shows their elected local government's positions overridden as a stated objective, not weighed as a cost.

Why: The grade lands at FAILS because the two heaviest items are not policy disagreements but accounting exclusions: a district left voiceless for fifty days by the office's own delay, and a signature law whose official score shows the losses concentrated on the poorest tenth — many of them outside his majority's electorate — while being sold as a gain for all. The FOR shelf (full-year funding for everyone, allied aid, bipartisan ceremony) is real but general; it does not answer the specific counts. A Speaker counts everyone when the people who cannot remove him still appear in his ledger as costs; here, in the biggest files, they appeared as savings.

Helped / Hurt ledger:

  • Helped: higher-income households, who gain most under the enacted law on CBO's distribution table (top tenth +$13,600 a year on average); taxpayers and service users in all districts from the completed FY26 funding; Ukraine, Israel, and Taiwan in April 2024.
  • Hurt: Medicaid and marketplace enrollees — 10 million more uninsured projected by 2034, 7.5 million of them from the Medicaid changes alone (CBO/KFF summary, 2025); the bottom tenth of households (−$1,200 a year on average, CBO, August 2025); the 800,000-plus residents of Arizona's 7th District during the seating delay; District of Columbia residents whose local laws were overridden; federal workers during the 43-day shutdown.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 25 October 2023, with dates. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes: keeping a word can still hurt, and breaking one can still help, and both are recorded in full.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The 2024 platform, delivered — the mandate rule's core case. Republicans won the 119th Congress on President Trump's America First platform, and Johnson's House carried it: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act passed the House 215–214 on 22 May 2025 and, after Senate changes, 218–214 on 3 July 2025, signed 4 July 2025 — permanently extending the 2017 tax cuts, funding border security and defense, and enacting Medicaid work requirements (House votes, May and July 2025; House Republican leadership statement, 3 July 2025). He had promised his conference delivery by Memorial Day for the first passage and met it, and promised the president a bill by 4 July and met that. Carrying out what the voters elected is FOR-shelf evidence by the method's own rule, whatever the law's merits (graded at Tests 1 and 4, not here).
  • Regular order in appropriations — kept, late. Johnson committed on taking the gavel to restore a committee-led, member-driven appropriations process and end omnibus government-by-crisis. In January 2026 the House passed all 12 FY26 bills individually, without an omnibus or a further continuing resolution, and Chairman Cole credited the Speaker's commitment on the record (Appropriations Committee and Speaker statements, January 2026). The promise was kept in the final cycle, after two years in which it was not (see against shelf) — trajectory over any single moment is this test's standard, and the trajectory ends at kept.
  • The seven tenets, in part. His 23 October 2023 letter to colleagues set seven tenets, including advancing an agenda "supported by Conference consensus" and promoting individual members' strengths. The conference-consensus tenet was visibly honored in the April 2024 aid fight, where he let the House "work its will" against his own earlier position, and in the unwhipped Santos vote (Test 1). Other tenets are graded below.
  • Crossing 1 — kept his word AND it helped people: the FY26 appropriations completion. He promised regular order; the 12 bills passed; full-year funding certainty helped every agency, worker, and service user in both parties' districts, on the Appropriations Committee's bipartisan account.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The border-before-Ukraine word — broken, openly, and for the better. Crossing 3 — went against his word AND it helped people. Through early 2024 Johnson repeatedly vowed the House would not approve more Ukraine aid without border security, and killed the Senate's bipartisan border-and-aid package as "dead on arrival" (The Dispatch, April 2024). In April 2024 he passed the aid without the border measure. The break is recorded here in full — his conference's hardliners recorded it too, in the motion to vacate — and the help is also recorded in full: Ukraine received the $61 billion around which its defense planning turned. Test 5 charges the break; Test 3 credits the reconsideration; neither launders the other.
  • Regular order and the 72-hour rule — broken in the first cycle. In March 2024 the House passed two large "minibus" spending packages that critics inside his own conference — Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene's "Dear Colleague" letter of 9 April 2024 is the named disputant — charged were passed under suspension with roughly a day, not the promised 72 hours, to review 1,000-plus pages and no amendments, breaking the transparency and regular-order tenet of his own October 2023 letter (Greene letter, April 2024, quoting Johnson's seven tenets). Johnson's side, in substance, was that funding deadlines forced the vehicles; the 2026 completion (FOR shelf) is the answer he eventually made good. The early break stands as dated fact about the word's first year.
  • "Always for full transparency" on Epstein — a word the record contradicts. Crossing 4 — broke his word AND it hurt people. Johnson's stated position was that he had "always been for full transparency of the Epstein files" (Johnson statement, November 2025). The same record shows months of procedural blocks (211–210, July 2025), opposition to the discharge petition in both 2025 and 2026, and a vote for the bill only after the petition forced it — with his stated disappointment that it passed unamended (Tests 1 and 3). The people hurt by the delay are named at Test 1: survivors and the public, whose law then, on the 2026 petition's documented account, missed its own 30-day release deadline at the Justice Department, with some 2.5 million pages still withheld per Democratic members' statements — figures recorded as the petitioners' account, contested by the department's position that it is reviewing and producing records (Epstein Files Transparency Act II petition reporting, November 2026).
  • Fiscal responsibility — the platform's word vs. the scorekeeper. Crossing 2 — kept his word AND it still hurt people. "Fiscal responsibility" is one of the seven core principles Johnson restated to his conference as late as 10 November 2026 (Speaker Johnson remarks, 10 November 2026).
