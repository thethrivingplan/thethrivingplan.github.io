The Ruler's Test — Kim Jong Un (North Korea)

Subject: Kim Jong Un, Supreme Leader of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (in power December 2011 – present)
Graded: November 2026
Standard: Documented public evidence only. Both shelves shown where a real second shelf exists. Allegations and defector testimony are labeled as such. Findings of the UN Commission of Inquiry are described as that body's findings, not as court verdicts.

Evidence note (read first): North Korea is a closed country. The UN Commission of Inquiry was denied access and built its findings on victim and witness testimony, satellite imagery, and North Korea's own published materials. South Korean intelligence (NIS) claims about internal purges have sometimes later proved wrong. Where a claim rests on a single or contestable source, it is labeled. North Korea's own statements are sourced to KCNA as reported by international wire services.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Is there one law applied to all, or a hereditary caste system and elite privilege?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence against (one law for all):

  • Every North Korean is assigned a hereditary songbun classification — three classes (core/loyal, wavering, hostile) in 51 categories, set by family background and perceived loyalty — which determines residence, education, employment, food access, medical care, and treatment by the courts. (Robert Collins, Marked for Life, Committee for Human Rights in North Korea, 2012; based on defector testimony and North Korean documents.)
  • The UN Commission of Inquiry found that discrimination is "rooted in the songbun system, which classifies people on the basis of State-assigned social class and birth" and determines where people live, work, and study. (UN Commission of Inquiry on Human Rights in the DPRK, report A/HRC/25/63, February 17, 2014.)
  • The core class receives concrete privileges denied to others, including permission to live in Pyongyang and preference in housing, food, medical treatment, education, and employment; the hostile class is assigned to dangerous labor in remote regions with lower food rations. (Collins/HRNK, 2012, as reported by Reuters, June 2012.)
  • Food distribution through the state Public Distribution System has historically prioritized Pyongyang, the military, and party elites; the UN FAO estimated a national food shortfall of roughly 860,000 tons in 2021 while distribution remained unequal. (FAO estimate reported by Korea JoongAng Daily, June 2021; UN COI, 2014, on discriminatory food distribution.)
  • Kim Jong Un himself holds power by heredity — he is the third generation of his family to rule — and his position sits outside and above the legal system that applies to everyone else; the UN COI found violations arose from "policies established at the highest level of State." (UN COI, February 2014.)

Evidence for (his shelf):

  • North Korea's Socialist Constitution formally guarantees citizens' rights and equality before the law, and Pyongyang's own 2014 human-rights report describes its system as people-centered, with state-provided housing, healthcare, and education. Sourcing quality: these are the state's own claims; independent verification inside the country is not permitted.
  • The state does operate universal-in-name systems of rationing, housing assignment, and free schooling and healthcare; that these systems exist is not in dispute — how unequally they deliver is the point at issue. (UN COI, 2014, describing the formal systems while finding discriminatory practice.)
  • Pervasive bribery and markets now let some citizens buy their way around songbun restrictions — a softening of the caste system in practice, though by corruption rather than by law. (Human Rights Watch, North Korea country summary, January 2017.)

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do members of his system — military, party, provinces, his own family — keep any autonomy, or is independence punished?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence against (autonomy survives):

  • On December 12–13, 2013, North Korea's own state media announced the execution of Jang Song-thaek — Kim's uncle by marriage, vice chairman of the National Defense Commission, and widely regarded as the regime's second-most powerful figure — after a special military tribunal convicted him of attempting to overthrow the state; two of his closest aides were executed the month before. This is documented by the state's own announcement, not outside allegation. (KCNA, December 13, 2013; reported by NPR, Korea JoongAng Daily, and CSIS analysis, December 2013.)
  • South Korea's Institute for National Security Strategy estimated at least 100 officials had been executed since Kim took power in December 2011. Sourcing quality: a South Korean state-affiliated institute's estimate, 2015; individual cases beyond Jang's are not all independently confirmed.
  • Kim Jong-nam, the leader's half-brother, was killed with VX nerve agent at Kuala Lumpur airport on February 13, 2017; the U.S. State Department formally determined (February 22, 2018) that the North Korean government used the chemical warfare agent VX to assassinate him. North Korea denied involvement. (U.S. State Department determination under the CBW Act, published March 2018; Congressional Research Service, July 2018.)
  • Senior military leadership has been rotated and demoted at a pace analysts describe as deliberate prevention of any independent power base; the party, military, and cabinet all answer to Kim personally through overlapping surveillance bodies, and no institution has a protected sphere of its own. (UN COI, 2014, on total state control; CSIS and KEI analyses of elite churn.)

Evidence for (his shelf):

  • Kim has governed increasingly through formal party institutions — reviving regular Workers' Party congresses (2016, 2021) and plenums — rather than by the military-first (songun) improvisation of his father's era, a shift analysts describe as partial institutionalization. (KEI and CSIS analyses, 2016–2021.)
  • In January 2024 he announced a "Regional Development 20×10 Policy" directing provinces to build local factories, an announced delegation of economic initiative to regional governments. Sourcing quality: the policy's announcement is documented (KCNA, January 2024); its actual local autonomy in practice cannot be independently verified.
  • Honesty note on the other side's evidence: some widely reported purge claims have proved wrong — after the failed February 2019 Hanoi summit, South Korean media reported that envoy Kim Hyok-chol had been executed; this was never confirmed, and other officials reported purged later reappeared in public. Claims of specific executions beyond state-announced ones should be treated as unproven. (Reuters and AP reporting, 2019.)

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When events contradict him — food crisis, disaster, failed launches — does he adjust or admit fault openly?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (he has admitted failure, on the record):

  • October 10, 2020: at the 75th anniversary parade of the Workers' Party, Kim appeared tearful and apologized directly to citizens: "Our people have placed trust, as high as the sky and as deep as the sea, in me, but I have failed to always live up to it satisfactorily. I am really sorry for that… my efforts and sincerity have not been sufficient enough to rid our people of the difficulties in their lives." (KCNA speech text; Reuters analysis, October 13, 2020.)
  • September 25, 2020: Kim sent a message to Seoul expressing that he was "very sorry" over the killing of a South Korean official by North Korean troops — a rare direct apology to South Korea. (South Korean presidential office, reported September 2020; Korea Economic Institute analysis, February 2021.)
  • January 5, 2021: opening the 8th Party Congress, Kim admitted the five-year economic strategy "fell extremely short of our goals in almost all areas" and called the preceding years the "worst of the worst." (KCNA, reported by NPR/AP, January 6, 2021.)
  • June 15, 2021: Kim told a party plenum, on the record, "The people's food situation is now getting tense as the agricultural sector failed to fulfill its grain production plan," and signed a special order on livelihoods. (KCNA, reported by AP, June 16, 2021.)
  • May 31 and August 24, 2023: after both failed launches of the Chollima-1 rocket carrying the Malligyong-1 satellite, KCNA publicly admitted the failures, named the technical causes, and in June 2023 a party plenum called the first failure "the most serious" shortcoming of the year and "bitterly" criticized the responsible officials — an unusual official acknowledgment rather than silence or a false success claim. (KCNA reports, May 31 and June 19, 2023; SpaceNews and Janes, 2023.)
  • May 2022: after two years of claiming zero COVID-19 cases, the state acknowledged an outbreak, published daily fever-case figures through KCNA, and Kim publicly criticized officials' response handling before declaring victory in August 2022. The case figures are state-reported and unverifiable. (KCNA; AP and Reuters, May–August 2022.)

Evidence against (the wobble denied or displaced):

  • In the same speeches, Kim attributed the hardships he acknowledged to international sanctions, typhoons, and the pandemic — not to his own policy choices; analysts note his apologies concede outcomes while never conceding that his course is wrong. (Korea Economic Institute, "Kim Jong-un's Use and Abuse of Apologies," February 2021; Reuters, October 2020.)
  • For over two years (January 2020 – May 2022) the state maintained the claim of zero COVID-19 infections, a claim U.S. and South Korean officials publicly doubted and which the May 2022 outbreak announcement implicitly contradicted. (Reuters, October 2020 and May 2022.)
  • Acknowledged failures are followed by blame assigned downward — officials criticized, and in the July 2024 flood response, South Korean media reported officials may have been punished severely. Sourcing quality: the flood-punishment reports rest on South Korean media and intelligence sourcing and are unconfirmed.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Who is inside the count — and who is outside it?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence against (everyone is counted):

  • The UN Commission of Inquiry found systematic, widespread, and gross human rights violations, many amounting to crimes against humanity under policies set at the highest level of the state — including extermination, murder, enslavement, torture, rape, forced abortion, and "the inhumane act of knowingly causing prolonged starvation." These are the findings of that UN body, reached without access to the country, from victim and witness testimony and satellite imagery. (UN COI, report A/HRC/25/63, February 17, 2014.)
  • The COI estimated between 80,000 and 120,000 political prisoners held in four large political prison camps (kwanliso), where it found deliberate starvation used as a means of control. (UN COI, February 2014.)
  • The COI documented yeonjwaje — punishment extended to up to three generations of an offender's family — meaning people are imprisoned for the alleged offenses of parents and grandparents. (UN COI, February 2014; HRNK, The Hidden Gulag.)
  • The songbun system (Test 1) formally sorts the entire population into counted and discounted classes from birth, governing food, residence, and schooling. (UN COI, 2014; Collins/HRNK, 2012.)
  • Chronic hunger remains mass-scale: UN agencies have estimated roughly 40% of the population undernourished, and monitors documented worsening food insecurity through the 2020–2023 border closure. (UN FAO/WFP estimates, 2019–2021; FAO shortfall estimate, 2021.)
  • North Korea rejected the COI report outright, refused the Commission access to the country, and denounced its findings as fabricated — a denial that stands on the record alongside the findings. (KCNA statements and DPRK statements to the UN, 2014.)

Evidence for (his shelf):

  • Kim's public rhetoric centers "people-first" politics (immin wicheon — revering the people as heaven); he has repeatedly and publicly made living standards the stated test of his rule, including the admissions catalogued in Test 3. (KCNA speech texts, 2020–2021.)
  • The state has delivered visible provision projects under Kim — new housing districts in Pyongyang, the Wonsan-Kalma coastal resort (opened 2025), and the 2024 regional development policy promising factories in 20 counties per year. Sourcing quality: documented by state media and satellite imagery; the distribution of benefits beyond Pyongyang and showcase sites cannot be independently verified.
  • During the July–August 2024 floods in the northwest, Kim personally visited flood zones repeatedly, apologized to victims, and ordered evacuees — including thousands of children — housed in Pyongyang; the response was extensively documented by state media and partially corroborated by satellite imagery of flood damage and construction. (KCNA; Reuters and AP, July–October 2024.)
  • Limits of this shelf: every "for" item concerns provision to the counted population. No evidence on this shelf addresses the prison-camp population, songbun-excluded classes, or any mechanism by which the excluded are brought inside the count.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Agreements signed — kept, or broken?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (commitments kept, for a time):

  • April 2018: Kim announced a self-imposed moratorium on nuclear tests and ICBM launches. The ICBM moratorium held for nearly four years, until the Hwasong-17 launch of March 24, 2022. (WPK decision, April 2018; Open Nuclear Network and Arms Control Association chronologies.)
  • The nuclear test moratorium has held longer: North Korea has conducted no nuclear test since September 3, 2017, through November 2026 — though satellite imagery shows restoration work at the Punggye-ri test site since late 2021, so the capability to resume is being rebuilt while the pause itself remains unbroken. (Open Nuclear Network site monitoring, 2021–2024.)
  • Under the June 12, 2018 Singapore joint statement, North Korea returned the remains of U.S. servicemen (55 cases transferred in July 2018) and began partial dismantlement work at the Sohae launch site, verified by satellite imagery at the time. (Arms Control Association chronology; 38 North imagery analysis, 2018.)
  • The September 19, 2018 inter-Korean military agreement's measures — guard-post withdrawals, no-fly zones, a halted-live-fire buffer — were implemented for a period before the relationship collapsed. (Pyongyang Joint Declaration and Comprehensive Military Agreement, September 2018.)

Evidence against (commitments broken or reversed):

  • The Leap Day agreement collapsed in about six weeks. On February 29, 2012, North Korea agreed to a moratorium on long-range missile launches, nuclear tests, and Yongbyon uranium enrichment, plus IAEA monitoring, in exchange for 240,000 metric tons of U.S. food aid. On March 16, 2012 it announced a satellite launch; it launched on April 13, 2012, and the deal died. Pyongyang's position was that satellite launches were excluded; the U.S. position was that it had been warned they were not. (Congressional Research Service reports, March 2012 and June 2013; NPR, April 2018.)
  • The Singapore statement's central commitment — Kim's pledge to "work toward complete denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula" (June 12, 2018) — produced no denuclearization steps after the February 2019 Hanoi summit failed, and Pyongyang instead expanded its arsenal, fielding solid-fuel ICBMs. (Singapore joint statement text; IAEA reports of continuing nuclear activity from August 2018 onward; Arms Control Association chronology.)
  • On June 16, 2020, North Korea demolished the inter-Korean liaison office at Kaesong — a building that was itself a product of the 2018 agreements — ending the Panmunjom-era framework in practice. (Reuters/AP, June 16, 2020.)
  • In September 2022 North Korea enacted a nuclear-forces law declaring its nuclear status irreversible and authorizing preemptive use — a formal repudiation of denuclearization as a goal. (Law on Nuclear Forces Policy, adopted September 8, 2022; KCNA.)
  • In December 2023 – January 2024, Kim formally reversed the foundational premise of every inter-Korean agreement he had signed: he declared reunification impossible, ordered South Korea defined in law as the "primary foe and invariable principal enemy," abolished the reunification agencies, and demolished the Arch of Reunification. (Kim's SPA speech, January 15, 2024, via KCNA; Korea Economic Institute analysis, 2025.)

Overall Summary

On the documented record, Kim Jong Un fails the tests that concern how power treats the people inside it: law is hereditary caste rather than one law (Test 1), no member of his system — not even his own uncle — holds protected autonomy (Test 2), and a UN Commission of Inquiry found a prison-camp system and collective punishment that place a large share of his population outside the count altogether, findings Pyongyang denies but has never allowed investigators in to test (Test 4). He grades mixed on the two tests where his record genuinely has two shelves: he has made public admissions of failure that are real, on tape, and unusual for his office, while consistently displacing the blame for them (Test 3); and his signature commitments show a pattern — tactical pauses (the test moratoria) kept for years, strategic promises (denuclearization, inter-Korean reconciliation) reversed by his own later declarations (Test 5). The honest limit of this dossier is how much cannot be known: no independent observer operates inside North Korea, several dramatic claims in the public record rest on defector testimony or foreign intelligence that has been wrong before, and everything Pyongyang publishes is state voice. Where the record was thin, this dossier has said so rather than guess.
