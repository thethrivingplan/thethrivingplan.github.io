The Ruler's Test — Kim Jong Un (North Korea) — RE-RUN under the Nov 24, 2026 method

Subject: Kim Jong Un, Supreme Leader of the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (in power December 2011 – present). This is a re-run of the original dossier (kim.md) under the method addendum of November 24, 2026: every test re-graded fresh, on the original evidence base plus the documented record through November 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Every test closes with why the grade lands where it lands and a ledger naming who his actions on that test helped and who they hurt. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

The mandate rule, applied: North Korea holds elections in form — Supreme People's Assembly ballots with a single approved candidate per district — but no electoral mandate operates in substance: Kim Jong Un did not win power on a platform voters could refuse, and no voter can remove him. The mandate rule therefore has nothing to protect here, and it is not held against him that he governs on his own program — hereditary succession and one-party rule are graded where they belong, under Tests 1 and 2, as facts about the holding. He is graded on the same five tests as every other seat.

Evidence note (read first): North Korea is a closed country. The UN Commission of Inquiry was denied access and built its findings on victim and witness testimony, satellite imagery, and North Korea's own published materials. South Korean intelligence (NIS) claims about internal purges have sometimes later proved wrong. Where a claim rests on a single or contestable source, it is labeled. North Korea's own statements are sourced to KCNA as reported by international wire services. Where the record is too thin to carry a grade, this dossier says NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE rather than guess — on this file, that discipline binds hardest of all, and it is stated test by test where it bites.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence against (one law for all):

  • Every North Korean is assigned a hereditary songbun classification — three classes (core/loyal, wavering, hostile) in 51 categories, set by family background and perceived loyalty — which determines residence, education, employment, food access, medical care, and treatment by the courts. (Robert Collins, Marked for Life, Committee for Human Rights in North Korea, 2012; based on defector testimony and North Korean documents.)
  • The UN Commission of Inquiry found that discrimination is "rooted in the songbun system, which classifies people on the basis of State-assigned social class and birth" and determines where people live, work, and study. (UN Commission of Inquiry on Human Rights in the DPRK, report A/HRC/25/63, February 17, 2014.)
  • The core class receives concrete privileges denied to others, including permission to live in Pyongyang and preference in housing, food, medical treatment, education, and employment; the hostile class is assigned to dangerous labor in remote regions with lower food rations. (Collins/HRNK, 2012, as reported by Reuters, June 2012.)
  • Food distribution through the state Public Distribution System has historically prioritized Pyongyang, the military, and party elites; the UN FAO estimated a national food shortfall of roughly 860,000 tons in 2021 while distribution remained unequal. (FAO estimate reported by Korea JoongAng Daily, June 2021; UN COI, 2014, on discriminatory food distribution.)
  • Kim Jong Un himself holds power by heredity — he is the third generation of his family to rule — and his position sits outside and above the legal system that applies to everyone else; the UN COI found violations arose from "policies established at the highest level of State." (UN COI, February 2014.)
  • Nothing in the 2025–2026 record disturbs any of this: the UN human rights office (OHCHR) reported in 2025 that the overall human rights situation in the country had deteriorated over the past decade, with increased surveillance, restrictions on information, forced labour, and severe punishments; in 2026 the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights described the situation as an ongoing "human rights crisis." (OHCHR report on human rights in the DPRK, 2025; UN High Commissioner Volker Türk, 2026, as summarized by the UN Regional Information Centre.)

Evidence for (his shelf):

  • North Korea's Socialist Constitution formally guarantees citizens' rights and equality before the law, and Pyongyang's own 2014 human-rights report describes its system as people-centered, with state-provided housing, healthcare, and education. Sourcing quality: these are the state's own claims; independent verification inside the country is not permitted.
  • The state does operate universal-in-name systems of rationing, housing assignment, and free schooling and healthcare; that these systems exist is not in dispute — how unequally they deliver is the point at issue. (UN COI, 2014, describing the formal systems while finding discriminatory practice.)
  • Pervasive bribery and markets now let some citizens buy their way around songbun restrictions — a softening of the caste system in practice, though by corruption rather than by law. (Human Rights Watch, North Korea country summary, January 2017.)

Why this grade: The test asks whether one law runs from the source. Here the source itself is the exception: the ruler is hereditary and above the law, and the population is sorted at birth into classes that decide what the law, the ration, and the map will give them. The for-shelf is real but it is a shelf of paper guarantees and universal-in-name systems whose unequal delivery is the documented point. No new evidence since the original grading moves this in either direction; the 2025 OHCHR deterioration finding confirms the direction of travel.

Helped: The core/loyal songbun class — Pyongyang residents, party and military elites — who receive the housing, food, medical, and educational preference the system reserves for them; the Kim family itself, whose position the arrangement places beyond legal reach. Hurt: The wavering and hostile songbun classes — by the COI's and HRNK's accounting, the majority of the population — whose birth classification caps where they may live, study, work, and eat; and every citizen, core class included, who lives under a law that stops at the ruler's door.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence against (autonomy survives):

  • On December 12–13, 2013, North Korea's own state media announced the execution of Jang Song-thaek — Kim's uncle by marriage, vice chairman of the National Defense Commission, and widely regarded as the regime's second-most powerful figure — after a special military tribunal convicted him of attempting to overthrow the state; two of his closest aides were executed the month before. This is documented by the state's own announcement, not outside allegation. (KCNA, December 13, 2013; reported by NPR, Korea JoongAng Daily, and CSIS analysis, December 2013.)
  • South Korea's Institute for National Security Strategy estimated at least 100 officials had been executed since Kim took power in December 2011. Sourcing quality: a South Korean state-affiliated institute's estimate, 2015; individual cases beyond Jang's are not all independently confirmed.
  • Kim Jong-nam, the leader's half-brother, was killed with VX nerve agent at Kuala Lumpur airport on February 13, 2017; the U.S. State Department formally determined (February 22, 2018) that the North Korean government used the chemical warfare agent VX to assassinate him. North Korea denied involvement. (U.S. State Department determination under the CBW Act, published March 2018; Congressional Research Service, July 2018.)
  • Senior military leadership has been rotated and demoted at a pace analysts describe as deliberate prevention of any independent power base; the party, military, and cabinet all answer to Kim personally through overlapping surveillance bodies, and no institution has a protected sphere of its own. (UN COI, 2014, on total state control; CSIS and KEI analyses of elite churn.)
  • The grip reached a new file in 2025. When the destroyer Kang Kon capsized at its launch at Chongjin Shipyard on May 21, 2025 — in Kim's presence — state media reported within days that law-enforcement authorities had detained the shipyard's chief engineer, the head of the hull construction workshop, and the deputy manager for administrative affairs; the shipyard manager Hong Kil-ho was summoned for questioning, and Ri Hyong-son, vice director of the Workers' Party Central Committee's munitions industry department and a Central Military Commission member, was detained as "greatly responsible." Kim called the accident a "criminal act" caused by "absolute carelessness" before any investigation could conclude. (KCNA, May 2025, as reported by AP, May 27, 2025, and RFA, May 2025.) Whatever the engineering facts, the documented sequence — accident, the ruler's verdict, arrests — is the grip operating in public.

Evidence for (his shelf):

  • Kim has governed increasingly through formal party institutions — reviving regular Workers' Party congresses (2016, 2021) and plenums — rather than by the military-first (songun) improvisation of his father's era, a shift analysts describe as partial institutionalization. (KEI and CSIS analyses, 2016–2021.)
  • In January 2024 he announced a "Regional Development 20×10 Policy" directing provinces to build local factories, an announced delegation of economic initiative to regional governments. Sourcing quality: the policy's announcement is documented (KCNA, January 2024); its actual local autonomy in practice cannot be independently verified.
  • Honesty note on the other side's evidence: some widely reported purge claims have proved wrong — after the failed February 2019 Hanoi summit, South Korean media reported that envoy Kim Hyok-chol had been executed; this was never confirmed, and other officials reported purged later reappeared in public. Claims of specific executions beyond state-announced ones should be treated as unproven. (Reuters and AP reporting, 2019.)

Why this grade: There is no referee in this system to grip, because no court, commission, press organ, or bank holds a sphere the ruler cannot enter — and the documented record shows him entering: an uncle executed on state television's terms, a half-brother assassinated abroad on a U.S. government determination Pyongyang disputes, and, in 2025, shipyard officials detained within days of an accident on the ruler's own characterization of it. The for-shelf shows process — congresses, plenums, an announced regional policy — but process that convenes at his call and answers to him is not autonomy. The grade was FAILS and the new record confirms it; where individual purge claims rest on single foreign sources, they are labeled and the grade does not rest on them.

Helped: Kim Jong Un personally, whose hold on every institution is the arrangement's product; the surveillance and party-control organs whose authority the grip enlarges. Hurt: Jang Song-thaek and his aides (executed, 2013, on the state's own announcement); Kim Jong-nam (killed, 2017; attribution contested — U.S. determination, DPRK denial); the Chongjin shipyard officials and Ri Hyong-son (detained, 2025, on state media's own reporting); and every official, officer, and citizen whose work is done under the knowledge that independence is punished.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades the other option: when another option became available, did he at least consider it openly? Considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (he has admitted failure, on the record):

  • October 10, 2020: at the 75th anniversary parade of the Workers' Party, Kim appeared tearful and apologized directly to citizens: "Our people have placed trust, as high as the sky and as deep as the sea, in me, but I have failed to always live up to it satisfactorily. I am really sorry for that… my efforts and sincerity have not been sufficient enough to rid our people of the difficulties in their lives." (KCNA speech text; Reuters analysis, October 13, 2020.)
  • September 25, 2020: Kim sent a message to Seoul expressing that he was "very sorry" over the killing of a South Korean official by North Korean troops — a rare direct apology to South Korea. (South Korean presidential office, reported September 2020; Korea Economic Institute analysis, February 2021.)
  • January 5, 2021: opening the 8th Party Congress, Kim admitted the five-year economic strategy "fell extremely short of our goals in almost all areas" and called the preceding years the "worst of the worst." (KCNA, reported by NPR/AP, January 6, 2021.)
  • June 15, 2021: Kim told a party plenum, on the record, "The people's food situation is now getting tense as the agricultural sector failed to fulfill its grain production plan," and signed a special order on livelihoods. (KCNA, reported by AP, June 16, 2021.)
  • May 31 and August 24, 2023: after both failed launches of the Chollima-1 rocket carrying the Malligyong-1 satellite, KCNA publicly admitted the failures, named the technical causes, and in June 2023 a party plenum called the first failure "the most serious" shortcoming of the year and "bitterly" criticized the responsible officials — an unusual official acknowledgment rather than silence or a false success claim. (KCNA reports, May 31 and June 19, 2023; SpaceNews and Janes, 2023.)
  • May 2022: after two years of claiming zero COVID-19 cases, the state acknowledged an outbreak, published daily fever-case figures through KCNA, and Kim publicly criticized officials' response handling before declaring victory in August 2022. The case figures are state-reported and unverifiable. (KCNA; AP and Reuters, May–August 2022.)
  • May 2025 — the wobble answered in days, in the open: when the destroyer Kang Kon capsized at its launch ceremony in front of him, KCNA reported the "serious accident" almost immediately, published Kim's assessment and his order that the ship be restored before the June party plenum — and the ship was righted, relaunched on June 5, and ceremonially launched on June 12, 2025, with Kim in attendance; it was commissioned on September 6, 2026. A state that once hid famines published its own naval humiliation and then its repair. (KCNA, May–June 2025; satellite imagery reported by CSIS Beyond Parallel, May–June 2025; KCNA, September 2026.)
  • September 21, 2025 — another option, considered openly: addressing the Supreme People's Assembly, Kim stated on the record that "if the U.S. drops its hollow obsession with denuclearization and wants to pursue peaceful coexistence with North Korea based on the recognition of reality, there is no reason for us not to sit down with the U.S.," adding that he still had "good memories" of President Trump. Whatever one thinks of the condition attached, the option of renewed talks was examined in public and answered with stated reasons and a stated price — consideration, not refusal to look. (KCNA speech text, September 21, 2025; NPR and Reuters, September 22, 2025.)

Evidence against (the wobble denied or displaced):

  • In the same speeches, Kim attributed the hardships he acknowledged to international sanctions, typhoons, and the pandemic — not to his own policy choices; analysts note his apologies concede outcomes while never conceding that his course is wrong. (Korea Economic Institute, "Kim Jong-un's Use and Abuse of Apologies," February 2021; Reuters, October 2020.)
  • For over two years (January 2020 – May 2022) the state maintained the claim of zero COVID-19 infections, a claim U.S. and South Korean officials publicly doubted and which the May 2022 outbreak announcement implicitly contradicted. (Reuters, October 2020 and May 2022.)
  • Acknowledged failures are followed by blame assigned downward — officials criticized, and in the July 2024 flood response, South Korean media reported officials may have been punished severely. Sourcing quality: the flood-punishment reports rest on South Korean media and intelligence sourcing and are unconfirmed. The May 2025 destroyer accident followed the same pattern at higher speed: the failure was admitted, and the admission arrived with detentions (Test 2).
  • The troop deployment to Russia was denied by silence for roughly six months. North Korean troops were documented in Russia from October 2024 by Ukrainian, South Korean, and U.S. intelligence; Pyongyang and Moscow neither confirmed nor denied the reports until April 26–28, 2025 — after Russia announced Kursk retaken — when both governments acknowledged the deployment and framed it as a completed victory. The fact was admitted only when it could be admitted as a triumph. (Ukrainian GUR and NIS reporting, October 2024; Russian General Staff statement, April 26, 2025; KCNA / Central Military Commission statement, April 28, 2025; INSS Issue Brief, May 2025.)
  • Toward Seoul, the other option was refused a look. South Korea's President Lee Jae Myung, in office from June 2025, extended repeated dialogue overtures; Kim's September 21, 2025 speech rejected any resumption of dialogue with South Korea outright and permanently — "no intention" of sitting with Seoul, ever — while leaving the Washington door conditionally open. One door examined with reasons; the other closed without examination. (KCNA, September 21, 2025; Reuters and NPR, September 2025; Kim Yo-jong statements rejecting Seoul's overtures, July 2025, via KCNA.)

Why this grade: This remains the most genuinely two-sided file in the dossier. The for-shelf is not propaganda gloss: the apologies are on tape, the economic failure was admitted to his own congress, a capsized destroyer was reported by his own news agency within days and repaired on the deadline he publicly set, and in September 2025 he considered the option of renewed U.S. talks openly, with his price stated. The against-shelf is equally documented: every admission routes blame to weather, sanctions, or subordinates; a pandemic was denied for two years; a war deployment was left unacknowledged for six months until it could be announced as victory; and Seoul's overtures were refused without a look. Under the other-option rule the Washington answer earns him credit the original grading had no column for — and the Seoul refusal costs him in the same column. The two roughly offset; MIXED stands.

Helped: North Korean citizens, to the limited extent that admitted failures (the 2021 food admission and special order; the flood responses) preceded corrective orders; the shipbuilding program, whose failure was owned fast enough to be repaired on schedule. Hurt: Subordinate officials, who absorb the blame component of nearly every admission — by criticism, demotion, and in 2025 by detention; the families of deployed soldiers, who learned of the deployment from foreign intelligence reports months before their own state acknowledged it; South Koreans who sought dialogue, refused a hearing.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole population — the disfavored classes, the prisoners, the poor, his own soldiers — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence against (everyone is counted):

  • The UN Commission of Inquiry found systematic, widespread, and gross human rights violations, many amounting to crimes against humanity under policies set at the highest level of the state — including extermination, murder, enslavement, torture, rape, forced abortion, and "the inhumane act of knowingly causing prolonged starvation." These are the findings of that UN body, reached without access to the country, from victim and witness testimony and satellite imagery. (UN COI, report A/HRC/25/63, February 17, 2014.)
  • The COI estimated between 80,000 and 120,000 political prisoners held in four large political prison camps (kwanliso), where it found deliberate starvation used as a means of control. (UN COI, February 2014.)
  • The COI documented yeonjwaje — punishment extended to up to three generations of an offender's family — meaning people are imprisoned for the alleged offenses of parents and grandparents. (UN COI, 2014; HRNK, The Hidden Gulag.)
  • The songbun system (Test 1) formally sorts the entire population into counted and discounted classes from birth, governing food, residence, and schooling. (UN COI, 2014; Collins/HRNK, 2012.)
  • Chronic hunger remains mass-scale and, on the newest UN figures, undiminished: the World Food Programme estimates some 10.7 million people — more than 40% of the population — undernourished and requiring humanitarian assistance, with about 18% of children under five stunted by chronic malnutrition. (WFP DPR Korea country overview, drawing on the State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World Report; UN Regional Information Centre summary, 2025.) UN reporting in 2026 put the undernourished share higher still — over 45%, roughly 11.8 million people — amid drought and continued isolation. Sourcing quality: modeled UN estimates; no UN agency operates a full in-country assessment program, and Pyongyang disputes UN characterizations of the situation. (UN reporting summarized by HRRC, May 2026.)
  • The count now excludes a new class of his own people: his soldiers in Russia. More than 11,000 troops were deployed to Russia's Kursk region from late 2024 (Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team report, 2025; Ukrainian, South Korean, and U.S. assessments of 10,000–15,000 across rotations), with a further 1,000 sappers and 5,000 military construction workers announced in June 2025 (Russian Security Council Secretary Sergei Shoigu, June 2025). Ukrainian and South Korean estimates put North Korean casualties — killed and wounded — in the thousands; neither Pyongyang nor Moscow has published a figure, and the dead were publicly honored at home only from mid-2025, months after the first reported deaths. The casualty figures are foreign estimates, labeled as such; the deployment itself is confirmed by both governments. (Reuters explainer, August 2026; MSMT, 2025.) In August 2026 a Ukrainian military-intelligence official reported a North Korean missile unit deploying to Russia's Voronezh region; Russia and North Korea had not confirmed that specific deployment when reported — recorded here as an ALLEGATION-level report, not a fact. (Reuters, August 2026.)
  • North Korea rejected the COI report outright, refused the Commission access to the country, and denounced its findings as fabricated — a denial that stands on the record alongside the findings. (KCNA statements and DPRK statements to the UN, 2014.)

Evidence for (his shelf):

  • Kim's public rhetoric centers "people-first" politics (immin wicheon — revering the people as heaven); he has repeatedly and publicly made living standards the stated test of his rule, including the admissions catalogued in Test 3. (KCNA speech texts, 2020–2021.)
  • The state has delivered visible provision projects under Kim — new housing districts in Pyongyang, the Wonsan-Kalma coastal resort (opened to domestic visitors in July 2025), and the 2024 regional development policy promising factories in 20 counties per year. Sourcing quality: documented by state media and satellite imagery; the distribution of benefits beyond Pyongyang and showcase sites cannot be independently verified.
  • During the July–August 2024 floods in the northwest, Kim personally visited flood zones repeatedly, apologized to victims, and ordered evacuees — including thousands of children — housed in Pyongyang; the response was extensively documented by state media and partially corroborated by satellite imagery of flood damage and construction. (KCNA; Reuters and AP, July–October 2024.)
  • Limits of this shelf: every "for" item concerns provision to the counted population. No evidence on this shelf addresses the prison-camp population, songbun-excluded classes, or any mechanism by which the excluded are brought inside the count.

Why this grade: A count that begins by sorting every newborn into a hereditary class, that holds an estimated 80,000–120,000 political prisoners outside it entirely — some punished for a grandparent's alleged offense — and that leaves more than 40% of the population undernourished on UN estimates is not a count of everyone; the 2025–2026 record adds his own deployed soldiers, sent to a foreign war unacknowledged for six months and honored only after the fact. The for-shelf documents real provision — to the counted. Provision to the counted is what this test exists to see past. FAILS stands, on fuller receipts than the original grading had.

Helped: Pyongyang residents and the core class, recipients of the housing, resort, and showcase provision; flood evacuees of 2024, who received a visible, personal response; soldiers' families, eventually — state honors and ceremonies for the fallen began in 2025, after the deployment was acknowledged. Hurt: The estimated 80,000–120,000 kwanliso prisoners and the families punished with them under yeonjwaje; the hostile and wavering songbun classes; the more than 10 million undernourished, including the roughly 18% of children under five whose growth chronic malnutrition has already impaired; the deployed soldiers killed and wounded in Kursk — foreign-estimated in the thousands — and their families, who were counted last.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record, with dates — and, per the two-axes rule, the keeping and the outcome are graded on separate axes: a word kept that hurt people earns its credit here and its full hurt in the ledgers.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (commitments kept, for a time):

  • April 2018: Kim announced a self-imposed moratorium on nuclear tests and ICBM launches. The ICBM moratorium held for nearly four years, until the Hwasong-17 launch of March 24, 2022. (WPK decision, April 2018; Open Nuclear Network and Arms Control Association chronologies.)
  • The nuclear test moratorium has held longer: North Korea has conducted no nuclear test since September 3, 2017, through November 2026 — though satellite imagery shows restoration work at the Punggye-ri test site since late 2021, so the capability to resume is being rebuilt while the pause itself remains unbroken. (Open Nuclear Network site monitoring, 2021–2024.)
  • Under the June 12, 2018 Singapore joint statement, North Korea returned the remains of U.S. servicemen (55 cases transferred in July 2018) and began partial dismantlement work at the Sohae launch site, verified by satellite imagery at the time. (Arms Control Association chronology; 38 North imagery analysis, 2018.)
  • The September 19, 2018 inter-Korean military agreement's measures — guard-post withdrawals, no-fly zones, a halted-live-fire buffer — were implemented for a period before the relationship collapsed. (Pyongyang Joint Declaration and Comprehensive Military Agreement, September 2018.)
  • A word kept at full price, 2024–2025: the Treaty on Comprehensive Strategic Partnership with Russia, signed by Kim and President Putin in Pyongyang on June 19, 2024, and in force from December 2024, obliges each party to render military assistance if the other is attacked. When Ukraine held part of Russia's Kursk region, Kim performed the commitment: more than 11,000 troops deployed from October 2024, combat participation confirmed by both governments in April 2025, and follow-on sappers and construction troops announced in June 2025. Pyongyang's own statement records that Kim ordered the deployment under the treaty. The keeping is documented and was costly; what it cost, and who paid, is recorded in the ledger below and in Test 4 — the two axes do not launder each other. (Treaty text, June 2024; KCNA / Central Military Commission statement, April 28, 2025; MSMT report, 2025; Reuters, 2025.)

Evidence against (commitments broken or reversed):

  • The Leap Day agreement collapsed in about six weeks. On February 29, 2012, North Korea agreed to a moratorium on long-range missile launches, nuclear tests, and Yongbyon uranium enrichment, plus IAEA monitoring, in exchange for 240,000 metric tons of U.S. food aid. On March 16, 2012 it announced a satellite launch; it launched on April 13, 2012, and the deal died. Pyongyang's position was that satellite launches were excluded; the U.S. position was that it had been warned they were not. (Congressional Research Service reports, March 2012 and June 2013; NPR, April 2018.)
  • The Singapore statement's central commitment — Kim's pledge to "work toward complete denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula" (June 12, 2018) — produced no denuclearization steps after the February 2019 Hanoi summit failed, and Pyongyang instead expanded its arsenal, fielding solid-fuel ICBMs and, in March 2025, publicly displaying a nuclear-powered submarine under construction, which Kim inspected and praised. (Singapore joint statement text; IAEA reports of continuing nuclear activity from August 2018 onward; KCNA, March 2025.)
  • On June 16, 2020, North Korea demolished the inter-Korean liaison office at Kaesong — a building that was itself a product of the 2018 agreements — ending the Panmunjom-era framework in practice. (Reuters/AP, June 16, 2020.)
  • In September 2022 North Korea enacted a nuclear-forces law declaring its nuclear status irreversible and authorizing preemptive use — a formal repudiation of denuclearization as a goal. (Law on Nuclear Forces Policy, adopted September 8, 2022; KCNA.)
  • In December 2023 – January 2024, Kim formally reversed the foundational premise of every inter-Korean agreement he had signed: he declared reunification impossible, ordered South Korea defined in law as the "primary foe and invariable principal enemy," abolished the reunification agencies, and demolished the Arch of Reunification. (Kim's SPA speech, January 15, 2024, via KCNA; Korea Economic Institute analysis, 2025.)
  • The reversal was restated, not softened, in the newest record: on September 21, 2025, Kim told the Supreme People's Assembly that North Korea would "never give up" its nuclear weapons, that there would be "no negotiations, now or ever," about trading them for sanctions relief, and that he had no intention of ever resuming dialogue with South Korea. The September 2025 speech is consistent with his 2022 law and his 2024 line — his word to his own system has been kept with precision; it is the word given at Singapore and Panmunjom that stands reversed, now by his own repeated declaration. (KCNA, September 21, 2025; NPR and Reuters, September 22, 2025.)

The no-good-choice clause — considered, and not applied to the arsenal: Kim's standing defense of the nuclear program is a trap claim: that a disarmed North Korea would meet the fate of Iraq or Libya — a claim he repeated in the September 21, 2025 speech ("the world already knows well what the United States does after forcing other countries to give up their nuclear weapons"). The clause's mechanics do not carry it. The options that existed are named in this dossier: the 2018–2019 diplomatic track was real, was entered, and produced signed commitments; it was abandoned after Hanoi by both sides' failures, and the arsenal's expansion long predates and postdates any single moment of cornering. The trap, insofar as it exists, was built over decades — by his grandfather's and father's choices and by his own, including the 2022 law and the 2024 reversal, each taken when other doors stood open and were considered in this dossier's Test 3 record. A constraint a ruler manufactures across fourteen years is not a situation he was put in; the clause grades it as his record, not as his excuse. What the clause does protect here is narrower and it is recorded: nothing in this dossier grades him down for declining, in 2019 or since, a disarmament-first deal whose terms required his state to disarm before guarantees were delivered — the record shows that option's price was real, and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence under the other-option rule either.

Why this grade: The pattern from the original grading holds and sharpens. Tactical words are kept for years (the test moratorium, unbroken since 2017; the treaty word to Moscow, performed in blood). Strategic words given to adversaries and to Seoul — denuclearization, reconciliation — were reversed by his own later declarations, most recently and most flatly in September 2025. And the newest kept word cuts the other way on the outcome axis: the commitment to Russia was honored exactly as written, and the honoring sent his own soldiers to die unacknowledged in someone else's war. Kept and broken, helped and hurt, sit on the same page without canceling. MIXED stands.

Helped: Russia, which received troops, munitions, and missiles at the moment of its need, under a treaty performed as written (MSMT, 2025); North Korea's deterrent posture, strengthened by an arsenal expanded in the open; U.S. MIA families, 55 of whom received remains in 2018. Hurt: His own deployed soldiers and their families — the price of the treaty word, foreign-estimated in the thousands of casualties, unacknowledged at home for six months; South Koreans, whose signed reconciliation framework he demolished, literally at Kaesong in 2020 and legally in 2024; the parties to the 2012 and 2018 agreements, whose counterparties' reliance his reversals stranded.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED. The re-run under the November 24, 2026 method confirms the original grades — F/F/M/F/M — on a fuller record, and the method changed the dossier's shape more than its verdicts.

Test 1 — FAILS, and why: law in North Korea is not one law; it is a hereditary sorting (songbun) under a hereditary ruler who stands above it, and the 2025 OHCHR finding of deterioration confirms the direction. Helped: the core class and the Kim family. Hurt: the wavering and hostile classes — most of the country — capped from birth.

Test 2 — FAILS, and why: no institution holds a sphere he cannot enter, and the record shows him entering — an uncle executed (2013), a half-brother assassinated (2017, attribution contested), shipyard officials detained within days of an accident he had already pronounced criminal (2025). Helped: the ruler's personal hold. Hurt: every official and citizen who works under the knowledge that independence is punished.

Test 3 — MIXED, and why: the admissions are real and on tape — failure conceded to his own congress, a famine-risk food shortage owned, a capsized destroyer published by his own agency and repaired on his public deadline — and so is the displacement: blame flows to weather, sanctions, and subordinates; a pandemic was denied for two years; a war deployment was acknowledged only as a victory, six months late. Under the other-option rule, his September 2025 answer to Washington — considered openly, price stated — earns credit; his permanent refusal to look at Seoul's overtures costs it back. Helped: citizens, marginally, where admissions preceded corrections. Hurt: subordinates who absorb the blame; soldiers' families kept in the dark; Seoul, refused a hearing.

Test 4 — FAILS, and why: a population sorted at birth, an estimated 80,000–120,000 political prisoners held outside the count (some for a grandparent's alleged offense), more than 40% undernourished on UN estimates — and now his own soldiers, sent abroad unacknowledged and honored late. The for-shelf is provision to the counted, which is what the test exists to see past. Helped: Pyongyang and the core class; 2024 flood evacuees. Hurt: prisoners and their punished families, the discounted classes, the undernourished, the deployed.

Test 5 — MIXED, and why: tactical words kept for years — no nuclear test since 2017 — and strategic words reversed by his own declaration: Singapore's denuclearization pledge buried by the 2022 law, the 2024 enemy redefinition, and the flat restatement of September 2025. Helped: Russia, paid in full under the 2024 treaty. Hurt: his own soldiers, Seoul, and every counterparty who relied on the reversed words.

The two axes, crossed, as they occurred: 1. Kept his word AND it helped people — the remains repatriation under the Singapore statement (55 cases, 2018); the nuclear-test moratorium, kept since 2017, which has spared the region a decade of test escalations. 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt people — the flagship crossing of this file: the 2024 Russia treaty, performed exactly as written, whose performance sent more than 11,000 of his own soldiers into a foreign war, thousands of them (foreign estimates) killed or wounded, unacknowledged at home for six months. The keeping earns its Test 5 credit; the hurt stands in Tests 4 and 5 in full. Neither launders the other. 3. Went against his word AND it helped people — no documented instance found in the record assembled here; the dossier says so rather than invent one. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt people — the Leap Day collapse (2012) and the reversal of the 2018 inter-Korean framework (Kaesong demolished 2020; reunification repudiated 2024), which stranded the South Korean counterparties and the border-region families the agreements had served.

The mandate note: no electoral mandate operates in North Korea in substance; he was not graded down for governing on his own program, and the absence of any mandate a voter could withdraw is itself recorded under Tests 1 and 2.

The honest limit of this dossier is unchanged and restated: no independent observer operates inside North Korea, several dramatic claims in the public record rest on defector testimony or foreign intelligence that has been wrong before, and everything Pyongyang publishes is state voice. Where the record was thin, this re-run has labeled it, and where foreign estimates carry a number — prisoner counts, casualty counts — the estimate and its source travel with the number. What is not thin is the spine of the file: songbun, the camps findings, the executed uncle, the admitted failures, the signed-and-reversed agreements, and the acknowledged deployment are documented by UN bodies, by treaty texts, or by North Korea's own announcements. On that spine, the grades stand.
