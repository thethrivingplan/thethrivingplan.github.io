The Ruler's Test — Sam Matekane, Prime Minister of Lesotho

Subject: Samuel Ntsokoane Matekane — Prime Minister of the Kingdom of Lesotho since 28 October 2022. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Lesotho is a constitutional monarchy in which King Letsie III reigns as head of state with ceremonial powers, while executive power — command of the government, the budget, the public service, and, in Matekane's own person since November 2023, the defence and national security portfolio — sits with the prime minister. The King reigns; the prime minister rules; the Test grades where the power sits. Matekane, born 15 March 1958 in Mantšonyane, Thaba-Tseka, made his fortune as a contractor to the Letšeng diamond mine through the Matekane Group of Companies, founded the Revolution for Prosperity (RFP) in March 2022 — six months before the election — and won the 7 October 2022 general election with 56 of the National Assembly's 120 seats, five short of the 61 needed to govern alone. He formed a coalition with the Alliance of Democrats (AD) and the Movement for Economic Change (MEC), announced at 65 seats, and was inaugurated on 28 October 2022. The coalition was expanded to seven parties in November 2023 to survive a no-confidence motion; as of September 2026 the governing side held 76 of 120 seats — short of the 80 needed to amend the constitution without opposition votes — and the RFP itself had grown to 59 MPs through floor-crossing. He is graded on four years in office. The next general election is due in 2027. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic of the Test, printed on its card, governs the grading: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one — the Test grades the holding, never the platform; carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution he is accused of bending did, in the event, stand its ground, and its stand is being tested in open court rather than settled by decree. Director of Public Prosecutions (DPP) Hlalefang Motinyane refused the instruction described below, filed her challenge at the High Court and Constitutional Court in July 2024, and the impeachment machinery against her has had to proceed — so far without a documented removal — through the courts she petitioned (BTI 2026 country report; Lesotho Times reporting via The Free Library, 2024). The treason prosecution of Health Minister Selibe Mochoboroane and Lesotho Congress for Democracy (LCD) leader Mothetjoa Metsing, alongside former army commander Tlali Kamoli over the 30 August 2014 attempted coup, remains on the court roll: the charges the executive asked to have withdrawn were not withdrawn (BTI 2026).
  • His government carried one genuinely structural anti-corruption measure into law: the Tenth Amendment to the Constitution, gazetted 13 August 2025, entrenches a new anti-corruption commission within the constitution itself and strengthens the independence of the Human Rights Commission (International IDEA, Global State of Democracy, August 2025; Civicus Monitor, 2025).
  • The anti-corruption directorate has at times moved against figures inside the state's own house: the Directorate on Corruption and Economic Offences' (DCEO) suspended head of its Asset Recovery Unit, Peter Matekane, faces documented allegations of abusing his office — holding a private company secretaryship while a DCEO officer, in breach of the Prevention of Corruption and Economic Offences Regulations 2021 — allegations he contests and which are recorded here as ALLEGATIONs, with the process against him running through the institution itself (Lesotho Times reporting via The Free Library, January 2026). No kinship between this official and the prime minister is established anywhere in the record assembled here, and none is asserted.
  • The outcome measure has not collapsed: Lesotho's Corruption Perceptions Index score stood at 37 of 100 in the 2025 index (99th of 182), the same as 2024 and as the 37 recorded in 2022, the year he took office, though down from 39 in 2023 (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released February 2026). Flat is not clean; it is recorded here because the shelf records movement in both directions or neither.

Evidence against:

  • The central file is admitted, not merely alleged. In September 2023, Deputy Prime Minister Nthomeng Majara — then doubling as Minister of Law and Justice, and herself a former Chief Justice who had campaigned on never interfering with the courts — instructed DPP Motinyane to withdraw the treason charges against Mochoboroane and Metsing, the leaders of two parties in Matekane's own coalition. Motinyane refused, in her words because the instruction "amounted to unlawful and unconstitutional interference with the prosecutorial independence of the DPP." Majara subsequently confirmed in her own court affidavit that she had indeed asked the DPP to drop the charges — framing it, in her account, as a request the DPP was free to refuse (BTI 2026 country report; Lesotho Times reporting via The Free Library, 2024; Attorney General Rapelang Motsieloa's public statements, January 2025). The request is therefore a documented fact; only its character is contested.
  • What followed the refusal is the sequence Test 1 grades. In February 2024, on Motinyane's affidavit, the Justice Minister and the Attorney General tabled a plan to move her sideways into a newly created post — Special Advisor to the Attorney General — with funds, she states, already secured. On 14 May 2024 Prime Minister Matekane himself invited her to make representations on why he should not advise the King to establish an impeachment tribunal into her fitness for office, on a show-cause letter citing complaints of dishonesty, ineptness and substandard prosecutions (Lesotho Times reporting via The Free Library, 2024). The government states its case plainly and it is recorded: complaints against Motinyane's handling of dockets long predate this government, including a written warning in June 2010, and the LMPS and DCEO had also complained of her lack of prosecution directives (government answering affidavit via Lesotho Times, 2024). The two accounts — retaliation for a refusal, or overdue discipline of an underperforming officer — are both in the record; what is not in dispute is the order of events: the refusal came first, and the removal machinery followed it.
  • The legislature his majority controls voted to keep the second door open for itself. On 1 November 2024 the National Assembly rejected the Conflict of Interest Bill, a private member's bill — introduced, it is worth noting, by Basotho Covenant Movement leader Tšepo Lipholo — that would have barred ministers and MPs from doing business with the state, as section 59 of the constitution requires parliament to legislate. It took the Constitutional Court to close the door: in a judgment reported in September 2026, the court nullified the rejection vote and ordered Parliament to enact conflict-of-interest legislation within 24 months (Sunday Express, September 2026). The case papers' leading example, as reported, was serving minister Lebona Lephema's acquisition of Mothae Diamonds — a company in which the government itself holds a 30 percent stake — without a competitive bidding process while he sat in the cabinet that oversees it (Sunday Express, September 2026; Lesotho Times reporting via The Free Library, December 2024). The acquisition is recorded as documented in the court papers and press reporting; no court has ruled on Lephema's own conduct, and none is asserted here.
  • The anti-corruption instrument itself has been run in ways that track the political map. The DCEO's director general, Knorx Molelle — appointed by Matekane in February 2023 — had his own appointment's legality questioned by the Law Society of Lesotho, which wrote to the prime minister stating that Molelle did not appear on the official roll of legal practitioners, a substantive qualification for the post (Law Society of Lesotho letter, reported 2025). In January 2025 leaked audio recordings captured Molelle in conversation with opposition BNP leader Machesetsa Mofomobe, disparaging the prime minister who appointed him and discussing matters still under investigation (Lesotho Times editorial via The Free Library, 17 January 2025). Analysts assessing the third anniversary recorded that the Government Assets Search Recovery Task Team was dissolved after, in Dr Moeketsi Kali's assessment, "the trail led to close allies" — an analyst's characterization, recorded as such — and procurement irregularities alleged around the M184 million Moshoeshoe I International Airport facelift tender (Lesotho Times three-year review via The Free Library, October 2025). The airport allegation is an ALLEGATION; no tribunal finding appears in the record assembled here.
  • The uniform has operated as its own law. Under Operation Hard Fist (Test 4), soldiers who killed and tortured civilians in 2024 faced, in the record assembled here, an internal investigation ordered by the government — and no documented prosecution, published finding, or compensation outcome, while civil claims against the LDF commander reached some M50 million in a single year (Lesotho Times via The Free Library, 2025). A law that reaches a minister's opponents and stops at the barracks gate is two laws.

Why the grade lands here: Test 1 is decided at the source, and at the source sits an admitted fact: the second-most senior member of this government asked the public prosecutor to drop treason charges against two of the prime minister's coalition partners, and when she refused, the prime minister's own hand set her removal in motion. The government's performance case against her may be genuine — it is printed above at full strength — but a performance process that begins only after a refused instruction teaches every future officeholder the same lesson, whichever motive was true. Around that center: a parliament that voted down the conflict-of-interest law the constitution obliges it to pass until a court ordered otherwise, an anti-corruption chief whose own credentials and conduct became the story, and soldiers unaccounted for in the courts. The FOR shelf is real — the DPP still stands, the charges still stand, a constitutional anti-corruption commission now exists — but those are the system's antibodies working against the executive's pressure, not the executive giving by one law.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Mochoboroane and Metsing, whose treason charges the government sought to end (the help was attempted, not delivered; the charges stand); ministers and MPs who wished to keep doing business with the state, whose door stayed open from November 2024 until the court ordered it closed; officials inside the security services, shielded so far from courtroom account. Hurt — DPP Motinyane, put through a removal process after refusing an instruction she swore was unconstitutional; the public, whose conflict-of-interest law was delayed by years; civilians abused under Operation Hard Fist, whose cases have produced claims and statements but no documented verdicts; and every honest official watching the sequence and pricing the cost of refusal.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts have ruled at the highest level against the preferences of sitting politicians throughout his tenure, and the rulings stand. In June 2024 the Court of Appeal, by majority, affirmed that the Ninth Amendment to the Constitution was invalid for violating the constitution's basic structure — the amendment had barred a prime minister facing a no-confidence vote from advising the King to dissolve parliament — and on 1 November 2024 the same court dismissed a review application and refused to suspend its order (Court of Appeal of Lesotho, C of A (CIV) No 13/2024 [2024] LSCA 17). The doctrine cuts both ways for Matekane — the ruling restored a power of his office — but the judiciary asserted, and exercised, the authority to strike down a constitutional amendment itself, and no judge was removed, packed, or punished for it in the record assembled here.
  • The referees are visibly working against his own signature measures in open process. The Law Society of Lesotho (7 September 2025) and activist Kananelo Boloetse with the civic organisation Section 2 (15 September 2025) have challenged the Tenth Amendment — his government's flagship reform — in the Constitutional Court, arguing it required a two-thirds majority or a referendum for entrenched clauses; the court has invited interested organisations to join the proceedings, and the challenges remained undecided in the record assembled here while implementation proceeds in parallel (Civicus Monitor, 2025; Enabling Environment Snapshot, January and June 2026). The Constitutional Court's September 2026 conflict-of-interest judgment likewise ordered his own parliament to legislate against its recorded vote (Sunday Express, September 2026).
  • The electoral referee that seated him has not been documented as interfered with: the Independent Electoral Commission ran the 7 October 2022 election that SADC and international observers certified as peaceful, free and fair (South African DIRCO briefing to Parliament, 2022), and no documented executive interference with the IEC or the central bank appears in the record assembled here. The next election is due in 2027, inside the term.
  • Critical media operate daily and name him freely: the Lesotho Times, Sunday Express, The Post and others publish the investigations and editorials cited throughout this dossier, including calls for his anti-corruption chief to resign. Lesotho's RSF Press Freedom Index ranking, after a collapse from 67th in 2023 to 122nd in 2024 — a fall driven substantially by the May 2023 murder of investigative radio presenter Ralikonelo Joki, addressed below — recovered to 107th in 2025 and 89th of 180 in the 2026 index, with a score of 54.37 (RSF World Press Freedom Index 2024–2026; Afrobarometer Dispatch AD1051).

Evidence against:

  • The prosecution's independence was punished in the open (Test 1): a refused instruction to drop charges against coalition partners, followed by a planned sideways redeployment and an impeachment-tribunal process initiated by the prime minister personally. Whatever the merits of the performance complaints, the DPP's tenure security — the mechanism that makes prosecutorial independence real — was placed under executive process immediately after the one documented act of independence in the file. That is the definition this test grades.
  • Parliament's core function was threatened by the men with guns, on his behalf and unanswered by him. On 16 October 2023, as the opposition tabled its no-confidence motion, the commanders of the Lesotho Defence Force, the Lesotho Mounted Police Service and the National Security Service issued a joint public statement warning MPs against removing the government — described in contemporaneous analysis as in effect a threat to step in and prevent a parliamentary vote (ACCORD, Conflict Trends; International IDEA, Global State of Democracy, October 2023; In Transformation monthly report, November 2023). No document in the record assembled here shows Matekane ordering the statement; equally, none shows him publicly repudiating it, disciplining its authors, or affirming parliament's right to vote as it pleased. The motion was then frozen by a court petition from one of his own MPs and never reached a vote (Test 3). He also holds the defence portfolio himself, since November 2023 — the army that warned parliament answers to a ministry he personally heads (Cabinet of Lesotho; UN General Assembly listing, September 2026).
  • The anti-corruption agency was used, once documented, as an instrument against the press. On 29 August 2025 the DCEO arrested Mohalenyane Phakela, editor of the Lesotho Times and Sunday Express, hours after his newspapers reported alleged corruption by the Lesotho Correctional Service commissioner; he was interrogated for seven hours, detained overnight, and his phones confiscated without a search warrant, on an accusation of impersonating a DCEO officer. The DCEO subsequently admitted there was no evidence to support the allegations and dropped the case (Civicus Monitor, 2025; Paradigm Initiative, 2025; Basotho Media Development Association statement, 2026). An arrest the arresting agency itself concedes was baseless, of an editor who had just published a corruption story, is a grip mark regardless of who ordered it.
  • The wider pattern around journalists and critics on his watch: activist and lawyer Tšolo "Tjekatjeka" Thakeli was arrested on 15 June 2025 after posting a video criticising Matekane's jobs record, and charged with inciting public violence and disturbing the peace (Civicus Monitor, 2025; The Guardian, 30 June 2025). News editor Kananelo Boloetse — the same Boloetse litigating against the government's constitutional amendment — was abducted from his home and assaulted by unidentified individuals on 5 September 2026; the perpetrators are unidentified and no attribution to the state is made here, but RSF called for an independent investigation, and the attack sits in a year in which a radio station, KEL Radio, was also vandalised (RSF, 9 September 2026; BMDA statement, 2026). The May 2023 murder of Ralikonelo Joki — shot outside his radio station after death threats over a corruption story, on Matekane's watch though not attributable to his government — remains unresolved in the record assembled here, and his government's first response to it was a nationwide curfew on the whole population (May 2023) rather than a documented breakthrough in the case (BMDA, 2026; Wikipedia, Sam Matekane).
  • The security forces' own conduct under his defence ministry compounds the grip picture: an LDF major general was reported in August 2024 to have threatened attorneys and judges handling cases of people detained by the army during Operation Hard Fist (Amnesty International reporting, September 2024). A serving general threatening the lawyers and judges in his cases is referee-gripping in its rawest form; no documented disciplinary outcome appears in the record assembled here.

Why the grade lands here: The courts are the strongest FOR shelf in this dossier — they have struck a constitutional amendment, ordered his parliament to legislate, and are hearing challenges to his flagship reform in the open. But Test 2 asks whether independence is punished, and in the two referees closest to the executive's daily reach — the prosecution and the press — the documented answer is yes: the prosecutor who refused an instruction was put into a removal process by the prime minister's own hand, and the editor who published a corruption story was arrested by the executive's anti-corruption agency on a charge the agency admitted was empty. Above both hangs October 2023, when the security chiefs publicly warned parliament off a confidence vote and the prime minister — who within weeks took the defence portfolio for himself — let the warning stand uncorrected in the record. The judiciary's survival keeps this from being a story of total capture; it does not acquit the seat of gripping the referees it could reach.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the executive itself, whose survival in October 2023 was underwritten by the security chiefs' warning and whose coalition partners were shielded from prosecution pressure; the DCEO's leadership, unaccountable in the record for a conceded baseless arrest. Hurt — DPP Motinyane and the institution of prosecutorial independence; editor Mohalenyane Phakela (a night in custody, phones seized, trauma reported); activist Tšolo Thakeli; MPs whose constitutional vote was publicly warned against by armed services; and the viewing public, whose confidence that referees are safe is the actual asset this test measures.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The defining external shock of his tenure was answered with open diplomacy rather than denial. In April 2025 the United States announced a 50 percent tariff on Lesotho's exports — the highest rate levied on any country — against a garment sector employing some 34,000–36,000 workers, about three-quarters of them women, and supplying the great majority of Lesotho's exports to its second-largest trading partner. His government negotiated; the rate was reduced to 15 percent effective 7 August 2025; when AGOA lapsed on 30 September 2025 his government campaigned for its renewal, and AGOA was reauthorised on 3 February 2026, retroactive to the lapse, though only to 31 December 2026; after the US Supreme Court struck down the reciprocal-tariff programme, the remaining tariffs expired on 29 July 2026 and Lesotho's duty-free access was restored, with Lesotho also escaping the July 2026 Section 301 round (Torch Media Africa, 2026; Moneyweb, 2026; IMF 2025 Article IV reporting). The tolls of the shock are kept separate, as they were counted separately: unions documented at least 12,000 garment workers laid off or placed on unpaid leave; the IMF assessed roughly 30,000 jobs at risk; the trade minister warned up to 40,000 could be lost across the wider value chain (Torch Media Africa, 2026). Real GDP growth fell from 5.2 percent in 2024 to 1.4 percent in 2025, with 0.5 percent projected for 2026 (African Development Bank country outlook, June 2026).
  • The no-good-choice clause is applied to that shock by its mechanics. The options actually open to the prime minister of an enclave economy of 2.3 million people, wholly surrounded by South Africa and dependent on a preference programme written in Washington, were: accept the 50 percent rate and watch the sector die in months; negotiate and lobby for reduction and renewal, accepting humiliating dependence in the meantime; or attempt retaliation Lesotho had no capacity to make felt. Each carried real harm; he took the negotiating door, in the open, and the constraint is recorded, not counted against him. What remains gradable is the trap's construction and the response's honesty: the AGOA dependence was built over two decades before he took office and is not his making, though two years of his government before the shock produced no documented diversification that changed the exposure; and his government stated the risk figures publicly rather than minimising them.
  • The 2024 drought was answered in the same open register: on 12 July 2024, with some 699,049 Basotho assessed food-insecure for the 2024/25 consumption year, he declared a national food insecurity disaster, published the numbers against his own government, and launched a costed national response plan with an international appeal (UN Lesotho; DG ECHO daily flash, 22 July 2024). The youth unemployment crisis was likewise named rather than denied: at a packed June 2025 youth summit convened by his own office, he declared unemployment a national emergency — the very declaration youth marchers had demanded since 2020 — and it was gazetted on 7 July 2025 as a two-year national state of disaster over unemployment, running to 30 June 2027 (Duke University field account, 2025; Torch Media Africa, 2026). The delivery against that declaration is graded on Tests 4 and 5; the answering is graded here, and naming a crisis against oneself is the opposite of denial.
  • Parliament's pushback was answered politically, in the open. Facing the October 2023 no-confidence motion with the opposition holding the numbers, he rebuilt his majority by coalition — bringing in the Basotho Action Party and smaller parties in November 2023 — a public, constitutional mechanism, whatever its price (graded on Test 5) and whatever shadow the security chiefs' statement cast over the same weeks (graded on Test 2). When the Court of Appeal struck down the Ninth Amendment in 2024, the ruling stood unchallenged by executive action; when his own Tenth Amendment was challenged in 2025, the government defended it in court rather than defying the forum (Court of Appeal, [2024] LSCA 17; Civicus Monitor, 2025).
  • The other-option rule is satisfied in the reforms file, in form: when the single omnibus constitutional bill proved unpassable — the same bill whose pre-election failure defined the 2022 campaign — his government split the reforms into separate amendment bills in 2023–2024 and carried the Tenth Amendment through as a standalone measure, gazetted August 2025. Another option became available; it was considered and taken openly, with the reasons (a decade of total blockage) stated. Critics — the Law Society, Section 2, and National University of Lesotho analysts — call the result piecemeal and top-down and dispute its procedure in court; that dispute is recorded on Tests 1 and 2. Considering an option and having its execution contested is not the same as refusing to look.

Evidence against:

  • The street's pushback has repeatedly been answered with the instruments of the state rather than with adjustment. The July 2025 youth march on unemployment was cancelled at the last moment because the police-issued permit bore the wrong date — 3 June instead of 4 July; organisers allege the error was a deliberate mishandling to stop the march, an ALLEGATION the police account disputes by silence in the record assembled here, and the march did not proceed (Lesotho NewsDesk, July 2025). The activist who asked, on video, where the promised jobs were was arrested within days (Test 2). In February 2025, police whipped and arrested Mokhotlong taxi operators, contractors and villagers protesting the allocation of Lesotho Highlands Water Project jobs to outsiders, and soldiers violently removed community members protesting at the Polihali Dam launch (Centre for Investigative Journalism, Enabling Environment Snapshot, March 2025). The November 2025 union petition — delivered to his representatives, not to him, a substitution the unions publicly protested — demanded, among other things, the release of two detained men and an end to government interference in the security agencies; the record assembled here documents no substantive public reply (Sunday Express, 2 November 2025).
  • His own words lit the fuse of the worst security wobble of his tenure, and the answer afterward was thinner than the event. In early August 2024 he urged the deputy army commander to "do everything in your power to ensure that peace returns to Lesotho"; in the weeks that followed, Operation Hard Fist soldiers killed at least two civilians and tortured at least four others on Amnesty International's count — at least five tortured on International IDEA's — the counts kept separate as sourced (Amnesty International, September 2024; International IDEA, September 2024). He subsequently clarified that he had not called for torture or killing, and the government ordered the LDF commander to investigate the Berea deaths and stated it had not authorised the killing of civilians (Sunday Express, 2025). But the investigation's findings, and any prosecution or compensation flowing from it, do not appear in the record assembled here two years on; the clarification answered the words, and then the file went quiet. The crime emergency that prompted the operation was real — famo-gang killings, including five members of one family shot in Leribe in 2024, and the unsolved murder of journalist Ralikonelo Joki — and the no-good-choice clause covers the decision to act: a police-only response through an under-resourced LMPS, an army deployment with its known risks, or leaving armed gangs in possession of the streets. He chose the army; the choice is not held against him. The vanished accountability afterward is not the choice, and it is graded.
  • The October 2023 survival also shows the limit of the adjustment: alongside the open coalition-building sat an appeal to SADC to intervene against a domestic parliamentary process, a court petition by his own MP that froze the motion while numbers were rebuilt, and the security chiefs' public warning (ACCORD, Conflict Trends). The wobble was survived by every instrument available, including the ones that do not belong in a parliament's arithmetic.

Why the grade lands here: Two different rulers appear in this file, sorted by what is pushing. When the push comes from events — a tariff shock, a drought, a jobs crisis, a blocked reform bill — he adjusts in the open: negotiates, declares the disaster against his own record, publishes the bad numbers, splits the bill and tries another door. When the push comes from people — marchers, petitioners, a critic with a phone camera, communities in the way of a dam — the documented answer runs to permits that fail, petitions received by proxy, arrests, whips, and soldiers. A test that asks one question gets a split answer, and MIXED is the honest grade for it.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — garment workers and their households, insofar as the negotiated path preserved a sector that a 50 percent tariff would have ended outright (partially, and for now: AGOA expires again on 31 December 2026); drought-affected households reached by the declared disaster response; the unemployed, whose crisis was at least officially named. Hurt — the 12,000-plus garment workers laid off or put on unpaid leave during the shock his diplomacy could only soften; the July 2025 marchers whose permit failed; the Mokhotlong and Polihali protesters met with force; and the families of the men killed in Operation Hard Fist, still waiting on an investigation the government announced and the record does not show completed.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the garment workers, the herd boys, the rural hungry, the youth, the prisoners, and the opposition — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The hungry are counted, in public, by his own machinery, and the counts are published even when they indict his government. The Lesotho Vulnerability Assessment put 699,049 people — roughly a third of the population — food-insecure in 2024/25, and his government declared the disaster on those numbers (UN Lesotho, 2024). The Disaster Management Authority's November 2026 vulnerability report records the food-insecure population falling from 524,963 in 2025/26 to 492,284 in 2026/27 — 23.7 percent of the population — with the worst months still ahead (DMA report via Mohale FM, November 2026). The IPC's rural-only counts, a different instrument measuring a different population, are kept separate: 403,000 projected in IPC Phase 3 or above for October 2024–March 2025; 258,000 for May–September 2025; 234,000 for May–September 2026, rising to a projected 311,000 in the October 2026–March 2027 lean season (IPC country analyses, 2025–2026; ACAPS, 2026). A government that publishes its own hunger is counting its hungry; the levels themselves — with 49.7 percent of Basotho below the national poverty line and 36 percent of young children chronically malnourished (World Bank figures via ACAPS, 2026) — are the context the counting serves, not a charge invented for this page.
  • The garment workers — the country's largest private-sector workforce, roughly three-quarters women — were treated as the constituency to be saved in the tariff crisis, not as collateral: the diplomacy, the disaster declaration over unemployment, and the public risk figures all centred them (Test 3). The 2026/27 budget attaches a mandatory 40 percent youth labour participation requirement to government infrastructure projects, expands the National Volunteer Corps, and prioritises a National Youth Development Bill (Torch Media Africa, 2026).
  • The marginalised were written into the constitution itself: the Tenth Amendment's section 20A introduces affirmative-action measures for groups marginalised by gender, age, disability or historical discrimination, and section 22A opens public-interest litigation to individuals and associations — counting-in mechanisms, on paper, whose implementation the court challenges have left uncertain (Civicus Monitor, 2025; Enabling Environment Snapshot, January 2026). His first cabinet, whatever its later growth, seated three women among fifteen ministers — 20 percent, against 14 percent in the larger cabinet he inherited (Wikipedia, Sam Matekane).

Evidence against:

  • The least-counted people in Lesotho are its herd boys, and two of them died in army custody. In Berea district in September 2024, Operation Hard Fist soldiers beat the area chief, Lesaoana Masupha, into intensive care and killed two young shepherds taken from a neighbouring village; the soldiers, on the community's account, were searching for firearms and found none (Lesotho Times via The Free Library, 2024; Sunday Express, 2025). Herd boys appear in no ministry's statistics; they appeared in this file as bodies. The wider operation's toll, kept in its sourced counts: at least two killed and at least four tortured (Amnesty International), at least five tortured (International IDEA), and M50 million in civil claims against the LDF commander in a single year from civilians alleging brutalisation (Lesotho Times, 2025).
  • Prisoners were counted as bodies to be beaten. The Ombudswoman's report of 19 March 2024 found that staff and trainees at Maseru Central Correctional Institution had intentionally provoked a confrontation in December 2023 to justify beating more than 300 inmates (Ombudswoman Tlotliso Polaki's report via International IDEA, March 2024). In early 2026, reports described inmates sleeping in shifts on concrete floors amid TB and skin-disease outbreaks (Human Rights Portal country profile, 2026). No documented prosecution arising from the Ombudswoman's findings appears in the record assembled here.
  • The opposition's own are counted as threats. BCM leader Tšepo Lipholo — an MP, and the mover of the conflict-of-interest bill — was arrested at his home on 2 July 2025 by police and soldiers and held at Maseru Central Correctional Institution for over five months, on charges that grew from sedition, incitement and disrespecting the Royal Family to human trafficking and treason, before the High Court admitted him to bail in December 2025; arrest warrants were also issued for four BCM members who had fled into exile (Sunday Express, 2025; Breaking Africa News, 2025–2026). Every charge against Lipholo is an ALLEGATION awaiting trial, and the state's security account — that his movement was recruiting young men for military-style training in South Africa — was stated publicly by the security agencies in July 2025 and is recorded; what the file shows is an opposition MP spending five months in a cell, on opposed bail applications, before any court heard the merits. Former army commander Tlali Kamoli, in custody since his arrest in 2017 with his trial still unfinished, marks the longer arc of the same counting: the unions' November 2025 petition demanded both men's release, and the leader of the opposition publicly called for bail for both (Sunday Express, November 2025).
  • The youth are counted in speeches and discounted in delivery: unemployment reached 30.1 percent in the 2024 Labour Force Survey, up from 22.5 percent in 2019, with youth unemployment at 38.9–39.1 percent (Bureau of Statistics LFS 2024; African Development Bank, 2026) — on the watch of a government elected by the young on a jobs promise, and in the same years that thousands of young Basotho queued overnight in winter cold for 500 army posts (Duke University field account, 2025). Communities at Mokhotlong and Polihali asking to be counted in the water project's jobs were whipped and removed by force (Test 3). Prof Motlamelle Kapa's third-anniversary assessment adds the administrative version of the same charge: food-security responses arrived late, and appointments ran on politics over merit (Lesotho Times, October 2025).

Why the grade lands here: The counting machinery itself is honest — arguably the strongest shelf in this dossier: his government publishes hunger counts against itself, declares disasters on them, and wrote the marginalised into the constitutional text. But a count is not only a statistic. The people with the least protection in the kingdom — herd boys in the mountains, inmates in Maseru Central, an opposition MP in a remand cell, villagers in the way of a dam — met the state as force first and accounting later, or not at all. Both halves are documented; neither cancels the other; MIXED is where the evidence leaves it.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — drought- and hunger-affected households inside the declared disaster response; garment workers whose sector the state fought to preserve; marginalised groups named in section 20A, whose gain is real on paper and pending in practice; young people inside the 2026/27 budget's labour-participation rules. Hurt — the two Berea herd boys, killed, and Chief Masupha, beaten; the 300-plus inmates of Maseru Central; Lipholo and the BCM members in exile; the Mokhotlong and Polihali protesters; and the unemployed young, counted at 39 percent and still waiting for the count to become a job.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 28 October 2022, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The lean state — delivered exactly as promised, on day one. The coalition's founding pledges included cutting the cabinet from 26 ministries to 15. The cabinet sworn in on 4 November 2022 had 15 ministries — the largest such cut since independence, from a predecessor cabinet of more than 30 including deputies — and ministers, himself included, were to declare their assets, use their own cars, and live in their own homes "until things have gone back to normalcy" (UN Common Country Analysis 2023; Business Day, 11 October 2022; Wikipedia, Cabinet of Lesotho). The first half of that word was kept in full and on schedule; its later erosion is on the against shelf, where it belongs, but the keeping happened.
  • The reforms — delivered late, in altered form, but delivered in part. The 100-day pledge (below) was broken on its own terms; the substance moved. His government split the blocked omnibus reforms into separate bills, carried the Tenth Amendment through the National Assembly in January 2025 and into the gazette on 13 August 2025 — parliamentary oversight of expenditure, a parliamentary service, a strengthened Human Rights Commission, an entrenched anti-corruption commission, public-interest litigation, affirmative action — and presented it himself at the SADC summit that month (International IDEA, August 2025; Civicus Monitor, 2025). The Eleventh Amendment, carrying the remainder of the SADC-mediated reform programme, remains unpassed as of grading (Wikipedia, Sam Matekane). At the August 2023 SADC summit he had committed to accelerating the reforms; the acceleration took two more years, and arrived.
  • The tariff word — worked as stated. He told the garment sector and the country that his government would fight the US measures; it negotiated the 50 percent rate down to 15 percent, campaigned AGOA back into law, and saw duty-free access restored in July 2026 (Test 3). The sector survives, wounded; the word was pursued with the means available, and the mandate rule applies: pursuing the platform he was elected on is credited here, not penalised.
  • Fiscal stabilisation — claimed, and partly corroborated. In November 2026 he told an RFP rally that government finances had stabilised after being depleted and investor interest was rising (Mohale FM, November 2026). The corroborated part is narrower: the state did not default, salaries and the disaster responses were funded through a drought and a tariff shock, and the growth collapse of 2025–26 is attributed by the African Development Bank to the external shock rather than to a fiscal blowout (AfDB, June 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The 100-day word — the founding promise, broken on its face. At the coalition's announcement in October 2022, Matekane pledged that within 100 days the government would downsize the ministries, pass the reform bills in Parliament, fight crime and corruption, and have all ministers declare their assets (Business Ghana, October 2022; Moneyweb, October 2022). The downsizing was done (above). The reform bills were not passed in 100 days, or in 1,000: the first tranche arrived in August 2025, nearly three years in, in a split form the opposition had not agreed, and the Eleventh Amendment is still outstanding four years on. National University of Lesotho analysts marking the third anniversary put it plainly: the administration "dithered in implementing the broad-based national reforms programme despite promises… to do so within 100 days" (Prof Motlamelle Kapa via Lesotho Times, October 2025).
  • The jobs word — stated with a number and a date, and missed entire. In June 2025, declaring the unemployment emergency, he promised 70,000 jobs within weeks — "by the end of the month" in the summit account (Duke University field account, 2025; Lesotho Times via The Free Library, 2025). No such jobs materialised in the documented record. By November 2025 the trade unions were petitioning against a promise they cited at 62,000 jobs — the public argument over whether the number had quietly shrunk is itself part of the record — stating that "nothing has materialised," and demanding a M1,000 unemployment grant by 1 December 2025 (Sunday Express, 2 November 2025). Unemployment, on the official survey, had risen from 22.5 percent (2019) to 30.1 percent (2024) during his tenure (Bureau of Statistics). A word given with a figure and a deadline, missed in full, with the shortfall answered by arresting the man who asked about it on video (Tests 2–3), is the heaviest single entry on this shelf.
  • The corruption word — the "hallmark" that the index cannot find. Anti-corruption was, in the analysts' phrase, "meant to be the hallmark of the RFP 'revolution'." Four years on, the CPI is exactly where he found it (37 in 2022; 37 in 2025, by way of 39 in 2023); the asset-recovery task team was dissolved amid the allegation that its trail reached his allies (ALLEGATION-level analyst assessment, recorded as such); the DCEO's leadership became a rolling scandal of credentials and leaked audio; and the conflict-of-interest law his MPs voted down had to be ordered into existence by a court (Tests 1–2; Transparency International CPI 2022–2025). The Tenth Amendment's entrenched anti-corruption commission is the counter-entry, and it is recorded above; a commission on paper in 2025 does not redeem a hallmark promised for 2022.
  • The lean word — kept, then spent. The 15-ministry cabinet of November 2022 became 20 ministries in November 2023, when survival required seats for new coalition partners, and 21 after the 2026 reshuffle (Wikipedia, Cabinet of Lesotho; Lesotho Times, 2026). The expansion bought stability — a real good, recorded in the crossings below — but the word as given ("downsize… until things have gone back to normalcy") was not the word as kept, and the austerity posture of personal cars and private homes faded with it.
  • The business word — substantially kept, with the allegation recorded. He pledged in 2022 to step back from his business interests to avoid conflict. The documented record shows his Matekane Mining Investment Company's Letšeng Diamonds contract was bought out by the mine (Lesotho Times court-case reporting, December 2024). Against that stands BNP leader Mofomobe's public claim that the prime minister receives "a M30 million cheque every month" from the diamond mines — made, as the same reporting notes, without producing evidence; it is recorded here as an ALLEGATION, unproven, and it is not treated as a finding anywhere in this dossier.

Why the grade lands here: The word file splits cleanly in two. The institutional words given at the founding — a lean cabinet, a completed reform process — were executed, the first exactly and the second years late and in contested form. The economic words — corruption as a hallmark, jobs by the tens of thousands on a stated deadline — were not: the index is flat, the jobs did not come, and the promise's own number became a public argument. A ruler whose kept words are the ones about the state's shape and whose broken words are the ones about people's livelihoods earns neither HOLDS nor the bottom grade; the crossings below show why the two axes must not be averaged away.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the fiscus and the state's credibility in 2022, by a lean cabinet delivered as promised; the reform process's beneficiaries-in-waiting, by a Tenth Amendment that eventually arrived; garment workers, by a tariff word pursued to a negotiated end. Hurt — the unemployed young, who were given a number and a date and received neither, and whose questioner was arrested; every citizen who priced the anti-corruption hallmark into their 2022 vote; and the government's own partners and appointees, seated and unseated as the cabinet grew from 15 to 21 to hold the coalition the lean word had not budgeted for.

The four crossings, named: 1. Kept his word AND it helped: the November 2022 downsizing — 26 ministries to 15, exactly as pledged, the largest cut since independence — and the Tenth Amendment's eventual enactment, which carried the promised reforms' first tranche into law. 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt: Operation Hard Fist. He promised to fight crime and restore safety, and the operation was that promise in uniform — launched after his own "do everything in your power" instruction — and it killed at least two civilians, tortured others, and left a chief in intensive care. The keeping earns its Test 5 credit; the hurt is recorded in full on Tests 1 and 4, and neither launders the other. 3. Went against his word AND it helped: the November 2023 coalition expansion. It broke the lean-cabinet word within a year of giving it, and it bought the one thing Lesotho's politics chronically lacks — a government that finished its term's middle years without falling, in a country that had cycled through five prime ministers in a decade. The break is recorded here; the stability is recorded with it. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt: the 70,000 jobs of June 2025 — a number, a deadline, and then nothing the unions or the statistics could find, in a country where youth unemployment stands near 39 percent; and the 100-day reform deadline, whose three-year slippage left the instability the reforms exist to cure unaddressed through most of his term.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Sam Matekane came to power as the outsider the system could not digest: a businessman who built a party in six months, self-funded it, and promised Basotho a revolution of competence — lean government, declared assets, reforms in 100 days, corruption fought as a hallmark. Four years on, the record shows two governments sharing one office. The first answers events like a grown-up: it published its own hunger counts and declared the disaster on them, negotiated a 50 percent tariff down and a trade lifeline back into law, named the unemployment emergency the street had demanded, and eventually carried the first tranche of a reform programme that had defeated every predecessor since 2012. The second government protects its own: its deputy prime minister asked the prosecutor to drop treason charges against coalition partners — admitted on affidavit — and the prime minister set the prosecutor's removal in motion when she refused; its security chiefs warned parliament off a confidence vote and were never publicly corrected; its anti-corruption agency arrested an editor on a charge it conceded was baseless; its soldiers killed herd boys and beat a chief, and the investigation it announced has no documented end. The why of the two FAILS is that both sit at the source — one law for allies is not one law, and a referee who can be removed for refusing is not independent — while the courts, to their credit and his constraint, kept ruling in the open against everyone including him. The why of the three MIXED grades is that the delivery record genuinely splits: the institutional words were kept (late, expanded, contested, but kept), the livelihood words were not, the hungry are honestly counted and still hungry at 492,284 souls on his own authority's November 2026 count, and the wobble is answered with diplomacy when it comes from the world and with force when it comes from the street. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the DPP's court fight ends with her independence vindicated or her office emptied; whether the Hard Fist investigation ever publishes findings or reaches a courtroom; whether parliament enacts the conflict-of-interest law inside the court's 24 months; whether the Eleventh Amendment completes the reforms before the 2027 election; whether AGOA's 31 December 2026 expiry is survived a second time; and whether the treason trial of an opposition MP who spent five months on remand produces a verdict the country can read as law rather than as arithmetic.
