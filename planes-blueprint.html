Why? — What it is

A plane blueprint that leaves the proven airplane exactly as it is, and adds one layer over it: the house absorption field, answering the loads and energies the airframe was never able to absorb — only endure.

How? — What it means

Every limit on a modern airliner — gust loads, maneuver loads, crash energy — is an airframe limit. The field is a candidate for sharing those loads before they reach the structure, the same move the Cars blueprint makes at road speeds, at flying speeds and flying energies.

Act. — What it could mean for you

The design is copyleft. You may build it, test it, improve it, and publish your version — the same freedom has to travel with it. Start at the Build Order: models and wind tunnels before anything with wings leaves the ground.

The Proven Base — Flight Is Solved

Start with what is true and printed as proven: a powered, controlled, fixed-wing aircraft is one of the most thoroughly solved machines humans have ever built. Four forces, understood for over a century: lift up, weight down, thrust forward, drag back. Balance them and the machine flies — millions of times a year, on schedule, in weather.

THIS BLUEPRINT · DESIGN

The field over the airframe

Not a new airplane. The same airplane — wings, engines, structure, procedures — with one layer added over it, aimed at the three hard cases the airframe alone still answers by enduring.

Why planes keep their wings: once an aircraft is moving, a wing makes lift for nearly free — the engine pays for drag, and the wing turns forward motion into altitude-holding force at a bargain. That is documented aerodynamics, and it is why this page's sibling, the Ship blueprint, uses its ringed-prop VTOL configuration for a different job — vertical lift where there is no runway and no forward speed to spend — while planes, which live at speed, keep the efficient answer. Different jobs, different shapes, one shelf.

02 / DESIGN

The Design — The Field Over the Airframe

One layer, wrapped around a proven airplane. The field does not fly the plane — the wing does that, as it always has. The field takes what the sky and the ground throw at the airframe, first. Tap a layer to read what it does.

The honest numbers — energy at flying speeds

A plane's kinetic energy is ½mv², and at flying speeds and airliner masses the number stops being a car number and becomes a power-station number. Pick an aircraft, drag the speed, and look at what a forced landing asks something — field, structure, terrain, procedure — to absorb. This is why field capacity sits on the Open Hows shelf at full size.

At cruise (≈560 mph), a 737-class aircraft carries roughly 2.5 billion joules of kinetic energy. Nobody sane proposes stopping that all at once — a forced landing is flown, braked, and spread over distance and time by procedure. The field's design job is to be the first absorber in that sequence, not the only one. The size of its share is the open how.

03 / THE QUESTIONS

What the Field Answers That the Airframe Can't

The airframe's answers to violence from the sky are all the same answer: be strong enough, and endure it. Strength has limits, and passengers feel every bit of what the structure endures. The field is a different kind of answer — absorption, placed outside the structure.

Design

Turbulence and gusts — absorbed, not endured

A gust is a sudden shove of air. Today the shove lands on the wing, the wing bends, the fuselage rolls and drops, and everyone inside feels the sky hit the airplane. In this design the field wraps the airframe, so the gust's energy is absorbed in the field layer before it becomes structural load and cabin motion. The ride smooths not because the sky calmed down, but because the shove never fully arrived.

Documented anchor: transport airliners are certified to maneuver load limits of about +2.5 g / −1 g, and gust loads are sized into the structure by regulation. Those numbers exist because gusts are real forces the metal must survive. The field's candidacy is to take its share before the metal is asked.

Design

Crash energy — the first absorber, at flying speeds

The Cars blueprint makes the field the first absorber in a crash, ahead of crumple zones and the safety cell. The logic is identical here — and the numbers are not. Use the calculator above: even at approach speed, a narrow-body airliner carries hundreds of millions of joules. The field is designed as the first layer of a sequence — field, then structure, then terrain and procedure — never as a magic stop.

Printed plainly: how much of that energy a field can actually take, at airliner scale, is unknown. It is the headline open how on this page, not a footnote.

Design candidate · Open How

Structural limits — a load-sharing layer

Every maneuver and gust limit placarded in a cockpit is, underneath, an airframe limit: pull harder and the wing, not the sky, says no. If the field can share load — carrying part of a gust's or a turn's force before it reaches the spar — the effective limits of the same airframe rise. That is the design candidate. Whether a field can share load at all, and how much, is unproven and labeled so: OPEN HOW.

Documented why

The Ship crossover — siblings, not rivals

The Ship blueprint flies on a ringed-prop VTOL configuration: lift straight up, no runway, no forward speed required — the right answer where you must rise from a deck, a clearing, or another world. A plane's job is the opposite: it lives at speed, where a wing produces lift for nearly free. So the shelf keeps both: VTOL where you start from zero, wings where you live at speed — and the same field over either, because turbulence and bad days do not care which one you chose.

Tap to open — Why absorption, when planes already survive turbulence?

Surviving is not the same as not being battered. Severe turbulence injures passengers and crew every year — people thrown against ceilings and cabin fittings — and gust loads are a standing fatigue and inspection burden on every airframe. The structure endures; the people and the maintenance log absorb the difference.

The whole transportation shelf runs on one move, repeated at every scale: move the violence out of the people and the structure, into a layer designed to take it. Crumple zones did it for cars. This blueprint asks the same move of the sky.

04 / ACT

Build Order — Small, Uncrewed, Measured

Nothing crewed flies on an unproven absorption claim. Models and wind-tunnel scale first, uncrewed before crewed, small before large — and every stage is gated on published absorption numbers. A stage that misses its numbers stops the line; it does not get averaged away.

  1. Bench and wind-tunnel scale

    Field units on instrumented models in a wind tunnel: calibrated gusts across the model, measuring how much load reaches the structure with the field off versus on. The gust case is proven on a bench before it is ever claimed in the sky.

    GATE: PUBLISHED GUST-ABSORPTION NUMBERS, REPEATABLE

  2. Uncrewed small aircraft

    A field fitted to a small uncrewed aircraft, flown through measured turbulence against an identical field-off airframe. Load cells and accelerometers in both. The difference between the two airframes is the field's measured contribution.

    GATE: FIELD AIRFRAME SHOWS LOWER LOADS ON EVERY CHANNEL

  3. Load-sharing trials

    Instrumented maneuver and gust-envelope flights, uncrewed, probing the load-sharing candidacy directly: how much force, if any, the field carries before the spar does. This stage exists to convert the structural open how into numbers — or to close it honestly.

    GATE: LOAD SHARE MEASURED, NOT ASSUMED

  4. Forced-landing sequence tests

    Uncrewed airframes put through the forced-landing sequence at rising energies: field first, structure second, terrain and procedure last. Includes deliberate field-disabled runs to prove the fallback at every energy.

    GATE: FALLBACK PROVEN — FIELD DEAD, PLANE STILL A PLANE

  5. Crewed, small — then scale up

    Only with every number above published and independently replicated (copyleft: anyone may replicate) does a person fly in one — in a small aircraft first. Airliner scale is the last step of the order, not the first claim of the page.

    GATE: INDEPENDENT REPLICATION BEFORE ANY CREWED FLIGHT
05 / HONEST LABELS

The Open Hows Shelf

Printed at full size, in the open. Each of these is an unknown — and an unknown is not a no. It is work waiting for a builder.

Open How · Unknown · The big one

Field capacity at airliner scale and energies

The numbers, printed, not waved at: a 737-class aircraft at cruise carries about 2.5 gigajoules of kinetic energy — roughly 0.6 tonnes of TNT equivalent — and even at approach speed, about 190 megajoules. A wide-body at cruise is several times that again. How much energy a field can absorb, at what rate, and how absorption scales from a suit's chip to an airliner is not yet engineered. This is the largest number this design owes, and it is owed in the open.

Open How · Design rule set

Field failure fallback — a plane must still be a plane

The rule is already decided even though the engineering isn't: with the field dead, the aircraft must glide, land, and protect its people exactly as a good aircraft does today. Wings, structure, systems redundancy, and crew procedure carry it — the field is an added layer, never a load-bearing promise. Any design in which a dead field means a falling plane is not this design, and the build order tests the fallback at every stage.

Open How · Unknown

Power budget at cruise

A field that absorbs gusts for six hours over an ocean has to be powered for six hours over an ocean. What the field draws at cruise, where that power comes from, and what it costs in fuel and range are uncomputed. An absorption layer that eats the aircraft's range to protect it has traded one problem for another — the budget has to close, on paper, before it closes in the sky.

Open How · Unknown

Load sharing — how much, and how

The structural candidacy: can a field carry part of a gust or maneuver load before the spar feels it? If yes, airframe limits rise and structures could one day be built lighter. If the measured answer is no, the field remains a turbulence-and-crash layer and this how closes honestly. Build Order stage 3 is where it gets decided — by instruments, not by this page.
