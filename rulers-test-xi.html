The Ruler's Test — Xi Jinping (re-run under the Nov 24, 2026 method)

Subject: Xi Jinping — General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party (since November 2012), Chairman of the Central Military Commission, President of the People's Republic of China (since March 2013). The seat graded is the combined party–state–military leadership, because in China's system executive power sits in those three hats worn by one man, and since 2018 no term limit stands in their way. One fact about the seat must be stated plainly at the top, because the method's mandate rule turns on it: no electoral mandate operates here. Xi has never faced voters on a platform. His elections by the National People's Congress are ratifications by a party-controlled body — the 2018 constitutional amendment passed 2,958 votes to 2, and his presidential terms have been confirmed effectively unanimously. The mandate rule therefore has no platform to attach to: governing on a program the voters chose cannot be credited or blamed, because no voters chose one. His program is self-set — announced in party congress reports, five-year plans, and his own public statements — and the Test grades the holding on the same five tests it applies everywhere else. Where his word is graded (Test 5), it is measured against those self-set commitments and against China's signed undertakings, which are the closest thing to a platform this seat has ever produced. He is graded on thirteen years in power, November 2012 to November 2026. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. This is a re-run of the original grading (One Law MIXED · Without Gripping FAILS · The Wobble MIXED · Counts Everyone FAILS · His Word FAILS) under the method addendum of 24 November 2026, using the original dossier as the evidence base, verified and extended through November 2026. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Every test closes with why the grade lands where it lands and a ledger of who his actions on that test helped and who they hurt. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption campaign broke the post-Mao norm that sitting and former Politburo Standing Committee members were untouchable: former PSC member and security chief Zhou Yongkang was investigated (2013–14) and sentenced to life imprisonment in 2015 — the highest-ranking official felled in the PRC's history (campaign record, CCDI announcements; Wikipedia campaign summary).
  • The campaign's scale is documented and system-wide: approximately 2.3 million government officials had been prosecuted as of 2023, spanning party, government, military and state enterprises (Wikipedia, "Anti-corruption campaign under Xi Jinping," citing official figures).
  • It has repeatedly hit Xi's own appointees: Foreign Minister Qin Gang was removed in 2023 and Defence Minister Li Shangfu expelled in 2024; the Rocket Force leadership was replaced in 2023 (Reuters/AP reporting, 2023–24).
  • The discipline reached his closest uniformed ally and finished the job in 2026. In January 2026 the Ministry of National Defence announced an investigation into Central Military Commission vice-chairman Zhang Youxia — a fellow "princeling" whose father fought alongside Xi's father, retained past retirement age at Xi's direction. The Central Committee expelled Zhang and Joint Staff chief Liu Zhenli from the party in September 2026, and the Fifth Plenum (26–29 October 2026) ratified the expulsions, confirming 33 senior officials expelled in the round — 16 of them military officers — including former Xinjiang party chief Ma Xingrui and former foreign-policy official Liu Jianchao, both Xi-era appointees (Reuters reporting, January 2026; AEI/ISW China & Taiwan Update, 30 October 2026; Jamestown Foundation China Brief, November 2026). Analysts (James Char, RSIS; Jonathan Czin, Brookings) noted the Zhang case rebuts claims the military purge had been selective (Reuters, January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The campaign's early "tigers" were concentrated in networks rival to Xi: Zhou Yongkang (security apparatus), Bo Xilai (Chongqing; jailed 2013), Ling Jihua (chief of staff to Hu Jintao), and Sun Zhengcai (a Hu-era successor candidate, jailed 2018) — a pattern analysts at the Observer Research Foundation and elsewhere describe as dismantling the Shanghai and Communist Youth League factions while Xi's own network rose (ORF Occasional Paper No. 300, February 2021; HKU Bulletin analysis by Zhu Jiangnan).
  • The process is party-run and opaque: suspects are held in the CCDI's extra-judicial shuanggui/liuzhi detention system, without independent judicial review — the "law" applied is discipline administered by the institution Xi heads. The specific offences alleged against even the most senior figures, Zhang Youxia included, have never been publicly itemised; his guilt or innocence on any specific act is an ALLEGATION until a court rules, and no open court process has been documented (campaign structure as documented in US Congressional-Executive Commission on China annual reports; AEI/ISW, October 2026).
  • The suspension of Ant Group's IPO on 3 November 2020, two days before listing, followed Jack Ma's public criticism of financial regulators; the Wall Street Journal reported, citing Chinese officials, that Xi personally made the decision to halt it (WSJ, 12 November 2020). Regulators' stated rationale — Ant's under-capitalised lending posed systemic risk — is also on the record (AEI analysis, 2020; CSRC statements).
  • Rule by campaign rather than by neutral institution: enforcement intensity rises and falls with political seasons and congress cycles rather than applying at a constant rate (analyst consensus, ORF 2021; Jamestown Foundation reporting).

Why the grade lands here: The mandate rule does not move this test — discipline enforcement is not a platform being carried out; it is the how, and the how is what Test 1 grades. On the how, the record is genuinely two-sided. In one direction, the campaign has now demonstrably reached the very top of his own camp — his closest military ally expelled, his own appointees felled by the dozen — which is more than any predecessor's discipline ever did, and the 2026 plenum strengthened that shelf from investigation to completed expulsion. In the other direction, the instrument itself is a second law: party detention without independent judicial review, administered by the institution he heads, with charges against the fallen never publicly itemised. A law that reaches everyone but runs through one man's institution, in secret, is not one law at the source. Both shelves stand at full strength; the grade is MIXED.

Helped: The public and honest officials, in principle and partly in fact — corruption rents at the top of the system were attacked at a scale no predecessor attempted, and officials who compete on merit face a system in which even the leader's own allies are not visibly immune (CCDI record; analyst assessments as cited). Hurt: The detained and their families — some 2.3 million prosecuted by 2023 and the senior figures held in liuzhi without judicial review, including Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli, whose specific alleged offences remain undisclosed and therefore ALLEGATION-level; and the early targets in rival networks (the Zhou, Bo, Ling and Sun files), whose prosecutions, whatever the underlying evidence, followed the map of factional rivalry closely enough that analysts across the spectrum record the pattern.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral bodies, the media, the prosecution, the military and the private sector keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralised in his hands?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • After a decade of tightening, Xi publicly re-embraced the private sector: at a 17 February 2025 symposium with entrepreneurs (including Jack Ma), he pledged equal treatment under law, equal access to production factors, and protection of entrepreneurs' lawful rights (Xinhua/SCIO readout, 17 February 2025).
  • That pledge was codified: the Private Sector Promotion Law — China's first foundational law on the private economy, guaranteeing equal legal status and fair market access — was passed 30 April 2025 and took effect 20 May 2025 (NPC Standing Committee; SCIO briefing, 8 May 2025).
  • China's stated foreign-policy doctrine under Xi remains formal non-interference in partners' internal affairs, and no allied or partner state's leadership has been installed or removed by Beijing in his tenure (official MFA statements; the observable record). The November 2026 APEC summit he hosted in Shenzhen ran on that doctrine's terms — 21 economies at the table, none seated or unseated by the host (AP reporting, 18–20 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On 11 March 2018 the National People's Congress amended the constitution (2,958 votes to 2) to abolish presidential term limits — the Deng Xiaoping-era safeguard against lifelong rule — enabling Xi's unprecedented third term in March 2023 (NPC record, 11 March 2018; NPR/AP reporting).
  • The October 2026 Fifth Plenum set the succession question aside for another cycle: it fixed the 21st Party Congress for the second half of 2027, the congress at which a fourth term as general secretary would be conferred, and Le Monde's summit coverage records the expectation plainly — "in a year, [he] will grant himself a fourth term" (Fifth Plenum communiqué, 29 October 2026; Le Monde, 18–19 November 2026).
  • On 30 June 2020 Beijing imposed the Hong Kong National Security Law directly through the NPC Standing Committee, bypassing Hong Kong's own legislature, despite the Basic Law's Article 23 providing for Hong Kong to enact such laws "on its own"; the US-China Economic and Security Review Commission found it placed Hong Kong's 7.5 million residents under direct CCP rule and ended the autonomy guaranteed through 2047 (NSL text and NPCSC record; USCC 2020 Annual Report, Chapter 5).
  • Party presence inside private firms was systematically expanded on his watch: the CCP Organisation Department reported party organisations in 73.1% of private companies by 2018, up from 58.4% in 2013, and 92.4% of China's top 500 private firms; the 2023 revised Company Law (Art. 18) requires companies to provide conditions for party organisations' activities (CCP Organisation Department figures via Lima Groupe summary; PRC Company Law, revised 29 December 2023).
  • "Xi Jinping Thought" was written into the state constitution in 2018 and the party charter in 2017 — a personalisation of doctrine not accorded to any leader since Mao (2018 constitutional amendment record).
  • The military's autonomy narrowed to a point the numbers state by themselves. Successive purges of two CMC vice-chairmen (He Weidong, expelled 2025; Zhang Youxia, expelled 2026), the Joint Staff chief, and both defence ministers of his own choosing left the seven-member Central Military Commission reduced to two members by the October 2026 plenum — Xi himself and discipline chief Zhang Shengmin, whom the plenum simultaneously promoted to the Politburo, the first such mid-cycle promotion in 32 years. The plenum named no replacements for the empty seats. Only 279 of the 376 Central Committee members chosen in 2022 attended — the lowest attendance of the post-Mao era (AEI/ISW China & Taiwan Update, 30 October 2026; Jamestown Foundation, November 2026; Reuters reporting, 2025–26).

Why the grade lands here: Test 2 grades whether the referees and the parts keep their own standing, and on that question the 2026 record deepened the original finding rather than complicating it. The body that would check a Chinese leader — the succession rule — was abolished in 2018 and its abolition was compounded in 2026: the next congress is scheduled as a fourth-term conferral, not a succession. The body that commands the military now consists of the chairman and his discipline inspector. Hong Kong's legislature was bypassed by direct legislation. Against that shelf, the for-shelf items are real but smaller in kind: a private-sector law that guarantees by statute what the grip can still override in practice (Test 1's Ant file is the standing example), and a non-interference doctrine that governs partners abroad, not institutions at home. A seat that has removed the referee who could end its tenure, and reduced its high command to two men, is gripping. The grade is FAILS.

Helped: Xi himself and the party centre, whose control over the military, the courts' political direction, and private firms' internal life is more complete than at any point since Mao; and, on the for-shelf, private entrepreneurs, who gained a statute guaranteeing equal legal status (Private Sector Promotion Law, 2025) — a real gain, recorded at full strength, whose practical weight the Ant precedent limits. Hurt: Hong Kong's 7.5 million residents, whose guaranteed autonomy ended decades early; the PLA's senior officer corps, five of seven CMC seats emptied by purge; Central Committee members — roughly a quarter of the body absent from its own plenum; and every institution whose independence is the thing this test measures — courts, media, and firms operating under an internal party presence they did not choose.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? Under the method addendum, this test also grades the other option: when another option became available, did he at least consider it openly? Considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clearest documented adjustment: after the "White Paper" protests of late November 2022 — the broadest public challenge of his tenure — the government dismantled zero-COVID within days. On 7 December 2022 the State Council ended mandatory quarantine and mass testing; on 26 December COVID was downgraded to a Class B disease. Analysts at the Asia Society describe the sudden change of course as a concession to the protesters' demands, alongside economic pressures (Asia Society Policy Institute, November 2023; NHC announcements, December 2022).
  • The early phase of the same policy is credited even by critics with results: the US Congressional-Executive Commission on China notes zero-COVID's implementation "initially saved lives" in 2020–21, when China's official death toll stayed far below those of comparable countries (CECC Annual Report, Public Health chapter, 2024).
  • Economic pushback produced a genuine course correction in 2024–25, and under the addendum it is also a clean other-option case: as private investment contracted, another option — re-embracing the private sector he had spent 2020–21 disciplining — became available, and he considered it openly and took it, with reasons stated in public: the February 2025 entrepreneurs' symposium, then the Private Sector Promotion Law (Xinhua, 17 February 2025; NPC record, April 2025).
  • The trade war of 2025–26 is the second clean other-option case. Facing US tariffs, negotiation was available alongside escalation; he took both in sequence and the negotiation in the open — four meetings with President Trump in 2026 alone, culminating at the Shenzhen APEC summit (18–19 November 2026), where he publicly pledged continued tariff cuts, expanded market access, and a predictable business environment for foreign investors, and convened the first formal Xi–Trump–Putin trilateral (AP reporting, 18–20 November 2026; Le Monde, 18–19 November 2026; Tatler Asia APEC review, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Before reversing on COVID, he doubled down for three years, and under the addendum the shape of that refusal is the debit: exit options were available and visible throughout 2022 — staged reopening sequences of the kind other states published, approval of foreign mRNA vaccines, an elderly-vaccination-first timetable — urged by economists and public-health voices inside and outside China. The record shows no open consideration of any of them while the policy stood; the option was not weighed and declined with reasons, it was not looked at, because the policy was personally branded and looking was treated as disloyalty (CECC Annual Report, 2024; Asia Society, November 2023).
  • The reversal, when it came, was unacknowledged and unprepared: no public admission the prior policy had failed, the shift was framed as "optimisation," and independent epidemiological estimates put excess deaths at roughly 1–1.5 million in December 2022–January 2023 alone, a toll the government has never officially acknowledged (CECC, 2024, citing epidemiological modelling).
  • The feedback channel itself was punished: participants in the White Paper protests were detained and questioned in the weeks after the policy changed, and the protests were never formally recognised by the state (ISDP Issue Brief, 19 January 2023; Asia Society, November 2023).
  • Because key policies are personally branded (zero-COVID, "common prosperity"), correction tends to arrive late and all at once rather than incrementally — the Ant IPO halt (2020) and the abrupt abandonment of the tutoring sector (2021) followed the same pattern of sudden, unexplained reversal without public accounting (Al Jazeera/Reuters review of the 2021 crackdowns, December 2021).
  • A quieter 2026 instance, recorded as analysts read it and contested as such: the Fifth Plenum's own coverage in the PLA Daily reportedly dropped longstanding doctrinal phrases cementing Xi's personal military authority and echoed collective-leadership language — read by Jamestown and other analysts as an institutional signal from inside the military. No adjustment followed; the purges the plenum ratified proceeded unchanged (Jamestown Foundation China Brief, November 2026; Chiang Rai Times summary of plenum coverage, November 2026). The reading of the signal is contested; the absence of any response is not.

The no-good-choice clause, applied to December 2022: By late November 2022 the situation had closed to options that all carried real harm. Continue zero-COVID indefinitely: mounting economic damage, local-government fiscal exhaustion, and a street already in protest. Exit at speed into a population whose elderly booster coverage and hospital surge capacity had never been built: the mortality wave that in fact followed. A staged exit over months: the door the first two options had already consumed — staging required the preparation that had not been done and time the protests were not offering. The dossier therefore grades the December 2022 exit decision itself as the best possible choice available, given the situation in the moment, and does not hold the exit against him. What remains gradable, and is graded on the shelves above, is the trap's origin and the honesty inside it: the trap was built by three years of refusing to look at the exit options while they were cheap, and the exit was announced as "optimisation" with its death toll never acknowledged. The clause shields the moment. It does not shield the years that manufactured the moment.

Why the grade lands here: The new parameters cut in both directions on this file, which is why the grade holds at MIXED with a fuller record rather than moving. The other-option rule adds genuine credit the original grading did not isolate: on the private sector and on the 2025–26 trade war, another option became available, he considered it openly, and adjusted in public with reasons. The same rule adds a sharper debit on the tenure's biggest file: on the COVID exit, the options were refused a look for years, and the no-good-choice clause — applied above in his favour for the decision of December 2022 — expressly leaves the trap-building graded, and the trap was his. Adjustment under this seat is real but structurally late: it arrives when the wobble becomes undeniable, all at once, and without acknowledgment. Both shelves stand; the grade is MIXED.

Helped: The locked-down public and the wider economy, by the December 2022 exit — graded above as the best choice available in that moment; private entrepreneurs and foreign investors, by the 2025 re-embrace and the 2026 trade de-escalation, both considered and taken openly. Hurt: The estimated 1–1.5 million excess deaths of the unprepared exit wave (epidemiological modelling cited by CECC, 2024 — an estimate, with the official toll never published); the White Paper protesters detained for supplying the feedback the system then acted on; Shanghai's residents in the two-month spring 2022 lockdown and Xinjiang's under prolonged lockdowns — the human cost of the years in which the exit options went unexamined.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does his accounting include the whole flock — Uyghurs, Tibetans, Hong Kongers, religious believers, the rural poor, Taiwan — or are some counted out of the family?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Poverty alleviation is the strongest documented item on this shelf: in February 2021 Xi declared "complete victory" over extreme poverty, with 98.99 million rural residents lifted above the national poverty line in the final eight-year campaign (Xinhua, 25 February 2021; State Council white paper, 2021).
  • The achievement is independently corroborated in scale: the World Bank's 2022 study with China's Development Research Center records close to 800 million people moved above the $1.90/day international line over four decades — about three-quarters of all global extreme-poverty reduction — and World Bank country director Martin Raiser affirmed that "China's eradication of absolute poverty in rural areas has been successful" (World Bank/DRC, "Four Decades of Poverty Reduction in China," 2022).
  • Beijing's stated position on Xinjiang is on the record and belongs on this shelf: the centres were vocational training facilities aimed at countering terrorism and extremism after a series of attacks, all trainees had "graduated" by 2019, and the region has had no major terrorist attack since 2016 (Chinese government white papers and MFA statements, 2019–2022).

Evidence against:

  • The UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights concluded on 31 August 2022 that the "extent of arbitrary and discriminatory detention of members of Uyghur and other predominantly Muslim groups… may constitute international crimes, in particular crimes against humanity," finding allegations of torture and sexual violence in detention "credible." This is a CONTESTED FINDING: China rejected the assessment as "a patchwork of false information" serving US strategy (MFA spokesperson Wang Wenbin, 1 September 2022), and member states remain divided on it at the Human Rights Council (UN OHCHR Assessment, 31 August 2022; UN News, 31 August 2022).
  • The US government determined on 19 January 2021 that China's policies in Xinjiang constitute genocide — a US GOVERNMENT DETERMINATION, not an international court ruling; several national parliaments passed similar non-binding motions; Beijing denies the charge categorically (US State Department, January 2021).
  • Tibetans are counted in on Beijing's terms only: the state asserts the right to approve reincarnations including the next Dalai Lama; the Panchen Lama recognised by the Dalai Lama in 1995, Gedhun Choekyi Nyima, has not been seen in public since childhood; UN experts reported in February 2023 that around a million Tibetan children were in a residential boarding-school system they described as assimilative (International Campaign for Tibet report, November 2026; CECC/Freedom House reporting; UN experts' statement, February 2023).
  • Hong Kongers' guaranteed distinct status was ended early in practice: the 2020 National Security Law and the 2021 electoral overhaul ("patriots administering Hong Kong") closed opposition politics and independent media (Apple Daily, closed June 2021) decades before the 2047 horizon (USCC 2020; NPC decisions, March 2021).
  • Taiwan's 23.5 million people are counted as already inside the family against their elected government's position, and the coercion defining the terms of inclusion escalated through autumn 2026. On 20–22 October 2026 the PLA Eastern Theatre Command ran a 72-hour exercise enclosing Taiwan on three sides — the largest since August 2022 — with 47 aircraft entering Taiwan's ADIZ on the peak day, 21 October (Taiwan Ministry of National Defence statistics, October 2026; CSS Eurasia exercise analysis, 22 October 2026). From 3 November 2026, more than 100 PLA Navy and China Coast Guard vessels were simultaneously at sea in an unannounced deployment that ISW/AEI assessed as rehearsing an extended isolation of Taiwan, with the Liaoning carrier group conducting area-denial operations east of the island; Taiwan's defence minister confirmed the drills on 10 November and its National Security Council reported large formations still in place on 12 November. Taipei's officials called the campaign "silent intimidation"; the Global Times answered that PLA activity around Taiwan is "legitimate arrangements within China's sovereignty" and is becoming "around-the-clock" — both characterisations recorded as the disputants' own (ISW/AEI China & Taiwan Update, 13 November 2026; Taiwan MND and NSC statements, November 2026; Global Times editorial, 11 November 2026).
  • Religious believers generally — Christians, Muslims, Buddhists, Falun Gong practitioners — practise legally only inside state-supervised bodies under the "sinicization" policy requiring religions to adapt to socialist society and Party leadership (Regulations on Religious Affairs, as revised under Xi; CECC annual reports).

The no-good-choice clause, considered and not applied to Xinjiang: Beijing's framing is counterterrorism, and the predicate is real — Xinjiang and China suffered genuine terrorist attacks in 2013–15, and any government in that seat faced a security problem that had to be answered. The clause, however, applies only where no good choice existed or appeared to. The record shows other options existed and were visible at the time: the actual attackers were prosecuted through criminal process, and targeted law enforcement against identified networks was the standing instrument before 2017 and remained available. Mass preventive detention of an ethnic population was one option chosen among several — at a scale the UN's human rights office later assessed as possibly constituting crimes against humanity, a finding Beijing disputes. A hard choice is not the same as no choice. The clause is therefore not applied here, and the file is graded on the shelves as they stand.

Why the grade lands here: The counting this seat does for the majority it counts in is historic and corroborated — the poverty file is the strongest single item in this entire dossier, and it is not close. But Test 4 asks whether everyone is counted, and the record answers no in four directions at once: a UN-assessed system of arbitrary detention aimed at one ethnic group; a Tibetan population included only on terms that reach into its religion's succession and its children's schooling; a Hong Kong whose guaranteed separateness was ended decades early; and a Taiwan whose 23.5 million are counted in against their stated position, under military pressure that escalated to the largest exercises since 2022 in the very weeks before this grading. The mandate rule does not soften this — counting out those who did not elect the ruler is precisely the failure the rule names, and here there was no election for anyone to be counted out of; the counting-out is done by category, not by ballot. The grade is FAILS.

Helped: The rural poor above all — 98.99 million people lifted over the national line in the final campaign and close to 800 million over four decades on the World Bank's count, the largest poverty reduction in recorded history; and residents of Xinjiang of all ethnicities, on Beijing's stated record, who have lived without a major terrorist attack since 2016. Hurt: Uyghurs and other predominantly Muslim groups detained in the system the UN assessed in 2022 (contested by Beijing, as recorded); Tibetan families — a missing Panchen Lama, roughly a million children in the boarding system UN experts described; Hong Kongers, whose opposition politics and independent press were closed; Taiwan's 23.5 million, living under escalated encirclement exercises in October–November 2026; and religious believers of every faith, whose practice is lawful only inside bodies the state supervises.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Promises made vs. promises kept, on the documented record. Because no electoral mandate operates in this seat (see Subject), his word is measured against his own announced program — congress reports, five-year plans, personal pledges — and against China's signed undertakings. Under the mandate rule, carrying out that announced program counts on this test's for-shelf; the rule gives no credit and no debit for the program's content, which the other tests grade.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The poverty commitment — kept, on schedule. The seat's headline domestic pledge of the era, elimination of extreme poverty by the national standard, was delivered and declared in February 2021, a decade ahead of the UN 2030 Agenda target Beijing had endorsed (Xinhua, February 2021; World Bank, 2022).
  • Climate delivery — the renewable word over-kept. China remained in the Paris Agreement and passed its target of 1,200 GW combined wind and solar capacity by 2030 in 2024, six years early, after a record 357 GW installed that year (National Energy Administration data; OilPrice/Newsbase reporting, February 2025). Climate Action Tracker's June 2026 assessment confirms China overachieved its 2030 NDC renewable and forest targets and that emissions were flat or declining from March 2024 through 2025.
  • A new climate word, personally given. On 24 September 2025 Xi personally announced China's 2035 NDC at the UN Climate Summit — the country's first absolute emissions limit: economy-wide net greenhouse-gas emissions 7–10% below peak by 2035, "striving to do better," non-fossil fuels above 30% of energy consumption, wind and solar to 3,600 GW, forest stock above 24 billion cubic metres, new-energy vehicles the mainstream of new sales, and the national carbon market extended to all major high-emission sectors; the formal NDC was published 3 November 2025 (Xinhua/SCIO, 25 September 2025; Carbon Brief Q&A, updated November 2025). The pledge is recorded here as a word given, not yet a word kept — its delivery date is 2035 — and its ambition is contested on the record: Carbon Brief's expert assessment is that the headline falls "far short" of a 1.5–2°C path and that the 3,600 GW figure implies a slowdown from recent installation rates, meaning the target may be set below the trajectory already running.
  • The anti-corruption program — carried out as announced. Whatever its selectivity and its instrument (Tests 1–2), the campaign promised at the 18th Congress era was in fact waged continuously for thirteen years, through the 33 expulsions ratified in October 2026 (CCDI record; AEI/ISW, October 2026).
  • New openness words, days old at grading. At Shenzhen on 18–19 November 2026 Xi pledged continued tariff cuts, expanded market access, and a predictable business environment for foreign investors (Tatler Asia APEC review; AP reporting, November 2026). Recorded as given; delivery ungradeable.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The Spratlys: on 25 September 2015, standing in the White House Rose Garden, Xi stated that "China does not intend to pursue militarization" of the Spratly Islands and that China's outposts would not "target or impact any country." By 2018 China had deployed anti-ship cruise missiles, long-range surface-to-air missiles and jamming equipment on the Spratly outposts, and built fighter hangars and combat-capable runways — documented by the US Department of Defense and commercial satellite imagery. Beijing's counter-position: the deployments are lawful defensive measures on Chinese territory (US State Department statement, 27 September 2020; ASD Schriver briefing, May 2019; MFA spokesperson Wang Wenbin, 28 September 2020).
  • Hong Kong: the 1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration — a treaty registered at the UN — committed China to leave Hong Kong's systems unchanged for 50 years. On 30 June 2017 Foreign Ministry spokesperson Lu Kang declared it "a historical document [that] no longer has any practical significance, and is not at all binding" on Beijing's administration of Hong Kong. The UK maintains the treaty remains in force; Beijing's position is that its policies were a unilateral statement fulfilled through the Basic Law (Reuters, 30 June 2017; UK Foreign Office response, 2017; MFA official translation).
  • Trade: under the January 2020 Phase One agreement, China committed to buy an additional $200 billion of US goods and services over 2017 levels by end-2021. The Peterson Institute for International Economics' final accounting: China purchased 58% of what it committed to buy — "none of the additional $200 billion" — falling short even of the 2017 baseline, though it reached 77–83% of its agricultural commitments and COVID-19 disrupted services trade (PIIE, Chad Bown, February 2022; USTR statement).
  • The climate shelf is itself mixed beneath the headline: China missed its 14th Five-Year Plan carbon-intensity target, achieving roughly a 12% reduction for 2020–25 against the pledged 18%, and the 15th Five-Year Plan lowered the next intensity goal — even as absolute renewable deployment beat every target (National Bureau of Statistics data, February 2025; Climate Action Tracker, June 2026). A target missed and then re-set lower is a word adjusted downward, and the shelf records it as such.

Why the grade lands here: The mandate rule, applied honestly, improves this test's for-shelf and does not save the grade. There is no voters' platform, but there is an announced program, and its domestic centrepieces were carried out: poverty eliminated on the seat's own schedule, the anti-corruption campaign waged without pause for thirteen years, the renewable build-out delivered six years early. Those are kept words, and they are recorded at full strength. Against them stand three specific, dated, documented undertakings to other parties — a personal assurance in the Rose Garden, a treaty registered at the United Nations, and a signed trade agreement — each broken in a way the other party can point to on paper, plus a domestic carbon-intensity target missed and quietly lowered. A ruler's word is graded where it was given to people who relied on it; on the international shelf, reliance was answered with repudiation in all three examined cases. The for-shelf is real; it does not outweigh the against-shelf. The grade is FAILS.

Helped: The rural poor (the poverty word, kept — the single largest kept promise in this dossier); the global climate ledger, by renewable deployment at a scale no other state has matched and emissions flat-to-falling since March 2024 (Climate Action Tracker, June 2026); and Chinese savers and borrowers, on regulators' stated rationale, by the Ant intervention recorded in Test 1. Hurt: Hong Kongers, who held a treaty and watched it declared non-binding; South China Sea claimant states and the US, who held a personal assurance on militarisation and watched missiles arrive on the outposts; US farmers and exporters, who received 58% of a signed purchase commitment (PIIE, 2022); and every counterparty now pricing Chinese undertakings — the diffuse but real cost a broken-word record charges to the next negotiation, visible in the contested reception of the 2035 NDC's ambition.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.
