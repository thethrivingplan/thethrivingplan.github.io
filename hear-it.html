Why Music Works — the Full Why

Why? → How? → Play. Every sound you just trained on obeys a few simple truths. Learn these and the games stop being guessing.

1. Pitch is speed

A note is air vibrating at a steady speed, counted in vibrations per second (Hz). Your reference A vibrates 440 times a second — or 432, if that’s your tuning. Double the speed and you don’t get a “different” note — you get the same note, higher. That 2:1 relationship is the octave, and it’s why an octave feels like home: every second vibration of the high note lands exactly on a vibration of the low one. Nothing clashes. Total agreement.

2. The fifth is the next-simplest agreement

Multiply a frequency by 3/2 — three vibrations up top for every two below — and you get the perfect fifth (C up to G). It’s the simplest ratio after the octave, so it sounds almost as stable: open, strong, hollow in a good way. That’s why fifths anchor everything from power chords to violin tuning to the Star Wars theme’s opening leap. Consonance is simple ratios: the simpler the fraction, the smoother the sound. Complex ratios (like the minor 2nd, roughly 16:15) rub against each other — that rub is tension, and music is the art of spending tension and paying it back with consonance.

Ratios shown are the pure (“just intonation”) versions. Modern pianos slightly compromise every ratio so all keys work equally — that compromise is called equal temperament, and it’s why a piano’s fifth is a hair narrower than a perfect 3:2. Your ear can learn to hear even that difference.

4. A chord is intervals stacked

A major chord is a major 3rd with a perfect 5th on top — two simple ratios agreeing at once, which is why it feels like sunlight. Lower just the middle note by one semitone and the 5:4 becomes a 6:5: minor. Same skeleton, one note moved, whole weather changes. Diminished squeezes the fifth itself down to a tritone — now nothing fully agrees, so the chord leans forward, begging to resolve. When you name a chord’s colour, you are really reporting which ratios you heard.

5. A scale is a chosen pattern, not a law of nature

The octave can be sliced many ways. Western music mostly slices it into 12 equal semitones, then chooses 7 of them in the pattern whole–whole–half–whole–whole–whole–half: that choice is the major scale. Other cultures and other centuries chose differently — five-note pentatonic patterns, modes that start the same pattern on a different step. A scale is a palette someone picked because its tensions and rests tell a good story. Melody Detective trains you inside one palette (C major) until its shape lives in your ear; then every other palette is a variation, not a mystery.

6. Rhythm is ratio in time

Everything above was ratios of pitch; rhythm is ratios of duration. A half note is 2:1 against a quarter note, exactly as an octave is 2:1 in pitch. Syncopation is tension in time — a hit where the pattern taught you to expect rest — and it resolves the same way a tense chord does: by landing home on the beat.
