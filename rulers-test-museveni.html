The Ruler's Test — Yoweri Museveni, President of Uganda

Subject: Yoweri Kaguta Museveni — President of Uganda since 29 January 1986, when his National Resistance Army took Kampala after a five-year guerrilla war. The seat graded is the presidency, because in Uganda the presidency is where executive power actually sits: he is head of state, head of government, and commander-in-chief; he appoints and dismisses the vice-president, the prime minister, the cabinet, the leadership of the Electoral Commission, the judges of the higher courts, and the command of the armed forces. He has now won seven elections (1996, 2001, 2006, 2011, 2016, 2021, 2026) and is graded on the full tenure, weighted to the last decade, as the brief requires. Three premise corrections to the expected picture, each material. First, the January 2026 election was held and he won it: on 15 January 2026, under a nationwide internet shutdown (13–18 January), the Electoral Commission declared Museveni re-elected with 71.65% (7,946,772 votes) against 24.72% (2,741,238) for Robert Kyagulanyi — "Bobi Wine" — of the National Unity Platform (NUP), on turnout of about 52.5%; he was sworn in for a seventh term on 12 May 2026 (Electoral Commission declaration, 17 January 2026; contemporaneous reporting, CNN, AP, January 2026). Kyagulanyi rejected the result as fraudulent; notably, he filed no court petition — the only petition, by minor candidate Robert Kasibante, was withdrawn, and the Supreme Court confirmed Museveni as duly elected on 26 February 2026. Second, the opposition's two best-known leaders are both absent from the field, in different ways: Kyagulanyi escaped a security raid on his home on election night, spent roughly two months in hiding inside Uganda, and resurfaced in the United States on 18 March 2026, remaining abroad as of grading (he appeared at a Washington, DC rally on 30 October 2026); Dr Kizza Besigye, the four-time challenger, has been in detention since 16 November 2024, when he was seized in Nairobi and rendered to Uganda, and is on trial for treason in the High Court, unconvicted, in deteriorating health (Monitor timeline, November 2026; Torch Media Africa, October–November 2026). Third, there is no settled succession: his eldest son, General Muhoozi Kainerugaba, has been Chief of Defence Forces since March 2024 and has publicly declared he will stand for the presidency after his father; an NRM executive meeting on 10 November 2026 convened on the succession ended without agreement on designating him, according to AFP's party sources — an account the NRM publicly rejected as "completely false" while confirming the meeting took place (AFP via BBC, 14 November 2026). Museveni, officially born in 1944, is 82; he is eligible to stand only because the two constitutional barriers written against a tenure like his were removed during it — the two-term limit in 2005 and the age-75 ceiling in 2017. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself graded as a failure: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out an elected platform is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Death tolls are kept in separate, sourced counts and never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machinery exists and sometimes bites his own side. In the Karamoja iron-sheets scandal — relief roofing sheets for Uganda's poorest region diverted to senior officials in 2023 — three ministers were charged before the Anti-Corruption Court: former Karamoja Affairs minister Mary Goretti Kitutu (six counts including corruption and conspiracy to defraud), her successor Agnes Nandutu, and junior finance minister Amos Lugoloobi; Museveni dismissed Kitutu and Nandutu from cabinet in his March 2024 reshuffle (Anti-Corruption Court charges, 2023; Afrobarometer dispatch AD942, 2025). In 2024 MPs, including parliamentary commissioners accused of awarding themselves UGX 1.7 billion in "service awards," were charged and remanded in separate fraud cases (Afrobarometer AD942, 2025).
  • Standing referees of legality operate and publish against the state: the Uganda Human Rights Commission — a constitutional body — opened tribunal sessions in February 2025 on torture and unlawful detention and publicly warned the UPDF and police that torture is forbidden under Article 221 of the constitution (Amnesty International, Uganda 2025 report). The Inspectorate of Government estimates corruption's cost and publishes the figure — at least UGX 9.1 trillion (~USD 2.3 billion) a year, roughly a quarter of the budget — even though it recovers only a small fraction (Inspectorate of Government, via Afrobarometer AD942).
  • Elections, whatever their other failures (Tests 2 and 4), are held on a regular constitutional cycle and opposition candidates do appear on the ballot and take parliamentary seats: the NUP won 57 seats in 2021 to become the largest opposition party in parliament, and eight presidential candidates stood in 2026 (Electoral Commission records; contemporaneous reporting).

Evidence against:

  • The eligibility law itself has been rewritten twice around one man's biography — the definition of a second law at the source. In 2001 Museveni campaigned on the manifesto promise that he was offering himself for a "last presidential term" under the constitution's two-term limit; in 2005, parliament under his party's majority removed the term limit (Constitution (Amendment) Act 2005, deleting the Article 105(2) cap). In a 2012 NTV interview he said he would not lead Uganda beyond the age of 75; in December 2017 parliament removed the age-75 ceiling in Article 102(b) — in a debate during which security personnel entered the chamber and removed opposition MPs — clearing his 2021 candidacy, and his own (Uganda parliamentary record, September–December 2017; NTV On the Spot, 2012; contemporaneous reporting). A law that is amended whenever it reaches the incumbent is not one law for all; it is one law, edited.
  • The iron-sheets file shows the asymmetry inside a single scandal. The beneficiaries named in the record include the Vice-President (Jessica Alupo), the Prime Minister (Robinah Nabbanja), and the Speaker of Parliament (Anita Among), among more than twenty ministers and 31 legislators. Of these, only the three ministers above were charged; the Director of Public Prosecutions closed 17 case files in January 2023 for "insufficiency of evidence," several recipients were permitted to return sheets or pay a replacement price below the procurement price, and the Speaker — retained in office with Museveni's support — was never prosecuted at home. It was foreign governments that acted against her: the UK (30 April 2024) and then the US imposed anti-corruption sanctions on Among, Kitutu, and Nandutu (UK Global Anti-Corruption Sanctions announcement, April 2024; US State Department designations, May 2024). Among attributes the sanctions to her role in passing the Anti-Homosexuality Act; the UK government states on the record they are for corruption, including the iron-sheets theft — the dispute is printed here as the record carries it. Either way, the domestic law did not reach her.
  • By November 2026 the operative law for the country's most sensitive detentions is, on the public record, one officer's permission. On 24 November 2026 the Chief of Defence Forces — the president's son — posted that Besigye, former Kampala Lord Mayor Erias Lukwago (Besigye's own lead defence lawyer, arrested in June 2026 and charged with misprision of treason), and activist Sam Mugumya "will only avoid the hangman's noose if they sign the surrender document we have prepared. Otherwise, they can die in jail!" and that "no one will release Besigye, Lukwago or the other criminals without my permission" (Muhoozi Kainerugaba, posts on X, 24 November 2026; People Daily and Pearl Times reporting, 24 November 2026). None of the three has been convicted of anything. A release decision that runs through the commander's signature rather than the court's bail ruling is a second law, stated in the open by the man exercising it.
  • The prosecutorial pattern across the decade runs one way: opposition figures are charged with treason, treachery, and unlawful assembly (Besigye, 2024– ; hundreds of NUP supporters charged after each election, Tests 3–4), environmental defenders opposing the state oil pipeline are detained incommunicado by the military (Stephen Kwikiriza, June 2024 — named here because his case is documented by the UN UPR submission record), while ruling-party figures facing documented corruption allegations at the level of the Speaker's office proceed through closed files and replacement payments. Uganda's Corruption Perceptions Index score stands at 26 of 100, 141st of 180 (Transparency International, CPI 2023, the most recent index in the assembled record), and two-thirds of Ugandans told Afrobarometer that corruption increased in the previous year (Afrobarometer, 2024 round).

Why the grade lands here: The FOR shelf is real — ministers have stood in the dock, and constitutional watchdogs publish against the state. But the test is whether the same law reaches everyone, and at the three points where the law met the seat itself — the incumbent's eligibility, the Speaker's file, the opposition leader's cell door — the law was amended, closed, or personally held. That is not one law unevenly enforced; it is two laws, and the boundary between them is proximity to the president.

Helped: NRM insiders and senior officials inside the protected circle — the Speaker, the Vice-President, and the Prime Minister kept office and liberty through a scandal that jailed their juniors; the president himself, whose eligibility the amended law twice restored. Hurt: the people of Karamoja, whose relief roofing went to officials' animal sheds and homes while their region remained Uganda's poorest; opposition figures and supporters, who face the criminal law at full weight; and every Ugandan taxpayer funding a budget of which the state's own inspectorate estimates a quarter is lost to corruption annually.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts have ruled against the seat's preferences, in public, more than once — and some of those rulings stood. On 31 January 2025 the Supreme Court delivered a landmark judgment declaring the trial of civilians in military courts unconstitutional and ordering all civilian files transferred to civilian courts; the government formally complied in Besigye's case, withdrawing the court-martial charges and re-charging him in the civilian High Court in February 2025 (Supreme Court judgment, 31 January 2025; Monitor timeline, 2025). In 2018 the Constitutional Court struck down parliament's extension of MPs' terms to seven years even while upholding the age-limit removal, and the Supreme Court confirmed that split outcome 4–3 in April 2019. In April 2024 the Constitutional Court nullified several provisions of the Anti-Homosexuality Act (including the duty-to-report clause) while upholding its core.
  • The referees are not all silent: the Electoral Commission runs elections on schedule; the Uganda Human Rights Commission warns the army in public (Test 1); independent media — the Monitor, the Independent, NTV Uganda — publish investigations and opposition voices daily, and the #UgandaParliamentExhibition citizen audit of parliament's finances ran openly on social media in 2024.
  • The Bank of Uganda operates a recognisably technocratic monetary policy, and when the governorship finally was filled it was filled by the career insider who had been acting — Michael Atingi-Ego, appointed governor in February 2025 — rather than by a political loyalist from outside the institution.

Evidence against:

  • When the Supreme Court's January 2025 ruling closed the military courts to civilians, the seat's answer was to legislate the referee's decision away. Parliament passed the UPDF (Amendment) Bill on 20 May 2025 and Museveni signed it into law on 16 June 2025, restoring military trials of civilians under broad "exceptional circumstances" — acquiring arms or equipment, impersonating an officer, collaborating with military personnel. The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights urged him to reject it; FIDH and the legal record note the Act sits against Article 92 of the constitution, which forbids parliament from passing laws that alter or render ineffective a court's decision (UPDF (Amendment) Act 2025; FIDH statement, 19 June 2025; Amnesty International, Uganda 2025). The law was then used as written: in November 2026 Sam Mugumya — disappeared in August 2025, held incommunicado for over a year with his whereabouts unknown to his family — was produced before the General Court Martial on treachery charges (Pearl Times, November 2026). A court whose judgment can be reversed by the litigant's legislature within five months is a referee whose whistle the seat holds.
  • The central bank's governorship was left empty for three years by the one man who could fill it. Governor Emmanuel Mutebile died in January 2022; Museveni made no permanent appointment until February 2025, leaving the bank under an acting governor through a full economic cycle (Bank of Uganda record; contemporaneous reporting). No interference in a specific decision needs to be shown: a referee seat kept vacant at the appointer's pleasure is itself the grip.
  • The electoral referee is appointed by the incumbent it referees: the Electoral Commission's chair and commissioners are presidential appointees, and the 2026 election it ran was conducted under a nationwide internet shutdown ordered by the executive (13–18 January 2026), with biometric voter-verification machines failing across the country on polling day and no full polling-station-level tally published afterwards (AU observer mission statements; Torch Media Africa, 2026). The African Union observer mission, led by former Nigerian President Goodluck Jonathan, recorded that it found no evidence of ballot stuffing; the opposition alleges stuffing, agent abductions, and blocked access — both positions are printed, because the absence of a published station-level tally is precisely what leaves the dispute unresolvable from the record.
  • The media referee is gripped by threat from the military itself. During the March 2025 Kawempe North by-election, soldiers beat and rounded up journalists covering the vote (CPJ and EU enabling-environment reporting, 2025). On returning to X in November 2026, the Chief of Defence Forces threatened media organisations and journalists who criticise Museveni with ten-year jail terms and foreign media personnel with court martial (Daily Advent/AFP reporting, November 2026). Uganda's army chief is the president's son, appointed by him in March 2024; the threat is therefore not a rogue voice but the seat's own household speaking.
  • Inside the military — the institution that decides successions in Uganda's history — the grip tightened visibly in the grading window: at least a dozen generals and senior officers arrested over fifteen months, including former deputy CDF Lt Gen Peter Elwelu (arrested 2 November 2026), in what a Ugandan political scientist at York University, Dr Gerald Bareebe, describes as arrests of officers "believed not to support" the succession project, with the allegations against some officers "widely viewed as politically motivated" — his assessment, printed as assessment (BBC News, 14 November 2026). On 9 November 2026 the CDF publicly threatened the sitting Vice-President, Jessica Alupo, with dismissal and arrest and demanded her office; Museveni neither removed him nor publicly countermanded him (BBC, November 2026; AFP reporting).

Why the grade lands here: Uganda's referees are not fictitious — the Supreme Court's 2025 ruling and the Constitutional Court's partial strikes are real, and they are the strongest FOR evidence in this dossier. But each institutional check that has bitten the seat has been answered by amendment (the military-courts override), by vacancy (the central bank), by appointment power (the Electoral Commission), or by threats from the president's own son in uniform, unrebuked by the president. Independence exists on sufferance, and sufferance is the grip wearing a glove.

Helped: the presidency and the NRM, which face no referee they cannot amend, appoint, starve of a head, or outshout; the CDF, whose personal authority over courts, media, and fellow officers has grown unchecked. Hurt: civilians facing military trial after the constitution's court said they must not (Sam Mugumya by name; the class by law); journalists, who now price a decade in prison into critical coverage; voters, whose referee counts behind an internet blackout; and serving officers outside the succession faction, for whom professional standing no longer protects against arrest.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? Pushback also arrives as a new fact: when another option becomes available, does he at least consider it openly? Considering it and declining with stated reasons is not held against him; refusing to look is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The rarest move in this file: a public apology, in the open, naming the failures. On 25 May 2025, at a national evangelical convention at Kololo Independence Grounds, Museveni and First Lady Janet Museveni read a joint prayer of repentance: "As the top leaders of the Movement, we humbly take responsibility for all the mistakes made by ourselves, our agents and representatives," acknowledging corruption, "marked laxity, negligence and lack of diligence," and asking forgiveness "especially from the people of Buganda and the whole country," with parallel NRM apologies to regions scarred by war and neglect, including Acholi (contemporaneous reporting, Daily Express, The Independent, May 2025). No policy reversal accompanied it, and critics — Sarah Bireete of the Centre for Constitutional Governance among them — answered that it "would make sense if he was retiring"; both the apology and the answer are on the record.
  • He has adjusted under partner pressure when the price of refusal rose, and said so through actions: after the World Bank suspended all new lending in August 2023 over the Anti-Homosexuality Act, his government spent two years implementing mitigation measures across Bank-funded projects until the Bank judged them "satisfactory" and resumed lending with three new projects in June 2025 (World Bank statement via Reuters, June 2025). On the Act itself, in April 2023 he returned the bill to parliament asking for revisions before signing the amended version in May — a marginal adjustment, openly made, before the doubling-down graded below.
  • Institutional pushback was, at first, answered with compliance: when the Supreme Court ruled military trials of civilians unconstitutional in January 2025, the state transferred Besigye's case to the civilian courts within weeks and the information minister stated publicly, "as a government, we are complying with the ruling of the Supreme Court" (Chris Baryomunsi, February 2025). The subsequent legislative override is graded in Test 2; the initial open compliance belongs on this shelf.
  • His government accepted the AU observer mission's presence and its published criticisms of the 2026 process (delays, machine failures) without expelling it, and restored internet access after the declaration — small doors, but opened rather than denied.

Evidence against:

  • Pushback aimed at the seat itself is answered with force, on a toll kept in separate counts because the counts are contested. November 2020: after Bobi Wine's arrest during the 2021 campaign, security forces killed 54 people in two days of protests (Uganda Police official figure, November 2020). January 2026: the government confirmed at least 7 deaths on election day (government statements, January 2026); the CDF stated on X that security forces had killed 30 NUP supporters and detained over 2,000, calling them "terrorists" and "hooligans" (Muhoozi Kainerugaba via Reuters, 23 January 2026); Amnesty International reported information indicating at least 16 killed between 15 and 18 January (Amnesty, 30 April 2026); the Agora Centre for Research documented 30+ killed across the electoral period and at least 374 arbitrary arrests (Agora, 2026); the opposition claims about 100 killed on election day (opposition statements via RFK Human Rights Center summary, 2026) — an ALLEGATION-level figure no independent body in the assembled record has verified. The counts are printed separately because they cannot honestly be merged; what no count disputes is that the answer to electoral pushback was live ammunition, mass detention, and a five-day internet blackout.
  • The street's other pushbacks met the same answer across the decade: the 2017 age-limit protests were dispersed with arrests and the chamber itself cleared by security personnel; the 2011 "walk-to-work" protests of his earlier tenure were met with force — the pattern's age is noted because Test 3 grades trajectory, and the trajectory does not bend.
  • When another option became available, the refusal to look is documented at the top of the system. The Supreme Court offered a lawful path in January 2025 — civilian trials — and within five months it was legislated away rather than considered (Test 2). Dialogue options pressed by the AU, IGAD, religious leaders, and the opposition after the disputed 2026 election were not taken up; the CDF publicly rejected national dialogue involving Besigye in November 2026 and replaced it with the surrender document (Test 1). The UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Volker Türk, stated on 30 July 2026 that dissent was being met with enforced disappearances, torture, and arbitrary detention — at least 50 opposition figures, five human-rights defenders, and five journalists subjected to violations since the election (OHCHR, 30 July 2026). Uganda had already declined to renew the OHCHR's in-country office mandate in 2023, closing the office of the referee who might have examined the file locally.
  • Foreign-partner pushback on the Anti-Homosexuality Act was answered by defiance as doctrine: AGOA eligibility terminated (1 January 2024), World Bank lending suspended, visa restrictions and sanctions imposed — and the Act retained in full, with Museveni framing the costs as the price of sovereignty. Under the mandate rule, defending an enacted law is not itself a failure; what Test 3 grades is the refusal pattern — the mitigation measures that eventually restored World Bank lending (FOR shelf) show the adjustment came only when the fiscal cost, not the argument, moved.

Why the grade lands here: This is the file's most genuinely two-sided test. The apology was real and public; the World Bank adjustment was real and costly to his stated position; the initial compliance with the Supreme Court was real. But every one of those adjustments sits at the margin of policy, while pushback touching the seat — voters in the street, a court's limit on his instruments, an opponent's freedom — is answered by force, blackout, or override, and the options that would have shared power were not considered and declined with reasons; they were refused a hearing. MIXED, and the mix is stated precisely: he adjusts on everything except the one thing the wobble is usually about.

Helped: by the adjustments — Ugandans dependent on World Bank-funded social protection, education, and refugee programs, whose funding resumed in 2025; defendants transferred, briefly, into civilian courts in early 2025. By the refusals — the NRM's hold on the state, and the succession faction around the CDF, whose room to act grew with each unexamined pushback. Hurt: protesters and bystanders in the November 2020 and January 2026 tolls above; the detained and disappeared counted by OHCHR in July 2026; journalists and defenders who now work under stated threat; and the wider public, whose petitions, votes, and court victories have repeatedly failed to move the one decision that matters to the seat.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition voter, the Karamojong, the LGBT Ugandan, the refugee, the northerner, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The widest counting in this entire series belongs to this file, and it is not close. Uganda hosts 2,024,416 refugees and asylum-seekers (UNHCR, 31 May 2026) — the largest refugee population in Africa — under the Refugees Act 2006, which Museveni's government enacted and maintains: refugees receive freedom of movement, the right to work and run businesses, plots of land for housing and cultivation in settlements rather than closed camps, and access to national schools and hospitals on the same footing as citizens (UNHCR UPR submission, 2026; UNHCR Uganda fact sheet, Q1 2026). These are people with no vote to give him, counted at full weight for two decades.
  • The early development record counted the poor in, measurably. The share of Ugandans below the national poverty line fell from 56% in 1992/93 to about 20–21% by the late 2010s (UBOS national household surveys; World Bank). Universal Primary Education — promised in the 1996 manifesto, delivered in January 1997 — abolished tuition and drove net primary enrolment from ~62% (1992) to over 90% within a decade, with the strongest gains among the poorest quintile and girls, whose enrolment in that quintile rose more than 300% (World Bank education assessments). The HIV/AIDS response of the 1990s — openly led from the presidency when other governments denied — cut adult prevalence from around 18% in the early 1990s to roughly 6% by the mid-2000s (Ministry of Health; UNAIDS historical series).
  • The security promise of 1986 was, for most of the country, delivered: after the LRA war ended in the north (2006–2008), the displaced returned from the camps, and Uganda has had no large-scale internal war since — a baseline of physical safety across regions and ethnicities that the generation before him did not provide.

Evidence against:

  • One class of Ugandans is counted out by statute, at the price of death. The Anti-Homosexuality Act, signed by Museveni on 26 May 2023, imposes the death penalty for "aggravated homosexuality," life imprisonment for homosexuality, and up to 20 years for "promotion"; the Constitutional Court upheld its core in April 2024, striking only peripheral provisions. Whatever the law's popularity — and its domestic support is real, and under the mandate rule its enactment is not itself the grade — Test 4 grades the accounting: a Ugandan's membership in the national count is, under this law, conditional on a private attribute, and the documented consequences include evictions, arrests, and flight (UN and human-rights reporting, 2023–2026). The external costs were also distributed onto everyone: AGOA terminated, World Bank lending suspended for two years, and an estimated USD 0.5–1.3 billion in economic losses in the Act's first years (Open for Business cost analysis, 2026 — an advocacy-research estimate, printed as such).
  • Opposition voters are counted, in the CDF's own words, as targets. "So far we have killed 30 NUP terrorists" (January 2026, Test 3) reclassifies the 2.74 million citizens who voted for Kyagulanyi — a quarter of the electorate — as outside the count's protection. The November 2020 toll (54, police figure) fell overwhelmingly on young urban supporters of the same movement. A 22-year-old supporter, Irene Nakibuuka, was reported by the CPJ's UPR submission and local media to have died in April 2026 after abduction and torture by military personnel in January 2026 for supporting the NUP — her account, recorded on video before her death, is printed as a documented report, not an adjudicated finding (CPJ UPR submission, 2026).
  • The poorest Ugandans were robbed by the seat's own household, in the record's plainest counting failure: the Karamoja iron sheets (Test 1) were relief goods for the region with the country's deepest poverty, diverted to ministers and legislators — and the region's poverty is the standing rebuke to the national average: poverty fell ~60% in the central and western regions over the long run while the north's fell by roughly 17–30% (World Bank regional analyses), and Karamoja's rates remain multiples of Kampala's.
  • The no-good-choice clause is applied here, once, by its mechanics — to the February 2026 refugee directive. In February 2026 the government suspended prima facie (automatic group) recognition for new asylum-seekers, moving to individual assessment (UNHCR fact sheet, Q1 2026). The options that actually existed: (a) maintain automatic recognition while UNHCR's Uganda appeal stood at 14% funded (USD 49.9M of USD 361M by March 2026), with rations already cut and field operations reduced 23% — counting everyone on paper while the food ran out; (b) tighten recognition to slow arrivals, counting some out at the border to keep the system standing for the two million already inside. Both carried real harm; no third door was on the table, and hindsight doors (donors paying in full) were not his to open. The tightening is therefore recorded as the best possible choice available in the moment and is not held against him. What remains gradable, and is graded, is the trap's longer construction: a two-decade model financed on donor promises he did not control — the model's generosity was his, and so was the decision to build it on money that could be switched off by other people's elections. The clause covers the 2026 choice; it does not retroactively grade the architecture.

Why the grade lands here: No dossier in this series holds a wider contradiction. The same state counts two million foreign refugees at full weight — people who can never vote for it — and counts its own LGBT citizens out by capital statute and its own opposition voters out by the army chief's arithmetic. The early record (poverty, schooling, HIV, peace) shows the seat can count everyone when it chooses. Test 4 grades the current accounting, and the current accounting has named exceptions written into law and read out on X by the commander of the army.

Helped: two million refugees and asylum-seekers, who hold work rights, land, and services no other large host state matches; the generation of poor and rural children — girls above all — carried into school by UPE; Ugandans who lived through the HIV turnaround and the post-2006 peace. Hurt: LGBT Ugandans, counted out by law under penalty of death; opposition supporters, counted as enemies in the tolls of 2020 and 2026; the Karamojong, whose relief was stolen by the officials sent to serve them and whose region the national miracle largely passed by; and new asylum-seekers at the border since February 2026, whose narrowed door is recorded above under the clause, with its constraint named rather than scored.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 1986, weighted to the last decade, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The founding promises of 1986–1997 — substantially kept. He took power promising "fundamental change, not a mere change of guard," security, and an end to sectarian misrule; the security delivery is graded in Test 4. His 1996 manifesto promised free Universal Primary Education, and UPE was implemented in January 1997, on schedule, at national scale (Test 4). The 2006 Refugees Act codified the open-door commitment and has been kept for twenty years, including through the 2026 funding collapse (Test 4). The 2021 manifesto, "Securing Your Future," and the 2026 campaign's "Protecting the Gains" platform were carried out as governing programs in the ordinary sense — and under the mandate rule, governing on the platform he was elected on is credited here, not penalised: the Anti-Homosexuality Act's retention, however graded in Test 4, was a promise made to his electorate and kept to it.
  • The World Bank word — kept the expensive way. Having framed the AHA as non-negotiable sovereignty, his government nonetheless did the unglamorous work of the 2023–2025 mitigation measures it had agreed with the Bank, until the Bank certified them satisfactory and resumed lending (June 2025). The commitment made to a partner was performed even where performing it sat awkwardly beside the rhetoric (crossing 3, below).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The retirement word — broken three times, each time by amendment. 1986, in the founding text of his own movement (Sowing the Mustard Seed): "The problem of Africa in general and Uganda in particular is not the people but leaders who want to overstay in power." 2001: his manifesto offered his candidacy as a "last presidential term" under the two-term constitution; the term limit was removed in 2005 and he stood in 2006. 2012: on NTV, he said he would not lead beyond 75; the age limit was removed in 2017 and he stood in 2021, at 76, and again in 2026, at 81. Each promise was specific, dated, and public; each was followed not by retirement but by a constitutional amendment deleting the obstacle the promise had pointed to (parliamentary records 2005, 2017; NTV, 2012; JamiiForums/Independent retrospective on his retirement declarations, 2026). This is the longest and clearest broken-word file in the series.
  • The anti-corruption word — given every cycle, performed selectively. Every manifesto since 1996 has pledged zero tolerance of corruption; the record of the last decade is a CPI score of 26/100, an inspectorate estimate of a quarter of the budget lost annually, and the iron-sheets asymmetry of Test 1 — prosecutions for the junior ministers, closed files and replacements for the Speaker, the Vice-President, and the Prime Minister (sources as Test 1). The word is not merely unkept; it is kept in a pattern that tracks the accused's distance from the seat.
  • The word to the courts — given in February 2025, withdrawn by June. The government stated it was complying with the Supreme Court's military-courts ruling; within five months the president signed the Act restoring the trials (Tests 2–3). Besigye's own case was moved to a civilian court and remains there — the compliance was real for the man whose name was on the file and reversed for the class.
  • The succession word — never given, and the absence is itself the record. Across forty years he has made no dated, public commitment to a transfer of power and named no successor; the November 2026 party meeting on the question ended, on AFP's sourcing, without agreement, and his son's parallel campaign proceeds on social media threats rather than any announced process (AFP/BBC, November 2026). A ruler's word over time includes the promise a whole country waits to hear; forty years of silence on it, from the man who diagnosed overstay in 1986, is graded as part of the file.

Why the grade lands here: The kept shelf is not small — UPE, the refugee settlement, the early security settlement, and the Bank mitigation were real promises really performed, and some of them changed millions of lives for the better. But Test 5 grades the word over time, and the words he gave about the seat itself — last term, not beyond 75, the founding diagnosis of overstay — were each broken by the deliberate instrument of constitutional amendment. A word that holds for programs and fails for power is a word with the same boundary as Test 1's law: it binds until it reaches him.

Helped: by the kept words — schoolchildren and their parents from 1997 onward; refugees; Bank-program beneficiaries in 2025; his electorate, whose chosen platform (including the AHA) was in fact delivered, which the mandate rule credits. Hurt: by the broken words — every Ugandan who organised a life, a party, or a candidacy around the 2001 "last term" or the 2017 age ceiling, including his own former allies (Besigye's challenge was built, in 2001, on the expectation the promise created); the defendants returned to military courts after June 2025; and the public, whose constitution has been amended so many times around one tenure that its promises now trade at a discount the CPI and Afrobarometer numbers merely echo.

Test 1 — FAILS. Why: the eligibility law was amended twice to fit one biography (2005, 2017); the Karamoja iron-sheets prosecutions stopped at the junior ministers while the Speaker, Vice-President, and Prime Minister were closed-filed or allowed to pay replacements; and by November 2026 the CDF publicly claims personal authority over whether unconvicted detainees are released. Helped: the protected circle around the seat. Hurt: Karamoja's poor, the prosecuted opposition, and the taxpayer funding a budget a quarter lost to corruption on the state's own estimate.

Test 2 — FAILS. Why: referees that bit were amended (military courts restored by statute five months after the Supreme Court barred them), starved (the central-bank governorship vacant 2022–2025), appointed by the incumbent (the Electoral Commission, counting behind an internet blackout), or threatened by the president's son in uniform (ten-year jail threats to critical media, November 2026), while a dozen generals were arrested amid the succession. Helped: the presidency and the succession faction. Hurt: civilians returned to military trial, journalists, voters, and officers outside the faction.

Test 3 — MIXED. Why: the adjustments are real — the May 2025 public apology accepting responsibility for his government's mistakes, the two-year World Bank mitigation performed until lending resumed, initial open compliance with the Supreme Court — but they all sit at policy's margin, while pushback touching the seat (the 2020 and 2026 streets, the court's limit, dialogue after a disputed election) is answered with force, blackout, override, or a refusal to look. Helped: Bank-program beneficiaries; briefly, military-court defendants. Hurt: the dead and detained of the two election tolls, kept separate in Test 3 because their counts are contested, and every petitioner whose victory failed to move the seat.

Test 4 — FAILS. Why: the accounting has statutory and military exceptions — LGBT Ugandans counted out by a capital law; a quarter of the electorate reclassified by the army chief as "terrorists" in his own kill count — even as the same state runs the world's most generous large-scale refugee regime (2,024,416 hosted, UNHCR, May 2026) and carries the early record of poverty halved, UPE, and the HIV turnaround. The February 2026 refugee-recognition tightening is recorded under the no-good-choice clause and not scored against him; the clause does not reach the exclusions, which were choices, not constraints. Helped: two million refugees; the UPE generation; the post-2006 peace's beneficiaries. Hurt: LGBT Ugandans; opposition voters; Karamoja.

Test 5 — FAILS. Why: the program words were largely kept (UPE 1997, the Refugees Act, the Bank commitments) and the power words were all broken — "last term" (2001, answered by the 2005 amendment), not beyond 75 (2012, answered by the 2017 amendment), and the 1986 diagnosis of overstay, answered by a seventh term sworn in on 12 May 2026. Helped: the beneficiaries of the kept program words, and an electorate whose platform was delivered. Hurt: everyone who planned around the constitution as written.

The four crossings, named as the method requires: 1. Kept his word AND it helped: Universal Primary Education (promised 1996, delivered January 1997) and the Refugees Act open door (kept twenty years, through the 2026 funding collapse) — the strongest kept-word-and-helped file in this dossier. 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt: the Anti-Homosexuality Act — pledged to his electorate, signed, defended, and retained at documented cost to LGBT Ugandans and to the wider economy (AGOA terminated, Bank lending suspended, an estimated USD 0.5–1.3 billion lost). The keeping earns its Test 5 credit under the mandate rule; the hurt stands in Test 4's ledger in full. Neither launders the other. 3. Went against his word AND it helped: the World Bank mitigation measures of 2023–2025 — performed after he had framed the Act's defence as non-negotiable sovereignty and foreign dictation as unacceptable; quietly qualifying that stance in Bank-funded projects restored lending for social protection, education, and refugee programs in June 2025. The bend is recorded; so is the help. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt: the retirement promises of 2001 and 2012, broken by amendment in 2005 and 2017 — each break extending a tenure whose subsequent elections (2021, 2026) were the bloodiest of his rule, on the tolls printed separately in Test 3.

Yoweri Museveni is the series' hardest file to grade honestly, because both shelves are load-bearing. The FOR shelves hold things no honest record can wave away: a country made governable after 1986, poverty cut by more than half, a generation schooled, an epidemic faced early and openly, and two million refugees counted at full weight — the single widest act of counting in the series, wider than any democracy in it has managed. The AGAINST shelves hold the mechanism by which all of it was kept: a constitution amended around one man's biography until its promises discounted, referees held by appointment, vacancy, override, and threat, an opposition whose leaders sit in a cell and in exile respectively, and a succession being settled by arrests and social-media threats inside his own household. The Sun in this series holds all five tests by giving without gripping; Museveni's forty years are the photographic negative — a record of genuine giving, held in a grip that has never once opened. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether Besigye's trial concludes and its verdict stands on evidence; whether the 2031 succession proceeds by any announced, lawful process; whether the Speaker's file is ever opened at home; and whether the word given in 2001 and 2012 is ever, at last, performed by the simple act of leaving.
