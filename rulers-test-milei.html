The Ruler's Test — Javier Milei, President of Argentina

Subject: Javier Milei — President of Argentina since 10 December 2023; economist and former congressman; leader of La Libertad Avanza (LLA), a party founded in 2021 that held only a small minority in both chambers of Congress when he took office. He won the November 2023 run-off with 55.7% of the vote on a chainsaw-austerity, anti-"caste" platform. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The fiscal rule at the center of his government has been applied without a documented exceptions list. The chainsaw fell on his own political class first and hardest in symbolic terms: ministries cut from 18 to 9 on his first day, tens of thousands of public-sector jobs eliminated (over 40,000 civil-service positions by academic counts), official government advertising — the traditional subsidy to friendly media — abolished outright, and discretionary transfers to provinces slashed across the board, including to provinces governed by his own allies. When the allied PRO-bloc governor of Chubut, Ignacio Torres, fought the withholding of 13.5 billion pesos in federal revenue in February 2024, the government did not exempt him; a federal judge ruled for the province, and the dispute proceeded in court (Reuters/AFP, February 2024).
  • Enforcement reached his own household's circle, at least at its edges, and was not stopped. When audio recordings attributed to Diego Spagnuolo — head of the National Disability Agency (ANDIS) and Milei's own former lawyer — describing kickbacks surfaced in August 2025, Spagnuolo was dismissed within hours, the agency was intervened, federal judge Sebastián Casanello's investigation proceeded with raids on Spagnuolo's offices and on the pharmaceutical distributor Suizo Argentina, and phones were seized (AP, 7 September 2025; court reporting, August–September 2025). The kickback claims themselves are ALLEGATIONs (see below).
  • His party's lead Buenos Aires candidate, José Luis Espert, left the ticket on 5 October 2025 after documents from a U.S. court case showed a $200,000 transfer from Federico "Fred" Machado, a businessman indicted in the United States for drug trafficking and money laundering. Espert acknowledged the payment, describing it as private consulting income, and denies wrongdoing; U.S. investigators have made no allegation against Espert himself. Milei accepted the withdrawal before the midterms (Argentina Reports, 5 October 2025). In October 2025 a federal judge ordered Espert processed on a money-laundering charge arising from the payment — an indictment-stage ruling, not a conviction (court reporting, October 2025).
  • The courts have applied the law against his government's own acts throughout his term (Test 2), and no prosecution of an opposition figure on a case originating with his government is documented. The Supreme Court's June 2025 decision upholding former president Cristina Fernández de Kirchner's corruption conviction closed a case begun years before Milei took office; her camp calls the case lawfare, the courts call it a completed trial — CONTESTED as to character, but the process was not his government's creation (Supreme Court ruling, 10 June 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The first rule he bent was bent for his own family. On 10 December 2023, his inauguration day, Milei modified the existing decree — issued under President Macri — that barred the appointment of a president's relatives to cabinet-level posts, and appointed his sister Karina Milei as Secretary General of the Presidency, a minister-rank office. She became his closest adviser and political gatekeeper. Whatever her abilities, the documented sequence is a general anti-nepotism rule narrowed at the source so that one insider could be seated (Clarín, 11 December 2023).
  • In the $LIBRA affair (14 February 2025), Milei used the presidency's megaphone in a way no outsider could. Hours after the token's creation, he posted its contract address on X, presenting it as a vehicle to fund Argentine small businesses; the token's market value briefly reached roughly $4.6 billion before collapsing over 90%, with insiders cashing out some $107–110 million and, by the congressional commission's count, 114,410 wallets losing money. Milei deleted the post and said he had "spread the word" about a project he did not know in detail, not promoted it; he denies wrongdoing. In June 2025 the Anti-Corruption Office — an executive-branch body whose head serves under the presidency — ruled he had violated no ethics rules because the post was a personal act. In May 2025, days after a judge ordered the central bank to lift bank secrecy on Milei's and his sister's accounts, Milei dissolved by decree (Decree 332/2025) the special investigative unit (UTI) his own office had created to examine the case, after it had delivered its findings to prosecutors. A Chamber of Deputies investigative commission reported in November 2025 that Milei's "essential collaboration" made the scheme's volume possible and recommended Congress evaluate misconduct in office; LLA legislators rejected the report, and the opposition did not carry it further (The Block, 20 November 2025; CoinDesk, April 2025; contemporaneous decree reporting, May 2025).
  • The judicial end of $LIBRA is itself contested. On 23 October 2026 a federal judge dismissed the complaint against Milei and ten others at the request of prosecutor Eduardo Taiano, who found no grounds to investigate a crime; the Administrative Investigations Office (PIA), a specialized anti-corruption prosecution office, appealed, arguing the file contained indications of a fraudulent scheme and that basic measures — blockchain analysis, forensic phone examinations — had never been performed. The dismissal is a court ruling; whether it closes the matter is CONTESTED between the prosecution offices themselves (Argentina Reports, 2 November 2026).
  • The ANDIS recordings cut the other way on symmetry. The voice attributed to Spagnuolo alleges that Karina Milei received kickbacks of 3–4% on disability-agency medicine contracts, with payments of $500,000–$800,000 routed via the distributor. These are ALLEGATIONs: the recordings' provenance is undisclosed, the fact-checker Chequeado's commissioned forensic analysis supported the voice's authenticity, Spagnuolo was fired before he could be heard in his own defense in public, and both Milei and his sister deny the claims — the president called them "a lie." What is documented is the asymmetry of treatment: the accuser was dismissed within hours; the accused — his sister — remained in office with his full public backing, and in September 2025 a federal judge, at the government's urging in a related espionage complaint, issued a precautionary order barring media from publishing further recordings of Karina Milei, a measure press-freedom organizations denounced as prior censorship. The criminal case remains open under judicial seal procedures, unresolved as of this grading (AP, September 2025; court reporting, September 2025).
  • In the Espert case, the order of operations is also on the record: Milei publicly backed Espert for days after the Machado transfer was documented — saying he would not ask for the resignation because that would imply guilt — and accepted the withdrawal only when Espert himself resigned (Argentina Reports, October 2025).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, Congress, the central bank, the media, and the provinces keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Congress has beaten him repeatedly, in the open, and the defeats stand. His flagship omnibus bill failed in the Chamber of Deputies in February 2024 and was withdrawn; the version that passed in June 2024 (Law 27,742, the "Ley Bases") had been negotiated down from 664 articles to 232, with the privatization list cut and Aerolíneas Argentinas, the postal service, and the state broadcaster removed by the Senate (Law 27,742 record; AFP, June 2024). In September–October 2025 Congress overrode three of his vetoes — the Disability Emergency Law (Senate, 63–7, 4 September 2025), the University Financing Law, and the pediatric (Garrahan) emergency law (Senate margins of 59–7 and 58–7, 2 October 2025) — the first successful veto overrides in Argentina in 22 years (IDEA/International IDEA country reporting, September–October 2025). His first budget was not passed until 26 December 2025, for 2026 — for two years he governed on an extended 2023 budget rather than impose one (Reuters, 26 December 2025).
  • The courts have ruled against his instruments at every level and he has complied with final outcomes. A federal labor court suspended the labor chapter of his mega-decree DNU 70/2023 in January 2024, and the National Labor Appeals Chamber later declared it unconstitutional (CGT/CTA amparo litigation, 2024). A federal judge ordered Chubut's funds released in February 2024. On 19 November 2026 the Supreme Court declared two articles of DNU 70/2023 unconstitutional — those that had freed private health insurers from state premium oversight — finding the government had never demonstrated the exceptional circumstances the Constitution requires for decree legislation (Supreme Court ruling, 19 November 2026). No judge is documented to have been removed, transferred, or sanctioned for ruling against him.
  • The Senate exercised its constitutional check on the judiciary itself: on 3 April 2025 it rejected both of his Supreme Court nominees outright (García-Mansilla 51–20 against; Lijo 43–27 against), and the rejection was honored — García-Mansilla resigned from the seat he had briefly occupied, and Lijo never took it (Reuters, 3 April 2025; IDEA, April 2025).
  • Elections remain free and were lost as well as won: his party lost the Buenos Aires provincial election of 7 September 2025 by 13 points (47% to 34%) and the result stood without challenge; the national midterms of 26 October 2025 were administered normally, with turnout just under 68% — the lowest since the return to democracy, a fact about the electorate's mood, not about the count's integrity (official results, September–October 2025).
  • The central bank, which he promised to abolish (Test 5), in fact operates under a published rules framework — a crawling peg replaced in April 2025 by an exchange-rate band agreed with the IMF — and in August 2026 his government sent Congress a bill to strengthen the bank's independence, barring it from financing the treasury and requiring two-thirds of both chambers to remove a governor (Bloomberg reporting, August 2026).

Evidence against:

  • He opened his presidency by legislating without Congress. Ten days after inauguration, DNU 70/2023 (20 December 2023) rewrote or repealed provisions of some 80 laws in a single decree of "necessity and urgency" — rent law, labor law, price controls, health-insurance oversight, customs, sports clubs, and more — while Congress was available to sit. The Supreme Court's November 2026 ruling on the health articles found the decree's recitals offered "not even a minimal justification" connecting the invoked emergency to the measures, and warned that the Constitution does not permit the executive to choose decree over legislation at its discretion. The labor chapter has been suspended by the courts since January 2024; much of the rest remains in force by inertia, Congress never having voted the decree down as a whole (Supreme Court ruling, 19 November 2026; CRS overview, 2024).
  • In February 2025, after ten months in which the Senate had neither confirmed nor rejected his two Supreme Court nominees, Milei appointed both justices by decree during the congressional recess — federal judge Ariel Lijo and scholar Manuel García-Mansilla — under a recess-appointment clause. Human Rights Watch called it "one of the most serious attacks on the independence of the Supreme Court in Argentina since the country's return to democracy." The Senate's April rejection undid it, but the attempt — seating a justice on the court that would judge his decrees, by signature, because the votes were not there — is the documented part (Human Rights Watch, 26 February 2025; Senate votes, 3 April 2025). Lijo's own record had drawn formal objections from rights groups, business associations, and scholars on ethics grounds (HRW, February 2025).
  • On the University Financing Law he went further than a veto. After Congress overrode his veto with two-thirds of both chambers in September–October 2025, making Law 27,795 law, his government issued Decree 759/2025 suspending its implementation until Congress specified funding sources — in effect a second, extra-constitutional veto of an enacted law. A federal judge (Judge Martín Cormick) declared the suspension unconstitutional and ordered immediate application; the government appealed, obtained elevation to the Supreme Court with implementation suspended meanwhile, and as of late 2026 the law remains substantially unimplemented while university budgets sit at two-decade lows (federal court ruling reported 2026; Times Higher Education, 2026; Argentina Reports, May 2026). The Congressional Budget Office had costed the law at 0.23% of GDP for 2025.
  • The provinces' autonomy was squeezed fiscally as well as politically: discretionary transfers were cut to near zero and automatic revenue-sharing was used as a debt-collection lever (the Chubut case, Test 1), with the president publicly branding resisting governors "fiscal degenerates" and Congress a "nest of rats" (presidential statements, February 2024). None of Argentina's 24 governors belongs to his party, and all absorbed cuts; the July 2024 "Pacto de Mayo" with 18 governors partially regularized relations, on his ten points.
  • The press keeps its presses but not its peace. FOPEA, the Argentine journalism forum, recorded 278 attacks on freedom of expression in 2025 — the highest since its monitoring began in 2008, up 55% on 2024 — and attributed 119 of them, roughly four in ten, to Milei personally: repeated public insults ("we don't hate journalists enough"; claims that 95% of journalists are criminals), named attacks on individual reporters, and, in October 2026, a speech branding five journalists covering the $LIBRA case corrupt and saying they should be burned at the stake, which drew condemnation from the Committee to Protect Journalists, the Overseas Press Club, and an announced Amnesty International complaint to international bodies (FOPEA monitoring report, 2025; OPC statement, 28 October 2026). Institutional measures accompanied the rhetoric: Decree 780/2024 narrowed the Access to Public Information Law and extended classification periods; from March 2025 Casa Rosada press conferences operated accreditation filters and a "mute button" for critical outlets; in April 2026 the accredited press corps was expelled from the Casa Rosada after a network aired footage filmed inside with smart glasses, which the government called illegal espionage (PEN International, 1 July 2025; AP reporting, April 2026). Argentina fell from 40th (2023) to 98th (2026) in the RSF World Press Freedom Index. No outlet has been closed by the state, and it was independent Argentine media that broke both the $LIBRA and ANDIS stories — the grip here is pressure and stigma, not seizure, and the distinction is documented in both directions.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, markets, Congress, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His first great legislative defeat produced his clearest adjustment. When the original 664-article omnibus bill collapsed in the Chamber of Deputies in February 2024, Milei withdrew it rather than govern against the vote, spent four months negotiating with governors and opposition blocs, and accepted a law cut by two-thirds — dropping the fiscal chapter, accepting Senate removals from the privatization list, and signing the trimmed Ley Bases on 28 June 2024 (AFP/Reuters, February–June 2024). His negotiators' own summary is on the record: the government moved away from "all or nothing."
  • The Senate's rejection of his Supreme Court nominees was accepted the same week: García-Mansilla resigned on 7 April 2025, Lijo stood down, and Milei did not attempt a second decree appointment or retaliate against the chamber (Reuters, April 2025).
  • The September 2025 provincial defeat was answered in words and in personnel. On election night Milei said, "Today we suffered a clear defeat… we had an electoral setback and we have to accept it," promised a "deep self-critique," and his chief of staff told radio it was time "for self-criticism, analysis, and seeing where we went wrong… why the macroeconomic results are not reaching the people" (Bloomberg/Reuters, 7–8 September 2025). After the October midterm victory he nonetheless reshuffled the cabinet, replacing the chief of staff and foreign minister, and his government negotiated the 2026 budget through Congress to passage on 26 December 2025 — the first budget law of his presidency (Reuters, December 2025).
  • On economic policy he adjusted the instrument when the market wobbled: the April 2025 IMF program ($20 billion Extended Fund Facility, approved 11 April 2025) was paired with a partial lifting of currency controls and a move from the crawling peg to a managed band — a pragmatic loosening his campaign rhetoric would not have predicted — and when the peso came under severe pressure in September–October 2025, his government accepted a $20 billion U.S. Treasury currency swap (announced 9 October 2025), of which $2.5 billion was drawn; U.S. Treasury Secretary Bessent stated on 9 January 2026 that Argentina had repaid the drawn amount in full (CRS Report R48780, December 2025; U.S. Treasury statements, 2025–2026). The swap's terms were not publicly disclosed, a transparency gap noted by the Congressional Research Service and U.S. lawmakers — recorded here because the wobble was answered partly with an instrument the public cannot inspect.

Evidence against:

  • On the university law, the wobble was answered by suspension, not adjustment: two-thirds of both chambers overrode his veto, a federal judge ordered compliance, and the government's response was an appeal that kept the law frozen (Test 2). On pensions, he vetoed increases in September 2024 and again in August 2025, and answered two years of weekly Wednesday retiree protests outside Congress — at times dispersed with tear gas and water cannon — with the slogan "there is no money" (Reuters, October 2025).
  • The $LIBRA response was denial in form: the post was deleted, the promotion re-described as merely "spreading the word," the investigative unit dissolved (Test 1), and the congressional commission's work dismissed by his bloc. He has never acknowledged any error in the episode.
  • His rhetorical answer to institutional pushback is documented and repeated: Congress as a "nest of rats" whose members he accused of seeking bribes (February 2024, remarks he later said were general, not specific), governors as "fiscal degenerates," journalists as criminals who are not hated enough. After the September 2025 defeat he paired the self-critique with a refusal to move "one millimetre" on policy and a vow to "deepen and accelerate" — the political method was conceded, the substance was doubled down, in his own words on the same night (Bloomberg, 7 September 2025).
  • Where the courts suspended his decree's labor chapter, the substance was re-attempted through the Ley Bases' narrower labor provisions and again in later reform bills — persistence through other doors is a legitimate legislative tactic, and it is recorded here only to note that the judicial wobble changed his route, not his direction.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — the poor, pensioners, people with disabilities, public-university students and staff, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • On the official count, the poor as a whole are back inside it. INDEC's household survey records poverty at 41.7% in the second half of 2023 (inherited), spiking to 52.9% in the first half of 2024 under his devaluation and shock adjustment, then falling for three consecutive semesters — 38.1%, 31.6%, and 28.2% in the second half of 2025, the lowest since 2018 — with extreme poverty (indigence) falling from 18.1% to 6.3% over the same span. The fall tracks the disinflation: annual inflation went from 211.4% (2023) to 117.8% (2024) to 31.5% (2025), and monthly inflation from 25.5% in December 2023 to roughly 2% by mid-2025 (INDEC releases, 2024–2026; World Bank Poverty & Equity Brief, October 2026). Inflation is the tax that falls hardest on cash-holding poor households, and its reduction is the mechanism his government cites; the World Bank's brief independently records the poverty decline.
  • Within the adjustment, one line was deliberately protected and raised: the universal child allowance (AUH) increased 47.8% in real terms in 2024 and was kept inflation-indexed, even as the workfare program Potenciar Trabajo was dismantled and its rolls purged. The design choice — direct transfers to mothers of children up, intermediary-mediated programs down — is documented in the academic record of his first year (Politics and Governance, 2026; Springer, 2026). Child poverty remained the highest of any age group (41.3% for under-14s in late 2025) but fell with the general rate (World Bank, October 2026).
  • His opponents' voters were counted at the ballot box and the count was honored in both directions (Test 2): the Peronist victory in Buenos Aires province stood, and his own midterm plurality (40.7% to 31.6% nationally, 26 October 2025) was won under the same electoral administration.

Evidence against:

  • The spike itself belongs on his ledger: the move from 41.7% to 52.9% poverty — roughly one in nine Argentines pushed below the line in six months, with a UCA observatory estimate of 57.4% at the January 2024 trough — was the direct, foreseeable result of his December 2023 devaluation (the peso's official rate cut by over half) and subsidy removals. His defense, that the alternative was hyperinflation and a deeper collapse, is an argument about a counterfactual; the documented path runs through the peak (INDEC; UCA-ODSA, 2024).
  • Pensioners were counted at a discount, deliberately and durably. The minimum pension — received by roughly 63% of retirees — lost an estimated 23–30% of its purchasing power across his first two years (economist estimates cited by Reuters, October 2025; Mate institute estimate, 2026), because his 2024 mobility formula indexed to inflation only after the initial devaluation shock had already eroded the base. Congress twice voted increases; he vetoed both (September 2024; August 2025). The August 2025 override attempt failed narrowly in the Deputies (160–83, short of two-thirds), so the pension veto stood while the disability veto fell the same day (AP, August 2025; chamber record, 20 August 2025). Retirees have protested outside Congress every Wednesday for over two years.
  • People with disabilities were counted out twice over. First by audit: the government terminated or suspended more than 110,000 non-contributory disability pensions in review drives it said targeted irregular grants; disability organizations documented cases of beneficiaries cut without individual assessment, and ANDIS itself was hollowed out — 170 staff dismissed, including 16 workers with disabilities, its inclusion fund "practically deactivated" (Argentina Working Group on Disability and Human Rights findings, reported September 2025). Then by veto: the Disability Emergency Law that Congress enacted over his veto in September 2025 was the legislature's answer, and the agency at the center of it became the site of the ANDIS kickback ALLEGATIONs (Test 1) — allegations about money skimmed from the counted-out, unresolved in court.
  • The universities were treated as an adversary's institution rather than a common one. Real funding for national universities fell roughly 40–45.6% from 2023 levels (National Inter-University Council figures, 2026), salaries and scholarships eroded, and after Congress legislated a remedy over his veto, implementation was suspended (Test 2). University staff struck nationally in September 2026; the sector's estimate of faculty resignations runs into the thousands (Times Higher Education, 2026).
  • The recovery's floor is uneven and, on the latest count, cracking: poverty rose again to 32.3% in the first half of 2026 (indigence 7.5%), the Gini coefficient worsened to 0.442 in early 2026, unemployment stood at 7.9% against 5.7% inherited, and roughly 300,000 formal jobs had disappeared by late 2026, with real wages still below their December 2023 level (INDEC, September 2026 release; World Bank, October 2026; contemporaneous reporting, November 2026). The flock's total count improved; identifiable parts of it — the old, the disabled, the university — were assigned the cost, by name and by veto.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since December 2023, with dates. His central promises were the chainsaw (austerity and fiscal surplus), dollarization, closing the central bank, and war on "the caste."

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The chainsaw was real, and it was the centerpiece. He promised in the 2023 campaign to cut state spending drastically and warned, unusually, that the adjustment would hurt before it helped — "there is no money" was a campaign line before it was a governing one. Delivered: public spending cut by roughly a third in real terms in 2024 (a fiscal consolidation of about five points of GDP), ministries halved from 18 to 9, over 40,000 public positions eliminated, energy and transport subsidies slashed, public works halted, and Argentina recorded a financial fiscal surplus in 2024 — its first since 2008 — repeated in 2025 (fiscal reporting, January 2025; academic and IMF assessments, 2025–2026).
  • The inflation promise was kept on its own terms. He said ending monetary financing of the deficit would bring inflation down; the treasury's monetary financing stopped, and annual inflation fell from 211.4% (2023) to 117.8% (2024) to 31.5% (2025), with monthly inflation at or near 2% through most of 2025–2026 (INDEC). Deregulation promises were likewise executed at scale — DNU 70/2023's hundreds of measures, the Ley Bases, rental-market decontrol (after which Buenos Aires rental supply rose sharply, by most market counts) — whatever their other grades, they are the program he sold (INDEC; market reporting, 2024).
  • The electorate's running verdict is part of the record: at the October 2025 midterms, after two years of austerity, his party won a national plurality of about 41% — the voters most exposed to the program renewed the mandate knowing exactly what it was (official results, 26 October 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Dollarization — the signature monetary promise of the campaign, the abolition of the peso itself — was abandoned without ever being formally renounced. The peso remains; the government instead ran a sequence of peso regimes (devaluation, crawling peg, IMF-agreed band), and by 2025 his economy minister was explaining in interviews why dollarization was not feasible; by late 2026 the president had largely stopped mentioning it (Reason, December 2026 issue; CRS overview). The substitute, "currency competition," was never announced to voters as a substitute.
  • The central bank he promised to close — he called it "the worst garbage that exists on this Earth" and, a year into his term, still pledged to close it within four years — remains open and central to his program. In August 2026 he sent Congress a bill to reform its charter to protect it: banning treasury financing, restoring price stability as its single mandate, and entrenching its governor's tenure. The reform's content is orthodox and defensible; it is also the institutional opposite of the promise, adopted without an acknowledgment that the promise changed (Bloomberg reporting, August 2026).
  • The war on "the caste" was compromised at its source on day one (Test 1: the anti-nepotism decree narrowed for his sister's appointment), and governing required the caste's machinery thereafter: an electoral and legislative alliance with the PRO of former president Macri, cabinet seats and candidacies for figures of the old parties, the Pacto de Mayo with 18 governors, and — in the Espert affair — his party's lead provincial candidate carrying a documented $200,000 payment from a U.S.-indicted businessman (Test 1). Milei's answer, that he uses the system's people without joining the system, is on the record; so is the list.
  • Two fiscal promises inverted in the small print: the original Ley Bases draft proposed raising some taxes (including reinstating an income tax Congress then restored in the final law) after a campaign against tax increases — export duties on agriculture were later cut by decree in July 2025, the direction he had promised, eighteen months in — and the zero-deficit rule itself remains a governing benchmark rather than the law he proposed, resting on his veto rather than on a statute (Ley Bases record, 2024; decree reporting, July 2025; fiscal-framework analyses, 2026).
  • On $LIBRA, his word to the public shifted within hours — from endorsement, to deletion, to "I didn't promote it, I spread the word" — and his assurance that he acted in good faith sits against the congressional commission's finding of "essential collaboration" and the PIA's appeal arguing the fraud file was closed early. Both characterizations are on the record; the courts have not finally resolved them (Test 1).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Milei's sheet is the mirror image of most of this series. The grip tests are where the failures cluster in other dossiers; here the institutions he tried to bend mostly bent him back — Congress overrode his vetoes for the first time in 22 years, the Senate refused his judges, the courts suspended his decree's core and struck its articles, and every one of those outcomes stands. His documented attempts are on the other shelf and they are not small: a mega-decree rewriting some 80 laws in ten days, two Supreme Court seats filled by signature, an enacted law suspended by decree after a veto override, and a presidency conducted as a running public attack on the press. What separates the attempt from the system is that, so far, the system has won each round — and that he has, in the end, accepted each defeat rather than break the board. On the count, the aggregate genuinely improved: poverty below where he found it, inflation broken, and the child allowance protected while the chainsaw swung — paid for by named groups, pensioners and the disabled and the universities, whose losses were not accidents of the program but items in it, defended by veto. On his word, the economic core was kept at a scale few presidents match, and the two institutional signatures — dollarization, the central bank's abolition — were quietly traded for their opposites. And on one law, the rule that governed everyone else stopped, twice, at his own family's door: once by a decree he rewrote on his first day, and once in the unresolved files of $LIBRA and ANDIS, where the only person in his government who has never faced a consequence is his sister.

Free. Always free.
