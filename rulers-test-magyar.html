The Ruler's Test — Péter Magyar, Prime Minister of Hungary

Subject: Péter Magyar — Prime Minister of Hungary since 9 May 2026; leader of the Tisza (Respect and Freedom) Party. A former Fidesz member and state-company executive, he broke with the governing camp in February 2024 over the presidential pardon scandal, took over the small Tisza party, and in the parliamentary election of 12 April 2026 won 141 of 199 seats on 53.18% of the party-list vote, against 38.61% and 52 seats for Fidesz–KDNP — ending Viktor Orbán's sixteen years in power and giving Tisza a two-thirds constitutional majority, governing alone. This dossier grades his record in office, roughly six months at the time of grading; where the record is thin, the grade says so. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The accountability drive against the Orbán era has been routed through legal institutions rather than decrees of guilt. The National Asset Recovery and Protection Office (NVVH) — a body Magyar had promised in the campaign — was set up with what reporting describes as a transparent multi-member leadership structure, and it works together with prosecutors and courts; Hungary joined the European Public Prosecutor's Office by the agreed 31 August 2026 deadline, submitting EU-funds cases to a prosecutor no Hungarian government controls (Carnegie Endowment assessment, September 2026; Euronews reporting, 2026).
  • The flagship cases rest on pre-existing documented files, not on charges invented after the election. The Elios street-lighting case had been documented by the EU's anti-fraud office OLAF in 2017–18 and closed twice by Hungarian police under the old government (see the Orbán dossier, Test 1); the central-bank foundations file rests on the State Audit Office's own 2025 audit. The investigations now running — former development minister Miklós Seszták (detained September 2026), former central bank governor György Matolcsy (detained October 2026), former culture minister Balázs Hankó and others — are ALLEGATIONs, contested by the accused, unadjudicated; what is documented is that they proceed through arrest, custody hearings, and courts, and that Fidesz's counter-claim — that the detainees are "political prisoners" — is the party's characterization, printed as such (Associated Press and Reuters reporting, autumn 2026).
  • The asset-declaration regime was tightened against his own camp as much as anyone's: the June 2026 anti-corruption package strengthened the Integrity Authority's power to verify officials' asset declarations, with omissions punishable by up to two years in prison — a rule that binds sitting Tisza officials from the day it passed (Reuters, 5 June 2026).
  • When his own side produced a compromised nominee, the process stopped him: parliament rejected President Baka's first nominee for chief prosecutor in October 2026 after reports linked the nominee's brother to the so-called "golden convoy" case, and a second, unconnected career prosecutor was nominated and elected instead (Reuters, November 2026). A system that can refuse the government's first choice for its top anti-corruption post is applying a rule, not a favor.
  • Magyar's own pre-office conduct toward the law cuts both ways and the favorable half belongs here: in March 2024 he testified for hours at the Metropolitan Prosecutor's Office in the Schadl–Völner corruption case and handed prosecutors the recording discussed below, rather than simply publishing it (contemporaneous reporting, March 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The drive's rhetoric is hotter than its procedure. Magyar has branded the anti-corruption campaign "Operation Purgatory," and five parliamentary committees with extraordinary powers — able to open archives and refer material to prosecutors and the EPPO — are auditing strategic contracts, intelligence activity, big business, and municipal finances simultaneously. Fidesz calls this a "criminal case factory" and a conveyor belt toward show trials; that is an interested party's characterization, labeled CONTESTED — but the documented scale and speed of the machinery, aimed in one direction only so far, is a fact of the record, and the new chief prosecutor's own office has cautioned that securing convictions could take years (Reuters, November 2026; committee mandates as reported, 2026). Six months in, no Orbán-era prosecution has yet produced a verdict that would test whether the process is law or purge.
  • The prime minister's own file under this test is open, and must be printed at full strength. In January 2023, while still married to Justice Minister Judit Varga, Magyar secretly recorded a private conversation in which Varga stated that Cabinet Minister Antal Rogán or his associates had had names removed from documents in the Schadl–Völner corruption case; he released the two-minute recording on 26 March 2024, after the pardon scandal had broken both their careers in the old system, and gave it to prosecutors. Varga's account — given in a televised interview the same day and repeated under questioning before a parliamentary committee on 11 November 2026 — is that her recorded statements were made under pressure, that Magyar threatened her with the recording through their divorce, and that he verbally and physically abused her during the marriage, including locking her in a room and pushing her against a door while pregnant. These are ALLEGATIONs. Magyar has denied the abuse allegations. A police investigation into alleged violation of personal freedom and endangerment of a minor was closed in April 2026 for insufficient evidence, with Varga exercising her right not to testify (reporting via Telex, April 2026). On 13 November 2026 two private complaints were filed with prosecutors — one by a former Fidesz MP — asking for investigation of possible blackmail and domestic violence on the basis of Varga's committee testimony; as of the reporting, no criminal proceedings had been opened, and the filing of a complaint is not a charge (Magyar Nemzet and 24.hu reporting, 13 November 2026). The test will be whether the prosecution service he has just rebuilt handles complaints against its own prime minister exactly as it handles complaints against the old camp. At six months, that question is unanswered in either direction.
  • The timing asymmetry of the recording itself is documented and was raised by Varga: the recording was made in January 2023 and released fourteen months later, during which Magyar held state-company positions paying, by her account, some 4.4 million forints a month. His stated reason for recording was protection of his family. Both accounts stand as their makers gave them; the fourteen-month gap is arithmetic, not interpretation.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do courts, the central bank, media, universities, municipalities, and the electoral system keep or regain autonomy — or is control re-centralized under new management?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The dismantling of the old grip has largely taken the form of restoring counterweights rather than seizing them. The Sovereignty Protection Office — the Fidesz-created body that investigated NGOs and media for alleged foreign service — was abolished by parliament on 30 June 2026. The June 2026 media law (Act XXI of 2026) created an Independent Public Media Board, multi-year public-media funding, a Press Fund for community and minority media, and open-tender selection for media executives and regulators, with the stated aim of aligning Hungary with the European Media Freedom Act (International IDEA democracy tracker; Council of Europe IRIS Merlin, 2026). The Media Council's new chair, Gábor Polyák — a media-law scholar and founder of the Mérték Media Monitor, long a critic of the old system — was elected in September 2026 following a public selection procedure, and the reconstituted council's first act was to adopt rules requiring published decisions, named voting records, and dissenting opinions (State Media Monitor, October 2026).
  • The Constitutional Court's stolen powers were handed back: the 17th Amendment restored the Court's power to review tax and budget legislation (removed by Fidesz in 2010) and transferred election of the Court's president from parliament to the judges themselves, while cutting judicial terms from twelve years to nine (International IDEA, July 2026). The reconstituted Court has since rejected Fidesz–KDNP challenges to the government's own constitutional amendment — a ruling for the government, delivered by the institution, through process (Worthy News/KLZ reporting, August 2026).
  • The university foundation system is being wound up rather than captured: the public-interest trusts (KEKVA) that held most universities are to be dissolved by 1 August 2027, sixteen foundations had closed by the end of August 2026 with several billion euros of assets returned to the state, and on 13 October 2026 the EU Council lifted the 2022 measures that had barred trust-run universities from Erasmus+ and Horizon Europe and froze €4.2 billion in cohesion funds (Council Implementing Decision, 6 October 2026; Carnegie, September 2026). In what form the universities will be governed after August 2027 is, as academic-freedom monitors note, not yet decided (Academic Freedom Blog, November 2026).
  • KESMA — the 476-outlet pro-Fidesz media conglomerate — was not expropriated or seized. It lost the state advertising and government funding flows that had sustained it, and much of the pro-government media ecosystem collapsed commercially; KESMA itself remains in place, politically aligned with Fidesz, now in opposition (State Media Monitor, 2026). The distinction matters to this test: the new government cut the subsidy, not the opponent's presses.
  • The central bank's sitting governor — Mihály Varga, appointed under Orbán in 2025 — remains in office, and the bank cut rates three times from June 2026 on its own stated reasoning as inflation eased; no documented attempt to remove or pressure him appears in the record graded here (Carnegie, September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The method of the transition repeatedly mirrors the method it replaced: mandates ended by law, at speed, by the same two-thirds instrument Fidesz used. Act XXI of 2026 terminated the mandates of the sitting Media Council, the Public Service Foundation's trustees, and the public-media executives by virtue of the law itself; the 17th Amendment (13 July 2026, passed 139–6 with Fidesz–KDNP boycotting) terminated President Tamás Sulyok's mandate outright, and a retirement age of 70 for Constitutional Court judges forced out the Court's president and three colleagues, whose five replacements were then elected on 15 September 2026 by the Tisza majority alone (International IDEA; Jogászvilág reporting, September 2026). Each individual change has a restoration argument behind it; the pattern — a parliamentary supermajority rewriting tenure rules to remove named officeholders — is the Orbán-era pattern with the polarity reversed, and the dossier records it as such.
  • Public media's "liberation" included a six-week silence: the interim leadership suspended M1's news service from 7 July to 20 August 2026 while the system was rebuilt, a step the European Parliament questioned under the EU Charter and the European Media Freedom Act; the Commission answered that it understood the suspension as temporary within the reform, and that the service had resumed (European Parliament written question E-003148/26 and Commission answer, September 2026). The State Media Monitor's October 2026 review classifies the public broadcaster as still "State-Controlled" on a provisional basis, noting that no systematic assessment of its editorial output since the relaunch had been completed — the removal of the old capture is documented; the arrival of independence is not yet (State Media Monitor, 2026).
  • The presidency sequence shows the grip's reflex as well as its release. Sulyok — who had refused Magyar's calls to resign — was removed by constitutional amendment after asking the Venice Commission for an urgent opinion on whether removing a sitting head of state by amendment meets European standards; Amnesty International called the approach unacceptable, and constitutional scholars warned of the precedent (Venice Commission urgent procedure, July 2026; Amnesty statement, 2026). His successor, András Baka — the former Supreme Court president whose own mandate Orbán's system terminated early in 2012, and who won at Strasbourg over it — used his inaugural address to warn the new government against "change built on revenge" and against replacing one dominant system with another (Hungarian Conservative and Reuters reporting, August 2026). The warning is on the record; that it was felt necessary is part of the record too.
  • The electoral system that converted 53% of votes into a two-thirds majority — single-round first-past-the-post, redrawn boundaries, winner compensation — remains in force, unreformed, now to its new beneficiary's advantage. A comprehensive new constitution has been promised (Test 5) but not yet drafted in public; the rules of the next election have not yet been touched.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, the EU, markets, his own coalition, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED (six months in office; the thinnest file of the five)

Evidence for:

  • The presidential-selection fiasco of July–August 2026 is the clearest documented adjustment. Magyar floated chess grandmaster Judit Polgár for the presidency without the public consultation he had earlier suggested would occur; Polgár declined and criticized how her name had entered the debate, Tisza's own supporters objected publicly — and Magyar apologized, took political responsibility for the failure, and re-ran the process through a shortlist of three names decided by secret ballot of his parliamentary group, producing Baka (IES Lublin commentary, August 2026; EU Today reporting). Carnegie notes the same pattern in a botched justice-minister appointment: an admitted mistake and a reset — "something that, many Hungarians note, Viktor Orbán never did" (Carnegie, September 2026).
  • His own camp's institutions have pushed back and been obeyed: parliament — his majority — rejected the president's first chief-prosecutor nominee over the "golden convoy" link (Test 1), and the government proceeded with a second nominee rather than forcing the first through. The Constitutional Court's rejection of Fidesz's challenges was accepted as the institution's word, not relitigated by amendment.
  • On asylum, the government adjusted toward compliance when the cost of defiance was priced: the foreign minister told parliament in May 2026 that asylum applications must again be submittable at the border to end the €1-million-a-day EU fines — a reversal of the Orbán-era embassy-only system, taken openly before a committee, against the grain of his own campaign rhetoric (see Test 5 for the tension) (Brussels Signal reporting of the 11 May 2026 hearing).

Evidence against:

  • On the largest wobble of the term — the removal of President Sulyok — he doubled down. The Venice Commission had opened an urgent procedure at Sulyok's request and sent a delegation to Budapest on 2 July 2026; Amnesty International and domestic constitutional scholars objected; the amendment was adopted on 13 July anyway, and Sulyok signed it on 18 July stating it breached constitutional principles but left him no legal option (Brussels Signal, July 2026; Sándor Palace communiqué). The government's answer — that the April mandate authorized it — is on the record; so is the fact that the external check was heard and overridden, not answered on its merits.
  • The speed itself has become the recurring complaint, including from friendly quarters: civic and media observers have criticized short-circuited consultation on the media law and other fast-tracked changes, and Bloomberg reported in July 2026 that the rush to undo the Orbán system had dented Tisza's support in polls — though other polling in July showed Tisza above 60% and later readings showed a slight decline within the margin of error; the polling record is CONTESTED between surveys and is recorded here at that strength (Bloomberg, July 2026; IES Lublin, August 2026).
  • Six months is short, and the hardest wobbles have not yet arrived: no court has yet ruled against a signature Magyar measure on substance, no prosecution of a Tisza figure has tested his response, and the Constitutional Court's five new judges have not yet had to tell their electors no. The grade reflects adjustment shown on appointments and compliance, doubling-down shown on the presidency, and a file that is mostly still to be written.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Fidesz voters, Roma, migrants, LGBTQ citizens, and Budapest — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • LGBTQ citizens were counted back in, in fact and then in law. Police announced on 29 May 2026 that the Budapest Pride march would proceed without restrictions; prosecutors dropped the charges against Budapest Mayor Gergely Karácsony (4 June 2026) and against the Pécs Pride organizer over the 2025 marches; tens of thousands marched in Budapest on 27 June 2026 on a police-secured route, and Pécs marched on 12 September 2026. On 13 October 2026 parliament voted to roll back the 2021 "child protection" provisions banning portrayal of homosexuality and gender diversity to minors, and the government has said it will amend the law that effectively bars same-sex couples from adopting (Washington Blade, September 2026; EU Reports, October 2026). The sequence was assisted by the Court of Justice's 21 April 2026 judgment striking down the 2021 law — a ruling the new government implemented rather than defied, which is itself the relevant contrast.
  • Budapest, governed by an opposition mayor, was dealt with rather than besieged: after years in which the Orbán government raised the capital's "solidarity contribution" roughly ninefold and the city's credit was downgraded, the Magyar government reached a funding agreement with Karácsony — a 30 billion forint state subsidy paid in October 2026, repayment of past overpayments, release of subsidies, and termination of the lawsuits over the solidarity contribution (XpatLoop and municipal reporting, October 2026). The deal carries a state-appointed budget supervisor for the capital — a condition the city's district mayors attacked from the other side, arguing the pact draws off district revenues; that dispute is between the city and its districts, recorded as such.
  • The poor are in the 2027 budget as submitted on 31 October 2026: the minimum pension quadrupled (from 28,500 to 120,000 forints), family allowances doubled including enhanced payments for children with chronic illness or severe disability, an additional 510 billion forints for healthcare, financed in part by a new wealth tax on assets above 1 billion forints and a corporate-tax rise to 15% (government budget submission reporting, October–November 2026). These are proposals before parliament at grading time, recorded as such.
  • Fidesz's voters — 38.61% of the country — have not been counted out of the state: Fidesz sits in parliament, its media (KESMA) still publishes, Orbán remains free and party leader, and no measure has touched Fidesz voters' rights, benefits, or ballots as a class. The measures aimed at the old system are aimed at officeholders and structures (Tests 1–2), a distinction the record so far supports.

Evidence against:

  • Migrants and asylum seekers remain counted out, by the new government's own stated choice. Magyar declared on 13 April 2026 — before taking office — that Hungary would keep the southern border fence ("indeed, we will also patch up the holes"), reject any EU allocation mechanism, and maintain a "very strict stance" on illegal migration; in office he has confirmed the fence and the practice of escorting irregular migrants back across the Serbian border, describing the EU's fines as unjust (Reuters, 13 April 2026; Policy Wire interview, October 2026). The foreign minister's May 2026 signal that border asylum applications would resume to end the daily fines has not, on the record graded here, become a functioning asylum system: the EU still holds roughly €2 billion in cohesion funds frozen partly over asylum, and the government says it intends to address the asylum conditions by the end of 2026 (American Hungarian Federation summary of Commission decisions, 2026). A population that can be fined over daily, at €1 million a day, and still waits, is a population placed late in the count.
  • The Roma file is thin to the point of near-silence. The documented record of the first six months contains no signature Roma measure comparable to the Pride or Budapest files — no revisit of the Gyöngyöspata school-segregation compensation politics, no named Roma inclusion program in the sources graded here; a 589-million-forint transfer toward minority support appears in October 2026 budget reallocations (CLSTR municipal-funding tracker, October 2026). Hungary's largest minority is, on this record, neither targeted nor visibly counted in; the grade records the thinness rather than inventing a position.
  • One measure counts against a class defined by party: the 12-year limit on parliamentary service introduced in the 17th Amendment applies from the next election and would bar roughly half of Fidesz's current deputies from standing in 2030, while Tisza's first-term MPs are untouched by it in practice. The government defends term limits as a general reform; critics, including the new president in remarks reported at his election, question a rule whose incidence falls so asymmetrically on one camp (Carnegie, September 2026; Hungarian Conservative, August 2026). The limit does not apply to members of the current term — the mitigation is documented — but its design was the government's choice.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since May 2026, with dates. Six months is early; the grade weighs what was promised for the first phase against what the first phase shows.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • EU funds — the central economic promise, kept at speed. Magyar campaigned on unlocking the frozen funds. He traveled to Brussels within weeks; on 29 May 2026 he and Commission President von der Leyen agreed a path to release €16.4 billion (€10 billion recovery, €6.4 billion cohesion), conditional on 27 "super milestones" and over 100 further milestones, including EPPO accession and a strengthened Integrity Authority. Parliament amended more than 100 laws over the summer; the Council adopted Hungary's revised recovery plan in July 2026; in September–October 2026 the Commission and Council released the €4.2 billion cohesion tranche, restored Erasmus+/Horizon access, and ended the 2022 conditionality measures; by mid-November 2026 the Commission was set to approve almost all of the €10 billion recovery allocation (Reuters, AP, Euronews reporting, May–November 2026).
  • The anti-corruption instruments he named were built: the asset-recovery office (NVVH) exists and is operating; the Integrity Authority was strengthened with asset-declaration verification and prison penalties for omissions (23 June 2026 package); EPPO membership was delivered by the deadline he accepted (Reuters, June 2026; Carnegie, September 2026).
  • The self-limiting promise was enacted against his own camp's future: the June 2026 constitutional amendment capping total service as prime minister at eight years — calculated retrospectively, which also bars Orbán's return — was a stated Tisza commitment to end the era of indefinite rule, and it binds any future Tisza premier as much as it bars the old one (Frontiers in Political Science analysis, 2026; Carnegie, September 2026).
  • Symbolic and structural pledges delivered on schedule: the Sovereignty Protection Office abolished (30 June 2026); the prime minister's office moved out of the Carmelite Monastery and the former premier's offices opened to the public; government poster-propaganda ended and banned going forward; the pardon-scandal documents declassification and a first-ever vetting of communist-era security officers announced (OSW commentary, August 2026; Carnegie, September 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, narrowed, or in tension):

  • The new constitution — the deepest promise — is not yet begun in public. Tisza's mandate narrative promised a comprehensive constitutional reform to replace the Fidesz Fundamental Law; six months in, the transition has proceeded by amending that same Fundamental Law (the 16th and 17th amendments), with the 17th expressly framing itself as transitional "before a new constitution is drafted" (International IDEA, July 2026). Amending the old text to remove the old system's people, while the promised new text waits, is a sequencing choice the government defends as necessity; the promise itself remains undelivered at grading time.
  • The electoral system is unreformed. Nothing in the first six months changed the single-round, winner-compensated electoral law whose distortions Tisza spent years condemning — the law under which the next election, too, would currently be run.
  • Migration wording vs. migration practice is in open tension. The April 2026 pledge — no pact, no allocation mechanism, fence kept — sits beside the foreign minister's May 2026 statement that border asylum applications must resume to end the EU fines and that the Migration Pact could mean "accepting a limited number of immigrants or offering financial and logistical support." Right-wing commentators and some Tisza voters called the shift a betrayal of the pledge; the government frames it as compliance without quotas (Brussels Signal, May 2026). Both the pledge and the softening are documented; they do not fully reconcile.
  • The Istanbul Convention announcement tests another edge: reporting in November 2026 described Magyar signaling readiness to ratify the Istanbul Convention on violence against women — a step Orbán's Hungary refused for a decade — as part of closing the remaining €2.2 billion funds tranche; at grading time it remains an announced intention, not a ratification (CSS Eurasia research note, November 2026).
  • Expectation management on justice: the campaign's promise of swift accountability for the old system's corruption meets the new anti-corruption leadership's own warning that convictions could take years (Reuters, November 2026). Not a broken promise — a promise whose timeline is being renegotiated in public, recorded as such.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Péter Magyar's file is the first in this series graded on a government whose founding act was losing nothing and removing someone else's system — and its central tension is visible in every test: the restoration is real, and so is the resemblance of its methods to the ones it is restoring against. The funds came home because the milestones were met; the Sovereignty Protection Office is gone; Pride marched; the captured media regulator is chaired by one of the old system's sharpest critics, chosen in the open; KESMA was starved of subsidy, not seized. Against that: a president removed by amendment over a pending Venice Commission opinion, judges retired by a birthday rule, a public broadcaster silenced for six weeks in order to be freed, and an electoral system left exactly as distorted as he found it — now distorting in his favor. The one-law test cannot yet be passed or failed, because its decisive cases are all still open: the prosecutions of the old camp have produced arrests, not verdicts, and the complaints against the prime minister himself — arising from a secret recording, a divorce, and allegations he denies, one investigation already closed for want of evidence — have produced filings, not proceedings. Six months in, the honest grade is the one the record forces: a transition that has kept most of its first-phase promises, counts in everyone except the people at the fence, and has not yet faced the moment that defines this Test — the moment its own machinery is asked to rule against its own side, and does, or does not.
