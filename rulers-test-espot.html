The Ruler's Test — Xavier Espot Zamora, Head of Government of Andorra

Subject: Xavier Espot Zamora — Head of Government (Cap de Govern, the prime minister) of the Principality of Andorra since 16 May 2019. The seat graded is the head of government, and the reason is stated plainly: in Andorra the executive sits with the Head of Government, while the heads of state are the two co-princes — the Bishop of Urgell (Josep-Lluís Serrano Pentinat, who succeeded Joan-Enric Vives on 31 May 2025 and took the oath as co-prince on 26 June 2025) and the President of France (Emmanuel Macron). Espot, born 30 October 1979 in Escaldes-Engordany, a former judge and minister under his predecessor Antoni Martí, leads Democrats for Andorra (DA). He was first elected in 2019 in coalition, and re-elected at the general election of 2 April 2023: the DA and its allies won 16 of the 28 seats in the General Council on 32.66% of the national vote, with turnout 66.93% of 29,958 electors — DA itself taking 14 seats, Committed Citizens (CC) 2, against Concord (Concòrdia) 5, the Social Democratic Party (PS) with Social Democracy and Progress 3, Andorra Forward (Endavant) 3, and Action (Acció) 1 (2023 election results; Who Governs EU analysis, 2023; Spain's Foreign Ministry country profile, September 2026). He is graded on his full tenure since 2019, with the weight on the second term since April 2023. The 1993 Constitution limits the Head of Government to two full terms; Espot is therefore barred from standing again at the election due no later than spring 2027, and no proposal to change that rule is documented. In September 2023 he came out publicly as gay, a first for an Andorran head of government. Andorra is a microstate of about 88,000–89,000 residents; its documented public record is thinner than a large state's, and where the shelf is thin this dossier says so rather than padding it. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure; carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the for shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The housing and foreign-investment rules of his second term were written as general laws applying by category, not by name. Law 5/2025 of 6 March 2025 — the "Omnibus Law" on sustainable growth and the right to housing, in force from April 2025 — imposed quantitative limits on property purchases by foreign investors as a class, banned foreign-funded residential development except for affordable rental housing held for at least 10 years, and applied a progressive tax on foreign real-estate investment of up to 10%, with a 90% bonus only where the investment itself provides affordable rental housing for at least 10 years (Law 5/2025, published in the BOPA, 26 March 2025; professional summaries, 2025). The 2026 Law on Continuity and Consolidation of Measures for Sustainable Growth preserved the same mechanisms — the 6–10% progressive tax, vacant-property mobilisation powers, and tourist-rental moratoriums — as a standing framework through 2030 rather than as discretionary deals (2026 consolidation law summaries, 2026).
  • The same general-law form was used against empty homes held by anyone: the Omnibus framework created powers to mobilise vacant properties into the rental market, with a government target of recovering approximately 1,500 housing units for rental over three years (Omnibus Law summaries, 2025).
  • The minimum wage was raised by decree for all employees, on a published formula: to €1,447.33 a month in 2025, then by Decree 8/2026 to €1,525.33 a month (€8.80 an hour) from 1 January 2026 — a 5.4% rise set at double the 2.7% CPI, capped at 6% — and again to €1,568.67 a month (€9.05 an hour) from 1 July 2026. The government spokesman stated the wage had risen 45% since 2019, from €1,050, reaching 58% of the average wage against the European Social Charter's 60% benchmark (Government statements via Alto, January 2026; WageIndicator, February 2026; Labour Inspection circulars, 2026).
  • Anti-corruption housekeeping was at least begun in general form: a Code of Conduct for members of the government and high-level officials was adopted in November 2023, and on 15 January 2026 the Council of Ministers approved Andorra's accession to the United Nations Convention against Corruption (GRECO report, 3 July 2024; Government statement via Alto, 15 January 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Council of Europe's anti-corruption body, GRECO, found in its report of 3 July 2024 — covering precisely the top executive functions Espot heads — that there were no rules requiring integrity checks before appointing ministers and other persons with top executive functions, that the November 2023 Code of Conduct lacked implementation guidelines and any supervision-and-sanctions mechanism, that lobbying was unregulated, that there were no post-employment restrictions, and that asset declarations did not extend to all top officials, were not public, and were not regularly checked. GRECO also recommended giving the Public Prosecutor's Office sufficient resources and powers for corruption cases involving top officials — a recommendation that records a gap, not a filled one (GRECO, 3 July 2024). The authorities were to report back by 31 December 2025; no compliance verdict is in the record assembled here.
  • GRECO's earlier round, closed 4 December 2024, recorded that parliamentarians' asset declarations are likewise not made public and do not cover household members, and that one 2017 recommendation — on the composition of the High Council of Justice — remained unimplemented after seven years (GRECO, 4 December 2024).
  • A second law by status runs through the system he governs, inherited but not reformed by him: more than 50% of the population consists of noncitizens who cannot vote in national elections or stand for office, under naturalisation criteria requiring more than 20 years' residence, renunciation of any other nationality, and Catalan; and the Roman Catholic Church enjoys a privileged position, drawing some state support and bypassing some processes other faith groups must follow (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022 and 2024). The reformed nationality law approved 7 May 2026 by 19 votes to 8, in force 4 June 2026, kept the no-dual-nationality rule and the long residence requirement in substance (press reports of the 2026 reform, summarised June 2026). The detail of Test 4 carries the counting side of this; Test 1 records it as a standing difference of law by category of person.
  • Within the rental law itself, the categories are not equal: the transitional protections of Law 11/2026 (approved 4 June 2026) do not apply to rents of €2,500 a month or more, to detached houses, or to tenants holding residence without gainful activity — the permit most retired and passive residents hold — whose landlords may agree a new rent freely when the contract ends (Law 11/2026 summaries, 2026).

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because the two shelves point in different directions on the same question. On legislation, Espot's government has repeatedly written one rule for a whole class — foreign investors, empty-home owners, employers — and applied it by category. On the integrity of the source itself, the external referee he answers to, GRECO, documents a top-executive regime with no pre-appointment integrity checks, unpublished asset declarations, unregulated lobbying, and no post-employment rules, seven years after some of those gaps were first named. A ruler cannot be credited with one law at the source while the source's own safeguards remain, on the documented record, unbuilt.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — tenants and would-be tenants as a class, by vacant-property mobilisation and affordable-rental incentives written into general law; minimum-wage employees in every sector, by formula-based rises. Hurt — foreign property investors and speculative buyers, by purchase limits and a tax of up to 10% (a burden imposed openly by law, recorded here as a burden, not as a wrong); passive residents, who were written out of the rental law's transitional protections while other tenants were written in. No documented case appears in the record assembled here of a law bent for a named ally of Espot's; that absence is recorded as an absence, not as proof none exists.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • The institutions that seated him acted independently of him, twice. Elections are run under an Electoral Law that Freedom House describes as providing a sound framework for free and fair elections, supervised impartially by the Electoral Board; the 2 April 2023 election returned his coalition but also seated a new opposition party, Concord, as the leading opposition, and cut his former coalition partners — results no gripped commission produces (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022 and 2024; 2023 election results).
  • The judiciary is assessed as impartial and independent and generally free from government pressure, scoring 4 of 4 on Freedom House's judicial-independence measure; judges are appointed and supervised by the Higher Council of Justice, whose five members are chosen one each by the two co-princes, the speaker of parliament, and the Head of Government, with the fifth elected by judges and magistrates — the Head of Government names one of five, not a majority (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024).
  • A court ruled against his government's preferred reading of its own housing law, and the ruling stood in public: a Superior Court interpretation of Law 24/2023 on rental-market measures held that tenants must explicitly request an extension for it to take effect, after which the opposition Social Democrats pressed the government in parliament on tenant protection — the sequence of an independent court, a government that must answer for the consequence, and an opposition free to exploit it (Alto reporting, October 2026).
  • Parliament checks him in the open. The General Council's housing working group became a public fight in 2024 when opposition councillors accused the government of leaving the table after presenting its bill, and Espot defended the government's conduct in the chamber rather than closing the forum (General Council reporting, 2024). Opposition parties routinely put urgent oral questions to the executive, as the Social Democrats did on tenants in October 2026 (Alto, October 2026).
  • The constitutional limit on his own seat is intact and unchallenged by him: the two-full-term limit in the 1993 Constitution bars him from the 2027 election, and no proposal by him or his party to change it is documented — claims that he would change the rules to stay are recorded in fact-checking coverage as unproven speculation, with the rule standing (2027 election records; fact-check summary, November 2026).
  • Critical media operate openly: Andorran outlets including La Veu Lliure and Alto publish sustained criticism of his housing record, his referendum timing, and his ministers, and the Housing Union's protests are covered in detail. Freedom House scores personal expression 4 of 4 and assembly 4 of 4, while noting criminal defamation laws remain on the books and that business, political, and religious interests have historically influenced media coverage — a standing weakness of the environment, scored 3 of 4 for media independence, not a documented act of his (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024).

Evidence against:

  • The one documented structural grip-point is the unimplemented GRECO recommendation on the High Council of Justice: GRECO regretted in December 2024 that the Council's composition "fails to afford appropriate representation of members of the judiciary elected by their peers," a recommendation outstanding since 2017 — throughout Espot's tenure (GRECO, 4 December 2024). The Head of Government's own appointee sits on that Council under the constitutional design he inherited; the failure recorded here is the failure to complete a reform the referee requested, not a documented seizure.
  • GRECO's July 2024 recommendation that the Public Prosecutor's Office be given sufficient resources and powers for corruption cases involving top executive officials records that, on the referee's assessment, the prosecution's capacity against the executive itself was not yet sufficient (GRECO, 3 July 2024).
  • His absolute majority — 16 of 28 seats — means the General Council's check is political rather than arithmetic: the same majority that seated him must vote any censure. That is the ordinary condition of parliamentary government and is recorded as context, not as an act of gripping; no documented instance appears in the record assembled here of Espot punishing, removing, or defunding a judge, an electoral official, a prosecutor, a central financial authority, or a media outlet.

Why: The grade lands at HOLDS because every referee named in this test is documented acting against, or independently of, his interest at least once on the record — an electoral board that seated a new opposition, a superior court that narrowed his housing law's protection, a parliament that interrogates him, a press that campaigns against his referendum timing — and because the strongest against-evidence is an unfinished reform and an under-resourced prosecutor, which are neglect of the referees' strength, not a grip on their decisions. Neglect is recorded on this shelf in full; it does not, on this evidence, convert to gripping.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — voters across parties, whose 2023 votes produced the parliament they cast them for, including the opposition's; tenants who litigated, in that the Superior Court's reading was given effect rather than overridden. Hurt — no group is documented as hurt by an act of institutional capture under Espot in the record assembled here. The standing risk falls on future corruption defendants and complainants alike, if the prosecution remains under-resourced for cases touching top officials (GRECO, July 2024) — a risk recorded, not an outcome documented.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The street's largest pushback of his tenure — housing — was answered with legislation, repeatedly, in the open. After protests over rents began in 2023, parliament approved Law 24/2023 on 28 November 2023 to stimulate and stabilise the rental market; the government followed with the Omnibus Law of March 2025 (foreign-investment limits and tax, vacant-home mobilisation, a target of about 1,500 units returned to rental), the 2026 consolidation law carrying those measures to 2030, and Law 11/2026 of 4 June 2026, which ends the old rent controls in stages from 2027 to 2030 by contract date, caps rises on renewal on a sliding scale, requires five-year leases and six months' notice, and fines overcharging at 50% of a year's rent (Law 24/2023; Law 5/2025; Law 11/2026 and Government summaries, 2023–2026). In September 2026 the government announced €6 million in rent aid aimed at keeping rent at no more than 30% of household income (Government announcement, September 2026).
  • On the abortion file — where the wobble is institutional, because one head of state is a Catholic bishop — he chose a negotiated adjustment over either denial or confrontation: talks with the Holy See since 2019, meetings between Espot, Minister Ladislau Baró, and Vatican Secretary of State Cardinal Pietro Parolin on 22 October 2025 and again in July 2026, and on 28 October 2026 cabinet approval of a bill amending Article 117 of the Penal Code so that a woman who consents to, requests, or collaborates in her own abortion "shall not be punishable," while penalties for those who perform abortions in Andorra (up to three years' imprisonment and five years' professional disqualification) and the exclusion of public funding remain (Government statement, 28 October 2026; ZENIT and Infovaticana reporting, October–November 2026). The bill was sent to the General Council under an urgent procedure; no vote had been scheduled at grading. This is the no-good-choice clause's shape, and it is recorded by its mechanics below.
  • On the EU association agreement, when another option became available — the opposition's demand, pressed by Andorra Forward leader Carine Montaner and echoed by the Social Democrats, for a referendum as soon as possible and before signature — Espot considered the sequencing openly and declined it with stated reasons: his government signed the agreement in Brussels on 19 October 2026 and states that a consultative referendum, whose result it has declared politically binding, will be called once the European Parliament has given its consent (provisionally planned for January 2027), that signature does not itself approve or apply the agreement, and that ratification would then require a two-thirds majority of the General Council (Government statements, October 2026; Brussels Signal, 12 November 2026). Declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence under the method; the dispute over what was originally promised is graded in Test 5, where it belongs.
  • Local voters pushed back within his term and the result stood: in the parish elections of 17 December 2023, DA lost Andorra la Vella, Sant Julià de Lòria, and Escaldes-Engordany and governed the remaining parishes in coalition (local election results, December 2023).

Evidence against:

  • Three years of housing mobilisation have not ended, because the underlying condition has not: in the second quarter of 2026 only 117 apartments were listed for rent in the whole country against 796 for sale, and long-standing tenants' protected rents remain far below asking rents (Pisos.ad listings data via rental-law summaries, 2026). In November 2026 the Housing Union (SHA) and the Andorran Trade Union (USdA) formed a Popular Mobilisation Committee, presented eight demands to Housing Minister Conxita Marsol on 13 November, set a 20 November deadline, judged the government's response — an offer of a meeting the following week, with no stated position on the proposals — insufficient, and announced a conditional border blockade for 4–5 December 2026 and a demonstration on 5 December, demanding written commitments so as not to reach 2027 without concrete solutions (La Veu Lliure, November 2026; La Ciutat via Barcelona Press, November 2026). The outcome of that deadline was not documented at grading.
  • On that same file, Espot is reported to have anticipated that he would not allow a mobilisation at the border, before the Committee's formal notification to the Interior Ministry — a statement the Committee publicly questioned as prejudging a legal, peaceful protest for which it planned to give notice, preserve emergency-vehicle passage, and consider residents and cross-border workers (Barcelona Press, November 2026). Freedom of assembly is scored 4 of 4 by Freedom House and the protest had not been banned at grading; the statement is recorded as a signal against adjustment, not as a suppression.
  • The pension wobble was not answered at all within the term: parliamentary sources and RTVA coverage in 2026 report pension reform — second pillar, retirement age, contributions — deadlocked and increasingly unlikely to pass before the election, with the political cost of unpopular measures named as an obstacle, leaving reform as a major unresolved issue for the next term (La Veu Lliure and RTVA reporting, 2026). CASS and pension-fund leaders voiced public frustration at a lost year (Alto reporting, 2026).
  • The housing working-group rupture of 2024 is contested: opposition councillor Carine Montaner accused the government of breaking constructive dialogue by presenting its bill after leaving the joint table; Espot defended that the measures had been worked on together and were presented to make the text's axes known (General Council reporting, 2024). The dispute is printed with its disputants named; what is not contested is that the joint process did not survive the bill's presentation.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because the pattern is genuinely split by file. On housing law and on abortion he adjusted in the open, more than once, with instruments anyone can read. On pensions he let the wobble stand unanswered until the term ran out. On the street in November 2026 the adjustment offered was a meeting without a position, which the protesters — after three years of documented mobilisation — judged no adjustment at all; and his reported pre-emptive statement against a border protest cuts against the openness the rest of the shelf shows. The no-good-choice clause applies to the abortion file by its mechanics: the options that actually existed were (a) full legalisation, which on the government's documented account would force the Bishop co-prince to sanction a law against his church's teaching and put the co-principality itself — the state's constitutional form since 1278, retained in 1993 — in crisis; (b) the status quo, under which women remained criminally liable; or (c) the course taken — decriminalising the woman only, keeping penalties for practitioners and no public funding, negotiated with the Holy See over seven years. Each open option carried real harm; the course taken was the least-harm door actually open, it was stated openly, and it is not held against him. Whether he built the trap earlier is also on the record: the trap is constitutional and centuries old, not of his making, though the seven years the negotiation took are his.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — tenants on pre-2022 contracts, whose protections were extended and then given a staged, published exit rather than a cliff edge; households receiving the €6 million rent aid; women in Andorra, who under the October 2026 bill would no longer face up to six months' imprisonment for their own abortion. Hurt — landlords, whose rent rises were capped and whose overcharging now carries a fine of 50% of a year's rent, and owners of vacant homes exposed to mobilisation powers; future pensioners and current contributors, for whom a term of deadlock leaves the system's documented viability risks unaddressed; housing protesters, whose November 2026 demands had received no substantive written answer at grading.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the noncitizen majority, the seasonal and cross-border workers, the protesters, the tenants, the women, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Low-paid workers were counted in money, repeatedly: the minimum wage rose 45% between 2019 and 2026 on a formula deliberately set above inflation (double CPI, capped at 6%), reaching €1,568.67 a month in July 2026 and 58% of the average wage, against a median salary of €2,299 a month at the end of 2025 across 49,375 salaried workers (Government statements, January 2026; Department of Statistics figures via Alto, 2026).
  • Tenants were counted as a group in successive laws (Test 3), and rent aid of €6 million announced in September 2026 was targeted by income — aimed at keeping rent at no more than 30% of household income (Government announcement, September 2026).
  • Women were counted on the file where Andorra counted them out most completely: Andorra banned abortion outright, with women facing up to six months' imprisonment, and in practice women travel to Catalonia for care. The bill approved by his cabinet on 28 October 2026 removes the woman's criminal liability entirely (Government statement, 28 October 2026; reporting, October–November 2026). Espot had stated the decriminalisation would "certainly become a reality before the end of the legislative term" (La Veu Lliure reporting of Espot's RTVA interview, 2026).
  • Seasonal and cross-border workers were counted in the system's quotas rather than outside them: for winter 2025–26 Andorra set 4,995 temporary work permits, unchanged since 2023, of which 4,834 had been issued by late January, 3,711 to people from outside the EU; the 2026 general quota was 800, split 624 for residents and 176 for cross-border workers living in Spain or France; and in July 2026 the government approved a regulation for hiring in the country of origin, with hospitality contracts of up to nine months and at least half the return fare paid by the employer (Government immigration figures and Decree 283/2026, via 2026 reporting).

Evidence against:

  • The largest counted-out group in Andorra is the majority of the people who live there. More than 50% of the population consists of noncitizens — mostly from Spain, Portugal, and France — who cannot vote in national elections or stand for office; the electorate in 2023 was 29,958 people in a country of roughly 85,000–89,000 residents (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022 and 2024; 2023 election records). The structure predates Espot, but his government's 2026 nationality reform — approved 19 to 8 on 7 May 2026 — kept its essentials: some twenty years' residence, a Catalan examination, and mandatory renunciation of any other nationality, with Andorran nationality itself losable by actively using another (2026 reform summaries, June 2026). No move under Espot toward local-election voting for long-term residents is documented, a gap the Council of Europe's Commissioner for Human Rights flagged as long ago as 2001.
  • Women remain half-counted even under his own reform: abortion still cannot be performed in Andorra, practitioners still face up to three years' imprisonment, and the CASS social-security fund will not cover the procedure — so a woman must still travel abroad and pay, a burden that falls hardest on women with the least money (Government statement and reporting, October–November 2026). The constraint behind this is the co-prince structure recorded in Test 3; the incidence is recorded here, where Test 4 grades it.
  • Passive residents — largely retired people holding residence without gainful activity — were expressly excluded from Law 11/2026's transitional rental protections, leaving their rents to free agreement at contract end while working tenants kept staged caps (Law 11/2026 summaries, 2026).
  • Small faith communities remain uncounted in the landscape he governs: Andorra's small Muslim and Jewish communities lack dedicated cemeteries and the country has no recognised mosque, and the Catholic Church's privileged position stands (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024). The 2022 Education Law amendment banning conspicuous religious symbols in public schools — which Amnesty International called discriminatory — was passed under Espot's government in his first term (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024).
  • Protesters were counted as interlocutors for three years and, in November 2026, judged themselves uncounted in substance: the Housing Union's eight demands received, on the Committee's account, a meeting offer with no stated position, prompting the conditional border-blockade announcement (La Veu Lliure; Barcelona Press, November 2026).

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because Espot's accounting is real where it runs through money and law — wages, rent aid, tenant protections, work quotas — and stops at the border of citizenship and of the co-principality. A majority of the people who live and work in Andorra have no vote for the government that sets their wages and rents, and his one nationality reform of the term kept that door essentially as narrow as he found it; women gained freedom from prosecution and kept the obligation to travel and pay. Counting everyone in the wage decree while counting only citizens at the ballot box is a mixed record, and the shelf shows both halves.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — minimum-wage employees (a 45% rise since 2019); tenants on older contracts and rent-aid households; seasonal and cross-border workers, whose permits and a hiring-in-origin scheme with employer-paid return fares were maintained in number. Hurt — noncitizen long-term residents, still without a vote after the 2026 reform; women needing an abortion, still required to leave the country at their own cost; passive residents, excluded from rental protections; Muslim and Jewish residents, still without a recognised mosque or dedicated cemeteries on the documented record.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 16 May 2019, with dates; the two axes — word-keeping and helped/hurt — graded independently, with all four crossings named.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The EU association agreement — negotiated to signature, as pledged. The DA-led majority's 2023 programme committed Andorra to concluding the association negotiations with the EU. Negotiations running since 2015 were concluded, the Council of the EU approved the text in July 2026, and Espot signed the agreement in Brussels on 19 October 2026, alongside San Marino — giving Andorra prospective access to the EU internal market for goods, services, capital, and people while remaining outside the EU and keeping border controls (European Commission audiovisual record, 19 October 2026; Government statements, October 2026). The agreement is not in force; signature is the step he pledged and controlled.
  • The minimum wage toward the European Social Charter benchmark — kept in motion. Successive rises at double inflation brought the wage to 58% of the average wage by 2026, approaching the 60% benchmark the government itself cites, from €1,050 a month in 2019 to €1,568.67 in July 2026 (Government statements, January 2026).
  • Decriminalising women who have abortions — delivered to parliament, late. Espot stated the reform would "certainly become a reality before the end of the legislative term" and that it would "very likely" be introduced in October; the cabinet approved the bill on 28 October 2026 and sent it to the General Council under urgent procedure (La Veu Lliure, 2026; Government statement, 28 October 2026). The promise as finally stated — a bill within the term — was kept; passage had not occurred at grading, and the bill's narrowness is graded in Tests 3 and 4.
  • Housing legislation — delivered, repeatedly. The laws of November 2023, March 2025, and June 2026 exist and do what their texts say (Tests 1 and 3). Whether they solved the crisis is an outcome question, recorded in the ledger below, not a word question.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The referendum's timing and binding form — the clearest word moved. The governing majority's shared 2023 election manifesto stated, in the wording quoted by its critics from the manifesto text: "During 2024, the ratification procedures on the part of Andorra and also on the part of the EU should take place, with a binding referendum in Andorra held beforehand" (2023 manifesto, as quoted in La Veu Lliure's referendum analysis, 2026). The record instead is: no referendum in 2024; signature first, on 19 October 2026; and a referendum now described by the government as consultative — albeit declared politically binding — to be called only after the European Parliament's consent, not held on the same day as the spring 2027 general election, with no date set at grading (Government statements, October–November 2026; Brussels Signal, 12 November 2026). Opposition figures dispute the government's claim that this sequencing is what was always said: Andorra Forward's Carine Montaner demanded the referendum as soon as possible, the Social Democrats demanded it be called now despite supporting the agreement, and a detailed opposition reading of the manifesto concludes the vote was pledged before ratification procedures on both sides (La Veu Lliure, 2026). The dispute is printed with its disputants named; the manifesto wording as quoted, the 2024 date, and the present consultative-after-consent sequencing are not themselves in dispute. Polling makes the delay consequential rather than technical: a spring 2026 survey by Andorra Recerca i Innovació (AR+I) found 46.4% would vote against the agreement and 33.5% in favour (AR+I survey via Brussels Signal, November 2026).
  • Pension reform — pledged sustainability, delivered deadlock. Reform of the CASS pension system was a standing commitment of the term; by 2026 parliamentary sources reported it increasingly unlikely to pass before the election, deadlocked over the second pillar, retirement age, and contributions, with pension-system leaders publicly frustrated at the lost time (La Veu Lliure and RTVA, 2026; Alto, 2026). Not yet formally abandoned; nowhere near kept.
  • Housing outcomes — the promise's substance receding even as its laws passed. The government targeted some 1,500 vacant units returned to rental over three years under the 2025 Omnibus Law; at grading the documented market figures show 117 rental listings nationwide in the second quarter of 2026 and a protest movement entering its fourth year with a conditional border blockade announced for December 2026 (Pisos.ad data, 2026; La Veu Lliure, November 2026). No official count of units actually recovered is in the record assembled here; the target is recorded as unverified, not as met.

The two axes, and the four crossings: Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded independently here, and all four crossings are named. (1) Kept his word AND it helped people: the minimum-wage formula — pledged, delivered above inflation, and raising the floor for the lowest-paid. (2) Kept his word AND it still hurt people: the housing laws were delivered as written, and their staged ending of rent controls from 2027 to 2030 will raise protected tenants' rents toward market levels — the keeping earns Test 5 credit; the hurt is recorded in Test 4's ledger in full. (3) Went against his word AND it helped people: not documented in the record assembled here — no instance was found of Espot breaking a stated pledge in a way that produced a documented benefit; it is recorded as not documented, not filled in. (4) Broke his word AND it hurt people: the referendum pledge — moved from a binding vote beforehand, in 2024, to a consultative vote after signature and after the European Parliament — hurt, in the first instance, the voters who were promised the earlier say, including supporters of the agreement such as the Social Democrats who demanded the vote be called now; whether the agreement itself helps or hurts Andorrans remains undecided, because the vote that would decide it has not been held.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because the kept shelf is substantial and dated — a decade of EU negotiation brought to signature, a wage floor raised on a stated formula for seven years, an abortion bill delivered in the month he last promised it — and the broken shelf contains the term's single most explicit dated pledge, the 2024 binding referendum held beforehand, which was not kept in its timing, its sequence, or its stated form. Pension reform's quiet slide into the next term confirms the pattern: Espot keeps words that run through his own executive action and loses words that require other people's consent on a schedule.

Overall Summary

Xavier Espot Zamora is a two-term head of government of a microstate who will leave office in 2027 because the constitution says so and who has not tried to change that. His record is a study in what a parliamentary executive with an absolute majority of 16 of 28 seats can and cannot do. Where one law was needed, he wrote general laws — taxing foreign property investment up to 10%, mobilising vacant homes, raising the minimum wage 45% since 2019 on a double-inflation formula (helping low-paid workers in every sector; hurting speculative buyers and landlords, openly, by law) — while the integrity safeguards at his own level, named by GRECO in July 2024, remain unbuilt: no pre-appointment integrity checks, unpublished asset declarations, unregulated lobbying (helping no one documentably; leaving every future complainant against a top official with an under-resourced prosecution). Where the wobble came, he answered it on housing with four laws in three years and on abortion with a seven-year negotiation that produced, on 28 October 2026, a bill decriminalising women while keeping practitioners punishable and care unfunded and abroad — the least-harm door in a constitutional trap he did not build (helping women out of criminal liability; hurting them still in travel and cost) — and he did not answer it on pensions at all, letting reform die of its political cost as the term expired (hurting future pensioners by deferral). His counting is generous in money and narrow in membership: wages, rent aid, and tenant protections reach residents broadly, while the noncitizen majority of the population still cannot vote, his 2026 nationality reform kept the twenty-year, single-passport door, and passive residents were written out of the rental law's protections. His word is kept where his own signature executes it — the EU association agreement signed in Brussels on 19 October 2026 after a decade of negotiation — and broken where the term's clearest dated pledge is concerned: a binding referendum held beforehand, in 2024, became a consultative referendum after signature, after the European Parliament, at no set date, with polls showing 46.4% against the agreement he signed. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the referendum is called and honoured, whether the abortion bill passes before the 2027 election, whether GRECO's 23 recommendations are implemented, and whether the December 2026 housing deadline produces the written commitments the protesters demanded or the border blockade they announced.
