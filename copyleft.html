How copyleft works — explained by the people who wrote it

Copyleft

Take it, use it, build on it — and whatever you make from it stays just as free.

We didn’t write a script for this. The video below does the explaining, in its makers’ own words: you own what you create — and a copyleft licence is how you give everyone permission to copy, share, and remix it, on one condition: the freedom travels with the work, every time it changes hands.

Law of this house — printed at the door

Everything made through this system is copyleft too — automatically

Made here? It’s copyleft. Everything built through these pages and tools carries the same freedom automatically — yours to use, everyone’s to build on, under the same terms. That’s the law of this house, printed at the door.

Anything built with these pages and tools — a page, a design, a build, a creation made through the house’s doors — carries copyleft by default, on the same terms as our own work: CC BY-SA for pages, designs, and writings, the GPL for code, and the CERN Open Hardware Licence for hardware blueprints. No extra step. Nothing to sign.

Why: the freedom can’t be captured — not even through our own tools. A door that lets people build closed things would be a capture machine with a free sign on it. So the door and the freedom are the same thing here, and you see the term before you build — a term of the house, never a trap.

Why copyleft at all?

The chain, asked all the way down. Why? → How? → Act.

Why share it?

Because work grows when other hands can build on it. A design one person can use helps one person; a design anyone can use, fix, and improve helps everyone it reaches.

Why not just give it away?

Because a plain gift can be captured. Put work in the public domain with no conditions and someone downstream can take it, change it a little, and lock their version up — the people after them get the work without the freedom. The GNU Project says it in their own words: middlemen can “strip off the freedom,” and the users who receive it “would not have freedom.”

Why does that matter?

A gift that can be captured stops being a gift. It becomes raw material for the next fence. The freedom has to be defended at exactly the point where it changes hands — that’s the only place it can be lost.

How?

Copyleft uses copyright itself — the creator’s own legal right — and points it the other way. The licence says: use it, modify it, redistribute it, but pass along the same freedom. The condition travels with the work, so no one in the chain can remove it.

Act

That’s the rule we build under, and the rule above: everything here is copyleft, and everything made through here is copyleft too — automatically. Take it, and build.

The three shelves we use

Copyleft is one idea with a licence fitted to each kind of work. Each shelf below is stated in its owner’s own words, with the door to them.

Pages · writings · designs

CC BY-SA 4.0

Creative Commons Attribution–ShareAlike. In Creative Commons’ own summary, you are free to:

“Share — copy and redistribute the material in any medium or format for any purpose, even commercially. Adapt — remix, transform, and build upon the material for any purpose, even commercially.”

Under these terms: give appropriate credit, link to the licence, say if you changed it — and if you build on it, distribute yours under the same licence. That last term, ShareAlike, is the copyleft condition.

Read CC BY-SA 4.0 at Creative Commons ↗

Code · software

The GPL

The GNU General Public License — the original copyleft licence, from the GNU Project and the Free Software Foundation:

“Copyleft is a general method for making a program (or other work) free, and requiring all modified and extended versions of the program to be free as well.”

And their reason, in one line: proprietary developers “use copyright to take away the users’ freedom; we use copyright to guarantee their freedom.” Distribute the code, and the source and the freedoms go with it.

Read the GPL at the GNU Project ↗

Hardware · blueprints

CERN Open Hardware Licence

CERN built this licence for physical things — the blueprints shelf. In CERN’s own words, it is:

“a legal tool to promote collaboration among hardware designers and support the freedom to use, study, modify, share and distribute hardware designs, and products based on those designs.”

Version 2 comes in three variants; the strongly reciprocal one, CERN-OHL-S, is the copyleft form: build and sell the physical thing freely — the design files stay free and travel with it.

Read the CERN OHL at CERN ↗

The one thing that is not granted: the name

The work is free for everybody. The name “The Thriving Plan” is not part of the grant — it stays ours.

Why, plainly: a name is how people tell the source from a copy. If anyone could put the name on their own version, a changed version could pass itself off as the source, and nobody could tell which record was the original. So: take the pages, the designs, the code, and the blueprints, build your own version, make it better — under your own name. The work travels. The name doesn’t.

How to copyleft your own work

Four steps. No lawyer, no fee, no account — this is the method the GNU Project itself describes.

The four steps−
  1. Keep your copyright. Copyleft doesn’t abandon copyright — it stands on it. You made it, it’s yours, and that ownership is the lever that makes the licence enforceable.
  2. Pick the shelf. Writing, pages, images, designs → CC BY-SA 4.0. Code → the GPL. Hardware designs → the CERN Open Hardware Licence.
  3. Attach the licence, whole. Include a copy of the licence text with the work and a notice naming it. The GNU Project’s instruction is plain: use the entire text of the licence you choose — partial copies are not permitted.
  4. Let the condition travel. Anyone who passes your work on — changed or unchanged — must pass the same freedoms with it. You don’t police the chain yourself; the terms are already attached, in the open, for every hand it passes through.
What people downstream may and may not do+
  • May: use it, copy it, change it, share it, build on it — and sell a physical thing built from it, or a service around it.
  • May not: lock it up. No version of it may be passed on with the freedoms removed, and no extra restrictions may be added on top.
  • Must: credit the source, link the licence, and mark what they changed — attribution travels with the work, just like the freedom does.

A derivative work — a new work built from an old one, like a remix or a product built from a blueprint — inherits the same terms. That inheritance is the whole mechanism: it is what “stays just as free” means in practice.
