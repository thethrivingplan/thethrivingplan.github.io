Why?

Why keep animals at all?

Hunting gives meat once. A living animal gives again — milk tomorrow, wool next season, eggs next week, strength for the plough, and young for next year. Why? Because a living storehouse walks, renews, and reproduces.

How?

How does it work?

Meet the animal’s needs fully — food, water, shelter, space, health, company — select for tameness and usefulness over generations, and the partnership holds. Skip a need and the whole design fails at that joint.

Act.

What do you do first?

Observe and help before owning. Learn one animal’s needs completely, check your local rules, and line up veterinary care before any animal arrives. See Start small.

Three layers

Layer 1 · What it is

Husbandry, plainly

The care, breeding, and raising of domesticated animals — animals whose breeding people guide, whose food and shelter people provide, and whose lives are intertwined with a household or community.

Layer 2 · What it means

A shift in the relationship

From taking animals out of the wild, one hunt at a time, to being responsible for animals across their whole lives — birth to old age. Power and duty arrive in the same package.

Layer 3 · What it could mean for you

A test for any keeping

Ask of any arrangement — a farm, a pet, a programme: does the animal benefit too? If the honest answer is no, the design is extraction, whatever it calls itself.

What it unlocked

In Civilization terms, this node is never just “+food.” It unlocks whole systems.

🥛 Milk, 🧶 wool, 🥚 eggs

Renewable harvests. Food and fibre taken without killing the animal — the first “income” instead of one-time loot. Documented

🐂 Traction — the plough’s engine

Oxen and horses turned animal muscle into farm power: deeper ploughing, more land worked, heavier loads hauled. Agriculture’s scale-up runs on this.

🐎 Transport

Riding and pack animals shrank distance — trade, messages, and migration all sped up wherever hooves could go.

💩 Manure

Dung returned fertility to fields. Animals ate what people couldn’t — grass, scraps, stubble — and handed back concentrated fertiliser.

The documented story — at honest size

Strong consensus

The dog came first — before farming

Dogs were domesticated from wolves before agriculture existed — hunter-gatherers did it. The exact date and place are still debated (estimates span roughly 15,000–30,000+ years ago), but the order is not: dog first, then — thousands of years later, in the early farming world — sheep, goats, pigs, and cattle, with chickens, horses, and others following in their own times and places.

How: generations of selection for tameness. Keep the calmest, least fearful young; breed from them; repeat. Dmitry Belyaev’s famous fox experiment (begun 1959) showed selection for tameness alone can bring along floppy ears, changed coats, and dog-like behaviour within decades — a documented demonstration of the mechanism, at lab scale.

Which animals could be domesticated — and which couldn’t?

The famous checklist comes from Jared Diamond’s Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997) — his “Anna Karenina principle”: a species needs every trait, and failing any one can block domestication. Honest grade: a popular framework, not a law. It organises real patterns well; later scholarship disputes parts of it, and real history is messier. Use it as a lens, not a verdict.

  • 1
    Diet — eats food people can supply cheaply; not a picky or competing carnivore.
  • 2
    Growth rate — grows fast enough to be worth feeding.
  • 3
    Breeds in captivity — will reproduce under human-managed conditions (cheetahs famously wouldn’t).
  • 4
    Temperament — not inclined to kill its keepers (a zebra bites and doesn’t let go).
  • 5
    Panic response — doesn’t smash itself to death against fences when startled (many antelope do).
  • 6
    Social structure — lives in herds with a dominance hierarchy a human can step into; solitary, territorial species resist.

Of the world’s large wild mammals, only a small set was ever fully domesticated — sheep, goats, pigs, cattle, horses, donkeys, camels, water buffalo, and a few more. That short list shaped which continents got traction, transport, and wool — which is why this framework, graded honestly, still earns its place on the sheet.

The working knowledge

An animal’s needs are the blueprint. Design to the needs, or don’t keep the animal.

🌿Food
Right diet, every day, in season and out
💧Water
Clean and always available
🏠Shelter
Dry, draught-free, safe from predators & weather
🌳Space
Room to move, graze, and be an animal
🩺Health
Prevention, prompt treatment, a vet who knows them
🐑Company
Of its own kind — herd animals alone are suffering
Breeding is selection — same rules as seed selection · Tap to open

Exactly as in the Agriculture sheet: keep and breed from the individuals with the traits you need (calm, healthy, hardy, good mothers), never breed from suffering or defects you’d be multiplying, and keep enough unrelated animals that inbreeding doesn’t quietly wreck the line. Selection is powerful precisely because it compounds — in both directions. Choose like it matters for generations, because it does.

Daily rhythm — what “care” actually looks like · Tap to open

Feeding and watering on a schedule the animals can rely on; checking every animal, every day, with your own eyes; cleaning shelter and rotating pasture before problems start; records — births, illness, treatments — written down, not remembered. Husbandry is mostly this: unglamorous, daily, and non-optional. An animal cannot take a day off from needing you.

The care standard, at full size: the animal’s benefit is part of the design.

Everybody Benefits includes the animals. Good food, real space, health care, company, a life shaped to the animal’s nature, and a humane end when the end comes — these aren’t extras or marketing. They are load-bearing. A keeping arrangement that only takes — milk, labour, young, profit — and returns the minimum it can get away with is leverage with hooves. This sheet does not blueprint that.

Honest negatives — printed, not hidden

Documented cost

Disease moves both ways

Living close to animals brought zoonoses — diseases that jump between animals and people. Measles and tuberculosis have roots in cattle diseases; influenza circulates through pigs and birds; many more are documented. Hygiene, quarantine of sick animals, and veterinary care are not bureaucracy — they are the price of the partnership, for both species.

Documented cost

It tied people down, too

Animals need feeding in winter, milking at dawn, watching at lambing — every day, in all weather, for life. Husbandry fed people and fenced their freedom at the same time. Herds also brought raiding, wealth gaps (a herd is stealable, countable wealth), and pressure on grazing land. The node unlocks power; it was never free of cost.

Starter practice — the smallest real version

Honestly scaled. Living animals are not a hobby project.

  1. Local rules outrank any page. Zoning, permits, and animal-welfare law where you live govern real keeping — check them first.
  2. The animal’s needs outrank your plans. If you can’t meet all six needs, every day, for the animal’s whole life — the honest move is not to start.
  3. Vet before animal. Know who will treat them, and what it costs, before anything arrives.
