The Ruler's Test — John Thune, Majority Leader of the United States Senate

Subject: John Randolph Thune (R-S.D.) — Majority Leader of the United States Senate since 3 January 2025. The seat graded is the Senate majority leadership itself, and the framing is stated plainly: the Majority Leader holds no vote beyond his own and commands no agency. What the office actually holds is the Senate's throttle — the right of first recognition on the floor, control of the floor schedule and the calendar of business, the power to decide which bills, amendments, and nominations are taken up and when, leverage over cloture filings and the filling of the amendment tree, a leading seat in every negotiation with the House, the White House, and the minority, and, through his conference, the chairmanships of every committee. He is graded on his exercise of those powers: agenda control, floor scheduling, recognition priority, amendment and cloture leverage, and his committee and negotiation position. What the office cannot do is equally part of the record: he cannot pass legislation alone (most legislation still needs 60 votes to end debate), cannot compel the House, cannot sign or veto anything, cannot remove the Senate parliamentarian's advice by wishing it away without a majority vote to overrule it, and cannot bind a future Congress. His wider career — senator from South Dakota since 2005, Republican whip and conference chair before that — is context only, except where it bears directly on this office. The premise was verified first, because it changed this month: Thune still holds the seat in November 2026. Senate Republicans won a 53–47 majority in November 2024 and elected him leader on 13 November 2024, defeating Sen. John Cornyn 29–24 on the second ballot after Sen. Rick Scott was eliminated on the first; he took office with the 119th Congress on 3 January 2025, succeeding Sen. Mitch McConnell (leadership election reporting, November 2024). In the midterm elections of 3 November 2026, Democrats won the Senate majority for the 120th Congress — flipping at least seven Republican-held seats on election night, with early counts standing at 54 Democratic to 44 Republican seats while Alaska and Mississippi were uncalled (Associated Press calls via WSJ live coverage, 3–4 November 2026; post-election report, 6 November 2026), and the count reaching a projected 56 Democratic-caucus seats to 44 Republican after Democrat Mary Peltola's Alaska victory was called on 17 November 2026 and the Mississippi flip was recorded (AP call reported 17–18 November 2026; post-midterm analyses, November 2026) — the two figures are printed separately because they are counts taken at different stages of the same count, not blended. The change of hands takes effect only when the 120th Congress convenes on 3 January 2027. On 10 November 2026, Senate Republicans re-elected Thune as their conference leader by acclamation, unopposed, after voting 31–15 against delaying the election — meaning he will become Minority Leader in January, and his tenure as Majority Leader ends, by the voters' decision and on schedule, on 3 January 2027 (AP, 10 November 2026; Reuters, 10 November 2026). He is therefore graded on a substantially complete tenure: 3 January 2025 to the grading date, with the final lame-duck weeks still running and recorded as open where they are open. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test, printed as it stands on the Test card: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one: carrying out the 2024 Republican platform is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for HOW it was done — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), or counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The 60-vote rule was applied to his own side's top priority, at his own president's demand. The SAVE America Act — the Trump administration's flagship election bill, requiring documentary proof of citizenship to register and photo ID to vote — passed the House on 11 February 2026 (218–213) but could not clear 60 votes in the Senate. Thune filed the cloture motions himself and let the votes fail on the record (Senate cloture record on S. 1383: 49–41 on 21 March 2026 and 53–47 on 26 March 2026), refused throughout 2026 to eliminate the legislative filibuster or convert it to a talking filibuster to pass the bill, and said on the record why: "It's about the votes. It's about the math… I'm the one who has to be the clear-eyed realist about what we can achieve here" (NPR reporting, June 2026; AP reporting, November 2026). The same threshold that blocked Democratic bills in his Senate blocked his own party's — one procedural law, applied inward.
  • The referee's rulings against his own side were accepted where accepting them cost him. In May 2026, Senate Parliamentarian Elizabeth MacDonough ruled that the proposal to fund the White House ballroom project (some $1 billion for the East Wing Modernization Project) could not ride the 2026 reconciliation bill under the Byrd rule; President Trump pressed Thune to remove her, and Thune declined, publicly: "We're going through a process that we go through every time we have a reconciliation bill and the people on both sides are mad at the parliamentarian" (reporting of the May 2026 ruling and Thune's refusal, May 2026). The ballroom money was then dropped from the package: the Secure America Act as passed in June 2026 differed from the May draft precisely in that the $1 billion was gone (AP reporting, June 2026). The parliamentarian likewise signaled that the SAVE Act's citizenship and voter-ID mandates were Byrd-rule violations in reconciliation, and the mandates were not forced through by that route (parliamentarian guidance as reported, autumn 2026).
  • The minority was given a real floor vote on its own bill, on a date certain, because he had promised it — even though his conference opposed it nearly unanimously. The December 2025 health-care votes (Test 5) were scheduled by Thune under the shutdown-ending deal: the Democratic three-year extension of the enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies was brought up as written by the minority and voted on 11 December 2025 (AP, 11 December 2025). A majority leader who writes one law for scheduling does not usually hand the minority a prime-time vote on the minority's own text; the deal's terms were applied as written to both parties' bills, side by side, on the same day.
  • Bipartisan bills were scheduled and passed under the same rules as partisan ones when they had the votes: the GENIUS Act stablecoin framework passed the Senate 68–30 in June 2025 and was signed 18 July 2025; the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act passed the Senate 89–10 on 12 March 2026 and again 85–5 on 22 June 2026 before becoming law on 11 July 2026 without the president's signature (Senate roll calls; legislative history of H.R. 6644). His appropriations process (Test 5) moved bills through committee for both parties' members rather than by leadership-drafted omnibus.

Evidence against:

  • The decisive vote on his signature bill was bought with state-specific relief — a favorite at the source, in the oldest Senate form. The One Big Beautiful Bill (H.R. 1) passed the Senate 51–50 on 1 July 2025 only with Vice President Vance's tie-break, after Thune's leadership focused its final hours on Sen. Lisa Murkowski of Alaska, the decisive vote. The documented price: the bill's rural-hospital relief fund was doubled from $25 billion to $50 billion, and Alaska received increased relief from the bill's new state cost-sharing requirements for SNAP, including lower work-requirement exposure and a postponed date for Alaska to begin contributing toward SNAP benefits; a parallel Alaska Medicaid carve-out was struck down by the parliamentarian and did not survive (Washington Examiner reconstruction, July 2025; pediatric-policy and Senate reporting on the final text, July 2025). Relief written for one senator's state, to purchase one senator's vote, on a bill whose burdens fell nationally, is the second-law pattern in its classic shape — even though every element of it was legal, disclosed, and voted in the open.
  • Where the referee ruled against a smaller Republican priority, the referee was overruled rather than obeyed — the one-law shelf cannot hold both this and the ballroom. On 21–22 May 2025, after both the Government Accountability Office and the parliamentarian had determined that California's Clean Air Act waivers are not "rules" subject to the Congressional Review Act, Thune set up the procedural sequence by which the Senate voted 51–46 on a point of order to treat the waivers as CRA-eligible anyway, then voted 51–44 to revoke them (Senate votes, 22 May 2025; Sen. Durbin's statement of 22 May 2025; NPR reporting, May 2025). The ruling class that was honored in May 2026 was set aside in May 2025, and the difference between the two cases is the size of the prize, not the principle.
  • His agenda control was exercised as a one-way door on the minority's priorities outside the deals he chose to make. The House-passed CLARITY Act (digital-asset market structure), which cleared the House 294–134 with more than 70 Democratic votes on 17 July 2025 and the Senate Banking Committee 15–9 on 14 May 2026, received no Senate floor vote in the year after House passage (crypto-policy reporting, July 2026). Scheduling silence is the majority leader's least accountable power: no vote is taken, no record is made, and the same leader who scheduled dozens of Congressional Review Act resolutions (25 passed, per his conference's own count) and repeated failed cloture votes on his party's bills found no floor time for a bipartisan House bill his conference had not prioritized (Senate Republican Leader's office summary, November 2026; committee records, May 2026).

Why this grade: The strongest one-law evidence is real and unusual — a majority leader who let his own president's top bill die on the same 60-vote rule that bound the minority, and who kept a parliamentarian the president wanted fired. The strongest against-evidence is equally concrete and sits at the source of his biggest win: Alaska-specific relief traded for the decisive vote, and a referee overruled when the prize was California's waivers rather than a ballroom. One law applied to procedure; favorites applied to substance. That is MIXED, not HOLDS.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — voters and election administrators in states that opposed the SAVE America Act's documentation mandates, who kept the existing registration rules because the 60-vote law was applied inward; the Senate as an institution, whose referee survived a presidential removal demand. Hurt — Californians and the residents of the states that follow California's vehicle standards, whose waivers were revoked by a procedure both referees had said was unavailable; taxpayers and SNAP recipients in the other 49 states, who received no equivalent of the Alaska relief written into H.R. 1; supporters of the CLARITY Act in both parties, whose bill was counted out of the schedule without a vote.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The legislative filibuster — the Senate's central internal check on any majority, including his own — was preserved against sustained presidential pressure, twice in the open. On 30 October 2025, on the 30th day of the government shutdown, President Trump publicly demanded the Senate "go nuclear" and end the filibuster to reopen the government; Thune refused, warning that Democrats would use the same power to pack the Supreme Court and admit new states, and the shutdown was eventually ended the old way, with a 60-vote coalition (accounts of the October–November 2025 confrontation; Senate vote of 10 November 2025, 60–40). Trump renewed the demand on 12 November 2026, after the midterms; Thune's conference again declined (reporting, 12 November 2026). This was the pledge of his first day in the office — "preserving the legislative filibuster" was named a priority in his opening remarks of 3 January 2025 — kept under the heaviest pressure a president of his own party can apply (Thune's first remarks as Majority Leader, 3 January 2025, Senate Republican Leader's office).
  • The parliamentarian kept her job and her authority in the case where it cost him most (Test 1): Trump's May 2026 call for MacDonough's removal was refused, and her Byrd-rule rulings stripped provisions from his own party's reconciliation bills in both 2025 and 2026 — including, in 2025, Medicaid and health provisions and the Alaska Medicaid carve-out — and the stripped provisions stayed stripped (parliamentarian rulings as reported, June 2025 and May 2026).
  • Committees, not the leader's office, wrote the money bills. Thune's stated and substantially executed appropriations method returned the lead to the Appropriations Committee: the Senate passed 11 of the 12 fiscal-year 2026 appropriations bills individually, the first three before the August 2025 recess by bipartisan margins — "something that hadn't been done here since 2018," in his words — rather than governing by leadership-written omnibus (Thune floor remarks on FY2026 appropriations, 2026, thune.senate.gov). The judiciary was stocked by the ordinary route: by his own count on 16 November 2026, 57 Article III judges had been confirmed in the Congress with floor votes locked in for every nominee the Judiciary Committee had reported — committee gate first, floor second (Thune floor remarks, 16 November 2026). No documented instance appears in the record assembled here of Thune punishing a court, an election administrator, a news outlet, a prosecutor, or the Federal Reserve for an adverse decision; the referees outside the Senate are simply not in his grip's reach in the record, and that absence is recorded as an absence, not as a virtue performed.

Evidence against:

  • Twice, his majority changed the Senate's rules by simple-majority precedent — the "nuclear option" — when the ordinary rules blocked his conference's business, and a precedent set by 51 votes is a grip that outlives the hand that set it. First, the May 2025 CRA point of order (Test 1) overruled the parliamentarian and the GAO together, which Sen. Durbin's statement of 22 May 2025 described as "eliminating longstanding guardrails and paving the way for a future Senate majority to overrule the Parliamentarian to achieve its partisan goals" — an opponent's characterization, recorded as such, but the underlying votes are Senate record. Second, on 11 September 2025, after Thune's en-bloc nominations resolution failed cloture 52–47, Republicans voted 53–45 to overrule the chair and establish that a list of executive-branch nominees may be considered together by simple majority; a second overruling followed on 17 September 2025, 52–47. By 7 August 2026, five such lists had confirmed 375 nominations in single roll calls (Senate roll-call records as compiled in the confirmations record through September 2026). The change was scoped — it does not reach Cabinet secretaries or Article III judges, who still receive individual votes — and it answered a real blockade: by Thune's account, Democrats had allowed none of President Trump's civilian nominees through by unanimous consent or voice vote by early September 2025, and package talks had collapsed on 2 August 2025 (Thune statements, September 2025; Roll Call reporting, August–September 2025). Scoped and provoked, it is still a rule rewritten by the majority for the majority's convenience, in a tenure that began with a promise that the Senate would stay the Senate.
  • The current-policy baseline moved a referee's question into the majority's own hands. To make the 2017 tax cuts permanent inside reconciliation, Senate Republicans measured the bill against a "current policy" baseline — treating the expiring cuts as if they already continued, so their extension scored as costing nothing — a determination made through the Budget Committee chairman's authority rather than by overruling the parliamentarian outright, after she gave an initial green light to proceed under the Budget Act (Deloitte Tax News & Views, March–April 2025; reporting on the parliamentarian's initial ruling, April 2025). The CBO's published arithmetic did not change — CBO still estimated the enacted law's deficit effect in the trillions (Test 4) — but the official yardstick by which his own bill was judged compliant was chosen by the side being measured. That is a soft grip: no referee removed, the measurement relocated.

Why this grade: The headline check — the legislative filibuster — survived two direct presidential assaults, and the parliamentarian survived a firing demand; those are the largest without-gripping facts available to any majority leader of this era. Against them stand two hard precedents set by simple majority (May 2025, September 2025) and one yardstick relocated (the baseline). He did not grip the referees outside the chamber, and he gripped the chamber's own rules at the edges while defending them at the center. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — future Senate minorities of both parties, who inherit an intact 60-vote legislative threshold because he refused to break it in October 2025 and November 2026; the parliamentarian's office, which kept its holder and most of its authority; nominees' opponents in one narrow sense — Cabinet and judicial nominees still face individual votes. Hurt — the Senate's precedent structure itself: any future majority now holds two Thune-era templates (the CRA point of order, en-bloc confirmations) for overruling referees by 51 votes; Democratic senators and the voters who sent them, whose individual-nomination leverage — a century-old minority tool — was abolished for sub-Cabinet nominees by party-line vote; states and regulated industries planning around California's waivers, whose governing rules were changed by a procedure both referees had ruled out.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The voters' pushback — the largest of his tenure — was answered in plain words within a week, without denial. After the 3 November 2026 midterms cost his party at least seven Senate seats and the majority, Thune told reporters: "We're not going to be running this place next year, the Democrats are" (AP, 10 November 2026). He stood for re-election as conference leader on schedule rather than maneuvering to avoid the verdict, faced a delay movement led by Sen. Ron Johnson and seven colleagues, won the procedural vote 31–15 and then re-election by acclamation, promised his conference the majority would "make the most of the time we've got" in its final weeks, called incoming Democratic leader Chuck Schumer to discuss working together, and prioritized confirming judicial nominees before the window closed (AP and WSJ reporting, 10 November 2026). Inside his own conference, the dissent was heard in three separate meetings on 10 November before the vote was taken (AP, 10 November 2026).
  • When another option became available on the SAVE Act, he considered it in the open and declined it with stated reasons — the exact conduct the Test's other-option rule credits. Senators of his own party, Sen. Mike Lee among them, pressed the "talking filibuster" option through 2026; Thune addressed it publicly and repeatedly — in February 2026, that there were "not even close to the votes, not even close, to nuking the filibuster," and in June 2026, that his job was to be "the clear-eyed realist about what we can achieve here" — a stated reason (the votes did not exist), given on the record, not a refusal to look (reporting, February and June 2026). The June 2026 roll calls partially corroborate the math: SAVE Act cloture attempts drew 49 and 53 votes, short of even a simple-majority-plus-margin cushion for a rules fight (Senate cloture record, March 2026).
  • The 2025 shutdown ended through an adjustment he negotiated rather than a surrender he denied needing. For 38 days Thune held the position that health-care negotiations would not happen while the government was closed; on the 38th day, after Republicans rejected a Democratic offer, a group of Democratic moderates — Sens. Jeanne Shaheen, Maggie Hassan, Angus King, and Tim Kaine — met with Thune directly, and the deal that emerged (a clean continuing resolution, three full-year appropriations bills, reversal of the administration's shutdown-period mass firings with protections against further reductions in force through January, and his promise of a December vote on the Democratic health-care bill) was advanced by eight Democrats and passed the Senate 60–40 on 10 November 2025 (CNN reconstruction of the negotiations, November 2025; Thune floor remarks, November 2025). The leader who had said "no negotiations under hostage conditions" negotiated — with the rank-and-file of the other party, over his counterpart's position — and said on the floor what the deal contained.
  • Pushback from inside his own bill was answered with amendment, not denial: when the parliamentarian struck the Alaska Medicaid carve-out and rural hospitals became the sticking point in H.R. 1, the bill was changed (the rural fund doubled) rather than the ruling defied (Test 1). When the FY2026 Homeland Security appropriations bill — the twelfth — met Democratic requests for more time after a bipartisan agreement including ICE de-escalation training and body-camera funding, Thune agreed publicly to the additional time while stating his condition that agent safety be part of the conversation (Thune floor remarks on FY2026 appropriations, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The shutdown's length is itself the against-shelf's centerpiece: 43 days, the longest in American history, at a cost of about $11 billion (CBO-based estimates as reported), with SNAP benefits at risk for some 40 million people and federal staff working unpaid — while the leader's public position for most of it was refusal. On 7 November 2025, day 38, Schumer offered simultaneous votes to reopen the government and extend the health-care subsidies for a year, with a bipartisan committee on ACA changes; Thune "quickly swatted down" the offer as a "nonstarter" and restated that nothing would be negotiated until the government reopened (AP reporting, 7 November 2025). If another option became available that day, the public record shows it declined within hours, with a slogan rather than the vote-math reasoning he gave on the filibuster. The deal he accepted two days later was built by moderates, not offered by him — the adjustment came, but it came to him.
  • The December 2025 health-care votes he scheduled were an answer in form more than in substance: both bills were known in advance to fail at 51–48, "Republicans and Democrats never engaged in meaningful or high-level negotiations on a solution" in the month between the promise and the vote (AP, 11 December 2025), and when the House later passed a subsidy extension by discharge petition, Thune said there was "no appetite" for another Senate vote and the bill stalled in his chamber (reporting, January 2026). The wobble — premiums set to rise an average of roughly 114%, per KFF's estimate — was acknowledged, voted on, and left where it stood.
  • On the no-good-choice mechanics, the shutdown deserves the clause's protection in part, and the record is printed so the reader can see the boundary. The options actually open to Thune in October 2025 were: (1) eliminate the filibuster, as the president demanded — breaking his first-day pledge and permanently changing the Senate to win a funding fight; (2) negotiate the subsidies under shutdown conditions — conceding the minority's central demand under the pressure his conference had publicly sworn not to reward; or (3) hold the clean continuing resolution and wait for the minority's coalition to crack — the door he took, at the price of 43 days of closure. Each carried real harm; hindsight options (an earlier moderates' deal on day 10, say) are not documented as having been available, since the moderates' channel only produced a deal on day 38. What remains gradable, and is graded above, is the origin and the honesty: the trap was partly of his conference's making (the clean-CR-or-nothing posture was chosen, not imposed), and his public framing throughout — "the Schumer Shutdown" — assigned the whole of the blame to one side of a two-sided standoff, which is less than the Test's openness standard even where the underlying choice was defensible.

Why this grade: He answers the biggest wobbles in plain words — the midterm verdict was owned in a sentence, the filibuster options were considered and declined with reasons stated, and the shutdown did end in a negotiated deal he personally closed with the other party's moderates. But the shutdown also shows the failure mode: 38 days of refusal, a same-day "nonstarter" for the one leadership-level offer made to him, and a promised health-care vote that was held as a messaging exercise with no negotiation behind it. Adjustment, when it came, was real; it was also late, and twice it had to be brought to him. MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — federal employees and SNAP recipients, whose pay, jobs, and benefits were restored by the November 2025 deal, including the reversal of shutdown-period mass firings that the moderates' channel with Thune produced; the incoming Democratic majority, handed a public commitment to an orderly transfer and a working call from the outgoing leader. Hurt — the same federal employees, SNAP recipients (some 40 million people, per Thune's own floor remarks of 6 November 2025), air travelers, and unpaid staff during the 43 days the standoff ran; ACA marketplace enrollees, whose subsidy question was voted on 11 December 2025 and then left unanswered as premiums rose; his own conference's dissenters, whose request for time to reckon with the midterm losses was voted down 31–15 on the day it was made.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the minority, the states that voted the other way, the poor, and those who did not elect his majority — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The bipartisan third of his agenda counted everyone in the room by construction. The ROAD to Housing Act passed 89–10 and 85–5 (Test 1); the GENIUS Act passed 68–30; the Laken Riley Act and the HALT Fentanyl Act moved with Democratic votes; and his appropriations process passed 11 of 12 FY2026 bills through a committee where the minority sits, the first three by "robust bipartisan margins" (Thune floor remarks, 2025–2026). The shutdown deal itself counted the minority's people: it funded SNAP and WIC for the full fiscal year, funded veterans' programs for the full year, and reversed firings of federal workers — provisions Thune announced on the floor as the deal's merits (Thune floor remarks, November 2025).
  • Rural America — a constituency counted out of many national bills — was counted in, visibly: the rural-hospital fund in H.R. 1 was doubled to $50 billion when rural providers' exposure to the Medicaid changes became the bill's pressure point (Test 1). The same mechanism that fails Test 1 (it was traded for a vote) is recorded here for what it also was: money directed to the hospitals least able to absorb the bill's cuts, in states of both parties.
  • The minority's voters were given their day on the floor on the question they had shut the government down over: the December 2025 side-by-side votes (Tests 1 and 3) put the Democratic subsidy bill to a vote under a Republican majority — four Republicans (Sens. Collins, Hawley, Murkowski, and Sullivan, per AP's count; a separate account of the same vote names Sen. Rand Paul in place of Hawley — the two accounts are printed as they stand, not reconciled) crossed to support it, and the vote's 51–48 failure was a counted defeat, not an uncounted one (AP, 11 December 2025; Cobb Courier account of the same votes, 11 December 2025 — the discrepancy in the fourth Republican name is noted because the record assembled here contains both).

Evidence against:

  • The fiscal center of his tenure counted the poor out of the ledger to pay the top of it. The Congressional Budget Office estimated the Senate version of H.R. 1 would leave 11.8 million more people uninsured in 2034 (CBO analysis of the Senate bill, 29 June 2025) — printed separately from CBO's estimate for the broader package of House bill plus subsidy expiration, which ran to 16–17 million more uninsured in 2034 in CBO's June 2025 letters as analyzed by KFF, because the two figures measure different things and are not blended here. The law cut federal Medicaid support by roughly $930 billion–$1 trillion over ten years and SNAP by roughly $186 billion (CBO-based summaries), imposed the first national Medicaid work requirement (80 hours per month) in the program's history, and added some $3.4 trillion to deficits over ten years by CBO's final estimate — $4.1 trillion including interest, per the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget's summary of CBO's score — while CBO's distributional analysis showed the lowest-income households losing resources (an average of roughly $1,600 a year in CBO's estimate as reported) as higher-income households gained. The mandate rule protects the platform, not the incidence: extending the 2017 tax cuts is what his voters elected (Test 5, FOR shelf); choosing to offset them against Medicaid and SNAP rather than elsewhere is the HOW, and the HOW is what this test grades.
  • ACA marketplace enrollees were counted as a talking point and not as a constituency: the enhanced subsidies expired on 1 January 2026 after the December votes failed, with KFF estimating average premium payments rising roughly 114% (an extra $1,000–$1,500 a year for a typical family), and the House-passed extension was left unscheduled in his Senate (Tests 1 and 3; KFF estimate as reported, December 2025).
  • Non-citizens and the communities enforcement touches were counted out of the second reconciliation bill entirely. The Secure America Act (S. 2), passed 52–47 on 4–5 June 2026 and signed 10 June 2026 as Public Law 119-98, appropriated roughly $70 billion for immigration enforcement through September 2029 — about $38.5 billion for ICE and $22.6–26 billion for CBP in the accounts assembled here (figures printed as reported by separate outlets, not blended) — with, in AP's words, "no limits on ICE" and no new guardrails on federal immigration agents; Democratic amendments on DACA adjudication, detention-facility inspection, and the Immigration Detention Ombudsman were each voted down in the vote-a-rama, and none was adopted (Congressional Record Daily Digest, 4 June 2026; AP reporting, June 2026). Thune's stated reason — that Democrats had "walked away" from negotiations that could have placed restrictions on agents (AP, June 2026) — is recorded; the outcome is that the counting was done by one party alone. The parliamentarian's removal of the ballroom money (Test 1) is the one documented instance of the process counting a presidential priority out.
  • California and the states following its standards were counted out by procedure as well as by policy (Test 1): a half-century-old statutory waiver arrangement, exercised 131 times since 1968 per the Congressional Record debate, was ended for vehicle emissions by a 51-vote procedural ruling both referees had said was unavailable (Congressional Record, 22 May 2025).

Why this grade: The process half of his tenure — appropriations, housing, stablecoins, the shutdown deal's full-year SNAP and veterans' funding — genuinely counted the whole chamber and the people in its bills. The fiscal half — the reconciliation agenda that is his tenure's center of gravity — distributed its costs downward onto Medicaid enrollees, SNAP recipients, and marketplace buyers, and its enforcement money outward with no guardrails negotiated with the other party. A leader who counts everyone in the small bills and counts the poor out of the big one is MIXED, with the hurt side of this ledger the heaviest in the dossier.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — taxpayers who kept the 2017 rates (the platform his voters chose; the gain concentrated at higher incomes per CBO's distribution tables); rural hospitals and their patients, via the $50 billion fund; homebuyers and renters addressed by the housing act; veterans, and SNAP and WIC recipients, whose full-year funding the shutdown deal secured; the stablecoin sector and its customers, given a federal framework. Hurt — the 11.8 million people CBO projected to lose insurance by 2034 under the Senate bill (separate figure: 16–17 million under the broader House-plus-expiration accounting); SNAP recipients facing new work rules and state cost-shifts; ACA enrollees facing roughly doubled premium payments in 2026; immigrants and mixed-status families in the path of a $70 billion enforcement appropriation passed without negotiated guardrails, and DACA recipients whose adjudication amendments were rejected; Californians and Section 177-state residents whose vehicle standards were revoked.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 3 January 2025, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The filibuster pledge — kept exactly, under maximum pressure. First-day promise, 3 January 2025: preserving the legislative filibuster named as a leadership priority (Thune's first remarks, 3 January 2025). Tested 30 October 2025 (presidential demand during the shutdown), through the 2026 SAVE Act fight (his own party's base and president), and again 12 November 2026 — refused every time, in public, with reasons (Tests 2 and 3). This is the clearest kept word in the file, and it is crossing 1 in part and crossing 2 in part, named as the addendum requires: kept, AND it helped people (the minority of the moment, and every future minority); kept, AND it hurt people his own voters wanted helped (SAVE Act supporters, whose bill the kept word killed). Both crossings are recorded; neither launders the other.
  • The platform — carried out, which the mandate rule places on this shelf. Senate Republicans in 2024 ran on extending the 2017 tax cuts, border and immigration enforcement funding, deregulation, and confirming the president's nominees. The record: H.R. 1 signed 4 July 2025 making the tax cuts permanent (51–50, Senate, 1 July 2025); a $9 billion rescissions package signed in July 2025, the first enacted in some 30 years by his conference's account; 25 CRA disapproval resolutions passed; the Secure America Act's roughly $70 billion enforcement appropriation signed 10 June 2026; the Cabinet confirmed "at the fastest pace in over two decades" and, by his counts on the floor, 605 civilian nominees confirmed as of his November 2026 remarks (a later floor speech the same month says 606 — his figures, printed with their dates, not blended) and 56 Article III judges as of 16 November 2026, with 57 confirmed by 12 November per the Senate's own vote record as reported — the small discrepancies among counts taken on different days are left as they stand (Thune floor remarks, November 2026; Senate Periodical Press Gallery record, 12 November 2026; Senate Republican Leader's office summary, November 2026). Crossing 2 applies to the largest item and is named: the tax platform was kept, AND its Medicaid and SNAP offsets hurt people (Test 4's ledger stands in full). The keeping earns the credit here; the hurt is not erased by it.
  • The regular-order pledge — substantially kept. Opening promise: "empowering committees, restoring regular order" (3 January 2025). Delivered in appropriations: 11 of 12 FY2026 bills passed individually through committee and floor, the first three before August 2025 for the first time since 2018, the work interrupted by the 43-day shutdown and finished in early 2026 (Thune floor remarks, 2025–2026). The twelfth (Homeland Security) was delayed at the minority's request with his stated condition — a delay he announced rather than concealed.
  • The shutdown-deal promise — kept to the letter. Promise, November 2025: a Senate vote on the Democratic health-care proposal "no later than the second week in December" (Thune floor remarks, November 2025). Kept: the vote was held 11 December 2025, on the Democrats' own bill, alongside a Republican alternative he also brought forward under pressure from his moderates (AP, 11 December 2025). He had promised a vote, not a result, and said so at the time — the word as given was the word as kept. What the keeping did not include — negotiation toward a passable bill — is graded in Test 3, not double-counted here.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "The Senate stays the Senate" — kept at the center, broken at the edges by his own hand. The same opening speech that pledged the filibuster pledged a Senate of "discussion and deliberation," extended debate, and all members heard. The two simple-majority precedents of his tenure — the May 2025 CRA point of order overruling the parliamentarian and GAO, and the September 2025 en-bloc nominations precedent overruling the chair — were each set by his majority, on his schedule, to get past rules that were working as the deliberative design intended (Tests 1 and 2). A crossing is named here as well: the September 2025 change went against the spirit of the opening word, AND it helped people — the nominees confirmed under it (375 by August 2026) include the ordinary sub-Cabinet officers whose absence leaves agencies leaderless; the help is recorded in full, and the break is recorded in full, and neither cancels the other.
  • The open-amendment promise — honored in appropriations, abandoned in the bills that mattered most. Both reconciliation bills — the vehicles of his tenure — moved under reconciliation's closed structure: H.R. 1's text was assembled in committee and leadership negotiation and passed after a vote-a-rama in which amendment votes are rapid-fire and largely symbolic, and S. 2's vote-a-rama adopted no amendments at all (Congressional Record, 4 June 2026; AP, June 2026). On ordinary legislation his floor was more open than his predecessor's by reputation and by the appropriations record; on the two largest bills of the Congress, the amendment process his first speech promised was the process reconciliation exists to bypass. The word was kept where it was cheap and set aside where it was dear — recorded as a partial break, not a total one.
  • Words he did not give are not graded as broken. Thune never promised the SAVE Act would pass — he promised effort and votes, filed the cloture motions, and reported the math honestly — so its failure is graded in Tests 1–3 as an exercise of power, not here as a broken word; the record on this point is stated because his critics' ALLEGATION-level framing ("he promised and failed") is not documented in the record assembled here. Likewise his 10 November 2026 promise to "make the most of the time we've got" in the lame duck — with a conference resolution committing to confirm nominees, pass core priorities, and attempt a further reconciliation bill before 3 January, and his 16 November floor commitment to confirm 100-plus additional nominees — is in motion and not yet gradable; funding for the government expires 11 December 2026, and whether the lame-duck words are kept will be written by December's record, not by this dossier. It is recorded as open, not documented as kept or broken.

Why this grade: His most specific procedural word — the filibuster — was kept without exception under the strongest pressure available, and his platform word was kept at a scale few majority leaders match: the tax law, the enforcement money, the nominees, the appropriations return. Against that, the deliberative half of his opening word was set aside at the two moments it constrained him (May and September 2025), and the open process he promised governed the small bills while reconciliation governed the large ones. A word kept on the platform and the filibuster, and bent on deliberation, is MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — everyone who relies on a Senate where 60 votes still matter, of both parties, present and future; the voters of the 2024 Republican majority, whose platform was in fact delivered (crossing 1 where the delivery helped them, crossing 2 where its offsets hurt others — both recorded); agencies and litigants who received confirmed officers and judges at the fastest pace in decades. Hurt — Medicaid and SNAP beneficiaries under the kept platform's offsets (Test 4's figures stand); SAVE Act supporters, hurt precisely by the kept filibuster word (crossing 2); senators of both parties whose deliberative tools — individual nomination consideration, the parliamentarian's CRA rulings — were narrowed by the bent half of the word.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

John Thune's two years as Majority Leader are a study in a throttle used hard in one direction and deliberately not used in another. The agenda powers of the office were exercised at full stretch: two reconciliation bills carrying the whole of his party's fiscal and enforcement program past the 60-vote wall by 51–50 and 52–47, 25 regulatory disapprovals, a rescissions package, hundreds of nominees — 375 of them under a new en-bloc precedent his majority created in September 2025 after overruling the chair — and, by his own November 2026 count, 605–606 civilian confirmations and 56–57 judges. And the same hand refused, three times in the open and once against a president of his own party demanding it during a shutdown, to break the legislative filibuster — the one act that would have made every other act easier — and refused to fire the parliamentarian who kept striking his own party's provisions out of its bills. One law lands MIXED because that procedural even-handedness (the SAVE Act died on the same rule that bound Democrats; MacDonough kept her job) coexists with substance-level favorites: Alaska relief doubled and targeted to buy the decisive H.R. 1 vote, and a referee overruled in May 2025 when the prize was California's waivers rather than a ballroom. Without gripping lands MIXED because the center held — filibuster and parliamentarian intact — while the edges were gripped: two simple-majority precedents and a budget baseline relocated into the majority's own committee. The wobble lands MIXED because the largest pushbacks were answered in plain words (the midterm verdict owned in a sentence on 10 November 2026; the talking-filibuster option considered and declined with the vote-math stated) while the 43-day shutdown showed 38 days of refusal, a leadership offer dismissed as a "nonstarter" within hours, and an eventual deal that moderates had to bring to him — a no-good-choice situation in part, graded with its origin (a posture his conference chose) left on the record. Counts everyone lands MIXED, with the heaviest hurt ledger in the file: the bipartisan third of his agenda (housing 89–10, stablecoins 68–30, 11 of 12 appropriations bills, full-year SNAP and veterans' funding in the shutdown deal) counted the whole chamber, while the reconciliation center counted 11.8 million projected uninsured (CBO, Senate bill; 16–17 million on the broader accounting, printed separately), SNAP's new work rules, doubled ACA premiums after the subsidies he would not save expired, and a $70 billion enforcement appropriation passed with no negotiated guardrails and every DACA amendment rejected. His word lands MIXED because the two halves of his 3 January 2025 opening speech had different fates: the filibuster pledge and the platform were kept completely — the platform's keeping recorded with its hurt in full, per the two-axes rule — while the deliberation pledge was bent at exactly the moments it bound him. His tenure ends the way the Test most respects: on schedule, by the voters' decision of 3 November 2026, with the loss acknowledged in plain words, the gavel set to pass on 3 January 2027, and the man re-elected by his own conference to lead the minority — the seat graded, as framed, on the holding, and the holding was strongest where he declined to use the power he plainly had.
