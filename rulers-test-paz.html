The Ruler's Test — Rodrigo Paz, President of Bolivia

Subject: Rodrigo Paz Pereira — 68th President of Bolivia since 8 November 2025 (Christian Democratic Party, PDC), with former police captain Edmand Lara as vice president. He won Bolivia's first-ever presidential runoff on 19 October 2025 with about 55% of the vote against former president Jorge Quiroga, ending nearly twenty years of government by the Movement Toward Socialism (MAS) of Evo Morales (2006–2019) and Luis Arce (2020–2025). He is the son of former president Jaime Paz Zamora. He inherited the country's worst economic crisis in four decades: an IMF-estimated fiscal deficit of about 11.4% of GDP in 2025, liquid reserves his government put at just $52 million, inflation of about 20% at the end of 2025, and chronic fuel and dollar shortages. He is graded on his first year in office only — November 2025 to November 2026 — and the file is thin by the series' standards: one year, several of its largest items (the judicial crisis, the Cerimedo case, the Morales warrants) still live and unadjudicated. The MAS era is context; where Paz inherited a file — the fuel subsidy, the reserves, the Morales prosecutions — he is graded on his handling of it, not on its origin. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The law reached the man closest to him in the year's largest scandal. Fernando Cerimedo, the Argentine consultant who ran Paz's second-round digital strategy and collaborated in his government's first months — a relationship Paz himself acknowledged — was arrested on 18 August 2026, a day after his former partner, lawyer Nadia Beller, was shot three times in a Santa Cruz hotel. Prosecutors charged him with attempted femicide and, in a parallel case, money laundering; judges ordered 180 days of preventive detention and on 20 November 2026 refused his release, and he remains in the Chonchocoro maximum-security prison. The charges are ALLEGATIONs, unproven, and his defense denies elements of the prosecution's account (including a reported "bot farm" seizure); the graded fact is narrower — the government did not extract its own former strategist from the process, and the courts kept him in (prosecution statements and court rulings, August–November 2026; AS/COA, 13 November 2026).
  • His own state fuel company was not spared: on 1 September 2026 the government placed YPFB and the hydrocarbons regulator ANH under a 180-day intervention (Supreme Decree 5697), citing alleged corruption and mismanagement inside the fuel-supply chain his administration now runs (government decree and contemporaneous reporting, September 2026).
  • The largest prosecution of an opponent rests on a file that long predates him. Former president Luis Arce was arrested on 10 December 2025 in the Fondioc case — an investigation into the Indigenous development fund that first surfaced in 2015, concerning Arce's years as economy minister (2006–2017). The anti-corruption judge who heard the case ordered five months of preventive detention on 12 December 2025 — more than the three months prosecutors had requested — which is the judiciary acting past the prosecution's ask, not under it (International IDEA democracy tracker, December 2025; AP, 10–13 December 2025).
  • At the transition, the courts moved on a case from the other side without him: on 5 November 2025, three days before his inauguration, the Supreme Tribunal of Justice overturned the conviction of former interim president Jeanine Áñez — the MAS era's most prominent prisoner — and she was freed (2025 chronology records, November 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Arce's arrest nonetheless came one month after he handed over power, on a decade-old file revived under the new government, and its presentation was political: the Minister of Government, Marco Antonio Oviedo, announced the charges publicly and named Arce "the main person responsible" before any trial, citing estimated damage of Bs 360 million (other official and press estimates ran far higher). Arce invoked his right to silence, denies the charges, and his former chief of staff María Nela Prada called the arrest a "kidnapping" and political persecution — her characterization, printed as such. The embezzlement itself is an ALLEGATION; no verdict exists (AP, 10–13 December 2025; prosecutor and government statements).
  • In July 2026 the government joined a prosecution of the opposition's central figure. After the May–June blockades, a complaint by the Comité pro Santa Cruz civic group accused Evo Morales of terrorism and armed uprising; the Interior Ministry formally joined the complaint, and on 29 July 2026 prosecutors issued an arrest warrant seeking, on the terrorism and insurrection counts, 20 to 30 years. Peasant leader Vicente Salazar, named in the same complaint, is in preventive detention; COB leader Mario Argollo was also named. Morales denies the charges and calls the case persecution; the warrant has not been executed (prosecutor statements, 29 July 2026; Reuters/AFP reporting).
  • The sequence around the prosecutor who investigated his adviser is the file's sharpest contested point. Attorney General Roger Mariaca was arrested on 30 September 2026 on money-laundering and drug-trafficking ALLEGATIONs, days after the United States revoked his visa under Section 7031(c); Congress then passed a law removing him and installed an interim attorney general. Mariaca — under investigation, not convicted, held on six months of preventive detention — states that his fall was pressure over his office's investigation of Cerimedo. The government and Washington state the opposite: that under Mariaca the prosecutor's office itself served drug trafficking, an account they tie to the March 2026 arrest and US extradition of fugitive trafficker Sebastián Marset, who implicated Bolivian judges and prosecutors. Both accounts are on the record; neither has been adjudicated (US State Department designations, September–October 2026; Mariaca's statements via counsel and press, October–November 2026).
  • Paz personally raised the temperature on the Morales file beyond the documented record: he publicly accused Morales of involvement in drug trafficking without presenting evidence for the claim, and said his administration was preparing an operation in Morales's Chapare stronghold, with no timeline announced (News Central TV / AFP, July 2026). The accusation is an ALLEGATION by the president, printed as his words, not as a finding.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, the central bank, departmental governments, and the media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Congress remained a real counterweight, not a registry. His coalition fractured in the open — Samuel Doria Medina, whose support helped elect him, formally left the alliance on 27 July 2026 citing corruption concerns and the pace of change, and Vice President Lara became a public critic inside the government itself. The IMF loan required and received a congressional vote (approved September 2026), and his flagship Investment Law, sent to Congress on 11 August 2026, had still not passed by the end of October — the government announced a new version rather than ramming through the old one (Reuters, 11 August 2026; first-year assessments, November 2026).
  • Elections under the inherited electoral tribunal ran on schedule and seated his opponents. The subnational elections of 22 March 2026 (runoffs 19 April) produced a fragmented map: his Patria alliance won the Tarija governorship outright and two governorships in total, while the opposition Libre alliance took Santa Cruz and Pando and an Evo-aligned candidate, Leonardo Loza, won the governorship of Cochabamba running under another banner. Paz publicly framed the result as the country closing a political cycle, and the winners took office (TSE records; Latin America Advisor, April 2026).
  • On monetary policy he accepted a self-binding framework: the IMF program he signed ends the practice of central-bank financing of the budget — the mechanism by which the MAS-era treasury had bridged its deficits — and the central bank moved the boliviano to a market-determined rate in late June 2026 under that framework (IMF program documents, October 2026; BCB resolution, June 2026).
  • No documented case appears in the record reviewed of a media outlet closed or a journalist prosecuted by his government in year one; the year's critical coverage — including of the Cerimedo scandal and the judicial crisis — ran openly in Bolivian outlets such as El Deber and Erbol.

Evidence against:

  • The year ended with the judiciary's summit jailed or fled. Attorney General Mariaca sits in preventive detention (Test 1); more than ten judges and prosecutors were charged within weeks of the US visa lists of September–October 2026; and on 4 November 2026 the president of the Supreme Court, Romer Saucedo — elected to the court in the December 2024 judicial elections with more than 700,000 votes in Santa Cruz — announced from abroad that he had left the country with his family, declaring himself in exile. Saucedo states that the government fabricated evidence against him and that his life was threatened; prosecutors had issued an arrest warrant against him on 3 November in the drug-network investigation, and the government responded that no investigation was known to the presidency, with a deputy interior minister characterizing the departure as a "flight" linked to the Mariaca case. The interim attorney general announced he would seek an Interpol red notice; the Supreme Court gave Saucedo six days to return or face abandonment proceedings. His persecution claim is an ALLEGATION, unadjudicated; the flight itself, and the public linking of an uncharged chief justice to a criminal network by government officials, are documented (Saucedo's statement, 4 November 2026; government statements; contemporaneous reporting).
  • The removal of the attorney general was executed through a law passed for the case. Whatever the merits of the charges against Mariaca, Congress legislated the removal of a sitting attorney general mid-investigation and installed an interim successor — a precedent that makes the office's tenure depend on the legislative majority of the day (Congress records, October 2026).
  • The executive reached directly into the fuel sector's institutions: the September 2026 intervention of YPFB and ANH by decree (180 days) replaced the state company's and the regulator's ordinary governance with government appointees, on stated anti-corruption grounds (Supreme Decree 5697, 1 September 2026).
  • Emergency rule became a governing instrument. On 8 June 2026, mid-protests, Paz signed legislation making it easier for the government to enact emergency measures; on 20 June he declared a 90-day state of emergency empowering the military to clear roads, and Congress extended it from 17 September to 17 December 2026 — meaning Bolivia closes his first year under an extended state of exception (Crisis Group CrisisWatch, June 2026; AP, 20 June 2026; congressional extension, September 2026).
  • The electoral tribunal he inherited issued rulings that cut against his opponents and, in one case, for his alliance — stated here with their critics' names attached, because the tribunal's members were not his appointees. The Andean Information Network's director documented that electoral-court rulings blocked four attempts by Morales supporters to register their own party or alliances for the subnational elections, and that the tribunal accepted a party's withdrawal from the La Paz gubernatorial runoff without its candidate's consent, declaring Paz's alliance the winner — a decision she characterized as illegal. These are documented criticisms by named analysts, not findings against the tribunal; they belong on this shelf because the evenness of the referee is part of what this test measures, whoever appointed it (Latin America Advisor Q&A, April 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, unions, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His first decree was withdrawn under pressure and renegotiated. Supreme Decree 5503 (December 2025) — the "gasolinazo" that ended the fuel subsidy and bundled 121 articles of wider reform — was declared by Paz himself to be "non-negotiable." After the COB's general strike and blockades at up to 70 points across the country in January 2026, the government annulled the decree and negotiated a replacement with the unions: the subsidy removal stayed, most of the rest was dropped. COB leader Mario Argollo declared the objective achieved (union and government statements, January 2026; conflict monitoring by Fundación UNIR).
  • He reversed a law outright when the countryside rose against it. Law 1720, which allowed land to be used as collateral for loans, triggered the April–May 2026 mobilization of peasant and Indigenous organizations, including a march from Pando; Paz annulled the law on 13 May 2026, in the middle of the protest wave rather than after it (2026 protest chronology; Crisis Group, June 2026).
  • The June settlement was negotiated before it was enforced: on 19 June 2026 his administration signed an agreement with the COB establishing sectoral working groups and the suspension of protest measures, and by 23 June all blockade points had been lifted (agreement records; AP, June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Each concession followed the same sequence: refusal first, reversal only after the country stopped. The "non-negotiable" decree lasted until the highways closed; Law 1720 was annulled a week after blockades began on 6 May; and the June agreement was signed after 53 days of blockades that the ombudsman's office linked to at least 17 deaths — most from patients unable to reach medical care through the blockades — with the compiled protest record putting deaths at 24 as of 9 July 2026, injuries at 37, and arrests at 583 (Defensoría del Pueblo and human-rights organizations, June 2026; protest casualty compilations, July 2026). Deaths are attributed here as the monitors attributed them — to the crisis as a whole, including blockade effects — not solely to state force; clashes between dynamite-carrying protesters and police are also documented.
  • The June wobble ended in doubling down, not only in agreement. Hours after signing the COB deal on 19 June, the government declared the state of emergency (Test 2), deployed soldiers and police with bulldozers to clear roads, and adopted the protesters-as-"narco-terrorists" framing that US officials had used days earlier — Paz signed the emergency legislation at a ceremony thanking the US defense secretary for his support. Hundreds of legal cases were opened against protest leaders, the Túpac Katari peasant leader was arrested in early July, and peasant leader Vicente Salazar remains in preventive detention (AP, 20 June 2026; Crisis Group, June 2026; protest chronology).
  • On the subsidy itself he never adjusted the policy, only its packaging: after the January renegotiation and the May–June trauma, the government raised fuel prices three more times between mid-August and 1 September 2026 and eliminated the diesel subsidy by decree (Supreme Decree 5716), with pump prices rising about 83% — the IMF program's condition, executed on schedule over the same unions' renewed objections (IMF program documents; reporting, September 2026).
  • The voters' verdict in the March 2026 subnational elections — his alliance reduced to two governorships — was absorbed rhetorically ("Bolivia closes a cycle") without a change of course or cabinet consequence that the record shows.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Indigenous communities, MAS voters, coca growers, the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Indigenous and peasant movements' central material demand of 2026 was met: the repeal of Law 1720 (Test 3) protected communal and smallholder land from the collateral mechanism they had marched against, and the movements that won it — the COB, the Túpac Katari Federation, CONAMAQ, lowland Indigenous organizations — negotiated with the government as recognized counterparts, not as enemies of the state (protest chronology; agreement records, May–June 2026).
  • MAS voters were counted at the ballot box even as their party collapsed. MAS holds 2 of 130 lower-house seats, but its voters were not disenfranchised: Evo-aligned and ex-MAS candidates won governorships and some 75% of Cochabamba's municipal governments under other banners in March 2026, and those results were seated (subnational election records, March–April 2026). Analysts across the coverage note Paz himself won the presidency substantially with votes from former MAS strongholds and from the null-vote campaign Morales had encouraged — his electorate was, in large part, the flock of the old order.
  • The adjustment program carries stated protections for the poorest: the IMF arrangement includes social-spending commitments, the World Bank approved $200 million for social protection, and the IDB approved a further $750 million credit in November 2026; the subsidy he removed was a general price subsidy whose benefits, on the IMF's and the government's documented analysis, flowed disproportionately to smugglers and higher-income consumers — the government's stated rationale, printed as its position (IMF program documents, October 2026; IDB, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The cost of the reset landed on the poor first and hardest, on the documented figures. Fuel prices rose 86% (gasoline) and 162% (diesel) in December 2025 and about 83% again in the August–September 2026 steps; transport and food prices followed. The economy contracted 3.27% in the first half of 2026 (El Deber-compiled data), the IMF projects a 3.3% contraction for the year, and the compensation side of the ledger — the transfers that were supposed to cushion the shock — remained small against a shock the budget itself values at close to 5% of GDP in subsidy savings (IMF, October 2026; El Deber data via 24EcoNews, November 2026).
  • Protesters were answered as a criminal class. Beyond the 583 arrests and hundreds of prosecutions (Test 3), the state's language classified the mobilized — miners, teachers, farmers, Indigenous and union organizations — under a "narco-terrorist" label borrowed from Washington, and the terrorism warrant against Morales (Test 1) treats the political leadership of the protest movement as a security case. Whatever the documented violence at some blockades, the counting-out is in the category: a fifth of the country's organized society was addressed as traffickers (government statements, June–July 2026; Hegseth statement as reported).
  • Coca growers, the social base of the Chapare, are counted as a threat rather than a constituency. Paz restored the counternarcotics partnership with the United States — a $20 million cooperation agreement signed 16 June 2026, eighteen years after the DEA's expulsion — and publicly accused the growers' historic leader of drug trafficking without presenting evidence (Test 1). No corresponding documented channel — consultation, compensation, or a coca-policy settlement — appears in the record for the growers themselves.
  • The departments were promised a share and offered a fraction (developed in Test 5): by October 2026 the governors of Oruro, Potosí and Tarija had walked out of the fiscal dialogue, and Santa Cruz's vice-governor warned publicly of regional mobilization — the regions experiencing the 50/50 promise as a counting-out (statements, 29 October 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record of his first year, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • End the fuel subsidy — kept, at full political cost. He campaigned on ending the subsidy and did it in December 2025, kept it through the January renegotiation that killed the rest of the decree, and completed the diesel step in September 2026 (Tests 1 and 3).
  • Open Bolivia to the world — kept, and then some. He pledged rapprochement with the United States and delivered the full restoration of ties after seventeen years: an ambassador-level reset begun with his 31 October 2025 meeting with Secretary of State Rubio as president-elect, a critical-minerals memorandum signed in Washington on 27 April 2026, the June 2026 counternarcotics agreement, membership in the US-backed Shield of the Americas, and restored diplomatic relations with Israel in December 2025. A $3.1 billion CAF program was agreed at his inauguration and a $4.5 billion IDB package for 2026–2028 followed (State Department and foreign-ministry records, 2025–2026).
  • Stabilization numbers — partially delivered, on his own government's figures. His economy ministry's first-year report states annual inflation fell to 7.08% by October 2026 from about 21% at the end of 2025, and liquid reserves rose above $1 billion from $52 million; net reserves stood at $3.93 billion on 30 September 2026, mostly gold. Fitch, S&P and Moody's all upgraded Bolivia's sovereign rating in 2026, and the IMF's board approved the $1.9 billion program on 2 October 2026 with about $214 million disbursed immediately. The IMF's own projections — a 3.3% contraction in 2026 — are printed beside the government's figures, because both are the record (economy ministry report, November 2026; IMF, October 2026; rating-agency actions, 2026).
  • Austerity at the center — kept in form. The cabinet was cut to 12 ministries, the Financial Transactions Tax abolished, and the 2026 budget reformulated to target a deficit of about 9.2% of GDP from the roughly 11.4% inherited (budget documents; first-year assessments).

Evidence against (promises not kept, reversed, or receding):

  • The IMF — reversed. On the campaign trail Paz rejected turning to the IMF, distinguishing his gradualism from Quiroga's proposed bailout; commentators noted the refusal as a deliberate signal (LRB, October 2025; AP, November 2025). In office he negotiated the largest available Fund instrument: staff-level agreement 29 July 2026, congressional approval in September, board approval 2 October 2026 — a 36-month, $1.9 billion Extended Fund Facility whose conditions (subsidy elimination, an end to central-bank financing, a market exchange rate) set the spine of his economic policy. The reversal may be defensible on the inherited numbers; it is a reversal, and it is documented in his own sequence.
  • The 50/50 — the signature promise, undelivered. In April 2025 Paz pledged that half of state resources would go to the regions and said the measure would be introduced the day after he took office. A year later no redistribution law exists; the government's standing offer, reported in November 2026, is an initial 1% share for the nine departmental governments from 2027, possibly rising over time — with the honest caveat, printed in the Bolivian analysis itself, that the comparison is not exact, since the promise covered municipalities and universities as well as departments. What is not contested: the day-after measure never appeared, technical talks stalled, three governors left the dialogue, and the mayor of Santa Cruz states the promise in terms — "what one commits to, one has to fulfill" (El Día analysis, 13 November 2026; Santa Cruz government statements, 29 October 2026).
  • Judicial reform — announced, not delivered. His platform promised a merit-based reform commission with international support, and after the election he pledged a "Justice Summit" in Sucre and said his government would not interfere with judges and prosecutors (HRW, November 2025). On 9 May 2026 he announced a partial constitutional reform of judicial selection; between April and August 2026 three competing reform bills were filed in the Assembly. As of November 2026 none has been enacted, the "self-extended" Constitutional Tribunal magistrates — who prolonged their own mandates in December 2023 — remain a standing feature of the system he inherited, and the year's actual judicial events were the arrests and the chief justice's flight (Tests 1–2), not the reform (ConstitutionNet analysis, 2026; Assembly records).
  • The flagship economic laws lag. The Investment Law — the centerpiece of "capitalism for all," promising legal security and protection against unilateral expropriation — was sent to Congress on 11 August 2026 and had not passed by the end of October; a new mining code draft is resisted by the mining cooperatives' federation; the hydrocarbons overhaul remains pending (Reuters, August 2026; sector reporting, November 2026).
  • Transparency, selectively. He campaigned on transparency for the lithium deals signed with China and Russia under Arce (contracts already suspended by a Bolivian court in June 2025, before he took office, and never ratified). His own critical-minerals memorandum with the United States, signed 27 April 2026, has not been published by either government — a Bolivian outlet filed a US Freedom of Information Act request for its text on 22 November 2026 (sala.red, November 2026).
  • The mandate's own measure. Ipsos Ciesmori polling put his approval at 65% on taking office and 32% at the first anniversary, with disapproval at 55%; rather than deliver the mandated annual accountability address in person, he submitted a written report to the Assembly on 6 November 2026, which Vice President Lara convened a session to receive on 11 November (Ipsos Ciesmori, November 2026; first-year assessments).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Rodrigo Paz's first year is a transition file, and it should be read as one: a president elected substantially by the old order's own voters, governing a bankrupt state through decree, negotiation, and — when negotiation failed — emergency. The strongest evidence in his favor is that the machinery still moves against his own side: his former campaign strategist sits in a maximum-security prison with release denied, his state fuel company is under intervention for alleged corruption, his Congress defeats and delays him, and his opponents govern Cochabamba, Santa Cruz, and Pando. The strongest evidence against him is the shape of the year at its edges: a predecessor jailed within a month of the handover, a terrorism warrant his ministry joined against the former president who still commands the Chapare, a prosecutor arrested weeks after investigating the president's adviser — on charges the prosecutor calls retaliation and two governments call a narco-network — and the president of the Supreme Court announcing his exile to an empty chair. He kept the promises that cost other people money — the subsidy, the opening, the austerity — and the two promises that would have cost him power or admitted error, the 50/50 and the IMF refusal, went the other way; the judicial reform that was to be his legacy is three unenacted bills. Every one of these grades is correctable in the open, and the files that would move them are named in the record: the Cerimedo and Mariaca trials, Saucedo's return or prosecution, the January 2027 subsidy step, and whether the reform bills ever reach a vote.
