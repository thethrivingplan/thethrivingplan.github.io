Reversi

Trap a line of your opponent's discs between two of yours and the whole line flips to your colour. Most discs when the board fills — or when neither side can move — wins. A minute to learn; the corners will take you a lifetime.

The documented game: this is Reversi, the game published in London in 1883 and described in print ever since — the original, public-domain game, played here under its documented rules (8×8 board, four centre discs, a move must outflank and flip, a side with no legal move passes). It is not the later trademarked variant sold under another name; nothing here copies that product's branding or text.

The mobility counter — the number pros actually watch

Mobility is how many legal moves a side has right now. Discs can be retaken; moves are freedom. A side rich in discs but starved of moves is losing the real game — the classic beginner trap is grabbing every flip and ending up with nowhere good to go.

Position map, in plain words

Corners can never be flipped once taken — the only truly safe discs on the board. The squares beside an empty corner (the two edge squares next to it, and the diagonal square inside) usually hand that corner to your opponent — pros call the diagonal one the X-square and treat all three as poison until the corner is settled. Edges are strong once they connect to a corner. The middle is weather: it changes every move, so don't fall in love with a big pile there.

How to play — tap to open

The one rule: place a disc so that one or more straight lines (across, down, or diagonal) of your opponent's discs are trapped between your new disc and one already yours. Every trapped disc flips to your colour. If a placement traps nothing, it isn't legal.

Passing: if you have no legal move, you pass and your opponent plays again. The game ends when the board is full or neither side can move — empty squares left over simply belong to nobody.

Winning: most discs of your colour when the game ends. The lead changes hands violently near the end — a 20-disc "lead" in the middle is often a loss wearing a costume.

Do the levels really differ?

Checked the honest way: the bots played each other head-to-head on the standard 8×8 board, colours swapped halfway so first move is shared fairly — 20 games per pairing among Learning, Steady, and Sharp, 10 per pairing against Pro (Pro thinks longer). The ladder is real: every level beat every level below it, and the two disc-grabbers sit close together, exactly as their strategies suggest.

How each level actually plays: Learning takes the biggest flip it can see and daydreams about a third of its moves. Steady counts discs and takes the move that leaves it with the most — the classic beginner strategy, played perfectly. Sharp scores squares the pro way (corners and mobility first) and peeks at your best reply. Pro searches several moves deep with that same pro scoring, and solves the endgame outright once ten or fewer squares remain.
