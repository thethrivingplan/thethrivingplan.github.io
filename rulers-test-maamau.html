The Ruler's Test — Taneti Maamau, President of Kiribati

Subject: Taneti Maamau — President of the Republic of Kiribati since 11 March 2016. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: in Kiribati the President (Te Beretitenti) is both head of state and head of government, chairs Cabinet, and — in Maamau's case — also holds the Foreign Affairs and Immigration portfolio, so executive power sits in this seat and no separate premiership exists to grade. Maamau, born 16 September 1960 on Onotoa, a former public servant and MP for Onotoa, won the presidency in 2016 with about 60% of the vote, was re-elected in 2020, and was re-elected again on 25 October 2024 with 20,676 votes (55.05%) to Kaotitaake Kokoria's 15,787 (42.03%), inaugurated for a third term on 1 November 2024 (Maneaba ni Maungatabu results via 2024 presidential election reporting; Australian Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade, Kiribati country brief). The constitution limits a president to three terms, so this is stated in the record as his third and final term. The 2024 mandate carries a qualification that runs through this dossier: his Tobwaan Kiribati Party (TKP) won 33 of the 44 elected seats in the August 2024 parliamentary elections, and when the Maneaba sat on 13 September 2024 the TKP used that supermajority to block every opposition nominee for the presidential ballot, nominating Maamau alongside three first-time TKP MPs described in the reporting as "dummy candidates" — one of whom, Kokoria, defected shortly before polling day and, with opposition support, became the only real challenger (RNZ reporting, September–October 2024; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). He is graded on the full documented record since 2016, with weight on the completed second term and the 2024–2026 third term, because the acts that decide several tests — above all the 2022 suspension of the superior judiciary — are continuing acts whose consequences were still live, and still being defended, in 2025. His wife Teiraeng Tentoa Maamau died in November 2025 (RNZ, 12 November 2025); that is a private loss, recorded here only because it is part of the public record of the tenure, and it grades nothing. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic printed on the Test card, verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Governing on the platform the voters elected is NOT inherently a failure and is never graded as one: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what a ruler ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for HOW it is done — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Every test closes with a plain-words Why and a named Helped / Hurt ledger. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as two independent axes in Test 5, with all four crossings named and "not documented" recorded rather than invented. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The big domestic instruments of his government are written as one law for the whole country, not as rewards for TKP islands. The copra price guarantee — the income floor for outer-island copra cutters — was doubled from $1 to $2 per kilo in his first term, fulfilling his 2016 manifesto, and by his own March 2026 account had been raised again to $4 per kilo (Office of Te Beretitenti biography; BPA News, 16 March 2026). Free education was extended through the senior secondary years (to Form 7, on his 2026 account), and the social-protection system built from fishing revenue — senior citizens' allowance, unemployment support, disability benefits, and the copra subsidy — is national in design and is credited in the 2023/24 Household Income and Expenditure Survey analysis with consumption growth concentrated among poorer households (Kiribati National Statistics Office / Pacific Community, Poverty and Inequality in Kiribati 2023–2024, June 2025; Devpolicy, 4 August 2025).
  • An anti-corruption architecture was created at the start, on paper for everyone: a committee to investigate corruption and abuse of power in 2016, and Kiribati's first National Anti-Corruption Strategy in September 2017, with the Public Service Integrity and Corruption Control Unit under the Public Service Office charged with promoting UNCAC principles (Office of Te Beretitenti; U4 Anti-Corruption Resource Centre overview of Pacific island countries). No documented pattern of corruption prosecutions aimed only at opposition figures appears in the record assembled here — the absence is recorded as an absence, not as proof none occurred in a system with no freedom-of-information law (CIVICUS Monitor Pacific research brief, November 2025).
  • The 2024 parliamentary election itself was genuinely competitive at the legislative level: turnout was 87.14% in the first round, government ministers lost seats — including the Minister for Infrastructure and Sustainable Energy in Abemama — and the opposition Boutokaan Kiribati Moa (BKM) won 8 seats (2024 parliamentary election results; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). The voters who counted his side out in their constituencies were counted in.

Evidence against:

  • The source of his third term was not one law for all candidates. On 13 September 2024 the TKP supermajority blocked every opposition nominee from the presidential ballot and nominated three first-time TKP MPs alongside the incumbent; opposition leader Tessie Lambourne — the only widely credited alternative candidate — said "Kiribati is now a one-party state," and former president Ieremia Tabai and other opposition figures called for a boycott of the vote in protest (RNZ, September–October 2024; PINA, 2 October 2024). One research assessment (Va-PoReg country report on Kiribati) dates a shift to an "electoral hybrid regime" from that day, on the ground that opposition parties kept a realistic path to the legislature but none to the presidency. That characterization is an analyst's judgment, recorded as such; the underlying facts — the blocked nominations and the all-TKP ballot — are not disputed in the record. Kokoria's late defection gave voters a real choice on the day (42.03% took it), but the choice existed because a government nominee broke ranks, not because the rule admitted the opposition.
  • The judge at the centre of Test 2 was the opposition leader's husband, and the sequence is documented, whatever the motive: High Court Judge David Lambourne — married to opposition leader Tessie Lambourne — was suspended in May 2022, and every judge who then ruled in his favour was suspended in turn (see Test 2). The opposition's reading, that he was targeted to pressure his wife out of politics, is recorded here as an ALLEGATION — it is the belief attributed to the opposition and to commentators by CIVICUS and Reuters reporting, not a finding. What is a finding, by the UN Special Rapporteur on the independence of judges and lawyers, Margaret Satterthwaite, on 26 April 2024, is that "Judge Lambourne was removed after proceedings that violated international standards. He was not allowed to have a fair hearing" (UN statement via CIVICUS Monitor). A process that fails international standards for one man — the one man married to the leader of the opposition — is a second law in operation even if the intention behind it is never proven.
  • The September 2019 switch of recognition from Taiwan to China — the largest foreign-policy act of his tenure — was taken without consulting his own MPs, on the account of the man who resigned over it: thirteen MPs left the TKP to form the Kiribati First Party under Banuera Berina, who alleged Maamau had not consulted them before the switch (contemporaneous reporting summarized in the Taneti Maamau record). Maamau called the defectors "traitors" while saying he respected their decision. Opposition ALLEGATIONS that China financially backed the TKP in 2024, and social-media ALLEGATIONS of Chinese campaign money and payments to MPs, circulate in the record assembled here; none is established by a documented finding, and none is graded as fact — they are recorded so the shelf shows what is claimed and what is proven, separately.
  • A rule reported in stakeholder submissions to the UN's 2025 Universal Periodic Review required new civil society organisations to obtain the signatures of all members of Parliament to register — a hurdle the Banaban Human Rights Defenders Network reported facing in practice (OHCHR summary of stakeholders' submissions, A/HRC/WG.6/49/KIR/3, 17 January 2025). A registration door that every MP must personally sign is not the same door for a group critical of the government as for a group aligned with it.

Why: The everyday law of his Kiribati — copra, school, allowances, fishing money shared out — genuinely runs one way for everyone, and the poverty figures in Test 4 show it reaching people who never voted for him. But at the two points where the law touched his own hold on power — who may stand against him for the presidency, and the judge married to his chief opponent — the rule bent. One law in the distribution, a second law at the source: that is exactly what MIXED is for.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — outer-island copra cutters, students, pensioners, people with disabilities and the unemployed, who receive the national schemes regardless of party. Hurt — opposition presidential aspirants, above all Tessie Lambourne, denied a place on the 2024 ballot by the nomination block; David Lambourne, removed through proceedings the UN's rapporteur found violated international standards; and voters who wanted an opposition name on the presidential ballot and were offered only TKP names until one defected.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Outside the judiciary, the referees mostly still stand. Freedom House rates Kiribati "Free" at 89/100 in Freedom in the World 2026 (political rights 36/40, civil liberties 53/60), with free and independent media scored 4/4 and freedom to express personal views without fear of retribution scored 4/4 (Freedom House, Kiribati country profile, 2026). The CIVICUS Monitor rates Kiribati's civic space "open" — one of seven Pacific countries so rated in its November 2025 brief — and private outlets, including a newspaper and radio station owned by former president and opposition figure Ieremia Tabai, operate freely (CIVICUS Monitor, November 2025; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024).
  • Elections are run and lost honestly at the level where he allows them to be contested: the August 2024 parliamentary elections were considered free and fair under universal suffrage, his ministers lost seats, and the opposition sits in the Maneaba and condemns him in public and in formal statements without documented reprisal against the MPs themselves (Va-PoReg country report; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024–2025).
  • He did, eventually, staff a court again: a High Court commissioner was appointed in August 2023 who heard Lambourne's applications, ruled in April 2024 that the suspension was valid but withholding the judge's salary was unconstitutional and that he must not be deported until the Maneaba had considered the tribunal's report — a ruling against the government's position on two of three points, delivered inside his own system (Reuters reporting, April 2024). A Court of Appeal panel heard Lambourne's appeal on 9 December 2024, with judgment reserved (CIVICUS Monitor, 2024).

Evidence against:

  • In 2022 his government suspended, in sequence, every superior judge who stood in its way. Judge David Lambourne was suspended in May 2022 over misconduct allegations whose details were, in the International Bar Association's Human Rights Institute's words, met with "opacity and a paucity of details." Chief Justice Bill Hastings was suspended on 30 June 2022 — the day he was due to hear Lambourne's constitutional challenge to his own suspension, so the hearing could not proceed. In August 2022 officials attempted to deport Lambourne despite a Court of Appeal order that he not be removed; the deportation failed only because the Fiji Airways captain refused to board him against his will. On 6 September 2022 the President suspended all three Court of Appeal judges — Sir Peter Blanchard, Rodney Hansen and Paul Heath, retired New Zealand judges — after they quashed the deportation order and held the government's actions unconstitutional, referring them to a tribunal as well (IBAHRI statement, September 2022; 2022 Kiribati constitutional crisis record; Reuters). The New Zealand Law Society's verdict at the time: "Kiribati now has no sitting judges," and the suspension of judges "owing to disagreement with a ruling — is an inappropriate interference with the judicial process."
  • The UN Special Rapporteur on the independence of judges and lawyers, Margaret Satterthwaite, wrote to Kiribati in September 2023 that she was "seriously alarmed" at the series of suspensions, which left Kiribati without a functioning High Court or Court of Appeal to act as a check on the power of parliament, and raised concerns that the tribunal formed to investigate Lambourne was headed by a lay magistrate with no formal legal qualifications (UN letter, September 2023, via Reuters). Her April 2024 finding on the removal itself is quoted in Test 1.
  • The grip did not loosen in the third term; it was defended and extended by omission. In October 2022 the President appointed his own Attorney General, Tetiro Semilota, as Acting Chief Justice while Hastings remained suspended — an appointment also noted, fairly, as a first: the first I-Kiribati and the first woman to hold the chief justice's office, acting (2022 crisis record). Lambourne left Kiribati in May 2024 after the Maneaba voted in April 2024 to accept the second tribunal's recommendation of dismissal, the tribunal having been established on 7 March 2024 and having ruled largely on the ground that some of his written judgments were not "promptly delivered" (CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). In August 2025 the opposition formally condemned the government's refusal even to debate the Court of Appeal suspension, then nearly three years old (RNZ, August 2025, via CIVICUS Monitor). In September 2025 the UN Human Rights Council adopted Kiribati's Universal Periodic Review, whose recommendations included taking "all necessary measures to ensure the independence of the judiciary"; on 8 January 2026 the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Volker Türk, wrote to the government raising, among other things, the need for judicial independence (UN Human Rights Council decision 60/102, 25 September 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2026).
  • Power is also centralized in the seat itself: Maamau is simultaneously President, head of government, and Minister for Foreign Affairs and Immigration — the combination that let a cancelled meeting with a foreign minister become, in January 2025, a review of an entire aid programme (see Test 3), with no separate foreign minister to absorb or answer for the decision.

Why: A ruler can survive one disputed suspension. What the record shows is a complete sequence: suspend the judge, suspend the chief justice on the morning of the hearing, attempt the deportation against a court order, suspend the entire appeal court for ruling against the government, install the government's own Attorney General as acting chief justice, complete the removal through a second tribunal, and then, three years on, refuse a parliamentary debate on the still-suspended appeal court. That is not holding the referees at arm's length; it is emptying the referee's chair and keeping it empty. The free press and honest vote-counting on the FOR shelf are real, and they are why this dossier does not call the whole system closed — but Test 2 names the courts first, and on the courts the grade is FAILS.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the executive itself, which governed from September 2022 without a functioning superior court able to check it, and the TKP majority, whose 2024 nomination block (Test 1) faced no sitting appeal court. Hurt — David Lambourne (suspended without pay — held unconstitutional — removed, and gone from the country that had been his home for some 30 years); Chief Justice Bill Hastings and Court of Appeal judges Sir Peter Blanchard, Rodney Hansen and Paul Heath, suspended for a ruling; and every ordinary litigant in Kiribati whose case needed a High Court or Court of Appeal that, for a period, did not sit.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, if another option becomes available, the ruler at least considered it openly. Considering it and declining with stated reasons is NOT against-evidence. Refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Pacific Islands Forum wobble was answered with a genuine reversal. Kiribati withdrew from the Forum on 9 July 2022 — the only Micronesian state to carry its withdrawal through after the June 2022 Suva Agreement, which Maamau said Kiribati had had no role in drafting and which had not answered its concerns. When another option became available — Fiji's new Prime Minister Sitiveni Rabuka travelled to Tarawa in January 2023, performed a traditional reconciliation ceremony, and the Forum chair offered an official apology — Maamau took it: he told Rabuka Kiribati had reconciled with the Forum, endorsed the Suva Agreement, and Kiribati formally rejoined on 24 February 2023 (Pacific Islands Forum record; Pacific Security College newsletter, 2023). Doubling down was available and would have been easy; he chose the door that opened.
  • The New Zealand wobble ended the same way, after a harder year. In January 2025 Maamau cancelled a planned meeting with New Zealand Foreign Minister Winston Peters a week before the visit — Kiribati said the President was on an outer island — and New Zealand put its whole development programme (NZ$102 million provided 2021–2024) under review, warning that resident visas and the seasonal-worker scheme could be affected (AP reporting, January 2025; 1News, 28 January 2025). Kiribati's first answer was denial in form: in February 2025 its position was that "New Zealand cancelled its own proposed visit" (Pacific news reporting, 3 February 2025), and in September 2025 Maamau publicly criticized Peters over the funding dispute. But the adjustment followed: after what Peters described as "significant political-level dialogue over the past six months," Peters visited Kiribati for the first time since 2019 and the two countries signed a Statement of Partnership on 19 January 2026 (Reuters, 19 January 2026). The wobble took a year and cost a public row; it ended in an open, signed adjustment, not a frozen rupture.
  • On climate — the existential wobble, which is not of his making — his answer has been stated openly and held consistently in the largest rooms available: at the UN General Assembly on 24 September 2025 he rejected the "sinking nation" narrative, said Kiribati "remains resilient" with "traditional and modern strategies to adapt and thrive," urged holding warming to 1.5°C and support through the Loss and Damage Fund, and welcomed the International Court of Justice's advisory opinion as "firm hope and assurance that Kiribati and other vulnerable countries will continue to exist" (UN General Debate, 80th session, 24 September 2025; PACNEWS via Pasifika News, 26 September 2025). A concrete piece of the adaptation answer was handed over on 23 October 2026, when he commissioned the completed coastal-protection seawall at Tungaru Central Hospital, built with Australia and UNOPS under the Kiribati–Australia Climate Security Initiative, saying the site had been "equipped only with technical plans" thirteen months earlier (UNOPS press release, 23 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The judicial wobble was never answered at all — it was refused a hearing. The pushback was as authoritative as pushback gets: the IBAHRI, the New Zealand Law Society, the Commonwealth judicial bodies, and the UN Special Rapporteur all said, in substance, restore the judges and respect the orders. If another option became available — reinstatement, a genuinely independent tribunal, even a parliamentary debate — the documented record shows no instance of it being considered openly. Instead: the removal was completed in April–May 2024, and in August 2025 the government refused to debate the Court of Appeal suspension in the Maneaba (RNZ, August 2025). On this file the answer to every wobble for over three years was the same act, repeated.
  • The 2024 voters' wobble was answered by narrowing the voters' next choice. The parliamentary result was a landslide, but the presidential ballot that followed it excluded the opposition entirely (Test 1) — where a normal answer to a strong mandate is to face the strongest opponent, his party's answer was to make sure none appeared. The boycott calls by former president Ieremia Tabai and others were not answered with any concession or explanation of the nomination block beyond the act itself, in the record assembled here.
  • The deep-sea mining turn shows a wobble answered by changing partners, not by opening the question. After the agreement with The Metals Company — through Kiribati's state-backed Marawa Research — was terminated at the end of 2024, the government announced in March 2025 that it had opened talks with China's ambassador to "explore potential collaboration for the sustainable exploration of the deep-ocean resources," calling it "an exciting opportunity" (AFP, 18 March 2025; Radio Free Asia, 17 March 2025). Environmental groups' objections to deep-sea mining — Kiribati holds exploration rights over some 75,000 km² of the Clarion-Clipperton Zone — were not, in the record assembled here, answered with a public consultation or a stated consideration of the no-mining option; the consideration documented is between mining partners.

Why: He adjusts where the pushback comes from peers he needs — Forum leaders with an apology in hand, New Zealand with an aid programme in hand — and the adjustments, when they come, are real and signed. He does not adjust, at all, where the pushback comes from judges, rapporteurs and his own opposition: there the record is three years of refusal to look. Both halves are documented; neither cancels the other.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — Kiribati's regional standing and its people, by the Forum re-entry (restoring the Micronesian bloc and the Suva Agreement's guarantees) and by the January 2026 New Zealand partnership, which took the aid programme, visas and seasonal work out from under the review cloud. Hurt — New Zealand–Kiribati relations and the aid-dependent programmes that spent a year under review; the judges and litigants of Test 2, for whom no adjustment ever came; and Pacific environmental advocates, whose objection to deep-sea mining was met with a new partner rather than a hearing.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the outer islands, the Banaban community, women, minorities, the opposition, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The headline accounting of his tenure counts the poor in, measurably. On the national measure, poverty fell from 21.9% in 2019/20 to 5.5% in 2023/24; urban poverty fell 15.3 points to 4.2% and rural poverty fell 17.4 points to 7.4%, with declines of around 20 points in some outer-island divisions; the Gini index fell from 27.8 to 24.7, reflecting consumption growth strongest among poorer households; roughly 19,000 people were lifted out of poverty (World Bank Poverty & Equity Brief, October 2026, on the 2023/24 HIES; KNSO/SPC report, June 2025). Conflicting framings are printed separately, as the method requires: the government-aligned account describes the same fall as "nearly 75%" with "extreme poverty effectively eliminated" (Devpolicy, 4 August 2025) — the same survey, a relative framing of the same 21.9%-to-5.5% move, not a second set of figures. The mechanism is documented: fishing revenue reported up from $43 million in 2010 to $210 million in 2024, reinvested in the senior citizens' allowance, unemployment support, disability benefits and the copra subsidy (Devpolicy, 2025).
  • The outer islands are in the design, not just the speeches: the copra guarantee is worth most where copra is the cash economy; mobile phone and internet connectivity were extended to all islands and a new government secondary school established on Kiritimati, on his March 2026 account of the previous term's 149 manifesto commitments said to have been delivered (BPA News, 16 March 2026 — the 149 figure is the President's own claim, recorded as a claim). The Banaban community's distinct place in the state is structural and maintained: the 45th seat in the Maneaba is nominated by the Banaban community resident on Rabi Island in Fiji (2024 parliamentary election record).
  • His climate accounting counts future I-Kiribati as people, not as a caseload for relocation: where his predecessor Anote Tong planned "Migration With Dignity," Maamau dismissed that policy, put aside what he called at COP23 in 2017 "the misleading and pessimistic scenario of a sinking nation," and committed instead to raising and protecting land in place — a stance restated at UNGA in September 2025 and September 2026 (COP23 statement, 2017; UN General Debate, 2025 and 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The remaining poor are concentrated where the state is thinnest. Of the roughly 7,000 people still below the national poverty line, nearly 42% live in South Tarawa and another 28% in the Southern Group, in communities under pressure on housing and basic services; and 23.6% of the whole population lives just above the line, one health emergency, climate disaster or job loss from falling back (KNSO/SPC analysis via Devpolicy, 2025). On the World Bank's upper-middle-income line ($8.30/day), 52.0% of Kiribati remained poor in 2023/24 — a separate sourced figure, printed separately: the national line says hardship is now rare; the international line says half the country still lives on very little (World Bank Poverty & Equity Brief, October 2026).
  • Some are counted out in law. Consensual sexual conduct between adult men remains a crime under sections 153–155 of the Penal Code; stakeholder submissions to the 2025 UPR recommended repeal, and Kiribati's 2025 review is recorded as rejecting recommendations to decriminalize same-sex relations and enact anti-discrimination protections (OHCHR stakeholders' summary, 17 January 2025; UPR outcome adopted 25 September 2025). Freedom House's standing summary likewise lists the ban on same-sex sexual activity and some forms of gender discrimination among the country's outstanding problems (Freedom House, 2026).
  • Women are counted thinly at the top: three women were elected in the August 2024 parliamentary elections, Tessie Lambourne among them, in a 44-seat elected house (2024 parliamentary election results). The one woman who led the opposition was then blocked from the presidential ballot (Test 1) — the counting-out there was partisan in form, and its effect fell on the country's most prominent woman in politics.
  • The Banaban file carries a specific documented friction: the Banaban Human Rights Defenders Network reported difficulty registering as a civil society organisation under the all-MPs'-signatures policy described in Test 1 (OHCHR stakeholders' summary, 2025). And the human-rights architecture for everyone remains unbuilt: Kiribati still has no national human rights institution, and in its fourth UPR cycle it reversed the commitment made in its third cycle to ratify the remaining core treaties including the ICCPR — a reversal the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights raised directly with the government in his letter of 8 January 2026 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026; CIVICUS Pacific brief, November 2025).
  • The no-good-choice clause, applied by its mechanics where it genuinely fits — the climate file: the options actually open to a state of atolls averaging near sea level were (a) plan the people's departure, his predecessor's road, which preserves lives by dissolving the nation in place; (b) stay and build — raise land, wall the hospital, demand 1.5°C of the world — which preserves the nation while carrying the real risk that the sea outruns the building; or (c) split the difference and do neither well. Each carried real harm; hindsight options do not count as options refused. His choice of (b) is graded as the best possible choice available, given the situation in the moment, and is not held against him in this dossier. What remains gradable, and is graded in Tests 3 and 5, is his honesty about the trap — the "refuse to sink" framing states the choice openly — and the pace of the building itself.

Why: On the material count — money, food, school, allowances — his Kiribati counts more of its poorest people in than it did when he arrived, and the survey evidence for that is strong and independent. On the legal and political count, named groups are still outside the accounting: gay men under an unrepealed criminal ban, the Banaban defenders at a registration desk, women at three seats, and the opposition leader off the ballot. A count that rises for almost everyone and still names its exceptions is MIXED.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the roughly 19,000 people lifted over the national poverty line between the 2019/20 and 2023/24 surveys, rural and outer-island households most of all; pensioners, people with disabilities and the unemployed inside the new social-protection floor; future generations addressed by the adapt-in-place climate stance. Hurt — the roughly 7,000 still below the national line, concentrated in South Tarawa and the Southern Group, and the 23.6% hovering just above it; gay men in Kiribati, whose consensual conduct remains criminal; the Banaban Human Rights Defenders Network, facing the registration hurdle; and rights-holders generally, by the reversed ICCPR commitment and the still-missing national human rights institution.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 11 March 2016, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The founding manifesto — substantially delivered. His 2016 "Te Motinnano" manifesto promised the first-ever $2-per-kilo copra price, free education for senior secondary students, an anti-corruption committee, and the long-term KV20 vision (Kiribati Vision for 20 Years, 2016–2036). The copra price was doubled on taking office and later raised again (Test 1); free education was extended to Form 7; the anti-corruption committee and the 2017 National Anti-Corruption Strategy exist; KV20 remains the stated frame his partners cite a decade on — Australia's High Commissioner invoked it at the October 2026 hospital seawall handover (Office of Te Beretitenti; UNOPS, 23 October 2026). In March 2026 Maamau claimed 149 manifesto commitments from the previous term delivered — his own count, recorded as his claim, not independently audited in the record assembled here (BPA News, 16 March 2026).
  • The social-protection word — kept, with the receipts in Test 4: a wealthier, healthier, peaceful nation that "leaves no one behind" was the 2020 manifesto's stated drive (Policy Statement to the 12th Parliament, 24 August 2020), and the 2023/24 survey shows poverty at 5.5% nationally from 21.9% — the strongest kept-word evidence in this dossier.
  • The climate word — kept as stated: he said from COP23 (2017) onward that Kiribati would adapt and thrive rather than sink or leave, and the same words, with the same asks (1.5°C, Loss and Damage, maritime zones that persist), were delivered at UNGA in September 2025 and again in September 2026, with physical works — the Tungaru hospital seawall — commissioned in October 2026 (UN General Debate records; UNOPS, 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The ICCPR commitment — reversed, on the UN's record. During its third UPR cycle Kiribati committed to ratify the remaining core international human rights treaties, including the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. In its fourth cycle (reviewed 28 April 2025, outcome adopted 25 September 2025) it reversed that commitment — a reversal significant enough that the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights, Volker Türk, wrote to the government about it on 8 January 2026 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026, reporting the High Commissioner's letter). This is the clearest dated broken word in the file: a commitment made to an international body, then withdrawn.
  • The governance word vs. the judiciary record. KV20 carries a good-governance pillar committing the government to counter corruption and uphold the rule of law, and on appointing his Attorney General as Acting Chief Justice in October 2022 the President said his government "is committed to upholding the rule of law and respect of the judiciary" (2022 crisis record). The documented record beside those words is Test 2: every superior judge suspended, a removal the UN rapporteur found violated international standards, and a debate refused in 2025. The words were repeated while the acts continued.
  • Raising the islands — stated at a scale the record cannot yet carry. After his 2020 re-election Maamau unveiled plans to combat sea-level rise by raising Kiribati's islands, partly through dredging and foreign support. A decade-scale promise cannot be failed in year six of its telling, and this shelf does not fail it; but the documented delivery by November 2026 is protective works at specific sites — seawalls such as Tungaru's — not raised land at settlement scale. Recorded as receding relative to its original framing, to be graded again on a longer record.
  • The China switch — a word question of a different shape. The 2019 switch was not a broken campaign promise; it was a major act taken mid-term without the consultation his own MPs expected (Test 1), and his 2020 re-election was then won running on the closer-China course openly, against Banuera Berina's promise to reverse it — so from 2020 the course itself was the platform the voters chose, and under the mandate rule its continuation earns Test 5 credit, not blame. What is graded against him is the 2019 manner, in Tests 1 and 3, not the policy.

The two axes, named separately: Word-keeping and helped/hurt are independent axes, and this record contains these crossings: 1. Kept his word AND it helped people — the copra price, free education and the social-protection build-out: promised in 2016 and 2020, delivered, and measured in the 2023/24 poverty fall (Tests 1, 4). 2. Kept his word AND it still hurt people — the adapt-in-place climate stance: stated in 2017, kept at every UNGA since, and its keeping carries the documented risk recorded in Test 4 — a population held in place on low atolls, 23.6% of it one shock above the poverty line, if the building is outrun. The keeping earns the credit here; the risk stands in the ledger in full. His persistence with the Lambourne removal, pursued to its stated end through 2024, has the same shape on a smaller file: the course was followed through, and the hurt is recorded in Test 2. 3. Went against his word AND it helped people — not documented as a clean case. The nearest instance, recorded with its limit stated: the Forum withdrawal of July 2022 was publicly justified as final in form ("with immediate effect"), and its reversal in February 2023 helped Kiribati and the region — but a withdrawn diplomatic position is a changed position, not a pledge broken, and this dossier does not promote it into the crossing. 4. Broke his word AND it hurt people — the ICCPR ratification commitment, reversed in the 2025 UPR cycle, which hurt the rights-holders the covenant exists to protect and drew the High Commissioner's January 2026 letter; and the rule-of-law commitment restated in October 2022 while the suspensions it contradicted were still in force.

Why: The domestic word — copra, school, allowances, "leaves no one behind" — is the best-kept word in this series' recent Pacific files, and it is kept in survey data, not just in speeches. The institutional word — rule of law, judicial respect, treaty ratification — is broken in documents his own government signed. A ruler who keeps the word people eat by and breaks the word that restrains him is MIXED, with the two halves named rather than averaged away.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — copra cutters, students, pensioners and benefit recipients, by promises kept in cash and kind; Kiribati's diplomatic position, by a climate word consistent enough that partners build on it. Hurt — those who relied on the ICCPR commitment (rights-holders, and the civil-society groups who had organised around ratification); the judges of Test 2, against whom the rule-of-law words were spoken while the suspensions stood; and future litigants, who inherit the precedent that a president's stated respect for the judiciary need not bind his hand.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Taneti Maamau has held Kiribati's presidency for a decade, won three elections, and — on the evidence that can be counted — materially improved the lives of his country's poorest people: national poverty down from 21.9% to 5.5% between the 2019/20 and 2023/24 surveys, inequality down with it, on fishing revenue shared through a copra guarantee that rose from $1 to $4 a kilo, free senior secondary education, and a social-protection floor that did not exist at this scale before him (Test 4's why; helped: outer-island and rural households above all; hurt: the 7,000 still below the line, concentrated in South Tarawa). That record is why Tests 1, 4 and 5 are not failures: the everyday law runs for everyone, the count rose for almost everyone, and the domestic word was kept. The failure is specific, documented, and still open: in 2022, when the courts ruled against his government in the case of the one judge married to his opposition leader, he suspended the judge, the chief justice, and the entire Court of Appeal, attempted a deportation against a court order, installed his Attorney General as acting chief justice, completed the removal through proceedings the UN's rapporteur found violated international standards, and in August 2025 refused even a parliamentary debate on the suspended appeal court (Test 2's why; hurt: the four suspended judges, and every litigant who needed a superior court). Test 1 is MIXED because the same hand that wrote one law for copra and schooling wrote a second law at the source of power — the 2024 presidential ballot from which his party's supermajority excluded every opposition name. Test 3 is MIXED because the wobble record splits cleanly by who is pushing: Forum leaders bearing an apology and New Zealand bearing an aid programme both got open, signed adjustments — re-entry on 24 February 2023, a Statement of Partnership on 19 January 2026 — while judges, rapporteurs and his own opposition got three years of refusal to look. Test 5 is MIXED because the two axes cross in both directions in his record: the word people eat by was kept and helped; the ICCPR word was broken and hurt; the climate word was kept and still carries its risk in full. The open files that will move these grades are named plainly: whether a fully independent Court of Appeal is restored and its suspended judges' cases resolved; whether the ICCPR commitment is re-made; and whether, when his third and final term ends in 2028, the seat passes — on a ballot his opponents were allowed onto — to whoever the voters choose.
