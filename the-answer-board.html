The Answer Board

The board gives you the answer. You supply the fact that fits it. Pick a square, read the statement, and type the person, place, or thing it describes — the way the documented quiz-board game has worked for decades: six categories, five clues each at rising values, hidden wager squares, a doubled second round, and one final clue everyone wagers on. This is our own version of that form — our name, our categories, every clue written here from scratch.

The stakes, plainly: a right answer adds the square's value to your score. A wrong answer subtracts it. Scores can go below zero. That is the game — knowing when a guess is worth the risk is half of it.

Standard game: two rounds, wrong answers subtract, two wager squares per board.

Set up your game

Any setting changed from standard makes the game a variant: the banner at the top says so while you play, and your record labels the game the same way.

Choosing squares

Hunt your strong shelves. Play the categories you know first, top to bottom: the points bank early, and the board's control stays with whoever answered last — so a strong category is also a way to keep picking. Trade-off: everyone can see your strong shelf emptying; in versus play a rival may jump into it to deny you the big squares.

Start low, climb. Open a new category at 100 to test its flavour before the 500 is at stake. Trade-off: slower scoring, and a rival may take the top squares while you test.

Hunt the wager squares. They hide most often where nobody has looked; sweeping fresh categories raises your chance of finding one while your score is high enough to bet big. Trade-off: a wager square found at a low score is worth little — the most you can bet is your score or the board's top value, whichever is bigger.

Wager squares

Bet to cover. If a rival is within reach, bet enough that a right answer puts you beyond twice their score — then they cannot catch you in one square. Trade-off: a miss costs the same amount; cover bets are big bets.

Bet small when ahead late. Late in a round with a lead, a small bet keeps the lead safe either way. Trade-off: you give up the chance to bury the game early.

Bet big when behind. A trailing score that plays safe stays trailing; the wager square is the one place a big swing is offered to you. Trade-off: a miss can put you out of reach of the final.

The final wager — from each seat

Leader: cover second place. Bet (second's score × 2) − your score + 100. If you answer right, second cannot pass you even with a maximum bet. Trade-off: if you miss and second bet small, the cover bet can hand them the game — some leaders bet one step less and dare second to bet it all.

Second place: bet on the leader missing. A wager that wins the game when the leader misses — often everything, or everything minus a small keep — beats a wager that only wins if you both answer right. Trade-off: if the leader answers right, no wager you make matters, so this costs nothing extra.

Third place (or far behind): bet it all. Only a double-up plus misses above you can win; a careful bet wins nothing. Trade-off: none that matters — a safe loss and a bold loss are both losses.

Solo: the final is you against your own best. Bet what a right answer is worth to your final total — the Coach panel works the numbers on your actual score.

Rules

Pick a square. The statement on it describes a fact; type that fact. Right adds the value, wrong subtracts it (unless you set the no-subtraction variant). Whoever answers right picks next. When someone misses, the other contestants get one chance at the same clue, in turn. Two squares per board (standard) hide a wager: find one and, before seeing the clue, you bet any amount from 5 up to your score — or up to the board's top value if your score is lower. Round two doubles every value. After the last round, one final clue: everyone wagers in secret, then answers.

The bots, honestly

Bots do not “know” anything. Each bot answer is a dice roll against the printed chance for that level and clue value, and when a bot's roll succeeds it is shown the right answer, same as you. On a wager square a bot bets half of the most it is allowed (rounded to 100). In the final, bots wager by seat: the leader bets to cover second place, second bets everything, third bets everything.

Round-two chances use the round-one row for the same square (a 400 square in round two rolls at the 200 row's chance, and so on). The final clue rolls at each bot's 300-row chance.

How your typed answer is matched

Your answer is compared kindly: capitals and punctuation are ignored, “a”, “an” and “the” are ignored, common alternate forms are accepted, and a small spelling slip (one letter off, two in a long answer) still counts. If the board says no and you believe you were right, that is a matching limit, not a knowledge verdict — the correct fact is always shown.

Your record — on this device only

Saved on this device only, in this browser. Nothing is sent anywhere, there is no account, and Forget wipes it. Every game is stored in one clean shape: date, alias, mode, bot level, rounds, variant label, final scores, result, and per-clue category results.

Tap to open — The clue bank and fact discipline

Fact discipline: every clue in the bank states a fact we are certain of — capitals, counts, and well-settled records. Facts that are genuinely disputed (the longest river, the first inventor of contested machines) are not used, even where they would make a tempting square. A smaller honest bank beats a bigger shaky one. Each game draws six fresh categories per round from the bank, so boards differ game to game.

The board, values, wager squares, and final wager are the generic documented mechanics of the quiz-board form. The name, categories, clues, explanations, and all text here are our own.
