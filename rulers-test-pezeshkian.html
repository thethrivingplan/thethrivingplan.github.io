The Ruler's Test — Masoud Pezeshkian, President of Iran

Subject: Masoud Pezeshkian — President of Iran since 28 July 2024, elected in the snap election called after President Ebrahim Raisi's death, defeating Saeed Jalili in the 5 July 2024 runoff with 53.6% of the vote. A cardiac surgeon, former Minister of Health (2001–2005), and five-term member of the Majlis from Tabriz; he is of Azerbaijani and Kurdish descent and campaigned partly in both languages. This dossier grades the presidency on its own constitutional weight, and that weight is limited by design: under Iran's constitution the Supreme Leader — Ali Khamenei until his killing on 28 February 2026, and since 8–9 March 2026 his son Mojtaba Khamenei, chosen by the Assembly of Experts — holds final authority over the armed forces, the judiciary's head, state broadcasting, and all major state policy. The Supreme Leader is a separate seat, graded separately in this series; where his overriding authority bears on a test below, it is stated as a documented structural fact, not as an excuse and not as a verdict on the president. Pezeshkian remains president in November 2026. After Ali Khamenei's killing he sat ex officio on the three-member Interim Leadership Council (with Chief Justice Gholam-Hossein Mohseni-Ejei and Guardian Council jurist Alireza Arafi) under Article 111 until the succession; reporting since describes his civilian government as heavily sidelined from military and security decisions by the IRGC, while he was authorized by Mojtaba Khamenei to negotiate the June 2026 memorandum of understanding with the United States (Wikipedia summary of Pezeshkian's presidency, November 2026; JINSA Iran War Update, 9 November 2026). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source, within the space the presidency actually holds.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He fired his own close confidant under the same standard he preached. On 5 April 2025 Pezeshkian dismissed Shahram Dabiri, his vice president for parliamentary affairs and a personal friend since their Tabriz years, after a photograph of Dabiri's luxury Antarctica cruise during the Nowruz holidays caused public outrage. His decree stated the rule in terms that applied it upward: "In a context where economic pressure on the population remains high… expensive leisure trips by officials, even if paid out of their own pocket, are neither defensible nor justifiable" (presidential decree via IRNA, 5 April 2025; AP reporting, April 2025). Dabiri had resisted resigning; the president removed him anyway.
  • When police killed a detainee, his government let the law reach its own officers. After Mohammad Mir-Mousavi died in police custody in Lahijan in August 2024, the local prosecutor announced the arrest of five police officers, and Pezeshkian personally ordered a thorough investigation; Iran confirmed to the UN that the officers were temporarily suspended (UN Secretary-General's report on human rights in Iran, A/HRC/59/22, June 2025).
  • He accepted parliament's verdict against his own minister rather than shielding him. When the Majlis impeached Economy Minister Abdolnasser Hemmati on 2 March 2025 (182–89), Pezeshkian defended Hemmati in the chamber — "the country's economic challenges cannot be attributed to one individual alone" — and then complied with the vote and replaced him, using no extra-constitutional instrument to keep him (Reuters and AP reporting, 2 March 2025).
  • His cabinet itself was formed by one public rule: a cross-factional "national unity" slate, including principlist figures, which the Majlis approved in full on 21 August 2024 — the first complete cabinet approval since 2001 (Majlis confidence votes, 21 August 2024).

Evidence against:

  • His one documented use of favor at the source was the system's highest currency: the Leader's name. To overcome parliamentary opposition to Farzaneh Sadegh, his nominee for Roads and Urban Development Minister, Pezeshkian told the Majlis that Sadegh was Ayatollah Khamenei's own choice and that the Leader had approved his cabinet — securing her confirmation by borrowed authority rather than by the argument applied to every other nominee (Wilson Center analysis of the confirmation, September 2024). The appointment itself is graded in Test 4; the method belongs here.
  • The law that reached his deputy over a holiday photograph has produced no documented corruption case against anyone in his own circle in twenty-eight months — no prosecution of a Pezeshkian appointee for graft appears in the record assembled here, in a state whose own officials describe corruption as systemic (Iranian officials' statements compiled in the economic-crisis record, 2025–2026). Absence of a case is not proof of protection; it is recorded as the limit of the for-shelf, not as a finding.
  • Under fiscal collapse in 2026, the presidency's discretionary hand moved toward the coercive apparatus: reporting documents that, as the government struggled to pay salaries, Pezeshkian diverted 20 million barrels of oil specifically to finance the IRGC (Iranian economic crisis record, citing 2026 reporting). The armed forces' pay is a state obligation; the documented prioritization is nonetheless a choice about whose claim on the state is honored first, made by decree rather than by a general rule.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, media, the electoral authority, the prosecution, and the central bank keep what autonomy the system allows — and does the presidency, in the space it controls, add grip or restraint?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He used the one brake he chairs to stop a law rather than to seize an instrument. The "Hijab and Chastity" law — passed by the Majlis in September 2023, carrying fines of $800 and $1,500 for first and second offenses and up to 15 years' imprisonment for a third — was due to reach his government in December 2024, and by law he would have had five days to endorse it and no power to veto it. Pezeshkian had called it a law with "many questions and ambiguities" that his administration could not implement; on 14–16 December 2024 the Supreme National Security Council, which the president chairs, intervened to halt its promulgation, and his vice president for parliamentary affairs announced it would "not be referred to the government by the parliament for now" (AP reporting, 17 December 2024; Brandeis Crown Center Middle East Brief No. 162, December 2024). The law remains formally suspended in November 2026.
  • The referees he does not control ruled and acted against his government's preferences in the open, and he did not retaliate against the ones he could reach: the Majlis that impeached his economy minister and initially blocked his Sunni vice-presidential appointee (25 September 2024, 129–107 against accepting Abdolkarim Hosseinzadeh's resignation to take the post; reversed on a second vote, and Hosseinzadeh took office 2 November 2024) suffered no presidential reprisal documented in the record (AFP/IRNA reporting, September–November 2024).
  • The central bank governorship changed by resignation and appointment, not purge: Governor Mohammad Reza Farzin resigned amid the December 2025 currency collapse and protest onset, and Pezeshkian appointed Hemmati — the minister parliament had removed — to the post (protests record, December 2025). The appointment of a previously impeached minister is a judgment critics may grade elsewhere; as a grip fact it used the ordinary legal instrument and is recorded as such.

Evidence against:

  • The total internet shutdown of January–May 2026 was imposed by "the authorities" of the state he heads as president and whose security council he chairs: a nationwide blackout from 8 January 2026 during the protest crackdown, and again from 28 February to late May 2026 during the war, restricting communication inside the country and the flow of information about the killings to the outside world (2025–2026 protests and massacres records, 2026). No public act by Pezeshkian opposing, limiting, or taking responsibility for the shutdown appears in the record; his government administered a state whose information space was switched off at the moments of its greatest violence. The structural fact stands beside it: communications restriction decisions in Iran run through security bodies answerable upward to the Supreme Leader, not to the presidency alone.
  • Media autonomy narrowed on his watch without presidential resistance: Iran's press operates under a judiciary and a state broadcaster (IRIB) whose head the Supreme Leader appoints — outside presidential control, stated structurally — but the executive branch Pezeshkian leads joined the late-2025 escalation over cultural enforcement, his Government Information Council confirming the authenticity of a leaked directive from Ali Khamenei ordering intensified action on hijab and "cultural dissent" while describing the leak as "incomplete" and "exaggerated" (NIAC Iran Unfiltered digest of the November–December 2025 directive episode). The presidency's contribution to the information order was confirmation and softening language, not protection of the journalists and outlets the directive targeted.
  • The Guardian Council's vetting — the instrument that decides who may stand for the presidency and the Majlis at all — operated before him and above him; he was himself its beneficiary in 2024 as the sole reformist-leaning candidate permitted to run after being disqualified in 2021. He has neither the constitutional power to reform it nor any documented attempt to use his office against it; it is recorded here because it bounds what "holds without gripping" can mean for this seat: the tightest grip in the system is not his hand, and the test grades only that he did not add a second one of his own.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When the Majlis, the Guardian Council, the street, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The single most direct answer to the street by any president of the Islamic Republic is his. After the December 2025–January 2026 protests and crackdown, Pezeshkian said on national television on 11 February 2026, the anniversary of the revolution: "We are ashamed before the people, and we are obligated to assist all those who were harmed in these incidents. We are not seeking confrontation with the people." He added, "We consider protest a blessing… we are ready to listen to the voice of the people" (televised address, 11 February 2026, via BBC Persian and Arab News reporting). No predecessor in the office had publicly apologized for a crackdown.
  • His first answer to the same protests, before the killing peaked, was dialogue: on 30 December 2025, two days into the unrest, he announced that officials would engage in dialogue with protest representatives and listen to their demands, and the central bank governor's resignation was accepted in the same days (IPRC rhetoric analysis of December 2025–February 2026 official statements; protests record).
  • Pushed by parliament, he adjusts in the open and absorbs the cost: he defended Hemmati to the chamber, lost, and complied (Test 1); when the Majlis blocked Hosseinzadeh's move to the vice presidency, he waited for the lawful second vote rather than installing him by decree (September–November 2024).
  • Pushed by war, he moved toward negotiation and said so plainly: authorized by Mojtaba Khamenei, he signed the memorandum of understanding with President Trump on 17 June 2026 to end the war and reopen the Strait of Hormuz; when Trump declared the MOU "over" on 8 July 2026 and fire resumed, Pezeshkian continued to state Iran's terms publicly, and on 9 November 2026 said the Supreme Leader "ordered us to end the no-war, no-peace situation," adding "we can hold talks with dignity and bring the matter to an end with dignity" (JINSA Iran War Update, 9 November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Between the dialogue offer and the apology came the denial. On 11 January 2026, at the height of the crackdown, Pezeshkian accused the United States and Israel of being behind the unrest and alleged foreign-backed "terrorists" were responsible — the standard externalization his February apology only partly walked back, and which he paired even in the apology speech with criticism of "Western propaganda" (protests/massacres record, 11 January 2026; apology coverage, 11 February 2026). The sequence matters: grievances acknowledged on 30 December, foreign plot alleged on 11 January, shame expressed on 11 February — after the suppression was complete.
  • On the count of the dead, his government did not adjust: the state maintained its figure of 3,117 deaths (2,447 of them described as civilians and security forces) and published an official list of 2,986 names, rejecting the documented higher counts — HRANA's named, verified list of 7,007 (23 February 2026) overlaps the official list by fewer than 100 names per Iran International's comparison — and no revision of the official figure appears in the record through November 2026 (official figures via the Pezeshkian administration/SNSC, February 2026; HRANA report, 23 February 2026). This is graded again as a count in Test 4; here it is the wobble refused.
  • On executions, the wobble has never been answered at all. UN Special Rapporteurs described more than 1,000 executions in the first nine months of 2025 — "an unprecedented execution spree… at an industrial scale" — and the UN Fact-Finding Mission confirmed over 1,000 in 2025 by October, the highest in three decades (UN Special Rapporteurs' statement, September–October 2025; UN Fact-Finding Mission statement, October 2025). The judiciary that carries out the sentences is headed by a Supreme Leader appointee and is not Pezeshkian's to command — the structural fact is stated — but no public word of his against the pace, and no use of the presidential pardon advocacy his office does hold, appears in the record.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include women, the ethnic and religious minorities, the protesters and the dead, the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The inclusion he could deliver by appointment, he delivered — at a level without precedent in the Islamic Republic. Farzaneh Sadegh became Minister of Roads and Urban Development (21 August 2024), only the second woman cabinet minister since 1979 and the first to lead a traditionally male-dominated ministry; Fatemeh Mohajerani became the first woman government spokesperson in the republic's history; Shina Ansari (environment) and Zahra Behrouz-Azar (women and family affairs) took vice-presidential posts; the government reported over 70 women appointed to senior positions by January 2025 (UN Secretary-General's report, A/HRC/59/22, June 2025; Wilson Center, September 2024; Iran Daily, February 2025).
  • The minorities' appointments were likewise firsts, not tokens of an existing pattern: Arash Zerehtan Lahoni, a Sunni Kurd, became governor-general of Kurdistan Province on 18 September 2024 — the first Sunni provincial governor since the earliest days of the revolution; Mansour Bijar became the first Baluch governor of Sistan-Baluchistan; Mohammad Reza Mavalizadeh the first Arab governor of Khuzestan; and Abdolkarim Hosseinzadeh, a Sunni Kurd, became vice president for rural development and deprived regions, the highest-ranking Sunni official since 1979 (AFP and Reuters reporting, August–November 2024; NIAC digest, September 2024). Pezeshkian, himself of Azerbaijani and Kurdish descent, stated the principle in cabinet on 18 September 2024: selection "regardless of political affiliation, religion, gender, ethnicity, or race."
  • After the January 2026 killings, his government did publish names: an official list of 2,986 victims, and his February apology pledged assistance to "all those who were harmed," without a loyalty test stated in it (official list, February 2026; televised address, 11 February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The official count of the January 2026 dead excludes most of the dead. The government's figure is 3,117; HRANA's verified, named list reached 7,007 by 23 February 2026 with over 11,000 further cases under review; Iran International's editorial board, citing leaked documents, estimated more than 36,500 killed in the 8–9 January crackdown alone, and Time reported a hospital-registered list of 30,304 deaths for those two days (HRANA, 23 February 2026; Iran International, 25 January–3 February 2026; Time, 25 January 2026). The true figure is CONTESTED across an order of magnitude — but on every independent count, the majority of the dead are absent from the accounting issued by the state Pezeshkian heads, and 53,552 arrests were recorded in the same weeks (protests casualty compilations, 2026). A count that omits most of those it counts is the test's definition of counting out. The structural fact stands and is stated once: the crackdown itself was reportedly initiated under orders from Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and senior security officials to use live fire (former officials' attributions compiled in the massacres record) — the killing was ordered above the presidency; the counting was done by it.
  • The execution ledger fell hardest on the counted-out, and rose on his watch: over 1,200 executions were recorded from the start of his presidency to mid-2025, and at least 1,172 in 2025 alone by October — nearly half for non-violent drug offenses, disproportionately targeting ethnic minorities, women, and the poor who cannot buy competent defense (UN written statement, A/HRC/59/NGO/248, June 2025; Abdorrahman Boroumand Center, October 2025; UN Special Rapporteurs, 2025). The sentences are the judiciary's, not his — stated structurally — but the flock they are drawn from is the one his campaign promised to protect, and his government's silence is part of the record.
  • The economic count left the poor further behind on every measure his own statisticians publish: inflation of 48.6% in October 2025 and 42.2% in December 2025, then 65.8% year-on-year by April 2026 on the Central Bank's figure, with later 2026 readings reported between 88.6% and 200%; the rial fell from roughly 600,000 per dollar when he took office to 1,750,000 in December 2025 and an all-time low of 2,020,000 on 24 August 2026; poverty estimates in March 2025 already ranged from 22% to 50% of the population (Statistical Center of Iran and Central Bank figures as compiled in the economic-crisis record, 2025–2026). War, blockade (the US naval blockade imposed 14 July 2026), and sanctions are documented causes alongside mismanagement; the test counts outcomes, and the outcome is that the poor of his first campaign speeches are poorer, in larger numbers, than when he promised them relief.
  • The periphery paid the highest price in the crackdown's geography as in the economy: the misery index peaked at 96.3% in Kurdistan region against a national 91%, and the 2026 Kurdish–Iranian crisis within the protest period fell on the same provinces his appointments had honored (economic-crisis record, 2026; protests record, 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since July 2024, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The hijab law — kept, by the only route open to him. He campaigned against coercive enforcement, said during the campaign that he would follow up so the morality police would not bother people, and in office called the new Hijab and Chastity law ambiguous and unenforceable; his security council halted it in December 2024 and it has never taken effect (campaign statements, June–July 2024; AP, December 2024). Street-level enforcement visibly receded: morality police were "reined in since the election of Pezeshkian" and largely absent from Tehran streets after the June 2025 war, SMS hijab warnings sent during the war were apologized for by the government as sent by mistake, and unveiled women in major cities faced no serious enforcement through late 2025 (Middle East Institute analysis, 2025). Compulsory hijab remains the law on the books — he never promised, and never had the power, to repeal it; the promise he made, against coercion, is the one measured here.
  • Internet — first step delivered on schedule. He promised to lift internet restrictions; on 24 December 2024 the Supreme Council of Cyberspace, in a meeting he headed, voted to unblock WhatsApp and Google Play, and his communications minister called it "the first step in removing internet limitations… this path will continue" (Reuters/IRNA, 24 December 2024).
  • Inclusion — delivered as appointments (Test 4): women and Sunni and ethnic-minority officials in posts none had held since 1979, matching his campaign's explicit criticism of their exclusion.
  • Engagement — attempted to the end. He promised to pursue sanctions relief through negotiation; his government negotiated with the United States in 2025 before the June war, and in 2026 he signed the 17 June MOU with Trump and continued publicly seeking a dignified settlement after its collapse (JINSA update, November 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Internet — the path did not continue; it reversed. Instagram and Telegram remain blocked, no further platform was unblocked after December 2024, and in January 2026 the state imposed the longest total internet shutdown in its history during the crackdown, repeated during the February–May 2026 war (protests record, 2026). Whatever body ordered the switch, the promise of an opening internet ended in a blackout administered by the state he presides over.
  • Economic relief — the central promise, unkept on the numbers. He was elected to "revive the economy and improve daily lives"; by his second year inflation had roughly doubled from the ~35–40% he inherited, the rial had lost two-thirds of its remaining value by December 2025 and more thereafter, rolling blackouts had become routine since late 2024 — halting production in roughly half the industrial sector by 2026 — and UN sanctions snapped back on 28 September 2025, the opposite of the relief he had sought (economic-crisis record, 2025–2026; snapback, September 2025). War and blockade are documented co-causes; the promise is graded against outcomes because he made it against outcomes.
  • Sanctions relief — not delivered. The engagement promise produced talks, then war (June 2025), then an MOU that expired within three weeks of signature (17 June – 8 July 2026), then blockade. His word was honestly attempted — the failure is recorded as a promise unkept, not as bad faith, and the distinction is stated because the record supports it.
  • The June 2025 war file, stated exactly: Israel struck on 13 June 2025; Iran's official toll was 1,060 killed and 5,800 wounded, HRANA's 1,190 killed (436 civilians, 435 military, 319 unidentified). On 16 June an Israeli strike on the building where Pezeshkian sat in a Supreme National Security Council meeting attempted his assassination; he escaped through an emergency exit with a minor leg injury (Fars News Agency and US intelligence confirmations, June–July 2025). His documented acts in the war were to order suspension of cooperation with the IAEA and to serve as the government's public face; command of the war itself sat, constitutionally and in fact, with the Supreme Leader and the IRGC. No promise of his caused or could have prevented the war; it is recorded because it consumed the presidency's second year and everything else on this page.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Masoud Pezeshkian holds the weakest strong office in this series: a presidency that administers a state whose sword, bench, broadcaster, and ballot-box gate all answer to a throne above it — a throne that changed occupants by assassination in the middle of his term. Graded on what the seat itself did, the record is unusually legible. He fired his own friend for a holiday photograph and let parliament take his economy minister; he spent his one real institutional brake to stop a hijab law he had promised to stop, and unblocked the first two platforms he had promised to unblock; he put women, Sunnis, Kurds, Baluch, and Arabs into offices none had held since 1979; and when the street rose against the economy he had failed to repair, he first offered dialogue, then repeated the foreign-plot line while the killing proceeded, then did what no president of the republic had done — apologized, by name, in shame, on television. Against all of that stands the count, and it is decisive: a crackdown ordered above him but administered and accounted by the state he heads killed thousands whose names his government's list does not carry, the gallows worked at a thirty-year record pace in silence from his office, and the poor he campaigned to rescue are measurably poorer and more numerous. The seat did not grip what it did not already hold, did not favor its friends beyond one borrowed name, and kept the promises that were within its reach — but the flock it was elected to count was counted out on its watch, in its own published figures, and the test that measures the counting cannot pass it.
