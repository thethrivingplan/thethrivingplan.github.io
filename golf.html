1 · Ball-flight laws — the why under everything

Read the ball first, the body second. Every shot you will ever hit is decided in the roughly half-millisecond the club meets the ball, by two things: where the clubface points and which way the clubhead is travelling (the path). Everything else in this page — grip, stance, faults, fixes — is just a way of changing those two numbers.

What works — the modern laws, as measured
  • Face sends it. The ball starts mostly where the face points at impact — about 75–85% of the start direction on iron shots, even more with a driver. Path contributes the rest.
  • Path bends it. The ball curves away from the path, toward the face. If the face points right of the path, the ball curves right (for a right-hander). Face and path pointing the same way = a straight shot — which is why truly straight is the hardest shot in golf.
  • The gap is the curve. The bigger the face-to-path difference, the more the ball bends. A slice is not a mystery swing flaw; it is a large gap, usually path well left, face well right of it.
  • Strike height and height on the face matter too: low on the face launches lower with more spin; high launches higher with less. Toe and heel strikes twist the face — see gear effect below.
Light · Two questions diagnose any shot: Where did it start? Which way did it bend? Start line tells you the face; bend tells you face vs. path.
Coach · The old teaching said "path starts it, face bends it." Launch monitors (Trackman, FlightScope) reversed that: face dominates start direction. If an older book or video tells you the reverse, it is repeating the pre-monitor law — the ball itself grades it wrong. Numbers to hold: a mid-iron face 2° open with a square path starts right and bends further right (a push-fade). Same face with path 4° left starts near target and slices. Same start, different bend, different fix — which is why "it went right" is not a diagnosis.
What works — gear effect, in plain words

Hit the ball off the toe of a wood and the clubhead twists — and the ball spins the opposite way, hooking back toward the middle. Hit it off the heel and it fades back. The club "gears" against the ball like two gear wheels meshing. This is why a toe hit with a driver often draws even when your swing didn't change, and why blaming your path for every curve is wrong: where on the face is a third witness. Chalk, foot spray, or impact tape on the face tells you the truth.

What really hasn't
  • "The path starts the ball." The pre-launch-monitor law. Measured and retired — face starts it.
  • "Sidespin is a separate thing you add." There is one spin axis; it tilts. Curve is tilt, not a second kind of spin you can coach away independently.
  • "Just aim left to fix a slice." Aiming left usually makes the path go more left while the face stays put — the gap grows and the slice gets bigger. It treats the symptom by feeding the cause.

2 · The swing as a chain

One link at a time. Each link lists what it does, and the fault it causes when it's off — and every fault reads back to the laws in section 1.

1 · Grip — your only contact with the club Tap to open · Tap again to close

What it does: The grip decides where the face wants to return at impact. Hands turned more to the right on the handle (a "strong" grip, right-hander) bias the face closed; hands turned left ("weak") bias it open. Placement matters as much as turn: handle in the fingers gives the wrists freedom to hinge and release; handle buried in the palms locks that down and slows the face.

Pressure: Squeezing hard tenses the forearms, and tense forearms can't hinge or release on time — the face arrives late and open. A useful scale: hold it like a tube of toothpaste you don't want to squirt — firm enough not to drop, light enough to feel the clubhead's weight.

When it's off: Weak grip + everything else fine = ball starts right and fades further right (face open). Over-strong grip = starts left, hooks (face closed). The grip is the cheapest face fix there is: it changes impact without changing your swing.

Light · Check your grip before you rebuild your swing. Most persistent one-way misses start in the hands.
Coach · Overlap, interlock, or ten-finger (baseball) grip? All three are played at the highest level. Interlock suits smaller hands, ten-finger suits sore fingers and can add release. The style is a What To Try; the face bias the grip creates is What Works physics. Change grip turn in small steps — half a knuckle at a time — and watch the start line, not just the finish.
2 · Setup & alignment — deciding the shot before you swing Tap to open · Tap again to close

What it does: Feet, hips, and shoulders set the path's neighbourhood; the face, set behind the ball, sets the start line. Ball position sets where in the arc you meet it: forward (driver, off the lead heel) catches the club travelling up; middle-to-back (short irons) catches it descending.

When it's off: Shoulders aimed left with feet aimed at target is the classic slicer's setup — path goes where the shoulders point, face goes where it was aimed, gap opens, slice. Ball too far back with an iron = thin, low shots; too far forward = fat or topped, because the arc's low point is behind the ball.

Coach · Use an intermediate target: a spot on the ground two feet in front of the ball, on the line you want it to start. Aiming at something near is measurably easier than aiming at a flag 180 yards away — and it aims the face, which is the part that sends it.
3 · Takeaway — the first two feet set the plane Tap to open · Tap again to close

What it does: A takeaway driven by the chest and shoulders turning — hands quiet — keeps the face square to the arc and the club on a path you can return on. Snatching the club inside with the hands rolls the face open; lifting it straight up with the arms disconnects club from body.

When it's off: Rolled-open face early usually stays open late (starts right, fades). Club whipped inside tends to come back over the top (path left) — the slice chain again, started in the first two feet.

What to try · Push a second ball straight back behind the first with the clubface at takeaway. If the spare ball rolls straight back, your first move is neutral. A feel, not a law — keep it only if your video agrees.
4 · Backswing — turn, don't sway; load, don't lift Tap to open · Tap again to close

What it does: The backswing stores energy: shoulders turn roughly twice the hips, weight presses into the trail foot, wrists hinge so the club sets on plane. Length is personal — a three-quarter swing with a square face beats a huge turn that loses the face.

When it's off: Swaying off the ball moves the arc's low point — fat and thin shots alternate. All-arms lift with no turn leaves nothing to power the downswing except a throw of the hands, which opens or closes the face at random.

What really hasn't

"Keep your head down" as a literal freeze. Pinning the chin stops the shoulders turning, so the arms lift instead — the cue creates the topped shot it claims to cure. What good players actually do: eyes on the ball, head free to rotate slightly, chest turning fully. Watch a tour swing in slow motion: heads move. Freeze is the myth; a steady gaze is the real skill.

5 · Transition — the half-second that decides path Tap to open · Tap again to close

What it does: The downswing starts from the ground: pressure shifts to the lead foot and the hips begin to unwind while the arms are still finishing the backswing. That sequence lets the club drop ("shallow") onto an inside path. Rushing — shoulders spinning first — throws the club outside the line: the over-the-top move, path hard left.

When it's off: Over-the-top + face square to target = pull-slice, the most common amateur shot in the game. It is a sequence problem, not an effort problem; swinging harder makes it worse.

Light · Feel the pause that isn't: finish the backswing, let the weight go first. Most golfers' transition improves the moment they stop trying to start down with their hands.
6 · Impact — the only position that counts Tap to open · Tap again to close

What it does: For irons: handle slightly ahead of the ball, weight mostly on the lead side, club still descending — ball first, then turf, divot after the ball. For driver: ball forward, club travelling slightly up. Impact is not a copy of setup; it's a different, more dynamic position.

When it's off: "Scooping" — handle behind the ball, weight back — adds loft, loses compression, and produces thin and fat shots on alternate swings. Trying to lift the ball in the air is the root error: the loft on the face does the lifting; your job is to deliver the face descending (irons) into the back of the ball.

7 · Finish — the receipt Tap to open · Tap again to close

What it does: A balanced finish — chest to target, weight fully on the lead foot, able to hold the pose — proves the forces stayed in sequence. You can't fake balance at speed.

When it's off: Falling back toward the trail foot means weight never transferred: expect weak, high, right shots. The finish doesn't cause anything; it reports everything. Read it like the ball flight — it's evidence.

3 · Swing schools — contested, shelved at the same size

Three philosophies coach most of the golf world. Each has produced tour winners and each has real costs. None is printed here as the only truth, because none is.

Traditional positions-based

The claim: Build the swing checkpoint by checkpoint — takeaway position, top position, impact position — and a repeatable swing is the sum of correct positions.

For it: Gives teacher and student a shared language; faults are easy to locate on video; decades of tour success.

Against it: Positions are snapshots of a motion that takes 1.2 seconds; golfers can hit every checkpoint stiffly and still time the face badly. Can produce mechanical, slow practice that doesn't transfer to the course.

Stack-and-tilt

The claim: Keep the weight favouring the lead side throughout, spine tilting toward the target at the top — strike becomes consistent because the low point stops moving.

For it: Genuinely helps chronic fat/thin strikers; its "fundamentals" (ball-first contact, predictable curve) are sound goals for anyone.

Against it: Can cost driver distance and height for some bodies; the steep shoulder turn it encourages bothers some backs; its strongest early claims ("this is how all good players swing") didn't survive measurement of players who clearly shift weight.

Natural / free-swing

The claim: The body self-organises around the task — throw the club toward the target, use external cues and games, minimal positions. Think of how you learned to throw a ball: nobody gave you checkpoints.

For it: Excellent for athletes, juniors, and the paralysed-by-analysis; skills learned by task tend to hold up under pressure.

Against it: "Just swing free" gives a true beginner no defence against a grip that guarantees a slice; self-organisation organises around your current habits, good or bad. Some structure is usually needed first.

What really hasn't — for every school

"The swing is one plane for everybody." Measured swings vary — upright, flat, and everything between win majors. Matching a swing to a magazine silhouette ignores limb length, mobility, and injury history. Any school that claims one geometry for all bodies is selling certainty the evidence doesn't carry.

Coach · Practical synthesis most good coaches land on: use positions as diagnostics (where was the face?), use task-based drills as training (can you start it on that line?), and let your ball flight — not loyalty to a school — grade the result.

4 · Faults & fixes — ball flight first, body second

Every fix below starts from the laws: what the face and path must have done, then the change most likely to move them, then one drill.

The slice — starts near target (or left), bends hard right Tap to open · Tap again to close

Ball-flight cause: Face open relative to path by a lot — classically path out-to-in (left) with the face nearer the target line. The gap is the slice.

Fix order: (1) Check the grip — strengthen it half a knuckle at a time. (2) Check shoulder alignment — aim shoulders at target, not left of it. (3) Then the path: feel the club start down from inside, trail elbow tucking toward your ribs.

Drill: Headcover (or a spare glove) placed just outside and behind the ball. Swing without hitting it: an out-to-in path clips it, an inside path misses it. Instant, honest feedback.

Light · Fix the face before the path. Half of all slices close up with grip alone — and face is 80% of the start line, so it's the fastest lever you own.
Coach · Transfer note: the headcover drill works on the range within a session. On the course it fades unless you keep one rehearsal feel — trail-elbow tuck — in your pre-shot routine. Expect the first week of path change to feel like you're swinging at first base. Video from down-the-line (section 7) confirms whether the feel is real.
The hook — starts left or bends hard left Tap to open · Tap again to close

Ball-flight cause: Face closed relative to path — often an in-to-out path with a face that has rolled shut, or an over-strong grip doing the closing by itself.

Fix order: (1) Weaken the grip slightly — rotate both hands a touch toward the target. (2) Check you're not aiming right and compensating. (3) Feel the face stay "looking at the ball longer" through impact rather than flipping.

Drill: Split-hands drill — hands two inches apart on the grip, half swings. It makes a flip obvious and teaches the forearms to stay quieter through the strike.

Fat — turf before ball, shot dies short Tap to open · Tap again to close

Ball-flight cause: The arc's low point is behind the ball: weight hanging back, or the club released early ("casting") so it bottoms out too soon. This one is a strike fault, not a face/path fault — the laws only apply once the ball is actually met cleanly.

Fix: Ball position back one ball-width, pressure into the lead foot earlier, handle ahead at impact.

Drill: Tee a ball normally but place a coin two inches in front of it; your job is ball, then brush the coin. Or spray the face / use a towel two inches behind the ball — hit the ball without moving the towel.

Thin — ball struck on its equator, low skimmer Tap to open · Tap again to close

Ball-flight cause: Low point at or above the ball's middle: standing up out of posture in the downswing, or weight back with the hands flipping up at it. Often the fat shot's sibling — same moving low point, opposite day.

Fix: Keep your height: feel your chest stay over the ball until impact, weight forward. If fat and thin alternate, the problem is a wandering low point, not two problems.

Drill: Tee the ball very low for iron practice and hit down through it into the turf; or practise from a slight downhill lie, which punishes standing up.

Topped — ball dribbles along the ground Tap to open · Tap again to close

Ball-flight cause: The club's leading edge meets the top half of the ball — the extreme thin. Usual chain: frozen head cue ("keep your head down!") locks the turn, arms lift the club, low point rises.

Fix: Free the turn (see the myth in the backswing section), keep the grip pressure light so the wrists can hinge, and let your eyes — not your whole head — stay with the ball.

Drill: Place a tee peg two inches in front of the ball, pushed almost flat. Strike ball then clip the tee out of the ground. It forces the club to stay down through the strike zone.

5 · Short game & putting

Chipping — landing-spot thinking

What works
  • Pick the landing spot, not the hole. A chip is a throw with a club: choose where the ball should land (a flat, predictable patch), then choose the club by the fly-to-roll ratio you need. Low loft (7- or 8-iron chip) flies short and rolls long; a wedge flies most of the way and checks sooner.
  • One motion, many clubs. Keep the chipping motion the same — weight forward, hands ahead, body turning through — and change clubs to change the shot. Changing the swing for every distance is three skills to maintain instead of one.
  • Get it rolling early. Around the green, a ball rolling on the ground behaves more predictably than a ball flying through the air — the old caddie's rule "putt when you can, chip when you can't putt, pitch only when you must" survives because it's true.
Coach · Trade-off, honestly: the high, soft pitch looks better and stops faster near a tight flag — and mishits cost more (fat into the fringe, thin through the green). For most handicap golfers the low runner takes the big number off the card. Tour players pitch more because their strike is tour-level; copy their strategy only with their strike.
What really hasn't

"Hands must stay dead and wrists locked on every chip." Rigid wrists on a long chip rob you of feel and distance control; and the flop shot is all wrist. The truthful version: quiet wrists for the short bump-and-run, hinge allowed as the shot grows.

What to try

The clock-face drill: same club, swing the arms to 8 o'clock, 9 o'clock, 10 o'clock, and write down how far each carries. Three distances you know beat ten distances you hope. Your numbers, your notebook — that's the try-and-see shelf working as intended.

Putting — pace before line

What works
  • Pace before line. From beyond 15 feet, almost all three-putts are pace failures, not line failures. A putt that finishes within a two-foot circle past the hole was well putted even if it missed — the read can be fixed; wild pace can't.
  • Read with your feet and your walk. Walking the low side of the line and feeling the slope through your feet is real information, not ritual. Break plays more as the ball slows — most borrow happens in the last third of the putt.
  • A routine beats a tip. Same number of looks, same practice stroke, same breath, every putt. Putting is the most routine-friendly shot in golf, and routine is what survives pressure.
Light · Ladder drill: putt to a fringe or a tee at 10, 20, 30, 40 feet, stopping inside a club-length. It trains pace alone, which is the skill that actually lowers scores.
Coach · Contested, shelved evenly: straight-back-straight-through vs. arc stroke. Every putter stroke arcs slightly (the putter swings around a tilted spine); the argument is about how much face rotation to allow. Gate drills and a start-line check (does the ball start on your intended line through a gate two feet out?) grade your stroke better than loyalty to either camp. If 9 of 10 start on line, your stroke is not your problem — your pace or read is.

6 · Strategy — the thinking game

What golfers actually play for: standing with the people in their group, a handicap trending down, a personal best, match play for pride or for who buys lunch. Name the stake before the round and the decisions below get easier — and note the honest part: the same thinking works with nothing on the round at all. A centre-of-green shot is smart at every stake level.

What works
  • Play to your miss. Your shots land in a scatter pattern, not on a point. Aim so the whole pattern fits the safe ground: if your 7-iron scatter is 20 yards wide, a flag 8 yards from water is not a 7-iron flag, whatever your best swing could do.
  • Centres beat flags, most of the time. Aim at the middle of the green unless the flag is in a wide, safe section and the shot is short. The maths of scoring average favours the fat part of the green for nearly every handicap.
  • Take your medicine. In trees, the shot back to the fairway costs one stroke; the hero shot through a gap costs one stroke plus whatever the ricochet adds. Bogey is a fine score from trouble; double is what greed makes of it.
  • Play the hole backwards. Choose the approach distance you like (a full wedge for most people), then pick the tee shot that leaves it — instead of hitting driver because it's a par 4.
What really hasn't

"Never lay up" / "always go for it." Both slogans fail the same way: they decide before looking. The honest skill is reading this lie, this wind, your scatter — and the slogans exist to save you from having to. Also retired: aiming at every flag because "pros do" — pros aim away from flags constantly; TV just shows you the ones that finish close.

Energy management — a named part of strategy

What works

Eighteen holes is four to five hours of walking and decision-making. Mechanics and judgement both degrade when you're tired, hungry, or dry — the last four holes punish an empty tank out of proportion to their length. The countermeasures are unglamorous and real: eat before you're hungry (a little, often), drink before you're thirsty, and pace the round — sit in the shade while others putt out, don't practise-swing yourself into fatigue on the tee. If your worst holes cluster late in the round, that is a fuel and pacing pattern before it is a swing pattern.

What to try · Grade your own record: in the practice log below, note which holes or which part of the session went wrong. Three rounds of "it fell apart after the 13th" is data, and the fix might be a banana, not a lesson.

Film yourself — the real self-check

This page cannot see your swing. Filming and a real teaching pro are the real checks, printed plainly. Two angles tell almost the whole story:

  • Down-the-line: phone at hand height, behind you, aimed along the target line through your hands. Shows path, plane, and where the club goes at takeaway and transition.
  • Face-on: phone at hand height, in front of you, square to your chest. Shows weight shift, sway, head movement, and low point.

Use slow motion if your phone has it, film 3 swings — not 30 — and compare against one checkpoint at a time. Then, if the fault persists, take the video to a teaching pro: an hour with a pro who can see you will beat a month of guessing from any page, including this one.
