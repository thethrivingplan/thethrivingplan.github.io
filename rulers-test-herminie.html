The Ruler's Test — Patrick Herminie, President of Seychelles

Subject: Dr. Patrick Herminie — President of the Republic of Seychelles since 26 October 2025. The seat graded is the presidency, which in Seychelles is both head of state and head of government: the executive seat, holding command of the government, appointments to state bodies, and the power to establish commissions of inquiry. Herminie, born in 1963, is a physician. He was Speaker of the National Assembly from 2007 to 2016 for the United Seychelles party (US) — the party of the post-1977 order, in power for 43 years until 2020 — and in 2017 became the founding head of APDAR, the Agency for the Prevention of Drug Abuse and Rehabilitation, where he built the methadone-based treatment model for the country's heroin epidemic. In October 2023, as US leader and presumptive presidential candidate, he was arrested at his party's headquarters and charged with witchcraft-related offences; he denied the charges and called them politically motivated, and in February 2024 prosecutors withdrew all charges against him and four co-defendants (charges against three remaining suspects stayed in force). In the general election of 25–27 September 2025 he placed first with 48.83% (30,736 votes), and won the runoff of 9–11 October 2025 with 52.73% (34,389 votes) against the incumbent, Wavel Ramkalawan of Linyon Demokratik Seselwa (LDS), at 47.27% (30,823 votes), on 86.73% turnout (Electoral Commission of Seychelles, October 2025). His party simultaneously retook the National Assembly, 19 seats to the LDS's 15 in the reported returns. He was sworn in at Unity Stadium on 26 October 2025; Ramkalawan conceded, attended the inauguration, and called the transition "peaceful and exemplary." He is graded on roughly thirteen months in office, and the file's thinness is stated where it matters. His predecessor Ramkalawan (president 26 October 2020 – 26 October 2025) appears as context where the record Herminie inherited — or the promises the seat carried into the election — bear directly on a test. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test governs the grading: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Under the mandate rule, governing on the platform the voters elected is not itself a failure and is never graded as one: the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5; marks are lost only for how it is done. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He submitted himself to the integrity law first, in public, before requiring it of others. In March 2026 Herminie became the first office-holder to submit his declaration of assets to the Anti-Corruption Commission of Seychelles (ACCS), followed by Vice-President Sebastien Pillay and all fourteen cabinet ministers in a coordinated submission; his maiden address had already announced new legislation requiring all politicians and their immediate families to declare their assets, and a Fair Administrative Act "to ensure equality and eliminate favouritism" in public administration (State House reporting; The People, 22 March 2026; maiden address, 14 November 2025).
  • The flagship corruption prosecution of the era runs against his own party's history, and his government has not stopped it. The "missing $50 million" case — the alleged diversion of a 2002 UAE grant — was opened by the ACCS under his predecessor (arrests from November 2021) against businessman Mukesh Valabhji and others, including Sarah Zarqani Rene, widow of France-Albert Rene, the founder-president of Herminie's own party tradition. The prosecution continued through Herminie's first year, and on 14 May 2026 the Constitutional Court refused Valabhji's bid to dismantle it, upholding the ACCS's power to prosecute its own cases without the Attorney-General's prior consent (Global Anti-Corruption Blog, 1 June 2026). The guilt of every accused in that case is an ALLEGATION until a court rules; what is graded here is that the case against the old establishment of his own side proceeds on his watch, through an independent commission and an independent court.
  • The centrepiece inquiry was built to be able to find against the grain, and did find limits. The Commission of Inquiry into the Assomption Island sublease was chaired by a sole commissioner, former Chief Justice Dr. Mathilda Twomey-Woods; it heard the former president in person (18 March 2026), took evidence from developers, officials of both eras, and conservation bodies, and delivered a 528-page final report on 27 June 2026 which was published in full. The report found that the 18 September 2024 sublease to Ocean Breeze Investments Ltd was "a deal settled before it was tested" — competitive in form, not in substance — and referred Ramkalawan's conduct to the prosecuting and integrity authorities; it also stated, in a dedicated section, what it did not find: no bribe, kickback or secret commission paid personally to procure the sublease, the sublease not void, sovereignty not ceded, and its own financial investigation incomplete (Commission of Inquiry Final Report, 27 June 2026). A pure instrument of revenge does not usually publish its own negative findings.
  • No criminal charge against his predecessor appears in the record as of grading. The referral sits with the Attorney-General, the ACCS, and the Financial Intelligence Unit — the ordinary authorities — rather than being converted into an executive verdict or a prosecution announced by the presidency.

Evidence against:

  • Every accountability instrument he has created points in one direction: backward, at the LDS government. The Assomption inquiry examines his predecessor's signature deal; the Commission of Inquiry into the December 2023 CCCL explosion — a disaster of the Ramkalawan years — opened public hearings in July 2026; an investigation into the STCL cold-storage project was announced by the Office of the President in the same period (The People, March 2026; Seychelles Pulse, July 2026). No inquiry examines his own party's 43 years in power (1977–2020), and none examines his current administration. The pattern is a matter of record whatever its motive.
  • The opposition's charge is stated in its strongest form and answered only in part. In a formal statement of 16 September 2026 the LDS called the present commissions of inquiry "an abuse of power": created on the president's whim to target Ramkalawan, under an outdated 1962 Act it calls unconstitutional, with commissioners it alleges have political ties, paid excessively, and — it claimed — with only one report delivered and no findings of wrongdoing (LDS statement, 16 September 2026). The "no findings" limb is contradicted by the Assomption report's own text, which found serious impropriety and made a criminal referral; the cost, mandate, and one-direction limbs stand as the opposition's contested position, not rebutted in the record assembled here. Ramkalawan himself has called the Assomption inquiry a "witchhunt" (reported in The People, 22 March 2026) — his characterization, recorded as his, and a word with a particular weight given the 2023 case against Herminie.
  • Removals in the first weeks clustered on the outgoing government's people. At a press conference on 4 December 2025, forty days into the term, journalists put to him a count of fifteen sackings; his supporters answered that the LDS had made similar removals in 2020 and that a new government staffs its own offices (SBC press-conference coverage and public reaction, December 2025). The count is the critics'; the pattern of early removals of predecessor-era office-holders is not disputed by either side — only its characterization is.
  • The symmetry is stated because Test 1 grades the source. In 2023 the prosecutorial instrument of the then-government was used against Herminie himself, on charges the prosecutor withdrew within five months; he called that case politically motivated, and no court ever tested it. As of grading he has not replicated the criminal-charge instrument against Ramkalawan — the distinction is recorded on the FOR shelf — but the inquiry instrument he does wield has so far had exactly one target government.

Why this grade: The one-law claim is genuinely two-sided. The same president who led the asset declarations by submitting his own, and who let an independent commission prosecute the founding family circle of his own party, has aimed every inquiry he created at the single government that preceded him. Neither shelf cancels the other; MIXED is where the record lands.

Helped: The public record itself — a published, evidence-tested account of the Assomption transaction now exists where rumour stood before; public officers whose independent judgment the report vindicates; the ACCS, whose flagship case survived constitutional challenge. Hurt: Wavel Ramkalawan, referred for criminal investigation by a commission his successor created (the referral's substance is the commission's finding; his denial and "witchhunt" characterization stand beside it); LDS-era office-holders removed in the first forty days; the developers of Assomption, whose conduct the report criticizes in detail.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His single largest institutional act loosened the executive's grip on the legislature. The National Assembly Administration Act 2026, to which he gave assent on 11 June 2026, separates the Assembly's administration from the public service and gives Parliament its own financial and institutional framework — a reform he had co-authored as Speaker more than a decade earlier and promised again in his maiden address: "My government will ensure that the Assembly receives the financial and institutional space it needs to do its work properly and freely" (State House, 11 June 2026; maiden address, 14 November 2025).
  • The constitutional programme he announced points away from his own side's advantage, and he conceded the limit on his power to enact it. In the maiden address he proposed a constitutional amendment for a Speaker "not affiliated with any political party," a reduction of the proportional-representation threshold from 10% to 5% "to give smaller parties a fairer chance," removal of the Tenth Amendment's grant of police powers to the military, and removal of the 30-day post-election transition period — each expressly to be sought by bipartisan agreement "since we do not have a two-thirds majority in the National Assembly" (maiden address, 14 November 2025). None is documented as enacted by grading; the posture is recorded here, and the undelivered word is graded in Test 5.
  • He dismantled the predecessor's coercive instrument rather than inheriting it. The "Ibou" military squad, created under Ramkalawan after the 2022 Tenth Amendment and accused by critics of intimidating civilians — including ALLEGATIONs of threatening a pregnant woman and of military involvement in fatal shootings of prisoners at Montagne Posée prison — was shut down on his taking office, as his manifesto had promised: "the famous 'Tim Ibou' that was inflicting terror on our youths for the last five years is now shut down" (maiden address, 14 November 2025; Seychelles Pulse, 30 July 2026).
  • The courts have ruled at the centre of his programme's terrain and he has accepted their rulings without retaliation. In July 2026 the Constitutional Court struck down the Tenth Amendment itself as unconstitutional — Parliament had failed to follow the required amendment procedure — restoring the pre-2022 position; in May 2026 the same court upheld the ACCS's prosecutorial independence against the country's most prominent corruption defendant (Seychelles Pulse, July 2026; Global Anti-Corruption Blog, June 2026). No documented interference with the judiciary, the Electoral Commission, or the Central Bank appears in the record assembled here; the Commission that ran the election which seated him had its count accepted by the incumbent he defeated.

Evidence against:

  • In November 2026 the grip closed on the state broadcaster. Bérard Duprès had been Chief Executive Officer of the Seychelles Broadcasting Corporation since August 2017, reappointed in 2022 for a second five-year term running to August 2027. On 18–19 November 2026 he announced to his staff that he had received a letter from the President revoking his appointment with immediate effect; a new SBC Board was appointed under the chairmanship of former minister Michael Benstrong (SBC staff announcement, reported 19 November 2026; LDS statement, 20 November 2026). The LDS charges a breach of Article 168(1) of the Constitution, which requires state-owned broadcasting to be independent of political influence — the opposition's legal claim, contested and untested in court as of grading. The Seychelles United Movement's account adds the sequence: an SBC Act passed in July 2026 gave the president the pick of the CEO and four of eleven board seats, months before the keys were used (SUM statement, 19 November 2026) — an opposition movement's account of the law, recorded as such. Defenders of the removal answer that the LDS itself removed an SBC chief, Antoine Onezime, in its own tenure; the precedent is real in the public debate and does not change what the act itself is.
  • The instrument is the presidency's alone. A chief executive of the constitutionally independent broadcaster, holding a fixed term with nine months to run, was removed by a letter from State House, without a documented public statement of reasons from the presidency in the record assembled here.
  • The commissions of inquiry (Test 1) are appointed by the president alone under a 1962 Act the opposition calls unconstitutional — their claim, not a court's finding — and their targets are set by the executive. The appointment pattern is noted here for what it is: the investigative agenda of the state is, in this tenure, written at State House.

Why this grade: On the structural referees — Assembly, courts, electoral commission, anti-corruption commission — the record shows loosening and acceptance, including a reform a decade in the making that gives Parliament its independence from the executive he heads. On the broadcaster, the record shows the opposite motion, executed days before this grading: law adjusted, then a fixed-term chief removed by letter. A HOLDS cannot survive the SBC file; a FAILS cannot survive the Assembly Act and the accepted court rulings. MIXED.

Helped: The National Assembly as an institution, and through it every future opposition; defendants and citizens exposed to military policing, by the Ibou squad's closure and the Tenth Amendment's fall; the ACCS, whose independence the courts confirmed on his watch. Hurt: Bérard Duprès, removed mid-term by letter; SBC's journalists and audience, whose constitutionally independent broadcaster now answers to a board chaired by a former minister of the ruling party; the LDS, whose own precedent (Onezime) is used to normalize a removal it now condemns.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He faced the first wobble in person and early. Forty days into the term, on 4 December 2025, he sat for a televised press conference at which journalists pressed him on the pace of sackings and removals; he answered questions rather than issuing a statement (SBC broadcast coverage, 4 December 2025).
  • The first-year account was given as an account, not a victory lap. In his televised address of 26 October 2026 he framed the speech explicitly as accountability rather than new promises — "he would not make new promises; instead, he wanted to account for the country's position after one year" — conceded that "the cost of living remained high for many families," and named the external shock in specifics: fuel prices increased, flights reduced, tourism revenue declined under the Middle East war (Victoria Scope, October 2026; Capmad, 26 October 2026).
  • The central failure of the year was named in his own voice, in the Assembly, months earlier. In the State of the Nation Address of 27 January 2026 he declared that Seychelles "has become the most expensive country in Africa" — the problem stated by the president himself, not extracted from him (TODAY in Seychelles, 28 January 2026).
  • Institutional pushback was absorbed, not attacked. When the Constitutional Court voided the Tenth Amendment in July 2026 — a judgment on the constitutional terrain of his own manifesto — the documented record shows acceptance and implementation, not retaliation against the bench. When the IMF's May 2026 review counselled that further social-welfare increases be "carefully calibrated" and made contingent on revenue gains, the caution was published within a completed review and an unlocked disbursement of about US$41 million, not denounced (IMF Executive Board, 26 May 2026).
  • His own body's wobble was disclosed rather than rumoured: a hospital stay in October 2026 was publicly announced, with his discharge described as a full recovery and a return to work on 28 October 2026 stated in advance (State House announcements via Seychelles press, October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The commissions wobble produced no documented adjustment. Through 2026 the inquiries drew sustained pushback — Ramkalawan's "witchhunt" charge, the LDS's September 2026 demand that mandates be terminated and reports published immediately, and a broad public argument about cost. In the record assembled here, no mandate was terminated, no costing published, and no presidential response to the September statement appears; the one substantial act of openness — publication of the Assomption report — was the commission's own delivery, and the criminal referrals it made sit without documented follow-up action by grading.
  • The SBC removal was answered with silence. The November 2026 revocation drew immediate constitutional objection from the opposition and wide public criticism; no documented public reasoning from State House appears in the record assembled here as of grading. Pushback met neither adjustment nor explanation.
  • On the cost of living — the defining wobble of the tenure — the response has been measures plus acknowledgment, not revision: the January naming of the problem was followed by the same programme restated in October. Explanation is more than denial; it is less than adjustment, and the shelf records it as such.

Why this grade: Where events and institutions pushed — the courts, the Fund, the external shock, his own health — he answered openly and inside the rules. Where the pushback accused him — the inquiries' one direction, the broadcaster's removal — the record shows no adjustment and, in the SBC case, no explanation. The split is the grade.

Helped: Citizens owed an account of the first year, who received one with the failures named; the courts, whose authority was not contested when it ruled; public officers and journalists at the December 2025 press conference, answered directly. Hurt: The LDS and the former president, whose objections to the inquiries received no documented answer; SBC's staff and audience, given a removal without stated reasons; households whose cost-of-living objection was acknowledged twice and answered with the same programme.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the outer islands, the addicted, the victims of the old regime, the disaster-hit, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The addicted — in Seychelles the largest counted-out population — are the centre of his record, not its margin. APDAR, the agency he founded in 2017, had been wound down under his predecessor; he re-established it on taking office, and its treatment model — medication-assisted therapy and methadone clinics on Mahé, Praslin, and La Digue, needle and syringe programmes, harm reduction that "prioritises the safety, dignity and well-being of people who use drugs" without requiring abstinence — operates as national policy (APDAR service records, 2026; State House, November 2026). A new National Drugs Survey and National Drug Control Master Plan were commissioned with UNODC support, and a Mental Health Care Agency established (State House, November 2026). The population at stake, on the dated official estimate: between 5,000 and 6,000 people addicted to heroin in a population of 94,000 — nearly 10% of the working population, the highest per-capita rate in the world (APDAR figures via BBC, November 2019; a fresh count is in preparation, so the 2019 figure is printed as the dated count it is). A faith-based rehabilitation home, the Farm of Hope, opened in 2025 with his public support.
  • The victims of the old regime were counted after three years of being filed away. The Truth, Reconciliation and National Unity Commission delivered its final report — 47 recommendations, including reparations — to President Ramkalawan in March 2023; it sat largely unimplemented through his tenure. Herminie, who had campaigned on acting on the findings, moved it: Cabinet approved the implementation bill on 24 February 2026; the Commission for the Enforcement, Implementation and Monitoring of the TRNUC Recommendations was established in law in June 2026 and its members sworn in before him on 6 August 2026, mandated to determine reparations for victims of unlawful killing, unlawful imprisonment, forced eviction, and forced exile, payable through a Reparations Trust Fund whose ring-fencing regulations Cabinet approved in October 2026 (State House Cabinet reports, February and October 2026; Converseer/APO, 6 August 2026).
  • The outer islands and the environment were counted against a signed contract. The stop notice of 13 November 2025 halted construction on Assomption; the inquiry then documented what the project had already done — thirty-seven villas approved on the republic's finest green-turtle nesting beach, a dune system more than a century old substantially destroyed, construction proceeding without a biosecurity regime on the threshold of Aldabra, a World Heritage site — and recommended that construction remain suspended pending a biodiversity assessment of not less than twelve months (Commission of Inquiry Final Report, 27 June 2026). Students from Praslin and La Digue studying on Mahé received a dedicated allowance from 2026; La Digue was slated for a full solar transition; desalination capacity was added for Mahé and Praslin (maiden address; first-year address, October 2026).
  • The disaster-hit and the poor were counted by name. A Commission of Inquiry into the December 2023 CCCL explosion — which devastated the Providence industrial area and the communities of Cascade and Petit Paris — opened public hearings in July 2026, hearing former officials, regulators, and residents (Seychelles Pulse, July 2026). The Seychelles Trading Corporation card was reintroduced for low-income families; the subsidized essentials list was expanded by eleven items; bus fares were cut from SCR 10 to SCR 8; the Department of Poverty Alleviation was revived; childcare assistance was doubled for the families identified as most in need, from SCR 750 to SCR 1,500 (first-year address, 26 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The cost of living still counts households out in practice, on his own evidence. The president who declared Seychelles Africa's most expensive country in January 2026 conceded in October 2026 that the cost of living "remained high for many households"; the IMF projects the primary surplus narrowing from 2.5% of GDP in 2025 to about 0.9% in 2026 as social spending rises into the external shock — the arithmetic of relief getting harder, not easier (IMF, 26 May 2026).
  • The fastest and most certain benefits flowed to insiders first. The 13th-month salary as a legal entitlement and the restored Long Service Allowance land, certainly and immediately, on public servants; the private-sector and informal majority received the slower instruments — subsidized goods, fare cuts, an essentials list — whose effect the president himself describes as incomplete.
  • The reparations machinery has counted the victims without yet paying one. The implementation commission was sworn in three years and five months after the TRNUC report; by grading, no payment to any victim is documented, and neither the Reparations Trust Fund's value nor a payment timetable has been published — "Cabinet approval is an enabling decision, not completed redress" (UANWorld, October 2026; commentary on the three-year delay, November 2026).
  • Migrant workers, identified by Freedom House as vulnerable to abuse in Seychelles, have no documented Herminie-era measure in the record assembled here. It is recorded as an absence, not an act.

Why this grade: No shelf in this dossier is longer than this one's FOR side — the addicted, the truth-commission victims, the outer islands, the explosion's communities, and the poor are all counted in policy, several of them after years of being counted out. What holds the grade at MIXED is the distance between counting and delivery: reparations unpaid, prices unbent, and the surest benefits routed to the state's own payroll first.

Helped: People in addiction treatment and their families, across all three main islands; TRNUC victims, whose claims now have a statutory door after three years without one; residents of Praslin and La Digue; the communities of Cascade, Petit Paris, and Providence, whose disaster is finally being heard in public; low-income households on the STC card and the bus network; Aldabra's ecosystem, by suspension of the works at its doorstep. Hurt: Households — disproportionately private-sector and informal — still carrying Africa's highest cost of living on the president's own description; TRNUC victims still waiting for a first payment; the Assomption developers and their workforce, whose project stands suspended (the hurt recorded; the inquiry's findings on how the project was obtained stand beside it).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 26 October 2025, with dates; his predecessor's completed word stated as context.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The Ibou Force — closed, on the manifesto's words. The United Seychelles manifesto promised to "close the team known as 'Ibou Force' and immediately eliminate the practice of harassing citizens" and to repeal the Tenth Amendment's military police powers. The squad's closure was announced in the maiden address of 14 November 2025; the Amendment itself was voided by the Constitutional Court in July 2026 — delivered by the court's route rather than his amendment, and recorded as such.
  • Assomption — the campaign's central promise, executed by the instruments he named. He campaigned to cancel the seventy-year Qatari-linked lease of Assomption, and accused Qatar of financing his opponent's campaign — an ALLEGATION never substantiated in the record assembled here, recorded as a campaign claim. In office: a stop notice halted construction on 13 November 2025; the inquiry he promised was appointed on 9 December 2025; its report was delivered on 27 June 2026 and published, with referrals to the Attorney-General, the ACCS, and the Financial Intelligence Unit. The sublease itself is not documented as cancelled by grading — the Commission found it is not void, and recommended suspension and renegotiation of scale — so the word stands kept in direction and instrument, open in final outcome.
  • The dated social package — delivered on the dates given. Full 13th-month salary for public employees in January 2026, as the maiden address dated it, and subsequently made a legal entitlement; the Long Service Allowance reintroduced from 1 December 2025, the date announced; the retirement age lowered from 65 to 63, as campaigned on; monthly retirement benefits raised by SCR 800 from January 2026 (with a stated target of SCR 10,000 by 2029, in progress); bus fares cut from SCR 10 to SCR 8; the STC card reintroduced; the housing-repair budget doubled, with more than 142 homes delivered in the first year against a target of 2,500 by 2028 (maiden address, 14 November 2025; first-year address, 26 October 2026).
  • APDAR and the Assembly — two restorations, both done. The maiden address promised legislation to re-establish APDAR and create a Mental Health Agency: APDAR was reintroduced on his taking office and operates nationally (State House, November 2026). The address also asked the Assembly to send him the Administration of Parliament bill "as soon as possible": the National Assembly Administration Act 2026 received his assent on 11 June 2026.
  • The TRNUC promise — machinery built. He promised to act on the truth commission's findings; the implementation commission and the reparations-fund framework exist in law as of 2026 (Test 4). Payments have not begun; the word is in motion, not yet discharged.
  • The predecessor's word, as context for the seat: Ramkalawan won in 2020 as the first opposition president in the country's history, on a reform platform. His completed record: an IMF programme that returned the economy to growth — 5.1% real growth and a primary surplus of 2.5% of GDP in 2025, the year straddling the handover (IMF, May 2026); an anti-corruption centrepiece, the Valabhji prosecution, still unresolved at his departure; a truth-commission report received in March 2023 and left largely unimplemented; the Assomption sublease executed by his government on 18 September 2024, which the 2026 inquiry found was settled before it was tested and which referred his personal conduct for criminal investigation; and the 2023 witchcraft prosecution of his challenger, withdrawn by prosecutors in February 2024, which shadowed his democratic word. His last public act of tenure was a kept norm: he conceded, attended his successor's inauguration, and called the transition "peaceful and exemplary."

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The cost of living — the headline word, unmet at one year. His victory speech of October 2025 pledged to lower the cost of living; it was the first promise of the campaign and the first line of the social contract. By his own account in January 2026 Seychelles had become "the most expensive country in Africa," and by his own account in October 2026 the cost of living "remained high for many households." The measures under the promise were delivered (Test 4); the outcome the promise named has not arrived. A partial break, owned in public — recorded as both.
  • The constitutional package — announced as imminent, not enacted. The non-partisan Speaker amendment, the 5% proportional threshold, and the removal of the 30-day transition period were trailed in November 2025 as amendments that would "soon be tabled"; none is documented as enacted by grading, thirteen months on. The one item of the package that did land — the Tenth Amendment's removal — arrived by court judgment on procedural grounds, not by his bipartisan route.
  • Housing — in motion at a pace that must multiply. More than 142 homes delivered in year one against 2,500 promised by 2028 (first-year address, 26 October 2026). Not yet broken; nowhere near kept.
  • Unity — the victory-speech pledge against the year's record. He pledged to unite the island nation. The other half of the country reads the year as division: one-direction inquiries, a contested count of early sackings, and a broadcaster's chief removed by letter (LDS statements, September and November 2026). The pledge is graded as contested, not broken — the division is documented, its authorship disputed by both camps.

Why this grade: This is the fullest kept-word shelf of any first-year dossier in the recent series — a run of specific, dated promises (the squad, the stop notice, the 13th month, the allowance, the retirement age, the agency, the Assembly Act) verifiably discharged on schedule. It is held at MIXED by the two words that matter most to the people who elected him: the cost of living, unmet on his own testimony, and the constitutional package, still untabled in any enacted form.

Helped: Public servants and pensioners, paid what was promised on the dates promised; young people exposed to the Ibou squad's policing; the National Assembly, autonomous by statute; people in addiction treatment, whose agency exists again. Hurt: Every household still paying the prices the headline promise was made against; Wavel Ramkalawan, against whom the kept Assomption word produced a published referral for criminal investigation — the keeping credited here, the hurt recorded in full; the developers and workers of Assomption, suspended mid-project.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Patrick Herminie came to the presidency by the most Seychellois route imaginable: a former Speaker who built the drug-treatment agency, was arrested by the incumbent's prosecutors on witchcraft charges that collapsed within five months, and then beat that incumbent at the ballot box by five points in a runoff the loser conceded graciously. Thirteen months on, the record shows a president whose instincts run in two directions at once. Toward loosening: a Parliament made financially independent by a law he first drafted a decade ago, a military squad abolished rather than inherited, courts ruling against the grain of his programme and being obeyed, an anti-corruption commission prosecuting his own party's founding circle undisturbed, and a first-year account delivered to the nation with the failures named in his own voice. Toward gripping and settling: every inquiry he created aimed at the one government before his, and — days before this grading — the constitutionally independent broadcaster's chief executive removed mid-term by presidential letter, under a board chaired by a former minister, on a legal theory the opposition is preparing to fight. The whys, test by test: One law, MIXED — his own asset declaration went in first and the Valabhji case proceeds against his own side's history (helped: the public record, the ACCS; hurt: Ramkalawan, referred for criminal investigation, and the LDS-era officials removed in the first weeks), but the investigative agenda has exactly one direction. Without gripping, MIXED — the Assembly Act and the accepted court rulings are structural loosening (helped: Parliament, future oppositions, citizens exposed to military policing; hurt: Bérard Duprès, removed by letter with nine months of his term to run, and the SBC's independence with him). The wobble, MIXED — events, courts, the Fund, and his own health were answered openly (helped: a public given accounts rather than denials; hurt: objectors to the inquiries and the SBC removal, who received no documented answer). Counts everyone, MIXED — the addicted, the truth-commission victims, the outer islands, the explosion's communities, and the poor are all counted in policy (helped: each of those constituencies, named in Test 4; hurt: households still carrying the continent's highest prices, victims still unpaid, and the informal majority who got the slow instruments).
