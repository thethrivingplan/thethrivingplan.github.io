The Ruler's Test — Nayib Bukele, President of El Salvador

Subject: Nayib Armando Bukele Ortez — President of El Salvador since 1 June 2019; previously mayor of Nuevo Cuscatlán (2012–2015) and of San Salvador (2015–2018), expelled from the FMLN in 2017, founder of Nuevas Ideas. He won the presidency in 2019 with 53.10% and re-election on 4 February 2024 with 84.65% (TSE final count). His second term, originally to 2029, was shortened by the July 2025 constitutional reform and now ends 1 June 2027. On 19 November 2026 he registered with the TSE as a candidate for a third consecutive term in the election of 28 February 2027, against four opposition candidates. His approval in late 2026 stood between 87.8% (IUDOP, May 2026) and 94% (CID Gallup, October 2026). Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution machinery has reached former presidents of both old parties. In June 2023, opening what he called a "war against corruption," Bukele announced the seizure of assets worth up to US$68 million from former president Alfredo Cristiani (1989–1994); Cristiani left the country and the illicit-enrichment case against him remains an ALLEGATION he denies, unadjudicated. An arrest order issued in July 2021 against former president Salvador Sánchez Cerén (2014–2019) likewise stands as an ALLEGATION; Sánchez Cerén took Nicaraguan citizenship in exile and has not faced trial (Attorney General's office statements, June 2023; contemporaneous reporting, July 2021).
  • The law has touched his own party at least once at senior level: in August 2023 the Assembly lifted the immunity of Nuevas Ideas deputy Erick García, who was charged with document falsification (an ALLEGATION, denied); an ARENA deputy, Alberto Romero, was arrested the following month on illicit-enrichment charges (also ALLEGATION) (Assembly and prosecution records, 2023).
  • He created the instrument the test would ask for: in September 2019, fulfilling a campaign promise, his government and the OAS established the International Commission Against Impunity in El Salvador (CICIES), which in 2020 referred twelve corruption cases arising from pandemic contracts to the Attorney General's office (OAS/CICIES records, 2019–2020).

Evidence against:

  • The instrument was expelled the moment it pointed inward. Bukele dissolved CICIES on 4 June 2021, weeks after the Attorney General who was pursuing its cases was removed (below); the stated reason was the OAS's appointment of a former mayor facing charges as an adviser. The twelve CICIES cases were never pursued by the successor prosecutor, according to the anti-corruption organization Acción Ciudadana's review of the Fiscalía's 2021–2023 record.
  • The pandemic investigations were closed by removal and then by law. In November 2020 Attorney General Raúl Melara's office was investigating some twenty government institutions for pandemic-procurement corruption, including the health and finance ministries. On 1 May 2021 the new Assembly removed Melara (Test 2); on 5 May 2021 it passed a law granting immunity to officials and contractors for pandemic emergency purchases, shelving the file by statute (Human Rights Watch, November 2021; Assembly decree, 5 May 2021).
  • The United States sanctioned his inner circle, and no domestic process followed. In December 2021 the US Treasury sanctioned prison-system director Osiris Luna and social-fabric chief Carlos Marroquín — accusing them of secretly negotiating with MS-13 and Barrio 18 leaders, offering financial incentives and prison privileges in exchange for reduced homicides and electoral support for Nuevas Ideas, and accusing Luna and his mother of stealing and reselling pandemic food aid — and sanctioned his chief of cabinet, Carolina Recinos, accusing her of directing a multi-million-dollar pandemic procurement scheme (US Treasury statements, 8–9 December 2021). These are US government ALLEGATIONs, never adjudicated; Bukele denied the gang-negotiation charge vigorously. The documented domestic fact is narrower and undisputed: none of the three was investigated or charged at home, and Luna and Marroquín remained in their posts. Further close officials — including his legal adviser, his press secretary, and a former finance minister — were placed on the US Engel List of corrupt and anti-democratic actors in 2021–2023; none faced a domestic case (US State Department Engel List reports).
  • The gang-pact file itself is the deepest one-law problem, and it is stated here exactly as the record stands. El Faro first reported in September 2020 that his government was negotiating with MS-13 (ALLEGATION; Bukele denied it and announced a money-laundering investigation of El Faro the same week). ProPublica reported in June 2025, from US officials and documents, that the US Joint Task Force Vulcan investigation found evidence his government impeded extraditions of MS-13 leaders who could testify about the negotiations and suspected diversion of US aid funds to the gang (ALLEGATIONs; the Bukele government has denied any pact). In March 2025, under the deportation agreement (Test 4), the United States returned 23 Salvadorans to CECOT custody, including MS-13 leader César López Larios, whose US prosecution US officials had regarded as a potential source of testimony about the alleged pact (ProPublica and New York Times reporting, 2025). No Salvadoran institution has investigated any of this.
  • His own national security adviser died in custody. Alejandro Muyshondt, arrested in August 2023 after publicly accusing the Attorney General of compromising an investigation, died in state custody on 7 February 2024 while awaiting trial; the official cause was health-related, his family disputed the circumstances, and no independent accounting has been published (family statements and contemporaneous reporting, February 2024).
  • The outcome measure moved the wrong way: El Salvador's Corruption Perceptions Index score fell from 36 in 2020 to a thirteen-year low of 30 in 2024 (rank 130th of 180), recovering slightly to 32 in 2025, with Transparency International attributing the fall to the co-optation of the judiciary and prosecutor's office and selective enforcement (Transparency International, CPI 2020–2025; HRW World Report 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, the prosecution, the comptroller, the central bank, and the media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Elections continue to be held on schedule with opposition participation: the February 2024 elections were contested by five other presidential candidates; the opposition holds 3 of 60 Assembly seats and won a minority of the 44 mayoralties; the three opposition deputies voted against the July 2025 reform and remain seated. In November 2026, registering for a third term, Bukele publicly invited UN, OAS, and EU observers to the February 2027 vote (TSE records; presidential statements, 19 November 2026).
  • The honest limit of this shelf should be stated: it is short because the documented record of an institution ruling against his core interests and standing is short. What follows is why.

Evidence against:

  • The grip began with a threat of force. On 9 February 2020 ("9F"), after the opposition-led Assembly declined to approve a US$109 million security loan on his timetable, Bukele invoked Article 167 to convene an extraordinary session, ordered some 40 armed soldiers and police into the legislative chamber, sat in the Assembly president's chair, and told supporters outside that if he wanted to "press the button" he would; the Constitutional Chamber ruled the convocation unconstitutional and ordered him not to use the military in unconstitutional ways (contemporaneous reporting, AP/NPR, February 2020; Constitutional Chamber ruling, 2020). In April 2020 he announced publicly that he would not comply with the Chamber's habeas corpus rulings ordering the release of people detained for violating pandemic quarantine (presidential statements, April 2020).
  • The referees were then replaced in a single day. On 1 May 2021 ("1M"), hours after his coalition took its seats, the Assembly voted 64–19 to remove all five magistrates of the Constitutional Chamber and their alternates — the chamber that had ruled against him — and to remove Attorney General Melara, whose term ran to January 2022 and who was investigating his cabinet; replacements were sworn in the same night without the constitutionally prescribed selection process. The Inter-American Commission on Human Rights characterized the removals as unconstitutional (Assembly record, 1 May 2021; IACHR statement, May 2021; US Congressional Research Service, September 2021).
  • A judicial purge followed: a September 2021 reform of the judicial-career law forced the retirement of judges and prosecutors over 60 or with more than 30 years of service, removing roughly a third of the judiciary at a stroke, with replacements appointed under the new authorities (Human Rights Watch, November 2021).
  • The new Constitutional Chamber's first major act was to permit his re-election: on 3 September 2021 it reversed long-standing jurisprudence and held that the president could seek an immediate second term, notwithstanding the constitution's text (Constitutional Chamber ruling, 3 September 2021; CRS analysis). The electoral referee was aligned in turn: in September 2024 the Assembly installed as presiding magistrate of the Supreme Electoral Tribunal (TSE) Roxana Soriano, a former Nuevas Ideas pre-candidate who had previously headed the transparency institute (IAIP) — where information classification expanded — and the Court of Accounts (CCR), the comptroller, which under her tenure announced no audits of the government's documented corruption cases; her move from the CCR to the TSE required circumventing an Electoral Code provision barring exactly that transfer, resolved by a same-day resignation (El Diario de Hoy and El Salvador Now reporting, September–October 2024). The TSE under the new magistrates then dismissed scores of experienced technical staff, per the tribunal's own staff union (STRATSE statements, November–December 2024).
  • The electoral map was redrawn by the incumbent's party before the vote: in June 2023, eight months before the 2024 elections, the Assembly cut its own seats from 84 to 60 with a new seat-allocation formula and reduced municipalities from 262 to 44, eliminating 218 mayoral races; opposition parties and analysts described the changes as consolidation, the government as efficiency (Assembly decrees, 7 and 13 June 2023).
  • The media's autonomy ended in exile. El Faro — the investigative outlet that reported the gang-pact ALLEGATION — was subjected to tax audits from 2020 after Bukele accused it of money laundering on national television; Citizen Lab and Amnesty International forensic analyses found Pegasus spyware on more than twenty of its journalists' phones (2022); in April 2023 it moved its legal and administrative operations to Costa Rica, and in May 2025, after it published interviews with gang leaders about the alleged pact, its journalists fled the country on learning of planned arrests (CPJ statement to the US House Tom Lantos Commission, 16 April 2026). Between February and April 2026 the tax authority froze a bank account and a property belonging to partners of El Faro's founding company (El Faro disclosure, 2026). The journalists' association APES counted 53 journalists pushed into exile in 2025; Reporters Without Borders ranked El Salvador 143rd of 180 in its 2026 World Press Freedom Index, down from 81st in 2019.
  • Civil society was closed by statute: the Foreign Agents Law of 20 May 2025 imposes a 30% tax on foreign funding of NGOs and media, requires registration as "foreign agents," and allows discretionary exemptions; Cristosal — the country's leading human-rights organization — suspended its El Salvador operations in July 2025 and relocated to Guatemala and Honduras, stating the choice was "exile or prison." Human Rights Watch counted at least 140 human-rights defenders and journalists leaving the country between May and September 2025.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He reversed his signature economic policy under documented external pressure. To secure the IMF's US$1.4 billion Extended Fund Facility (approved 26 February 2025), his Assembly amended the Bitcoin Law on 29 January 2025: merchant acceptance became voluntary, tax payments in bitcoin ended, and the state began withdrawing from the Chivo wallet — a genuine, public retreat from the 2021 law's core, executed through the constitutional channel (IMF programme documents; Decree 199, 29 January 2025).
  • He complied, formally, with the condition his own court set for his candidacy: on 30 November 2023 he and his vice president took the six-month leave the Constitutional Chamber's interpretation required, and an Assembly-designated official exercised the presidency until 31 May 2024 (Assembly Decree 901, November 2023).
  • The 2025 reform that abolished term limits also shortened his own current term by two years, ending it on 1 June 2027 rather than 2029 — a real, if self-chosen, concession embedded in the change (Decree 371, 31 July 2025).
  • Detainees have been released through negotiation and review: the government states that some 8,000 people detained under the state of exception have been released as innocent, and in July 2025 El Salvador released the roughly 250 Venezuelans held at CECOT (Test 4) to return to Venezuela under a trilateral arrangement in which Venezuela freed ten US citizens and residents and a group of political prisoners (government statements; contemporaneous reporting, July 2025). In June 2025 his government also handed Kilmar Abrego García over to US custody (Test 4) after refusing in April.

Evidence against:

  • The pattern on power itself is defiance, in sequence: the soldiers in the Assembly after a refused loan (February 2020); the announced refusal to obey the Constitutional Chamber's pandemic habeas rulings (April 2020); the removal of the chamber and the prosecutor when rulings and investigations accumulated (May 2021); and, when the United States sanctioned his officials over the alleged gang pact (December 2021), denial plus continuity — the sanctioned men kept their jobs.
  • The emergency that was to last thirty days has been renewed fifty-six times. The state of exception of 27 March 2022 — adopted after gangs killed 87 people in a single weekend — was still in force in November 2026, its 56th monthly extension approved on 21 October 2026, with homicides at historic lows (82 in all of 2025) and the government's own count of arrests above 93,000 (Assembly record, 21 October 2026; Ministry of Security figures via EFE). No sunset, narrowing, or evidentiary review has accompanied the renewals.
  • His public answer to the dictator charge is on the record: he briefly set his social-media biography to "dictator of El Salvador" in September 2021 and later to "the world's coolest dictator," and told the Assembly on 1 June 2025: "I don't care if they call me a dictator" (presidential address, 1 June 2025).
  • Pushback from the detained population's advocates has been answered by extending the machinery to them: the May 2025 arrests of Cristosal's anti-corruption chief Ruth López and constitutional lawyer Enrique Anaya, and the Foreign Agents Law, followed within days the protests and reporting they were associated with (Test 2; HRW, 2025).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the detained and their families, the poor, his opponents' voters, returnees and deportees — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The security gain is real, broadly shared, and belongs at full strength on this shelf. On the government's official count — which excludes alleged gang members killed in confrontations with security forces, a methodological limit stated here because the count's critics rest on it — homicides fell from 2,398 in 2019 (38 per 100,000, down from 6,656 and roughly 106 per 100,000 in 2015, when El Salvador was among the most violent countries on earth) to 495 in 2022, 114 in 2024, and 82 in 2025 (1.36 per 100,000), with 52 recorded through 20 November 2026 (National Civil Police figures; CRS, October 2026, noting recorded 2025 homicides approximately 97% below 2019). The pre-2022 reality the policy answered is documented and undisputed: MS-13 and Barrio 18 controlled territory, taxed residents and businesses by extortion, and made whole districts no-go areas for the state. Residents of formerly gang-held communities, small businesses freed of extortion, and the poor who bore the gangs' tax first are the beneficiaries, and they are the majority of the country; tourism and investment followed (World Bank, October 2026: growth accelerated to 3.9% in 2025 on construction, tourism, and record remittances).
  • The economic count improved modestly and is credited: international poverty (US$8.30/day, 2021 PPP) stood at 29.9% in 2023 and was projected by the World Bank toward roughly 28% by 2025–2026, and the government's household survey put monetary poverty at 22.5% in 2025 (World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, 2025–2026).
  • His opponents' voters were not excluded from the count's formal side: elections proceeded, opposition votes were counted, and opposition candidates will contest February 2027 (Test 2).

Evidence against:

  • The detained are counted out by design, at a scale without parallel in the hemisphere. Under the state of exception, the government counted 93,162 arrests by September 2026; the Attorney General's office has stated that around 90% of those held under the regime remain in detention without trial; the prison population is estimated by Human Rights Watch at roughly 118,000 — the world's highest incarceration rate — in a system built for a fraction of that (government figures via EFE; FGR statement reported October 2026; HRW estimates). The government's own release figure — some 8,000 freed as innocent — concedes a wrongful-detention population in the thousands; the OMCT estimated at least 33,000 of the detained had no gang links (an advocacy estimate, labeled as such).
  • The dead in custody are counted by others because the state does not publish its own count. Cristosal — a human-rights organization, labeled as such — testified to the US House Tom Lantos Commission in April 2026 that it had verified 513 deaths in custody since March 2022, checking 420 against medical and forensic records and finding signs of physical violence in 181; Socorro Jurídico Humanitario, a legal-aid organization relying more heavily on family reports, counted 554 by August 2026 and 582 by late October 2026; Amnesty International's July 2026 report said at least 470. The figures differ by method and are kept separate here. The government's position is that deaths in custody are overwhelmingly from natural causes and prior illness; it has not published a reconciled official total, did not attend the Inter-American Commission's October 2026 hearing on the subject, and families continue to report learning of deaths months late or not at all (Cristosal testimony, 16 April 2026; SJH registries; IACHR hearing, 26 October 2026).
  • Children are inside the machinery: Human Rights Watch and Cristosal documented more than 1,600 children detained in the regime's first months, and reforms adopted in 2026 extended life sentences to children from age twelve (HRW/Cristosal, 2022; country-conditions reporting, November 2026).
  • The counters themselves were then counted out. Ruth López, Cristosal's anti-corruption and justice chief, was arrested at her home on 18 May 2025 — first on an embezzlement charge, then illicit enrichment (ALLEGATIONs she denies; no verdict) — and remained in pretrial detention more than a year later, her case under judicial seal; constitutional lawyer Enrique Anaya was arrested on 7 June 2025 on money-laundering charges (ALLEGATION, denied); an evangelical pastor and a community lawyer who had accompanied families protesting an eviction near the presidential residence were arrested in May 2025. Cristosal, the organization that kept the custody-death count, left the country in July 2025 (HRW and contemporaneous reporting, 2025–2026).
  • Foreign deportees were held outside any count at all. Under the February–March 2025 arrangement with the United States — publicly reported at roughly US$6 million for some 300 detainees for a year (AP/Reuters, March 2025), with the US also returning 23 Salvadorans including MS-13's López Larios — some 288 people transferred by the US were imprisoned at CECOT incommunicado, without charge or access to courts, 137 of the Venezuelans under the Alien Enemies Act of 1798 (UN submission by RFK Human Rights et al., 10 June 2025). The Venezuelans were released to Venezuela in the July 2025 swap (Test 3). One case is documented to the end and stated exactly: Kilmar Abrego García, a Salvadoran living in Maryland under a 2019 US immigration-court order withholding his removal to El Salvador, was deported on 15 March 2025 in what the US government conceded was an "administrative error," and imprisoned at CECOT. On 10 April 2025 the US Supreme Court unanimously directed the US government to "facilitate" his release; at the White House on 14 April 2025, Bukele refused, asking how he could "smuggle" a man into the United States and stating he did not have the power to return him. El Salvador handed him to US custody on 6 June 2025, when he was returned to face a Tennessee indictment; a federal judge dismissed that indictment in May 2026, finding the presumption of vindictive prosecution unrebutted (the Justice Department appealed in August 2026); Judge Xinis ordered him released from ICE detention on 11 December 2025, and as of November 2026 he is free in the United States with removal litigation continuing (US federal court records: Abrego Garcia v. Noem, D. Md.; United States v. Abrego Garcia, M.D. Tenn.).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since June 2019, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Security — the central promise, kept on its own terms. He campaigned in 2019 on breaking the gangs, launched the Territorial Control Plan in June 2019, and the documented homicide collapse (Test 4) is the fact his two landslides rest on: 53.10% in 2019, 84.65% in 2024, approval between roughly 88% and 94% through 2026 (TSE; CID Gallup/IUDOP/LPG Datos polling, 2026). Whatever the other tests grade, the promise the electorate twice voted on was not abandoned.
  • The CICIES promise was made and initially performed: the anti-impunity commission he pledged in the 2019 campaign existed by September 2019 and referred real cases (Test 1) — its expulsion in 2021 is graded where it belongs, under Tests 1 and 2, and noted again below.
  • Fiscal normalization, late but real: after years of confrontation with markets and the IMF, his government delivered the IMF programme of February 2025, debt buybacks, and fiscal consolidation; sovereign spreads fell from over 3,500 basis points in mid-2022 to roughly 326 by end-2025, and growth reached 3.9% in 2025 (IMF; World Bank, 2025–2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The re-election word — the clearest broken promise in this series, because it was constitutional, repeated, and his own. During his first term he denied he would seek immediate re-election, citing the constitutional prohibition: "No, there is no reelection," he said in 2021 (presidential statements, 2021, as compiled by El Salvador Now, August 2025). After his appointed Constitutional Chamber reversed the jurisprudence on 3 September 2021, he announced his candidacy on 15 September 2022, won in 2024, abolished term limits entirely on 31 July 2025, and on 19 November 2026 registered to run for a third consecutive term. His vice president stated in November 2026 that he may seek re-election as many times as the people vote for him (Univision interview; TSE registration record).
  • Anti-corruption — the slogan was "money is enough when no one steals it." The CICIES he created was expelled in June 2021; its twelve cases were shelved; the pandemic-purchase immunity law passed in May 2021; his US-sanctioned officials were neither investigated nor removed; and the CPI fell from 36 (2020) to 30 (2024) before a partial recovery to 32 (2025) (Tests 1–2; Transparency International).
  • Bitcoin — promised as transformation, delivered as a reserve. The June 2021 Bitcoin Law made El Salvador the first country with bitcoin as legal tender; usage never followed (an NBER household study found 4.9% of sales paid in bitcoin and most Chivo users inactive after spending the US$30 bonus); the promised Bitcoin City (announced November 2021) has not been built and the "volcano bonds" never issued as of November 2026; and in January–February 2025 the legal-tender core was repealed under IMF conditionality, leaving a state reserve of some 7,825 BTC published by the National Bitcoin Office (NBER, 2022; IMF programme documents; ONBTC dashboard, November 2026).
  • Transparency — promised openness, delivered classification. He campaigned against the secrecy of the old parties; in office, official information was placed under reserve across government, the transparency institute was headed by a loyalist (Test 2), and El Salvador fell 61 places in the World Press Freedom Index between 2019 and 2025 before reaching 143rd in 2026 (RSF indices).
  • The long arc, factually: a mayor expelled by the FMLN who built a party around his own name and won the presidency in 2019 as the anti-system candidate — against the two parties that had alternated since the civil war — then, in sequence: soldiers in the Assembly (2020), the referees replaced (2021), the emergency made permanent (2022– ), the electoral map redrawn (2023), re-election under a reinterpreted constitution (2024), term limits abolished (2025), and a third-term candidacy registered (November 2026). Each step was taken through institutions his coalition controlled and ratified, in most cases, by large popular majorities. The word he kept was the one about the gangs; the words he broke were the ones about the rules.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Nayib Bukele has built the most complete grip in this series: a Constitutional Chamber and prosecutor installed by removal, an electoral tribunal and comptroller headed by loyalists, a legislature of 54 of 60, an emergency regime renewed fifty-six times, an independent press and human-rights community in exile, and a constitution rewritten in an evening to let him run forever — while more than 93,000 people have been arrested, around nine in ten of them untried, and the only systematic count of the hundreds who have died in custody is kept by organizations his government drove out of the country. Against that record stands the fact that explains it, which this test does not discount: he inherited a country where gangs taxed the poor at gunpoint and killed 106 per 100,000 in a single year, and on the documented count homicides have fallen roughly 97% — a transformation his people have ratified at 84.65% and approval near ninety percent, and which neighboring electorates now demand for themselves. The Sun in this series holds because it gives by one law to everything in its reach. Bukele's El Salvador gives safety by one law and justice by another: the count that made him — the homicide count — is the most honest number in the country, and the counts that would grade him, of the detained, the dead in custody, and the stolen, are the ones no institution he left standing is permitted to keep.

Free. Always free.
