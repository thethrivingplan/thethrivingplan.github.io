The Ruler's Test — Viktor Orbán, former Prime Minister of Hungary

Subject: Viktor Orbán — Prime Minister of Hungary from 29 May 2010 to 9 May 2026 (also Prime Minister 1998–2002); leader of Fidesz since 2003. His sixteen-year second premiership ended at the ballot box: in the parliamentary election of 12 April 2026, Péter Magyar's Tisza party won 141 of 199 seats on 53.18% of the party-list vote, against 38.61% and 52 seats for Fidesz–KDNP, at a turnout of roughly 79% — the highest since 1990. Orbán conceded on election night, congratulated Magyar, and handed over power; the new National Assembly elected Magyar prime minister on 9 May 2026. This dossier grades the completed 2010–2026 tenure. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution did, in a small number of cases, reach sitting Fidesz figures. Pál Völner, a Fidesz state secretary in the Justice Ministry, was charged in December 2021 with accepting bribes from the head of the judicial bailiffs' chamber (the Völner–Schadl case) and resigned; his trial proceeded in open court. Fidesz MP György Simonka had his immunity lifted in 2019 and was indicted for budget fraud. Both cases are documented; critics note how few such cases there were in sixteen years, and how slowly they moved, but they exist.
  • The state's own audit institutions occasionally bit: the State Audit Office's 2025 audit of the central bank reported serious deficiencies in the asset management of the bank's foundations — published while Orbán was still prime minister (State Audit Office audit reporting, 2025; see Test 2).
  • Hungary repaid the EU the €43.7 million at issue in the Elios case (below) — the financial recommendation was executed, albeit from Hungarian taxpayers' money rather than from the company, and only after the police had closed the criminal side.

Evidence against:

  • Elios. Elios Innovatív Zrt., co-owned until 2015 by István Tiborcz — Orbán's son-in-law — won 35 EU-funded municipal street-lighting contracts worth €43.7 million between 2011 and 2015. The EU's anti-fraud office OLAF investigated for two years and reported in late 2017 that it had found serious irregularities, conflicts of interest, and, in 17 of the projects, indications of organised fraud: tenders written to maximise Elios's price, comparison bids prepared on the same computer by the same person. OLAF recommended full repayment and sent judicial recommendations to Hungary's chief prosecutor. Hungarian police opened an investigation in 2015 and closed it finding no crime; reopened it after OLAF's referral in 2018 and closed it again within four months, in November 2018, stating the findings were "not underpinned" — no charges were ever brought (OLAF findings as leaked and summarised by Transparency International Hungary; Hungarian National Police statements via MTI, November 2018). The asymmetry is the documented fact: the EU's own investigators described organised fraud around the prime minister's family; the domestic system found nothing at all.
  • The procurement state. A Corruption Research Center Budapest study of procurement data calculated that companies affiliated with Lőrinc Mészáros — a former gas fitter from Orbán's home village of Felcsút and his childhood friend, who became Hungary's richest man during the tenure — won public contracts worth roughly €9.5 billion between 2011 and 2023, about 59% of them EU-funded (CRCB study, cited 2026). Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index placed Hungary last in the European Union for three consecutive years (2022–2024; score 41/100 in 2024). The European Commission's conditionality proceedings documented systemic public-procurement shortcomings and conflicts of interest as grounds for freezing funds (Council Implementing Decision (EU) 2022/2506).
  • The prosecutor's office as a filter. Chief Prosecutor Péter Polt — a former Fidesz parliamentary candidate, elected by the Fidesz supermajority in 2010 and re-elected in 2019 for a further nine years — headed the prosecution throughout the tenure. OLAF's annual reports repeatedly recorded Hungary among the member states least likely to indict on OLAF judicial recommendations. Cases touching the government's circle — Elios above all — died at this desk; the office's defenders note it did prosecute Völner (above). The documented pattern across sixteen years is that prosecution reached insiders rarely, late, and never at the centre.
  • Post-tenure contrast, stated carefully. Within months of the change of government, investigators detained a former Orbán development minister, Miklós Seszták (September 2026), and former central bank governor György Matolcsy (October 2026) in corruption investigations, and the new asset-recovery office reported recovering billions of forints in cash from hiding places (Associated Press and Reuters reporting, autumn 2026). These are ALLEGATIONs against those individuals, contested by them, unadjudicated, and they establish nothing about Orbán personally. They are recorded here only because their timing is itself part of the public record on how the previous system had handled — or not handled — the same files.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do courts, the central bank, media, universities, municipalities, and the electoral system keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The grip was never total, and its limits are documented. Independent online media — Telex, 444.hu, HVG, Direkt36 — published investigations throughout the tenure and were never banned; the courts occasionally ruled against the government, most strikingly the Kúria's 12 May 2020 judgment upholding compensation to segregated Roma schoolchildren against the government's publicly stated position (Test 4); and the electoral administration counted votes honestly to the end — the system Orbán built transmitted his own defeat accurately in April 2026, a fact graded in full under Test 3.
  • In May 2023, under EU financial pressure, parliament adopted a judicial reform strengthening the National Judicial Council — budgetary autonomy and binding opinions on key appointments — which the European Commission assessed as meeting its judicial-independence milestone, releasing some €10.2 billion in cohesion funds in December 2023 (European Commission decision, December 2023; see Test 3).
  • Local elections were held on schedule throughout, and opposition victories — Budapest and a string of cities in 2019, Budapest again in 2024 — were seated, however fiscally squeezed (Test 4).

Evidence against:

  • The judiciary, restructured first. The 2011 Fundamental Law — adopted by Fidesz's votes alone — lowered judges' retirement age from 70 to 62 from 1 January 2012, forcing out some 274 judges, including a disproportionate share of court presidents and twenty Supreme Court judges. The Hungarian Constitutional Court declared the measure unconstitutional (Decision 33/2012) and the Court of Justice of the EU found it unlawful age discrimination (C-286/12, 6 November 2012); the judges were compensated but, with few exceptions, not reinstated. The same law terminated the mandate of Supreme Court president András Baka — who had publicly criticised the reforms — three and a half years early; the European Court of Human Rights' Grand Chamber held in Baka v. Hungary (23 June 2016) that this violated his rights to a tribunal and to freedom of expression. The Constitutional Court itself was enlarged from 11 to 15 judges, its judges' terms extended from 9 to 12 years, and the consensual nomination rules replaced so that after 2010 every member was elected by the Fidesz majority alone. The Fourth Amendment (2013) then stripped the Court of substantive review of constitutional amendments — after the Court had annulled provisions the government wanted — and voided the Court's pre-2012 case law as precedent. Court administration was centralised in a National Office for the Judiciary whose first president, Tünde Handó (2012–2019), held sweeping appointment and case-assignment powers the elected National Judicial Council could not counterbalance — a standing finding of the European Commission's rule-of-law reports from 2020 onward.
  • The electoral system, rewritten by its winner. The 2011 electoral law — drafted without opposition input — cut parliament from 386 to 199 seats, replaced two-round constituency voting with single-round first-past-the-post, redrew all 106 constituency boundaries without an independent boundary commission, extended voting rights to ethnic Hungarians abroad (who vote overwhelmingly for Fidesz), and introduced "winner compensation," which adds the winning candidate's surplus votes to the winner's party list total. The Venice Commission criticised the process and key features. The results are arithmetic: in 2014 Fidesz converted ~45% of the list vote into a two-thirds supermajority; in 2018 ~49% into two-thirds; in 2022 ~54% into two-thirds. OSCE/ODIHR found the 2022 election well administered but "marred by the absence of a level playing field," and its preliminary statement on 2026 (13 April 2026) found "no equal opportunities for contestants": systemic misuse of public office and resources, government messaging fused with party campaigning, biased public media, unregulated campaign finance, and election-dispute handling that "was not impartial."
  • Media, captured by ownership and regulator. The 2010 media laws created the NMHH and its Media Council — president appointed by the prime minister for nine years, council members elected by the Fidesz supermajority — with regulatory power over broadcast, print, and online media alike, a structure criticised by the OSCE, the Council of Europe, and the Commission from 2011 on. In November 2018, owners loyal to the government donated 476 media outlets to a single foundation, KESMA; a government decree declared the merger one of "national strategic importance," exempting it from competition and media-authority scrutiny, and the Constitutional Court declined to intervene (Commission rule-of-law reporting, 2020). Monitoring organisations estimate pro-government outlets came to cover roughly 80% of the media market; state advertising was channelled to them; the public broadcaster MTVA functioned as a government outlet, as OSCE monitoring of both the 2022 and 2026 campaigns documented. Independent outlets were squeezed rather than closed: Klubrádió lost its frequency in 2021 on the Media Council's decision; Index.hu's editor-in-chief was dismissed in 2020, prompting the staff's resignation and refoundation as Telex. Hungary fell from 23rd to the high-80s/low-90s range in RSF's Press Freedom Index over the tenure (68th in the NYU/Mérték 2010–2025 review's count). In December 2025 the Commission opened the first-ever infringement action under the European Media Freedom Act against Hungary.
  • Pegasus. In July 2021 the Pegasus Project consortium, with forensic confirmation by Amnesty International's Security Lab, reported that the phones of Hungarian investigative journalists (Direkt36's Szabolcs Panyi and András Szabó among them), a major independent media owner, lawyers, and an opposition politician had been infected with NSO Group's Pegasus spyware. In November 2021 a senior Fidesz official and then the interior minister acknowledged Hungary had purchased and used Pegasus, maintaining all use was lawful and authorised. The prosecution's investigation concluded no crime had been committed; the data-protection authority found no violation (Hertie School/CPJ reporting, 2021–2022). The documented fact pattern — forensic traces on critics' phones, official confirmation of purchase, and domestic oversight finding nothing — is recorded here at exactly that strength; who authorised each target remains CONTESTED between the government's legality claim and the journalists' accounts.
  • Universities, expelled and then absorbed. The April 2017 "lex CEU" amendments imposed conditions (a home-state treaty, teaching in the country of origin) that in practice applied to one institution, the Soros-founded Central European University; the government refused to sign the agreement CEU had negotiated, and CEU moved its US-accredited teaching to Vienna in 2019. The Court of Justice ruled on 6 October 2020 (C-66/18) that the law violated EU law and the Charter's guarantee of academic freedom — after the university had gone. From 2019, 21 public universities were transferred to "public interest asset management foundations" (KEKVA) whose boards — initially appointed by the government, including sitting ministers and Fidesz-linked figures, effectively irremovable — took over budgets, strategy, and rector appointments from university senates; the model was entrenched in cardinal laws requiring two-thirds majorities to undo. The Council's December 2022 conditionality decision barred the foundation universities from new Erasmus+ and Horizon Europe commitments over conflict-of-interest risks, cutting their students and researchers off from the EU programmes until after the change of government (Council Implementing Decision (EU) 2022/2506; CJEU judgments of 30 September 2026 dismissing the universities' annulment actions).
  • The central bank's foundations. Governor György Matolcsy (2013–2025), Orbán's former economy minister, moved some HUF 260 billion of central bank funds into Pallas Athéné foundations; a 2016 law and Constitutional Court ruling held that the money thereby "lost its public character," removing it from freedom-of-information reach. The State Audit Office's 2025 audit reported serious deficiencies in the foundations' asset management; no Orbán-era prosecution followed. (Matolcsy's October 2026 detention came under the successor government; see Test 1.)
  • Municipalities and the referee institutions. The State Audit Office (headed from 2010 by a former Fidesz MP), the Budget Council, and the media and election bodies were all led, across the tenure, by officials elected by the Fidesz supermajority for long terms — the staffing pattern the Commission, the Venice Commission, and ODIHR cite as the system's connective tissue. A Sovereignty Protection Office created in December 2023 — criticised by the Venice Commission and referred by the Commission to the Court of Justice — opened investigations in 2024 into Transparency International Hungary and the investigative outlet Átlátszó, both government critics, under a mandate to expose "foreign interference."

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the EU, markets, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The final wobble, answered completely. On 12 April 2026, facing a documented structural advantage in media, finance, and electoral law, Hungarian voters removed him anyway — and Orbán accepted it the same night. He conceded at about 9:30 p.m., before a third of the votes were counted, telephoned Péter Magyar to congratulate him, and said publicly: "The result of the election is painful but clear." He did not allege fraud, did not blame foreign interference in his concession speech, did not attempt to use the institutions his party still staffed to obstruct the transfer; his government handed over power, and Magyar was elected prime minister by the new Assembly on 9 May 2026 (Le Monde, FIPRA and FEPS post-election analyses, April 2026; National Election Office final results). Sixteen years of grip-building ended with an orderly exit through the door the voters opened. That is the single strongest wobble answer in this entire series of dossiers, and it is on his record permanently.
  • The internet tax, 2014. When a proposed tax on internet traffic brought up to 100,000 protesters into Budapest's streets in October 2014, Orbán withdrew it within the week — "This tax in its current form cannot be introduced" — and shelved it permanently (BBC/Reuters reporting, 31 October 2014).
  • Money moved him, openly. The May 2023 judicial reform (Test 2) was a genuine, if partial, institutional concession — strengthening the judges' own council — made for one documented reason: to unlock frozen EU funds, which the Commission then released in part (€10.2 billion, December 2023). Similarly transactional adjustments recur: Hungary withdrew from the Russia-linked International Investment Bank in April 2023, a day after US sanctions on it; and in February 2024 parliament ratified Sweden's NATO accession after a two-year delay once Ankara had moved and concessions on fighter jets were in hand.
  • Local defeats accepted. The opposition's 2019 municipal victories — Budapest above all — were seated without annulment campaigns or legal overturning, whatever fiscal pressure followed (Test 4). The February 2024 presidential-pardon scandal — President Katalin Novák's pardon of a man convicted of covering up child sexual abuse — was answered with the resignations of Novák and of former justice minister Judit Varga, the two officials responsible, within days of the story breaking: the system's own people paid the price, publicly.
  • The 2025 "transparency" bill shelved. A May 2025 bill that would have let the government blacklist foreign-funded media and NGOs drew protests and EU warnings; in June 2025 the Fidesz caucus postponed it, and it was never enacted before the 2026 election.

Evidence against:

  • Asylum: defiance as doctrine, at a documented price. After the Court of Justice's 17 December 2020 judgment (C-808/18) held Hungary's asylum regime — transit-zone detention, pushbacks to Serbia, blocked access to procedure — in breach of EU law, the government closed the transit zones but kept the substance: applications effectively only via embassies, pushbacks continuing. On 13 June 2024 the Court imposed a €200 million lump sum plus €1 million per day — the first financial sanctions of their kind in asylum — finding Hungary had "deliberately evaded" applying the common policy (C-123/22). Orbán refused compliance and denounced the fine; the penalties accrued through the end of his tenure. On migration, the wobble was answered with a decade of doubling down, and Hungarian taxpayers hold the bill.
  • Gyöngyöspata: the state campaigned against a court judgment. When courts ordered compensation for Roma children segregated in the village school (final judgment, Kúria, 12 May 2020), Orbán attacked the ruling in January 2020 as violating "the people's sense of justice," suggested the families were receiving "money for nothing," attributed the case to the "Soros network," and announced a national consultation on whether such compensation should be paid — the executive publicly mobilising against a final judgment protecting a minority (see Test 4). The judgment was eventually executed, but the government's first answer to the courts was a campaign.
  • The pattern on core projects: rulings absorbed, projects kept. The Court of Justice struck down lex CEU in 2020 — CEU stayed in Vienna, the law's purpose already achieved. It struck down the "Stop Soros" criminalisation of asylum assistance in 2021 (C-821/19) and parts of the NGO transparency law in 2020 (C-78/18) — the laws were repealed or lapsed only after serving their term, and successor instruments (the Sovereignty Protection Office, the 2025 bill) pursued the same targets. The Article 7 procedure opened by the European Parliament in September 2018 ran for the rest of the tenure without Hungarian concessions on its substance. Emergency governance itself became the standing answer to pressure: a "state of danger" of one kind or another was in force, and decree rule available, for most of 2016–2026 (migration emergency from 2016, COVID from 2020, war emergency from 2022).
  • Pride, 2025: doubling down at the end. Rather than absorb criticism of the 2021 child-protection law, the government in March–April 2025 banned Pride assemblies outright and wrote child-protection supremacy into the constitution (Test 4). When 100,000–200,000 people marched anyway on 28 June 2025, police ultimately declined to fine participants — a late, quiet retreat — but prosecutors charged Mayor Karácsony with organising the march in February 2026.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Roma, migrants, LGBTQ citizens, Budapest and opposition-run cities, and his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The domestic majority was counted in, deliberately and materially. The family-policy architecture — CSOK housing grants, the 2019 "baby loan," lifetime income-tax exemption for mothers of four or more children (2020), the restored 13th-month pension (2021–22), utility price caps from 2013 — reached millions of Hungarian households regardless of party, and public-works and employment programmes raised measured Roma employment substantially from its 2010 base; kindergarten became compulsory from age three. Ethnic Hungarians in neighbouring countries were counted in with citizenship and voting rights (2010–2011) — an inclusion, though one that also served the ruling party (Test 2).
  • Opposition voters' ballots were counted honestly throughout: the 2019 and 2024 Budapest results and the 2026 national result were all transmitted accurately, and opposition mayors took and retook office. Whatever the tilts of the field, no Hungarian voter's vote was discarded.

Evidence against:

  • Migrants and asylum seekers: counted out by design. Hungary built a border fence in 2015, declared a migration "state of emergency" that lasted a decade, confined asylum seekers to transit zones (held unlawful detention by the Court of Justice, 2020), replaced them with an embassy-only application system under which protection grants fell to a few dozen a year, and conducted pushbacks to Serbia that UNHCR and the Council of Europe documented in the tens of thousands. The June 2024 €200 million-plus-€1-million-a-day judgment (Test 3) is the priced record of the policy. A 2018 constitutional amendment declared that "no alien population shall be settled in Hungary," and the 2018 "Stop Soros" law criminalised assisting asylum seekers — struck down by the Court of Justice in 2021, after years in force.
  • LGBTQ citizens: legislated out of public view, then out of assembly. The June 2021 child-protection law banned portraying homosexuality or gender transition to minors — folding LGBTQ content into a law nominally against paedophilia — prompting the Commission to sue and twenty member states to protest; the Court of Justice ruled against the law in April 2026. In February 2025 Orbán announced Pride organisers "should not bother preparing this year's parade"; on 18 March 2025 parliament, in a one-day procedure, banned assemblies "violating" the child-protection law, with fines for participants and facial-recognition identification authorised — the first Pride ban in an EU state — and the Fifteenth Amendment (14 April 2025) wrote into the constitution that a person "is male or female" and that child protection overrides all rights but life. The 28 June 2025 march went ahead as a municipal event with 100,000–200,000 participants; police dropped participant proceedings, but the mayor who sheltered it was charged (Test 3). Adoption by same-sex couples had already been constitutionally barred in 2020 ("the mother is a woman, the father is a man").
  • Roma: the government's own words. Hungary's largest minority was counted into work programmes (above) — and, when Roma families won in court, counted against by the head of government personally. In the Gyöngyöspata case, courts found that some 60 Roma children had been segregated in inferior classes between 2004 and 2017 and awarded roughly HUF 100 million in compensation. In January 2020 Orbán called the award "deeply unfair," asked how members of "an ethnic group" could "receive large amounts of money without doing any kind of work for it," blamed the "Soros network," and floated replacing cash compensation with training; the Kúria upheld the cash award on 12 May 2020 and it was paid — but the prime minister's public position, maintained for months, was that a court-ordered remedy for segregated Roma children offended the nation's sense of justice (AFP and ERRC reporting, January–May 2020; his February 2020 State of the Nation speech). School segregation itself persisted: the Commission pursued infringement proceedings over Roma children's education through the tenure.
  • Budapest and opposition cities: governed as opponents. After Gergely Karácsony won the Budapest mayoralty in 2019, the "solidarity contribution" levied on the capital rose roughly ninefold by 2025 to about a fifth of the city's revenues; the local business tax was cut centrally in 2020; promised transfers were disputed; and in December 2025 Moody's downgraded Budapest to junk (Ba1), citing liquidity risk amid the standoff, while Karácsony accused the government of pushing the capital toward bankruptcy and the government blamed his spending — the fiscal facts (levy scale, downgrade) are documented, the blame CONTESTED between them. In February 2026 the government used emergency decree powers to terminate the city's court challenge to the levy — the dispute ended not by judgment but by decree (Hungarian reporting, February 2026; the levy framework was later held unconstitutional in its existing form by the Constitutional Court under the successor government). Other opposition-run cities reported parallel squeezes; Fidesz-run municipalities faced no comparable instrument.
  • The homeless and the poor at the margin: a 2018 constitutional amendment prohibited "habitual residence in public spaces," criminalising rough sleeping — criticised by the UN special rapporteur and the Venice Commission — while the utility-cap and family programmes above remained tied to formal housing and employment that the poorest often lacked.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Signature promises and stated positions vs. the record, 2010–2026, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept):

  • The illiberal state — announced, then built. In his 26 July 2014 Tusnádfürdő speech Orbán promised openly to build an "illiberal state" modelled on systems "not Western, not liberal" that make nations competitive. Whatever its content, the promise was kept with unusual fidelity: the constitutional, media, electoral, and university structures described in Test 2 are that speech executed over twelve years. His word and his deed match here more exactly than in any other file in this series.
  • "Hungary will not become an immigrant country." Promised from 2015 onward; kept to the letter — fence, transit zones, embassy-only asylum, near-zero recognition — at the legal and financial cost recorded in Tests 3 and 4. Voters who chose him for this promise received it.
  • Family policy. The 2018 "demographic turnaround" programme was delivered as legislated: CSOK grants, baby loans, the mothers' tax exemptions, childcare expansion. (The demographic target itself was not met: the fertility rate rose from 1.23 in 2011 to about 1.6 in 2021, then fell back toward 1.35–1.4 by 2024–2025 — the instruments were built as promised; the outcome was not.)
  • Work instead of benefits. The 2010 pledge of one million new jobs in ten years: official employment rose from roughly 3.7 million to about 4.7 million by 2019 and unemployment fell from 11.2% (2010) to under 4% — the headline promise substantially delivered on KSH figures, with the documented caveats that public works counted toward it and several hundred thousand Hungarians worked abroad.
  • 2022 war promises. Campaigning in 2022, he promised Hungary would stay out of the Ukraine war, send no weapons, and block sanctions that raised Hungarian energy prices. In office he did exactly that — refusing Hungarian weapons transfers, securing exemptions from the EU oil embargo, and repeatedly delaying sanctions packages for concessions.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or reversed):

  • Accountability for the old regime. In 2010 Fidesz promised to hold the previous Socialist governments to account for corruption and the 2006 Őszöd speech crisis. No former prime minister or senior Socialist minister was convicted of corruption in sixteen years; the promised reckoning dissolved into rhetoric while, per Test 1, the new system's own files went unprosecuted.
  • Debt. The 2011 Széll Kálmán Plan promised to cut public debt toward 50% of GDP. Debt fell from ~80% (2010) to ~65% (2019), then reversed — ~80% again in the COVID year and roughly 73–75% through 2024–2025, with heavy pre-election spending in 2025–26 that the successor government's budget documents put at the centre of a deficit surge (Reuters, June 2026). The promise ended further from its target than it began.
  • Catching up with the West. Orbán repeatedly promised convergence — in 2018, that by 2030 Hungary would be among the EU's five most liveable countries. On Eurostat's actual-individual-consumption measure, Hungary instead ranked last in the EU in 2023 and 2024 — the documented opposite direction.
  • The EU money. From 2022 the government promised the frozen funds would arrive ("the money is coming") on its own terms. The record: €6.3 billion suspended under conditionality in December 2022; €10.2 billion released in December 2023 only after the judicial concession of Test 3; some €1.04 billion definitively forfeited at the end of 2024 and further tranches lapsed at the end of 2025 for unmet conditions, with roughly €17–19 billion still blocked when he left office (European Commission statements, 2024–2026). The successor government secured the release of €16.4 billion within months of taking office, on the reforms Orbán had declined to make (Reuters, June 2026) — the clearest available measure of what his word on the funds was worth.
  • Utility price protection. The 2013 "rezsicsökkentés" (utility price cuts) was a signature promise maintained for a decade — then partially rolled back in August 2022, when above-average consumption was moved to market prices, in the middle of the energy crisis the government had promised households would be shielded from.
  • The long reversal, stated once. The young Orbán of 1989–1998 demanded Soviet troops' withdrawal, championed liberal democracy and a free press, and took a Soros Foundation scholarship to Oxford. The statesman of 2010–2026 built his system against each of those positions and said so himself, framing the change as maturation and the nation's interest. It is recorded here not as hypocrisy-hunting but because Test 5 measures a word over time: on the deepest commitments of his early career, the later record is a repudiation — openly avowed, which is the most that can be said for it.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Viktor Orbán built the most complete system of institutional capture documented in this series inside a functioning electoral democracy: a judiciary restructured in a single winter, a media market consolidated by decree into one foundation, an electoral law that turned 45% into two-thirds, universities handed to irremovable boards, a prosecution service that found no crime where the EU's investigators found organised fraud in the prime minister's own family circle. Migrants were counted out by constitutional amendment, LGBTQ citizens out of the public square by statute, Roma schoolchildren's court-won compensation publicly begrudged by the head of government, and the opposition's capital fiscally besieged until its credit was junk. And then the system's own honest count removed him: on 12 April 2026, on a field his observers-certified advantages had tilted for sixteen years, the voters chose otherwise — and he conceded before midnight, congratulated the winner, and went home. The grip grades fail on the evidence of what he built. The wobble grade is mixed because of how he left it. No other dossier in this series contains both of those sentences at full strength, and the record requires both.
