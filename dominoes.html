Dominoes

The classic Draw Game — the proven, documented domino game played with a double-six set, as recorded in standard domino rule collections for well over a century. Match the open ends, draw from the boneyard when you're stuck, and empty your hand — or be left holding the fewest pips when the table blocks. Simple on the surface; underneath, it's a counting game, and this table teaches the counting as you play.

One device, pass-and-play. Bots can fill any seat. Nothing is wagered — there is nothing to wager with; it's tiles and bragging rights.

How the Draw Game works — the documented rules (tap to open)
  • The set. Standard play uses a double-six set: 28 tiles, every pairing of 0–6. Each player draws 7 tiles in a two-player game, 5 each with three or four players; the rest form the boneyard.
  • The lead. The player holding the highest double leads it. If no double was dealt, the holder of the heaviest tile leads it.
  • Matching ends. Each tile played must match the number on one open end of the chain. Doubles are laid crosswise but count as their number on that end.
  • Stuck. In the draw game, a player with no matching tile draws from the boneyard until they can play or the boneyard runs dry; only then do they pass.
  • Domino! A player who plays their last tile ends the round and scores every pip left in every other hand.
  • Blocked. If every player passes in a row, the round is blocked. The hand holding the fewest pips wins and scores the difference: all other hands' pips, minus their own. A tie for fewest scores nothing.
  • The match. First to the target score takes the match.

This page's rules follow that documented game. The double-nine set and the no-draw pass rule are house variants, labelled as variants wherever they are in play.

The strategies the Coach teaches (tap to open)
  • Shed the weight. Play your heaviest tiles first. Pips left in your hand at the end of a round become somebody else's points — the 6-6 is 12 points waiting to be given away.
  • Keep both doors open. Pros play so their remaining tiles can answer either end. A hand that can only play on one number is a hand one bad end away from the boneyard.
  • Starve the table. Count what's been played. If six of the seven tiles carrying a number are already down, making that number an open end leaves opponents almost nothing to answer with — they draw, you breathe.
  • Spend the double. A double can only ever answer one number, and it's usually heavy. Play it while its number is up; don't carry it hoping.
  • Watch the draw. When an opponent draws three tiles hunting one number, they've told you what they don't hold. The Coach points at this when it happens.

Where more than one strategy fits a position, the Coach offers each one with its trade-off — a single line only when there is genuinely a single play.
