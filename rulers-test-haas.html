The Ruler's Test — Brigitte Haas, Prime Minister of Liechtenstein

Subject: Brigitte Haas — Prime Minister (Head of Government, Regierungschefin) of Liechtenstein since 10 April 2025, and the first woman to hold the office. The seat graded is the head of government, and the framing is stated plainly because Liechtenstein's constitution does not put executive power in that seat alone: under the 1921 constitution as amended in 2003, state power is held jointly by the Prince and the people (Article 2), every law requires the Prince's sanction to take effect, and the Prince may dismiss the government, dissolve the Landtag, rule by emergency decree, and discontinue criminal investigations. The reigning Prince is Hans-Adam II (head of state since 1989), but since 15 August 2004 he has delegated the exercise of his powers to his son, Hereditary Prince Alois, as regent — so it is Alois who swore Haas in, Alois who gives or withholds sanction to laws, and Alois whose powers are graded here wherever they bear on the tests, above all Test 2. The honest summary of the record below is that Haas runs the day-to-day government, but the Prince is the final executive: no law she or the Landtag passes exists until he signs it, and in October 2026 he refused to sign one. Haas, born 27 December 1964 in Vaduz, is a lawyer and the former managing director of the Liechtenstein Chamber of Commerce and Industry. She was the Patriotic Union's (VU) candidate for prime minister in the general election of 9 February 2025, after the incumbent Daniel Risch (VU) declined to seek another term. The VU held its plurality of 10 of 25 Landtag seats on 38.32% of the vote; the Progressive Citizens' Party (FBP) fell to 7 seats, the Democrats for Liechtenstein (DpL) rose to 6 — the best third-party result in the country's history — and the Free List (FL) took 2, on turnout of 76.34%. The VU and FBP renewed their long-standing coalition, and Haas's five-member government — herself (General Government Affairs and Finance), Deputy Prime Minister Sabine Monauni (FBP; Foreign Affairs, Environment and Culture), Hubert Büchel (VU), Daniel Oehry (FBP) and Emanuel Schädler (VU) — was sworn in by Hereditary Prince Alois at Vaduz Castle on 10 April 2025, for the 2025–2029 term. She is graded on roughly nineteen months in office. The microstate record is thinner than the large-state records in this series — Liechtenstein has about 41,000 residents and limited international coverage — and where the shelf is thin, this file says so rather than padding it. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure; the Test grades the holding, never the platform, and carrying out an electoral mandate is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes, and all four crossings are named in Test 5 where the record carries them; where a crossing is not documented, the file records "not documented" rather than inventing one. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • No documented case in the record assembled here shows Haas's government applying a rule to an opponent that it waived for an ally, prosecuting selectively, or directing a benefit to her own party's networks. The absence is recorded as an absence, in a country where the Council of Europe's anti-corruption monitor describes perceived corruption levels as low and virtually no corruption cases in the judiciary or prosecution service (GRECO Fourth Round Evaluation Report on Liechtenstein, adopted September 2020, published December 2020).
  • The coalition itself is the country's standing cross-party arrangement, applied the same way after this election as before it: the VU (10 seats) governs with the FBP (7 seats), the deputy premiership sits with the junior partner, and the constitution requires the five-member government to include at least two members from each of the Oberland and Unterland regions (Government of Liechtenstein; Liechtenstein-USA embassy report, April 2025). The rule that allocates the seats is regional and partisan-neutral on its face, and it was followed.
  • The state's own anti-corruption machinery continues to grade the government from outside, and the government submits to it: GRECO's December 2025 follow-up concluded Liechtenstein had fully implemented 10 of its 16 fourth-round recommendations on MPs, judges and prosecutors, partly implemented five, and not implemented one — progress and gaps both published, with a further report demanded by 30 June 2026 (Council of Europe / GRECO, 4 December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • At the source, there is a second law, and it is constitutional: the Prince stands outside the rules that bind everyone else. He is not answerable to the courts for his official acts, and — the sharpest item on this shelf — he holds the power to block or discontinue criminal proceedings. GRECO's Fifth Round evaluation report of 27 May 2025, covering persons with top executive functions including the government and the Prince himself, "reiterates its long-standing concern regarding the Prince's powers to block or discontinue criminal proceedings in respect of persons with top executive functions suspected of having committed corruption related offences," calls those powers "a potential threat to the independence and impartiality of the criminal justice system," and recommends they be revised (GRECO / Council of Europe, 27 May 2025). A system in which one unelected office-holder can stop a corruption case against a minister is not one law at the source, whatever the day-to-day practice. This is graded against the seat's settlement, not as an act of Haas's — no use of that power by Alois is documented — but Haas's government has not, on the documented record, moved to revise it, and GRECO also recommends, still unmet on the record here, publishing information on meetings between the Prince and the Prime Minister (GRECO, 27 May 2025).
  • The integrity framework around the government's own people has documented gaps that GRECO's May 2025 report names: no co-ordinated integrity strategy for top executive functions yet, shortcomings in the access-to-information framework, and a recommendation — not yet a rule — that all top executive officials be required to declare assets, income, liabilities and financial interests publicly and regularly (GRECO / Council of Europe, 27 May 2025). At parliamentary level, GRECO's December 2025 follow-up notes no tangible progress on transparency of Landtag commission proceedings (GRECO, 4 December 2025).
  • Political finance remains a lighter law for insiders than the standard allows: parties may not accept anonymous donations above CHF 300 under the 2019 reform, but they are not required to publicly disclose known donors, and campaign financing remains largely unregulated (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2023, reporting the GRECO position).

Why this grade: On Haas's own conduct the shelf is clean but thin — no documented favoritism, in a small state with few watchdogs. On the source itself the shelf is not clean at all: the constitution she governs under reserves to the Prince an exemption from, and a hand inside, the criminal law that GRECO has flagged for years. One law for the governed, a reserved law for the palace — that is a MIXED file, and the failing half is structural rather than personal.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — no group is documented as helped by favoritism, which is itself the point in Haas's favor; the coalition and regional-balance rules distribute office predictably across parties and both regions. Hurt — anyone whose corruption complaint ever ran against a top executive official stands, in law, one princely decision away from seeing the case discontinued (GRECO, May 2025); that exposure is documented as a power, not as an event. The general public is hurt in a smaller, documented way by the unpublished Prince–Prime Minister meetings and the un-disclosed donor lists: it cannot check what it cannot see.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • In day-to-day practice under Haas, the referees have visibly acted on their own. The February 2025 election was administered under an electoral framework the OSCE needs-assessment generally praised (while noting the high 8% party threshold and constituency-size questions), and it seated an opposition — the DpL — at its strongest ever, six seats (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026, abridged report via ecoi.net; International IDEA election summary, 2025).
  • The courts have ruled against the palace's stated preferences before and survived it: in June 2021 the Constitutional Court held the ban on adoption by same-sex couples unlawful, months after Hans-Adam II had publicly threatened a veto of adoption legislation; the adoption law took effect in June 2023, and the same-sex marriage law — passed by the Landtag 24–1 on 16 May 2024 — received Alois's assent on 9 July 2024 and entered into force on 1 January 2025 (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022; same-sex marriage legislative record). A judiciary that can do that is not a puppet judiciary in ordinary times.
  • The media file cuts in Haas's favor personally: the closure of Radio Liechtenstein, the country's only public broadcaster, on 3 April 2025 was not her government's grip but the voters'. A DpL initiative to repeal the public-broadcasting funding law passed a referendum on 27 October 2024 by 55.4% to 44.6%; the outgoing government had campaigned against it, warning it could end radio broadcasting in the country; no private investor came forward, and the station closed days before Haas took office (2024 Liechtenstein referendums record; State Media Monitor, LRF profile; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026). Haas herself had publicly opposed the privatization initiative during the campaign, against her own party's supporting position — she implemented a verdict she disagreed with (Brigitte Haas political record, 2024–2025).

Evidence against:

  • The Prince's grip on the referees is written into the constitution and was widened by the 2003 settlement. In the referendum of 14 March 2003, the Princely Initiative — extending the Prince's power to dismiss the government, nominate judges and veto legislation — passed with 64.32% after Hans-Adam II had threatened to leave the country and live in exile in Vienna if voters restricted his powers; the Venice Commission of the Council of Europe had opined in December 2002 that the amendments were not compatible with the European standard of democracy (2003 constitutional referendum record; Venice Commission opinion as reported). A 2012 referendum proposal to revoke the Prince's veto over referendum outcomes was rejected by 76% of voters (Freedom House). The grip is popular; it is still a grip, and this Test grades the holding, not its popularity.
  • The grip is not theoretical. The Prince appoints the government on the Landtag's proposal but may dismiss the government or any minister at will, may dissolve the Landtag, may appoint a provisional government for up to four months without the Landtag's consent, and may issue emergency decrees. The dismissal power has been used: on 15 September 1993 Hans-Adam II dismissed both the government of Prime Minister Markus Büchel and the Landtag and called early elections, against the wishes of Büchel's own party, after the Landtag had passed no confidence in Büchel the day before (Markus Büchel cabinet record, 1993). Haas holds her seat, in law, at the pleasure of the man who swore her in.
  • The judiciary's appointment chain runs through the palace: judges are selected through a board chaired by the Prince or his appointee, and Freedom House scores judicial independence 3 of 4 precisely because "the constitution gives the prince powerful influence over the appointment of judges, meaning the process lacks a key element of democratic accountability" (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2023). GRECO's standing recommendations include significantly increasing the judiciary's own role in selecting judges (GRECO Fourth Round, 2020). The prosecution's autonomy carries the same reservation from Test 1: the Prince may discontinue investigations (GRECO, May 2025).
  • The grip was exercised, for the first time in Alois's regency, inside the grading period — on legislation, the referee that matters most. A popular initiative, "Time-Limit Solution for Liechtenstein" (Fristenlösung), submitted on 9 February 2026 with 4,970 signatures — roughly a quarter of the electorate, against a threshold of 1,000 — would have decriminalized abortion in the first twelve weeks, lifted the information ban, and required health-insurance coverage. Alois announced in June 2026, before the Landtag voted, that he would veto it. The Landtag approved it on 2 September 2026 by 13 votes to 12, and by the same margin declined to send it to a popular vote. On 20 October 2026 the Princely House announced Alois would withhold his sanction, stating he "remains convinced that the proposed reform is not compatible with ensuring adequate protection of unborn life as a fundamental legal value," while seeing room for "targeted measures" within the existing framework. Under Articles 9 and 65 of the constitution, no law takes effect without the Prince's sanction and there is no parliamentary override (Law Library of Congress, Global Legal Monitor, 17 November 2026; Princely House media release, 20 October 2026; National Catholic Register / CNA, October 2026). One unelected office-holder set aside both a citizen initiative backed by a quarter of the electorate and the elected chamber's majority — lawfully, openly, and completely. That is the definition this Test grades.

Why this grade: Haas personally has not gripped a referee — the documented interferences are the Prince's, not hers. But the task's framing, confirmed by the record, is that the Prince is the final executive of this state: he appoints and can dismiss her government, chairs the chain that appoints its judges, can stop its prosecutions, and in October 2026 personally nullified a law. A head of government who holds office inside that grip, and whose countersignature the constitution requires beside the Prince's sanction, cannot be graded as holding without gripping when the system she heads gripped in the grading period itself. FAILS — with the responsibility named where it sits: the hand on the referees is Alois's; Haas's share is that she governs within the settlement and has not, on the documented record, sought to loosen it.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the Princely House, whose authority over legislation, government tenure and judicial selection was confirmed in practice in 2026; voters who support the veto as a moral safeguard, including the 76% who voted in 2012 to keep it, hold a protection they chose. Hurt — the 4,970 initiative signatories and the 13-member Landtag majority, whose completed democratic act was annulled by one signature withheld; women in Liechtenstein seeking a first-trimester abortion, who remain under a criminal-law framework with narrow exceptions (rape, grave danger to life or health) and must travel to Switzerland or Austria (Law Library of Congress, November 2026); judges and prosecutors, whose formal independence remains qualified by the appointment and discontinuation powers above them (GRECO; Freedom House). About 250 people marched to Vaduz Castle in protest on the day of the veto announcement (initiative committee statement via PR Newswire, November 2026 — a participant-side source, recorded as such).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clearest wobble of the period was a referendum her side lost, and the state adjusted in the open. The government campaigned against repealing Radio Liechtenstein's funding law; voters approved the repeal 55.4% on 27 October 2024; the funding law lapsed, privatization was attempted and failed for want of an investor, and the station closed on 3 April 2025 — the verdict implemented, not litigated, delayed or reversed (2024 referendums record; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026). Haas, who had personally opposed the initiative, took office a week later and did not reopen it.
  • The voters' other recent pushbacks were likewise absorbed: the September 2024 referendum approving IMF accession (55.8% for) was carried through — Liechtenstein's IMF membership proceeded and its second Article IV consultation took place in January 2026 (2024 referendums record; IMF, January–March 2026) — and the February 2024 referendum rejecting direct election of government members was accepted by the governing parties that had opposed the proposal, with no attempt to achieve it by another route (International IDEA, 2025).
  • Her government's response to economic pushback has been published reasoning rather than denial: facing flat projected growth for 2026, weaker corporate tax revenue and IMF advice to avoid an overly tight fiscal stance, the government's 2026–29 budgetary plan raised investment, social welfare and digitalization spending while projecting continued surpluses — the adjustment and its numbers in the open (IMF Article IV Staff Report, March 2026).
  • On the abortion file, the Prince — whose wobble this primarily was — did engage the other side before deciding: the Princely House states he held numerous discussions on the initiative over months, and he met the initiative committee on 20 October 2026, the day the veto was announced (Princely House media release, 20 October 2026; initiative committee statement, November 2026). Under the Test's other-option rule — "if another option becomes available" — considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence, and his stated reason was printed with the decision. What keeps this off the FOR shelf in full is the timing: the veto had been announced in June 2026, before the Landtag had voted, so the consideration ran toward a conclusion declared in advance (EWTN News / Keystone-SDA reporting, September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The street's pushback after the veto was answered with the decision restated, not with any adjustment: about 250 protesters marched to Vaduz Castle on 20 October 2026, and an open letter from hundreds of feminist leaders, physicians and scholars urging Alois to trust women was sent on 23–24 November 2026; no documented response, concession or reopened process from the Princely House or from Haas's government appears in the record assembled here as of grading (initiative committee / PR Newswire, November 2026). The initiative's supporters characterize the outcome as one man setting aside a parliament — their characterization, recorded as theirs.
  • Haas's own public position on the largest pushback of her tenure is not documented. The abortion initiative ran through her parliament and her countersignature line for nineteen months of her term; the record assembled here contains no statement by Haas for or against the reform, the veto, or a compromise within the "targeted measures" opening Alois named. Silence is not denial, and this shelf records it as an absence — but a head of government who does not answer the wobble in public has not answered it openly either.
  • The deeper wobbles against the settlement itself have been answered by the electorate, not by its holders: the 2003 expansion passed under a princely threat to leave the country, and the 2012 attempt to limit the veto failed 76–24 — after which neither the palace nor any government, including Haas's, has put a revised settlement to the voters (2003 and 2012 referendum records; Freedom House). The no-good-choice clause does not apply here: putting a question to this electorate is cheap, lawful and routine — eight referendums were held in 2024 alone — so the un-asked question is a choice, not a trap.

Why this grade: Where the wobble arrived through the ballot box, this government and its predecessor absorbed losses and implemented them — that is the FOR shelf, and it is real. Where the wobble arrived against the palace's own position in 2026, the answer was a pre-announced veto, a same-day meeting, and then silence from the head of government. Both halves are documented; neither cancels the other.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — voters as a body, whose referendum verdicts (radio funding, IMF accession, direct election) were each carried out as cast, including the ones the government opposed; economic actors and households, who got a published, IMF-reviewed fiscal adjustment rather than austerity by stealth. Hurt — the initiative committee and its 4,970 signatories, who won in parliament and lost at the castle with no documented governmental answer; listeners of Radio Liechtenstein, whose station the adjustment closed — hurt by a democratic verdict, recorded here because the ledger names who bore it, not because the verdict was wrongly taken.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the women, the foreign residents, the opposition's voters, and the minorities — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The headline count of this term is a first: the country that introduced women's suffrage only in 1984 — among the last in Europe, after male voters rejected it in 1971 and 1973 — is governed by a woman, elected as her party's named candidate for the office, not appointed to it as a placeholder (Government of Liechtenstein; The European interview with Haas, 2025, in which she herself ties the milestone to the 1984 date). Women in the Landtag rose from 7 to 8 of 25 (32.0%) at the 2025 election (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026; IPU Parline, 2025).
  • The count widened on the palace's own side in 2026: on 15 August 2026 Alois announced a House Law amendment, adopted by the required two-thirds of voting family members on 12 August, moving succession from male-only primogeniture to absolute primogeniture and granting female descendants the same participation rights as male descendants (Princely House media release, 15 August 2026). The limit is printed with it: the change applies only to future births and does not alter the current line — Alois's daughter, Princess Marie Caroline (born 1996), remains outside the succession her younger brothers stand in (Princely House release; succession record, 2026).
  • The equality legislation of the previous term was carried into force, not stalled, under the state Haas now heads: same-sex marriage entered into force on 1 January 2025, with full adoption rights already in effect since June 2023 — in a file where the palace had once threatened a veto and then, in 2024, assented (legislative record; Freedom House). No documented step by Haas's government to narrow any group's rights appears in the record assembled here.
  • The government's own programme counts the expensive people in: its stated priorities include long-term social security, an excellent healthcare system that "must not become a luxury," and prevention and support for mental illness (Haas, Ambassador Information Day speech, 14 November 2025). The fiscal record behind it is broadly shared prosperity by the available measures: unemployment averaging about 1.5%, general government net financial assets above 140% of GDP, virtually no public debt (IMF Article IV, March 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The largest counted-out group in the country is counted out by citizenship, and nothing in Haas's documented programme addresses it: as of 30 June 2025 the permanent resident population was 41,024, of whom 35.2% were foreigners — roughly one resident in three, many of them long-term — while political rights at national level belong to citizens only (Liechtenstein-Institut electoral study, 2025, citing official population figures; Constitution, Article 29). These residents are taxed, schooled and policed by a state they cannot vote on, and the 21,183 eligible voters of February 2025 are barely half the population.
  • Women are counted in at the top and still undercounted in the chamber: 8 of 25 Landtag seats (32%), no quotas, and OSCE-noted low female candidacy — fewer than a third of the largest parties' list places in 2025 (IPU Parline; OSCE/ODIHR as reported by International IDEA, 2025). The 2026 veto file belongs on this shelf as well: the group that bore its cost is women, specifically women who cannot afford or manage the trip abroad that the current law makes the practical route (Law Library of Congress, November 2026). The decision was the Prince's; the counting-out happened inside Haas's state and term.
  • The opposition's voters are counted at the ballot and not in the government: the DpL's 23.32% — nearly a quarter of the electorate, the strongest third-party vote in the country's history — holds no seat in the five-member government, which the two historic parties divided between themselves as they have for decades (2025 election record). Coalition exclusion is ordinary parliamentary practice and is not graded as a wrong; it is recorded because the ledger asks who is inside the accounting, and a quarter of the voters are outside this one.
  • The 8% national threshold for Landtag representation, flagged by the OSCE needs assessment, is a standing count-out of small-party voters in a 25-seat chamber (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026).

Why this grade: Haas's own term adds genuine firsts — her office itself, a succession reform that counts future daughters, an equality law carried into force, a programme that names healthcare affordability and mental health. Against that stand the structural exclusions she inherited and has not, on the documented record, moved on: a third of residents without a vote, women at a third of the chamber, and a 2026 decision by the Prince whose cost fell on women alone. The counting improved at the top of the state in her term; it did not widen at the base.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — women as a political class, by the precedent of the office and by the succession amendment's future effect; same-sex couples, whose marriage and adoption rights took full effect in this period; patients and the socially insured, named as protected priorities in the government's programme with the fiscal reserves to fund them (IMF, 2026). Hurt — the roughly 14,400 foreign residents (35.2% of 41,024) with no national vote; women seeking abortions, counted above; Princess Marie Caroline and women of the current princely generation, expressly left outside the succession reform that counts their unborn nieces (Princely House release, 15 August 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 10 April 2025, with dates; the Prince's word graded where his acts decided outcomes.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The government programme — delivered, and governing to it. Haas's government published its 2025–2029 programme, "Shaping the state, securing the future – working together for tomorrow," covering 35 topics under the stated key points of stability and security, with stable public finances, a holistic national security strategy, affordable healthcare and mental-health provision among its named commitments (Haas speech, 14 November 2025). The IMF's January 2026 mission records the new government as having "maintained a broadly unchanged economic policy stance while placing greater emphasis on national security" — the programme's own description of itself, confirmed by an outside examiner (IMF Article IV Staff Report, March 2026). One lateness is recorded: the programme arrived seven months into the term, as Haas's own speech notes ("in the first seven months of our term… this has resulted in our government programme").
  • The tax word — kept so far. During the 2025 campaign Haas stated she did not intend to introduce an inheritance tax or a gift tax; none has been introduced in the documented record of her term (Haas campaign position, 2025; IMF fiscal record, 2026). Per the mandate rule, governing on the platform the voters elected is FOR-shelf evidence here, not a demerit.
  • The fiscal word — kept in numbers. The programme's promise of stable public finances is, nineteen months in, the documented fact: a 2025 general government surplus of 2.8% of GDP, a projected 3% surplus for 2026, net financial assets above 140% of GDP and virtually zero debt (IMF Article IV, March 2026).
  • The Prince's word, on the file where his word decided the outcome — kept, and the keeping is recorded on both axes. Alois stated in June 2026 that he would veto the time-limit initiative; on 20 October 2026 he did. That is crossing 2 in the Test's terms: kept his word AND it still hurt people — the keeping earns word-credit here; the hurt (to the initiative's signatories, the Landtag majority, and women under the unchanged criminal framework) is recorded in full in Tests 2 and 4 and is not laundered by the keeping. Supporters of the veto record the same act as kept-word-and-helped — protection of unborn life, in the Princely House's stated terms — and that characterization is printed as theirs (Princely House release, 20 October 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The countersignature silence. A Liechtenstein law requires, beside the Prince's sanction, the countersignature of the head of government (Constitution, Article 65, as cited by the Law Library of Congress, November 2026). Haas's government neither championed the Landtag's September 2026 decision nor publicly opposed the veto of it, on the record assembled here. No pledge of hers on this file is documented — she made no campaign promise on abortion that the record shows — so this is not recorded as a broken word; it is recorded as the absence of a word where her office stood in the chain.
  • The programme's long promises are stated as direction, not yet as record: the comprehensive national security strategy, the healthcare-affordability guarantee and the mental-health expansion are, at nineteen months, programme text and early work, with the IMF noting that aging, climate and security spending pressures of 3–3½% of GDP are not yet systematically quantified in fiscal plans (IMF, March 2026). Recorded here so the record can grade them later; neither kept nor broken now.
  • The remaining crossings, named as the method requires: went against her word AND it helped people — not documented for Haas in this term. Broke her word AND it hurt people — not documented for Haas in this term. The file records both as absences rather than filling them: in nineteen months, no documented broken pledge by Haas appears in the record assembled here, and the grade rests on that record, not on a presumption either way.
  • Context — the seat's inherited word: her predecessor Daniel Risch's government (2021–2025) delivered the IMF-accession process and the same-sex marriage legislation that took effect at the turn of 2025, and Risch's own stated decision not to seek another term was honored — the orderly handover that seated Haas is part of the seat's word-keeping record (2025 election record; legislative record).

Why this grade: What Haas has promised in specific, checkable terms — no inheritance or gift tax, stable finances, a published programme — she has so far kept, and the IMF's numbers carry the fiscal half of that. What would move the grade either way is mostly still in the future: a 2025–2029 programme graded at month nineteen is a promise with a good first chapter, not a kept one. The one fully completed word on the state's biggest 2026 file was the Prince's, not hers — kept exactly as spoken, with its hurt recorded beside it. MIXED, leaning on documented delivery, with the thinness of a nineteen-month microstate record stated rather than concealed.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — taxpayers and heirs, by the kept no-new-inheritance-tax word; households and firms planning on stability, by surpluses delivered as programmed; supporters of the existing abortion framework, by the Prince's kept veto word. Hurt — the initiative's supporters and women under the unchanged law, by that same kept word (crossing 2, recorded in full above); future patients and pensioners bear a documented risk, not yet a harm, in the unquantified aging and healthcare spending pressures the IMF flags (IMF, March 2026).

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED.

Brigitte Haas is the mildest holder of the hardest seat in this batch: a competent, coalition-bound head of government, elected on a plurality, who in nineteen months has broken no documented pledge, favored no documented ally, and gripped no documented referee — governing a state whose constitution reserves all three of those powers, in their strongest European form, to a hereditary prince who is not her. That is the dossier in one line. Test 1 is MIXED because her own administration shows no second law while the source itself is one: GRECO's May 2025 report still flags the Prince's power to discontinue corruption prosecutions against top officials as a threat to impartial justice, unrevised on her watch. Test 2 FAILS — the one failing grade, and it belongs to the settlement more than to the woman: in October 2026 Hereditary Prince Alois exercised the first veto of his 22-year regency to annul a citizen initiative signed by a quarter of the electorate and passed by the Landtag 13–12, under a constitution that also lets him dismiss Haas's government at will, dissolve the Landtag, and chair the chain that appoints the judges; the Prince, on this record, is the final executive of Liechtenstein, and the head of government holds without gripping only because the grip above her is not hers. Test 3 is MIXED because the ballot-box wobbles — the lost radio referendum, IMF accession, the rejected direct-election proposal — were each implemented as cast, including by a prime minister who personally opposed one of them, while the 2026 wobble against the palace was met with a pre-announced veto and no documented public answer from Haas at all.
