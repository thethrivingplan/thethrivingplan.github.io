The Ruler's Test — Lawrence Wong, Prime Minister of Singapore

Subject: Lawrence Wong Shyun Tsai — Prime Minister of Singapore since 15 May 2024, when he succeeded Lee Hsien Loong as the country's fourth prime minister; he also remains Minister for Finance, and became secretary-general of the governing People's Action Party (PAP) on 4 December 2024. The seat graded is the premiership. Wong, a former civil servant (chief executive of the Energy Market Authority, principal private secretary to Lee Hsien Loong) who entered politics in 2011, rose through the finance portfolio: as Minister for Finance he delivered the GST increase from 7% to 9% (announced 2022, phased in January 2023 and January 2024) and chaired the Forward Singapore consultation exercise whose report set the social-policy agenda he now governs on. He led the PAP into a general election for the first time on 3 May 2025 and won a strengthened mandate: 65.57% of the popular vote (up from 61.23% in 2020) and 87 of 97 elected seats, with the Workers' Party (WP) retaining all 10 of its elected seats and taking two Non-Constituency MP seats (Elections Department final results, May 2025). He is graded on his premiership — roughly thirty months, May 2024 to November 2026 — with the longer PAP system's record used as context only where it bears directly on his seat: several of the structures graded below (the electoral machinery's reporting lines, POFMA, the mandatory death penalty, the migrant-labour framework) were built before him, but they are his to keep, amend, or repeal, and the record of what he did with them is his. Two major files straddle the handover and are dated exactly where they do: the prosecution of former transport minister S. Iswaran was begun under Lee (charged January 2024) and concluded under Wong (guilty plea September 2024, sentenced October 2024); the GST increase was Wong's own policy as finance minister, completed four months before he took the top seat. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machinery reached the top of his own party and his own cabinet table, and was not stopped. The Corrupt Practices Investigation Bureau's investigation of Transport Minister S. Iswaran — the first graft investigation of a minister since 1986 — ran to completion across the leadership transition: Iswaran pleaded guilty on 24 September 2024 to four counts of obtaining valuable items as a public servant under Section 165 of the Penal Code and one count of obstructing justice, and on 3 October 2024 was sentenced to 12 months' imprisonment — the first Singaporean minister jailed since 1975. The sentencing judge imposed a term substantially above the six-to-seven months the prosecution had sought, in open court, against the government's own former minister (High Court, Public Prosecutor v S Iswaran [2024] SGHC 251; CPIB and court reporting, October 2024). Iswaran completed his sentence in June 2025. A constitutional safeguard exists on paper for exactly this case — if a prime minister refused consent to a CPIB investigation, the Bureau may seek the President's concurrence — and on this record it never had to be invoked, because the investigation was never blocked.
  • The gift-giver was prosecuted too. Property tycoon Ong Beng Seng, whose gifts to Iswaran (Doha flights, hotel stays, Grand Prix and football tickets, valued in total at more than S$384,000) sat at the centre of the case, was charged in October 2024 and pleaded guilty on 4 August 2025 to abetting the obstruction of justice, with a second charge of abetting a Section 165 offence taken into consideration (Attorney-General's Chambers statement, 4 August 2025).
  • The same legal machinery was applied to the government's chief opponent, through the same courts, with the same rights of appeal. Workers' Party leader Pritam Singh was convicted on 17 February 2025 of two counts of lying to Parliament's Committee of Privileges, fined S$7,000 per count — a sum below the constitutional threshold that would have cost him his seat — and his appeal was heard and dismissed by the High Court on 4 December 2025 (Justice Steven Chong). The government's stated position, maintained throughout, is that both cases are the one law operating: a minister jailed, an opposition leader fined, each by judges, each with appeal rights exercised (PMO and ministerial statements, 2024–2026).
  • The outcome measure stayed at the top of the world table through his tenure: Singapore ranked third of 182 countries, score 84 of 100, in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index for both 2024 and 2025 (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • In the Iswaran case the prosecution narrowed its own case in ways critics across the spectrum questioned. Iswaran faced 35 charges, including two of corruption under the Prevention of Corruption Act; the prosecution ultimately proceeded on five lesser charges — four of obtaining gifts as a public servant and one of obstruction — and the corruption charges were not among them. The Attorney-General's Chambers attributed the decision to the evidence available; opposition politicians and legal commentators publicly questioned whether a case that began as corruption ended as something smaller — a dispute recorded here as contested, not resolved (AGC statements and contemporaneous commentary, September 2024).
  • The two ends of the same transaction landed very differently, and the contrast is the single most-cited exhibit against the one-law claim. Iswaran, the gift-taker, went to prison for a year. Ong Beng Seng, the gift-giver, received the maximum district-court fine of S$30,000 and no jail, after both prosecution and defence agreed that his advanced multiple myeloma warranted judicial mercy — a court's decision on medical evidence, stated at its exact strength (State Courts sentencing, 15 August 2025). In November 2025 the Immigration & Checkpoints Authority reviewed Ong's permanent residency and kept it, issuing a letter of warning instead of revocation (ICA statement via press reporting, 10 November 2025). Whether the outcomes are each defensible in isolation is not the test; the test is whether the source gives by one law, and the documented public perception — recorded in opposition statements and public commentary throughout 2025–2026 — is of a gradient that runs downhill toward wealth. Perception is recorded here as perception; the judicial reasoning in both cases is on the record beside it.
  • The speech laws' incidence is one-directional on the documented record. Correction directions under POFMA, and defamation suits by ministers, have in his tenure fallen on opposition figures, independent media, activists, and a foreign news agency (Tests 2 and 4); no comparable documented instance exists of the same instruments being used against a government supporter or a state-linked outlet for a falsehood of similar prominence. Ministers respond that directions issue only where false statements of fact are shown, whoever utters them; critics respond that the pattern is the finding. The pattern itself is documented; its explanation is contested.
  • The Leader of the Opposition file (Test 2) began in this machinery: the Committee of Privileges that found Singh had lied was a committee of a PAP-majority parliament, its findings were referred to the public prosecutor by that parliament, and the parliamentary motion of January 2026 that preceded his removal was moved by the Leader of the House from his own party. Each step was lawful and each is documented; the sequence is also one in which, at every institutional step before the courts, the referee and the opposing team were the same people. The courts' part of the sequence was independent (above); the parts before and after it were his party's.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts ruled against the prosecution's own asks in the most politically charged case of the tenure (Iswaran, Test 1: 12 months imposed against six-to-seven sought), and ruled in the opposition leader's case by the book in both directions — convicting him, fining him below the disqualification threshold the constitution sets, and hearing his appeal in full before dismissing it. Singh kept his seat, contested the May 2025 election, and was returned by his voters. No judge has been removed, transferred, or publicly disciplined on this record; no court order against the government has been defied.
  • Elections on the day are clean, and the opposition's wins stand. The 3 May 2025 election was administered without documented irregularity in counting or results; the WP retained all ten of its elected seats, increased several of its margins, and received the two NCMP seats the scheme guarantees the best-performing opposition losers. The result the machinery produced was accepted by all parties the same night (Elections Department results, May 2025).
  • The central bank's autonomy is undocumented as a problem because it is undocumented, full stop: the Monetary Authority of Singapore adjusted monetary policy on its own published cycle through 2025–2026, and the record assembled here contains no instance of the executive directing, overriding, or punishing an MAS decision. The same is true of the judiciary's budget and the civil service's senior appointments on this record — the grip, where it exists in Singapore, is structural and legal rather than personal and episodic, and the shelves below grade the structure.
  • Even the hardest press case of the tenure ran through an open court rather than an administrative shutdown. When two of his ministers sued Bloomberg and its reporter for defamation over the December 2024 Good Class Bungalow article, Bloomberg defended the suit at trial, maintained it stood by its reporting, and the matter was decided by a High Court judgment (Justice Audrey Lim) awarding each minister S$178,000 in damages in 2026 — a loss for the press, reached by litigation the press could contest, not by licence or closure (New York Law Journal case summary, July 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The electoral referee sits inside his office. The Elections Department is a department under the Prime Minister's Office, not an independent commission. The Electoral Boundaries Review Committee is convened by the prime minister, works to terms of reference the prime minister issues, and is chaired by the Secretary to the Prime Minister. For the 2025 cycle Wong convened the EBRC on 22 January 2025; its report of 11 March 2025 redrew the map from 93 to 97 seats across 33 divisions, with only nine constituencies left untouched, and the changes fell heavily on districts where the PAP had faced its strongest 2020 challenges (West Coast, East Coast, and the dissolved Bukit Batok SMC among them). Opposition parties called the changes drastic and the process opaque; gerrymandering is alleged by the opposition and by independent media (Jom, March 2025) and denied by the government, which attributes every change to elector growth — the allegation is recorded as an ALLEGATION, the structural reporting lines as fact. In August 2024, under Wong, parliament voted down (76 votes against) an opposition motion to reform the EBRC, including appointing a judge as its chair — the documented point at which the grip was challenged in the open and kept.
  • The Leader of the Opposition exists at his pleasure, and the pleasure was exercised. The office is not in the constitution or standing orders; it is designated by the prime minister. On 14 January 2026 parliament — his party's majority — passed a motion expressing regret at Singh's conduct and deeming him unsuitable to continue; on 15 January 2026 Wong removed him, stating that the conviction and parliament's view made the position "no longer tenable," and invited the WP to nominate a replacement who had not been implicated in the Committee of Privileges' findings — a condition excluding the WP's other senior figures the COP had cited. The WP declined on 21 January 2026, holding that the office belongs to the leader of the largest opposition party; the post has stood vacant since, and Singh also lost the office's allowance and right of first reply (PMO statement, 15 January 2026; Reuters, 15 and 21 January 2026). Every step was within his lawful discretion; that is precisely the finding — the institution's independence lasts exactly as long as the seat-holder's forbearance, and in January 2026 the forbearance ended. Singh's own party re-elected him secretary-general in June 2026; his voters and his party kept him, and the office their votes had sustained stayed empty.
  • The media environment is held by law, funding, and litigation, and the independent measures moved the wrong way on his watch. Singapore stood 123rd of 180 in RSF's World Press Freedom Index in both 2025 and 2026, with its underlying score falling (45.78 to 44.57) and its legal indicator at 164th (RSF, 2025 and 2026 indexes). On 23 December 2024 his government issued POFMA correction directions to Bloomberg, The Edge Singapore, The Independent Singapore, and The Online Citizen over the GCB article — Bloomberg appended the notice while stating it "respectfully disagrees" and stood by its reporting (Ministry of Law press release, 23 December 2024). In March 2026 a further correction direction issued to The Online Citizen over an article on the Attorney-General's re-appointment; TOC's editor Terry Xu was ordered in the ministers' defamation proceedings to pay S$420,000 in damages (March 2026) and a further S$154,462.50 in costs (May 2026) (CIVICUS Monitor reporting, 2026). In April 2026 an activist, Kokila Annamalai, became the first person charged with the criminal offence of failing to comply with a POFMA correction direction — the direction having issued in October 2024 over anti-death-penalty content (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The main domestic media group, SPH Media Trust, operates on government funding committed by the previous administration and continued under his; no structural reform of the Newspaper and Printing Presses Act framework has been proposed in his tenure.
  • The prosecution service's two most sensitive decisions of the tenure — the narrowing of the Iswaran charges (Test 1) and the charging of the opposition leader on a parliamentary committee's referral — were both taken by an Attorney-General's Chambers whose head is appointed on the executive's advice. No document in the record shows the AGC acting on instruction; the structural point stands beside the clean personal record, and both are recorded.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Income–Allianz reversal is the strongest open adjustment in this series' recent files. In July 2024 Allianz's proposed S$2.2 billion acquisition of a 51% stake in Income Insurance — the former NTUC co-operative insurer — was announced with NTUC's support and no initial government objection. Public and parliamentary pushback was immediate and sustained, centred on Income's social mission and a planned capital reduction of some S$1.85 billion to shareholders. The government re-examined the deal, and on 14 October 2024 Minister Edwin Tong told parliament the transaction "would not be in the public interest" in its current form; an urgent amendment to the Insurance Act was passed to let the MAS minister block it, and Wong stated the position personally — concerns were with "the structure and terms of this specific transaction," and the government remained open to a revised deal addressing them. Allianz withdrew its offer on 16 December 2024 (Parliament, 14–16 October 2024; PMO social-media statement, 14 October 2024; Allianz media release, 16 December 2024). The adjustment was made against the government's own labour movement, in public, with reasons, within three months of the pushback beginning.
  • The ACRA/NRIC failure was owned at the top. When ACRA's new Bizfile portal exposed full NRIC numbers in free search results (9–13 December 2024), the service was suspended, two ministers apologised at a press conference on 19 December 2024, Wong directed the Head of Civil Service to convene a review panel, the panel's findings of coordination failure were accepted by the government and published, and the restored service shows no NRIC numbers at all (ACRA and MDDI statements, December 2024; ministerial statements to Parliament, 8 January 2025 and March 2025). A bureaucratic error was answered with apology, inquiry, publication, and policy change — the full sequence, in the open.
  • The worst rail failure in the network's history was investigated in public and paid for. After the 25 September 2024 East–West Line derailment disrupted services for six days and affected more than two million commuters, the transport minister apologised, the LTA and the Transport Safety Investigation Bureau published full findings on 3 June 2025 (degraded axle-box grease; a detection-system fault that could have caught the train), SMRT was fined S$2.4 million, and the entire Kawasaki C151 fleet was withdrawn by September 2025 (LTA investigation report, 3 June 2025).
  • The voters' pushback was accepted where it landed. The WP's retention of its seats in May 2025 — including Singh's own, three months after his conviction — was not contested or administratively revisited; Wong formed his cabinet on 21 May 2025 and governed with the opposition the electorate returned.

Evidence against:

  • On migrant-worker transport, the pushback has run for over twenty years and the answer, through most of his tenure, was no. In February and April 2025 his transport minister told parliament that banning the ferrying of workers in the backs of lorries was "not operationally possible," citing SME costs and driver supply, and declined the recommendations of the HOME report, including phased timelines and subsidies (Ministry of Transport written reply, 8 April 2025). The documented toll the policy accepts: 161 migrant workers injured in lorry accidents in 2020–2024 and fatalities averaging about one a year over a decade (MOT figures cited in NGO correspondence, 2025–2026). Only in March 2026 did the government announce a partial step — lorries fitted with cages banned from ferrying workers from 1 January 2027 — which rights groups welcomed as a step while noting it leaves the underlying practice in place (MARUAH and CNA reporting, March 2026). Twenty years of wobble, answered with a refusal for most of his tenure and a fraction of the ask at the end of it.
  • On the death penalty, international pushback produced no adjustment at all. Executions rose to 17 in 2025 — the highest annual total since 2003, 15 of them for drug offences — and at least 16 more people were executed for drug offences in the first eight months of 2026, drawing condemnation from UN human-rights experts who call the mandatory death penalty for drug trafficking unlawful under international law; Singapore's national report for its May 2026 Universal Periodic Review did not mention the death penalty (UN News and OHCHR statements, August 2026; World Coalition Against the Death Penalty UPR submission summary, May 2026). The government disputes the UN characterisation and maintains the penalty deters trafficking and commands public support — its position is recorded at full strength in Test 4; for this test the finding is narrower: the wobble arrived, from the UN, from UPR member states, and from domestic activists, and the policy did not move by an inch. One counterweight inside his own tenure: on 14 August 2025 President Tharman, acting on the Cabinet's advice, commuted a death sentence to life imprisonment — the first grant of clemency since 1998 (Amnesty International, August 2025).
  • On electoral reform, the wobble was answered with a denial and a vote. The government denies gerrymandering (an ALLEGATION it contests), attributes all boundary changes to elector growth, and in August 2024 used its majority to reject the reform motion that would have made the boundaries commission independent (Test 2). No part of the criticism — the PMO reporting line, the PM-chaired appointment, the timing of reports weeks before elections — has been taken up.
  • The January 2026 removal of the Leader of the Opposition can be read, and was read by the WP, as the opposite of answering the wobble: the electorate had returned Singh after his conviction, his party retained him, and the executive's response to that verdict was to vacate his office on a motion of its own majority. Wong's stated reasoning — that a convicted liar cannot hold an office of parliamentary integrity, and that the final court outcome "must be respected, and given full effect" (PMO statement, 15 January 2026) — is recorded as his answer. Whether it was adjustment or override is the contested question; the sequence of facts is not contested.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the migrant workers, the minorities, the opposition's voters, and those on death row — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The fiscal transfers are universal at the base and progressive at the margin, and they were delivered at scale through his tenure. Every household received CDC vouchers (S$800 per household in Budget 2025); the SG60 package paid S$600 to every adult citizen and S$800 to seniors in 2025; the Assurance Package — the GST-offset scheme he designed as finance minister — paid out on schedule through its final instalment in December 2026; and Budget 2026, the largest in the country's history (S$154.7 billion), continued cost-of-living support with the GST rate held at 9% (Budget statements, 18 February 2025 and 12 February 2026; DollarsAndSense Assurance Package tracker, 2026).
  • The safety net acquired a genuinely new floor under him: the SkillsFuture Jobseeker Support scheme, launched April 2025 as promised, pays involuntarily unemployed citizens up to S$6,000 over six months while they train and search — Singapore's first unemployment-support scheme of its kind, and a Forward Singapore commitment converted into a running programme (Budget 2025; NTUC and SkillsFuture programme documentation, 2025).
  • Minority representation is structurally guaranteed in the legislature he leads: the GRC system requires every group-representation team to include a minority candidate, so no parliament can be ethnically monochrome; and in 2025 his government passed the Maintenance of Racial Harmony Act, framed as protecting minority communities against foreign and domestic racial agitation. The August 2025 clemency (Test 3) — a death sentence commuted on his Cabinet's advice, the first in 27 years — is also recorded here: at least once, the man at the very bottom of the count was counted.
  • Migrant workers' conditions moved, measurably if slowly, on his watch: post-COVID dormitory standards are being raised under the dormitory transition programme, with new purpose-built dormitories opening under improved specifications (Bloomberg News, April 2026); the caged-lorry ban was announced in March 2026 for January 2027 (Test 3); and the Ministry of Manpower's own 2025 survey reported nine in ten migrant workers satisfied with their working conditions — a government survey, cited as such, beside the NGO record below (CNA reporting of MOM survey, 21 August 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Roughly 1.1–1.2 million low-wage work-permit and manual S-Pass holders — over 30% of the labour force, the people who build and clean the city — remain outside the count by design, under a framework his government administers and has not structurally changed: no pathway to permanent residence or citizenship (pathways open to higher-paid pass holders), no right to bring dependants, housing in segregated dormitories, and transport to work in the backs of goods lorries under a legal exception that applies to no other class of person in the country (MOM foreign-workforce data; HOME and MARUAH documentation, 2025–2026). The lorry file (Test 3) is the sharpest single exhibit: a safety standard the state would not accept for any citizen worker was defended for migrant workers, on cost grounds, into 2026.
  • The death-row count runs the other way from the clemency. Seventeen executions in 2025 (15 for drug trafficking) and at least 16 more drug-related executions by August 2026, all under mandatory sentences that remove judicial discretion to weigh the individual case; those executed are drawn heavily from the bottom of the drug trade — couriers and mules, including Malaysian nationals (five of the 2025 drug executions) — rather than its organisers (UN experts' statements, August 2026; Amnesty International death-penalty reporting, 2025–2026). One documented case states the seam precisely: Pannir Selvam Pranthaman was executed on 8 October 2025 while a complaint he had filed against his former lawyer remained unresolved (World Coalition UPR summary, May 2026). The government's position — that the death penalty deters drug trafficking, that Singapore's low drug-abuse rates are its fruit, and that it retains majority public support — is its standing public defence and is recorded here at full strength; it is a defence of the policy, not a dispute of the numbers.
  • The opposition's voters are counted at the ballot box and discounted after it. The WP's electorate — 14.99% of the national vote, half the vote in the seats it contested — returned its leader after his conviction and kept its ten seats; within eight months the executive had vacated the one institutional office that electorate's representation had created, and the office stands empty because the only acceptable replacement was one the WP's voters had not chosen to lead them (Test 2). No law was broken; the count of those voters' preference, expressed twice (May 2025 and in the WP's internal re-election of Singh in June 2026), was set aside by discretion.
  • Domestic workers — overwhelmingly foreign women working inside citizens' homes — appear in the record of his tenure mainly at its edges: rights groups documented through 2025–2026 the persistence of punitive moonlighting rules, exclusion from ordinary employment protections, and cases of workers turned away from shared facilities as "servants" (HOME news documentation, 2025–2026). No structural reform of domestic workers' status appears in the record assembled here; it is recorded as an absence, not an act.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises and public pledges vs. the record since 15 May 2024, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The GST ceiling — kept. On 28 February 2024, closing the Budget debate as Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister, Wong stated: "We have closed the funding gap up to 2030… We do not need further GST increases up to 2030." The GST rate has remained 9% through Budget 2025 and Budget 2026, and no further increase has been proposed (Budget statements, 2024–2026).
  • The Assurance Package — delivered in full, on its original design. The scheme he built as finance minister to offset the GST increase — at least five years of additional GST expenses for the majority of households, about ten years for lower-income households — paid out annually from December 2022 to its final instalment in December 2026, enhanced by a further S$1.9 billion in Budget 2024 rather than trimmed (DollarsAndSense tracker, 2026; Budget 2024).
  • Budget 2025's dated promises — delivered on the dates given. The SkillsFuture Jobseeker Support scheme launched in April 2025 as announced; SG60 vouchers were disbursed in July 2025 at the announced values; CDC vouchers issued at S$800 per household (Budget statement, 18 February 2025; programme reporting through 2025). His round-up pledge in the Budget 2025 debate — cost-of-living support "for as long as needed, and within our means" — was followed by continued support in Budget 2026.
  • The Forward Singapore agenda — its flagship social commitments converted. The consultation he chaired as DPM produced the October 2023 Forward Singapore report; its signature departures from the old model — unemployment support for the involuntarily jobless, expanded training allowances for mid-career and lower-wage workers — are running programmes as of 2025–2026, not paper (Forward Singapore report, 2023; Budget 2025 implementation reporting).
  • The Income undertaking — kept in both directions. Blocking the Allianz deal, Wong stated the government remained open to a new deal if its stated concerns were fully addressed; no revised deal has been approved or smuggled through since, and the stated concerns remain the stated test (PMO statement, 14 October 2024; record through November 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The style pledge — the gap his critics document most. Wong took office promising a more open, consultative mode of governance — to listen more, engage widely, and lead "in my own way" (inaugural and Forward Singapore statements, 2023–2024). The documented record of the same tenure includes the first criminal charge for non-compliance with a POFMA direction, correction directions against a foreign news agency that publicly maintained its reporting was accurate, damages and costs of over S$570,000 imposed on an independent editor, and the removal of the opposition leader from his office. Supporters answer that none of this breaches any specific pledge and that consultation is not concession; the pledge graded here is the one he actually made — a different style — and on the speech-and-speech-adjacent files the style is continuous with his predecessors', not different. Contested as to meaning; documented as to events.
  • The post-election undertaking — partially kept. Accepting the May 2025 result, Wong said Singaporeans had given the PAP "a clear and strong mandate" and pledged to govern for all Singaporeans, including those who had not voted for the party (post-election press conference, 3–4 May 2025). Eight months later his discretion vacated the Leader of the Opposition office over the objection of the party that 15% of voters had chosen, and the office remains unfilled — the single most concrete instance in the file of a universal-service pledge meeting its limit. The pledge was general; the act was specific; both are on the record.
  • The social-mission assurance his government inherited and enforced against others was first strained on his side of the table. The 2022 corporatisation of Income proceeded on assurances that its social mission would be safeguarded; the 2024 Allianz transaction — developed by NTUC Enterprise with no initial government objection, while Wong was prime minister — put those assurances in question, and it was public pressure, not the government's own monitoring, that surfaced the capital-reduction term that triggered the block (Test 3). The word was ultimately enforced; it was not kept by vigilance, and the enforcement required reversing his own government's initial position.
  • The long promises are stated as direction, not yet as record: the Forward Singapore commitments on housing affordability and on narrowing inequality run past this grading; HDB resale prices and cost-of-living pressure remained the top public grievances in polling through 2025–2026 despite the transfers in Test 4. Recorded so the record can grade them later; neither kept nor broken on the evidence assembled here.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Lawrence Wong's file is the mirror image of the compromise candidates elsewhere in this series: where they held nothing, he holds nearly everything — a two-thirds parliamentary supermajority refreshed at 65.57% in May 2025, the finance portfolio kept in his own hands, the party secretary-generalship, and a state whose instruments (a boundaries commission chaired by his own secretary, an elections department inside his office, a falsehoods law wielded by his ministers, an opposition-leader office existing at his pleasure) were built by his predecessors and remain, unamended, in his hands. What the record shows him doing with that completeness is two things at once. As an administrator he is among the strongest in this series: a corruption case carried through to the imprisonment of his own party's minister, a S$2.2 billion deal with his own labour movement blocked in public when the terms failed the public interest, a bureaucratic privacy failure answered with apology, published inquiry, and policy reversal, and a fiscal word — on GST, on the Assurance Package, on dated Budget programmes — kept almost to the letter. As a holder of the referees he has moved in one direction only: the boundaries machinery unreformed after an open challenge, the press environment measurably worse on RSF's index, the first POFMA prosecution, and, in January 2026, the removal of the opposition leader from an office that exists at the prime minister's discretion — lawfully, reasoned in public, and final, leaving the post vacant because the voters' party would not nominate on his conditions. The count is similarly split: citizens are counted with unusual completeness — universal vouchers, a new unemployment floor, transfers delivered on schedule — while the million-plus migrant workers who build the city remain outside the count's protections by design, and the death penalty's count rose to a 22-year high in defiance of every external wobble. The grades are all MIXED because the file genuinely runs both ways on every test; the open files that will move them are named in the dossier: whether the boundaries and elections machinery is ever made independent, whether the Leader of the Opposition office is filled and on whose terms, whether the lorry exception and the mandatory death penalty survive his tenure, and whether the different style he promised ever reaches the speech laws.

Free. Always free.
