The Ruler's Test — Sadyr Japarov, President of Kyrgyzstan

Subject: Sadyr Nurgozho uulu Japarov — President of Kyrgyzstan since 28 January 2021. He came to power from a prison cell: convicted in August 2017 to 11.5 years for organizing the 2013 hostage-taking of a regional governor at a rally he led — a charge he denies — he was freed by protesters on 5–6 October 2020 during the unrest that annulled that month's parliamentary elections, was made prime minister within days and acting president within ten, resigned in November 2020 to run, and won the 10 January 2021 early presidential election with 79.2% of the vote on 39.16% turnout, alongside a referendum choosing presidential rule. He remains in office in November 2026 and filed on 10 November 2026 to run in the presidential election set for 27 January 2027, after the Constitutional Court ruled in February 2026 that his current six-year term, running to 2027, counts as his first term under the 2021 constitution and that he may run again. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption machinery moved first and fastest against the old order's most protected figure. Days after Japarov publicly named former deputy customs chief Raiymbek Matraimov the main suspect in the theft of some $700 million, the State Committee for National Security (GKNB) detained him on 20 October 2020 and announced an investigation into roughly 40 customs officials in his network (OCCRP and RFE/RL reporting, October 2020). In November 2023 the GKNB announced that about 200 government employees had been dismissed for connections to Matraimov and to the late organized-crime figure Kamchy Kolbaev (OCCRP, November 2023).
  • Enforcement also struck inside his own government. The head of the State Tax Service, Altynbek Abduvapov, was arrested in late 2024 — the GKNB's chairman stated he had accumulated some $55 million in illegal assets, an ALLEGATION Abduvapov's process had not adjudicated at the time — and on 16 December 2024 Japarov dismissed his own prime minister, Akylbek Japarov, as the scandal broke, replacing him with Adylbek Kasymaliev. In July 2025 six officials, including district tax-service heads, were arrested on camera during a government meeting in Osh, following arrests of mayors and senior officials in the Chui and Issyk-Kul regions (Times of Central Asia, 10 July 2025).
  • The 2026 purge reached the family of his closest ally: Shairbek Tashiev, brother of the dismissed security chief, was arrested on 1 April 2026 on corruption charges (Central Asia-Caucasus Analyst, April 2026). The charges are ALLEGATIONs; the arrest itself is documented.
  • A court acquitted where the state demanded conviction: on 14 June 2024 all 22 defendants in the Kempir-Abad case were acquitted despite prosecutors requesting 20-year sentences (Human Rights Watch UPR submission, October 2024). That acquittal is also a Test 2 and Test 3 fact; it is recorded here because the law, once, ran against the prosecution's ask in the most political case of his tenure.

Evidence against:

  • The signature enforcement method of his tenure is payment in place of punishment — known in Kyrgyzstan by the security chief's own term, "kustoo." Matraimov, detained in October 2020, was released after paying $24.5 million to the state budget; in 2021 he pleaded guilty to corruption and was fined $3,000, with freezes on his property lifted (OCCRP). Detained again after extradition from Azerbaijan in March 2024, he was released from custody after paying the equivalent of $200 million — the GKNB chairman explained publicly that there was "no point in keeping him in jail after he paid" — and later pleaded guilty to hooliganism and illegal border crossing and was fined $1,150 (OCCRP, 2024). The pattern repeats below him: businessman Tursuntai Salimov was released after paying $15 million compensation, and businessman Alexander San after paying 500 million soms (about $5.8 million) and relinquishing a tourism complex (OCCRP investigative reporting, 2025). Whether any individual payment was lawful settlement is for courts; the documented pattern is that the price of walking free is a price, and it is negotiated with the security service, not set by a verdict.
  • Investigative reporting documents assets flowing toward the seat's circle after such enforcement. Joint investigations by Kloop, Temirov Live and OCCRP — reported, not adjudicated, and graded here as ALLEGATION-level reporting — found that businesses seized or surrendered after arrests and tax pressure, including Bishkek's Madina textile market, passed to companies linked to the president's family and friends, and that state megaprojects have been awarded to companies owned by apparent proxies of the president and his appointees while transparency into public spending was reduced (OCCRP, "All the President's Men" and "Kyrgyz Elite Targeted as Japarov Allies Gain Key Assets," 2024–2025). Japarov's former adviser Aidar Khalikov accused him from exile of corruption and business seizures — ALLEGATIONs the presidential press secretary denied, stating Khalikov had been fired for abuse of office and smuggling; Khalikov was detained in Moldova on a Kyrgyz fraud warrant (OCCRP, 2025). Both the accusation and the denial are on the record; neither has been tested in an open court.
  • The law's first beneficiary was the seat-holder himself. Japarov was freed from prison by a crowd on 5–6 October 2020; on 9 October the Supreme Court denied it had acquitted him, stating a panel had annulled the verdict and returned the case to prosecutors; on 19 October 2020 the Supreme Court overturned the separate 2012 attempted-seizure convictions of Japarov, Kamchybek Tashiev and Talant Mamytov; and after Japarov took office the Supreme Court reviewed and overturned the 2017 kidnapping verdict itself (RFE/RL analysis, October 2020; Times of Central Asia, 2026). UN Human Rights Committee experts recorded that fair-trial procedures were reportedly not followed in his appeal (OHCHR, October 2022). In January 2021, days after his election, the Supreme Court also overturned the conviction of his sister, Raykul Japarova, in the Issyk-Kul Bank case (Times of Central Asia, 2026).
  • The outcome measure moved the wrong way: Kyrgyzstan scored 25 of 100 and ranked 146th of 180 on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index for 2024, down from a score of 31 in 2020 (Transparency International).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, media, the electoral authority, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Elections are still observed, technically competent, and capable of registering discontent with procedure. The OSCE/ODIHR mission on the 30 November 2025 early parliamentary elections found them "administered efficiently," election day "assessed overall positively," new voting technologies reliable, and noted the new system reserves 30 of 90 mandates for the underrepresented gender, with 41 per cent of candidates women (ODIHR preliminary statement, 1 December 2025; final report, 14 August 2026). ODIHR missions are invited and their reports published in full.
  • Courts have ruled against the state's asks in the highest-profile cases: the Kempir-Abad acquittals of June 2024; the Supreme Court's 10 March 2026 decision overturning Temirov Live director Makhabat Tazhibek Kyzy's conviction and ordering a retrial, followed by her release under travel restrictions on 23 March 2026 (CPJ, August 2026); and the Bishkek City Court's November 2025 reduction of two Kloop camera operators' five-year prison sentences to probation (OCCRP, November 2025).
  • One closure was reversed: Radio Azattyk, RFE/RL's Kyrgyz service, ordered shut by a district court on 27 April 2023, was restored after a settlement approved by the Bishkek City Court on 12 July 2023, and continues to broadcast (CPJ, July 2023). The terms of that settlement are graded under Test 3; the outlet's survival is a fact of this shelf.

Evidence against:

  • The grip was constitutionalized first. The constitution adopted by referendum on 11 April 2021 concentrated appointment power in the presidency: the Venice Commission and ODIHR's joint opinion of March 2021 warned of the president's power "to single-handedly appoint and dismiss almost the entire state administration and/or key office-holders" and his "far-reaching powers in the selection and dismissal of judges," finding a real risk of undermining separation of powers and judicial independence, in a process held with little public consultation (Venice Commission/ODIHR Joint Opinion CDL-AD(2021)007). Parliament was cut from 120 to 90 seats. In September 2025 parliament voted to dissolve itself, triggering early elections under an electoral system rewritten the same year.
  • Media independence was dismantled by statute and by case. The Law "On Protection from Inaccurate (False) Information," signed in August 2021, lets an authorized state body block websites without a court order; it was used to block Azattyk in October 2022, after which the Culture Ministry sued to close it permanently and its bank accounts were frozen under money-laundering rules, before the July 2023 settlement restored it on the basis that the disputed video was no longer available (OCCRP; CPJ). Kloop, the country's leading investigative outlet, had its website blocked in September 2023 and its foundation liquidated by a Bishkek district court on 9 February 2024 — the prosecutor's suit cited its "sharp criticism of [the] government" — with the Supreme Court upholding liquidation in a closed hearing (Human Rights Watch, September 2024). On 27 October 2025 a Bishkek court declared the publications of Kloop and Temirov Live "extremist" (CPJ, October 2025). The Law on Mass Media No. 188, signed 6 August 2025 and in force from 23 August 2025, requires every media outlet including online resources to register with the state, puts registration, re-registration and termination procedures in the Cabinet's hands, and caps foreign ownership at 35 per cent (Interfax, 7 August 2025; ARTICLE 19, August 2025). In July 2025 Japarov signed a decree giving the state-owned company ElCat a one-year monopoly over international internet traffic (Reuters, July 2025).
  • Journalists were imprisoned, deported, and prosecuted as a class. Bolot Temirov was acquitted of drug charges in September 2022 — charges brought two days after Temirov Live published an investigation into the security chief's family — then convicted of document fraud, stripped of his Kyrgyz citizenship, and deported to Russia on 23 November 2022; media-freedom organizations state the expulsion was retaliation, and he has been banned from the country for five years (CPJ; Amnesty International). On 16 January 2024 eleven current and former Temirov Live staff were arrested; its director, Makhabat Tazhibek Kyzy, was sentenced on 10 October 2024 to six years for "calls for mass unrest" (CPJ). On 28 May 2025 the GKNB detained eight current and former Kloop staff; two former camera operators were sentenced on 17 September 2025 to five years each for videos Temirov stated they had no part in producing — Human Rights Watch recorded that the prosecution failed to provide tangible evidence (HRW, September 2025). Kyrgyzstan's ranking in the RSF World Press Freedom Index fell from 82nd in 2020 to 122nd in 2023, 144th in 2025, and 146th of 180 in 2026 (RSF indexes, 2020–2026).
  • Civil society was placed under a foreign-agent regime. Law No. 72, signed 2 April 2024 and in force 15 April 2024, requires foreign-funded NGOs engaged in broadly defined "political activity" to register as "foreign representatives," subject to inspections, suspension for up to six months, and liquidation (Venice Commission Opinion CDL-AD(2024)033, October 2024; OHCHR, April 2024). The Venice Commission warned the law's implementation poses a real risk of "stigmatising, silencing and eventually eliminating" foreign-funded NGOs (Venice Commission, October 2024). In 2025 Japarov signed the transfer of the National Prevention Mechanism against torture from the independent National Center for the Prevention of Torture to the Ombudsman's office, an institution criticized for lacking full independence (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025).
  • The electoral referee's rulebook is rewritten by the players before each game. ODIHR's final report on the November 2025 elections (14 August 2026) concluded the legal framework "falls short of a number of international standards," that 2025 amendments "changed almost all core components of the process" shortly before election day without meaningful consultation, that party involvement was "significantly diminished," and that the framework "does not address almost all previous ODIHR and the Council of Europe's Venice Commission recommendations."
  • The central bank's chair has changed twice in under two years — Kubanychbek Bokontaev departed in June 2024 and was replaced by Melis Turgunbaev, a sitting cabinet minister; Turgunbaev resigned in March 2026 "of his own accord" (Akchabar, 18 March 2026). Both departures were formally voluntary and no instruction-taking is documented; the pattern is recorded at exactly that strength. In February 2026 the Constitutional Court ruled, on the question that decided the incumbent's own eligibility, that Japarov's six-year term counts as his first under the new constitution and he may run again (Times of Central Asia, November 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the street, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Azattyk case ended in negotiation, not closure: after the April 2023 shutdown ruling, the government settled in July 2023 and the outlet resumed work (CPJ, July 2023). The settlement's condition — the disputed video's unavailability — is part of the record; so is the fact that the state accepted a negotiated outcome under international pressure rather than completing the closure.
  • On the media law, he once sent the bill back: in March 2024 Japarov ordered the draft mass-media law returned for further work and a second working group including journalists was established; the January 2025 draft that emerged removed mandatory registration and made registration voluntary — a documented adjustment to criticism (OCCRP, August 2025). What parliament then did to that draft, and what he signed, is on the other shelf.
  • After the Kempir-Abad acquittals, Japarov said in a state-media interview on 15 June 2024 that the defendants deserved punishment — and also insisted he had no bearing on the court's decision; the acquittals stood and took effect (RFE/RL, June 2024; CIVICUS Monitor, 2024). A president's grumble is not an annulment; the record shows the verdict survived his displeasure.
  • He absorbed a failure inside his own government rather than defending it: when the tax-service scandal broke in December 2024, he dismissed his prime minister of three years (16 December 2024). In April 2025 he pardoned Temirov Live presenter Azamat Ishenbekov, convicted in the January 2024 journalists' case (Wikipedia/CPJ records, April 2025), and in June 2025 he stated publicly that he would consider pardoning former president Atambayev if asked (Fergana, June 2025) — a stated openness, recorded as such, not an act.

Evidence against:

  • The referees' findings are answered by being ignored. ODIHR's final report on the 2025 elections records that the legal framework "does not address almost all previous ODIHR and the Council of Europe's Venice Commission recommendations" — the same posture as toward the Venice Commission's 2021 constitutional opinion and its 2024 opinion on the foreign-representatives law, neither of which produced amendment (ODIHR final report, 14 August 2026; Venice Commission, 2021 and 2024).
  • The media-law adjustment was reversed in substance by his own signature. At second reading, lawmakers reinserted mandatory registration into the compromise draft, against the working group's stated agreements; the Committee to Protect Journalists publicly urged a veto on 2 July 2025; Japarov signed the law on 6 August 2025 with mandatory registration and the 35 per cent foreign-ownership cap intact (OCCRP; CPJ; ARTICLE 19, 2025).
  • The purest wobble of his tenure was answered with a coup prosecution. On 9 February 2026, seventy-five public figures — former officials, politicians, intellectuals — published an open letter calling for an early presidential election to resolve the dispute over his term's length: a lawful, public, constitutional request. Within hours, signatories holding office were detained; on 10 February Japarov dismissed Kamchybek Tashiev — his closest ally of five years, then in Germany for medical treatment — saying the move was needed "to prevent a split in society" (Times of Central Asia; OCCRP, 2026). Tashiev had neither signed nor published the letter (OCCRP). Formal charges followed in late April 2026, including, as first clarified by a parliamentary deputy, planning a coup; the trial opened 15 June 2026 with limited transparency. On 2 July 2026 all eight defendants — Tashiev, former prosecutor general Kurmankul Zulushev, former parliamentary speaker Nurlanbek Turgunbek uulu, and five others — were convicted of plotting the violent overthrow of the government and sentenced to four years with property confiscation, commuted to probation; on 10 September 2026 the Bishkek City Court upheld the convictions but changed the offense to "interfering with electoral rights," a charge the defense stated it had received no warning of, retaining four-year terms as two years' probation and lifting confiscation (Times of Central Asia, September 2026; RFE/RL, May 2026). Prosecutors had sought nine-year prison terms. The conviction bars Tashiev from the January 2027 presidential race. In August 2026 Japarov explained that he had planned to step down and hand power to Tashiev but had concluded Tashiev "was not ready" (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026) — his own account, printed as his account.
  • When courts softened outcomes, the narrowing continued by another door: the Bishkek City Court reduced the Kloop camera operators' sentences to probation on 25 November 2025 — a month after another court had declared the two outlets' publications "extremist" (CPJ, October 2025; OCCRP, November 2025).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — ethnic Uzbeks, labor migrants, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The material count rose for nearly everyone. Real GDP growth reached about 9 per cent in 2023 and about 11 per cent in both 2024 and 2025; national poverty fell from 29.8 per cent in 2023 to 25.7 per cent in 2024 and 24.0 per cent in 2025, lifting roughly 264,000 people out of poverty in 2024 alone, with pensions among the drivers (World Bank, Poverty & Equity Brief, October 2026; Listening to the Kyrgyz Republic, 2026). GDP per capita rose from $1,230 in 2020 to $3,081 in 2025 (World Bank data).
  • The border settlement counted in the people who had been dying at the border. The treaty with Tajikistan signed on 13 March 2025 closed a conflict whose September 2022 round killed Kyrgyz border villagers and displaced tens of thousands; land was exchanged on an equal basis (190 hectares each way, per the security chief's presentation to parliament), the village of Dostuk was transferred to Tajikistan with its residents compensated in larger plots, and checkpoints closed since 2021 reopened (UN News, 13 March 2025; Khujand treaty record, March 2025).
  • Formal inclusion widened in one documented respect: the 2025 electoral system reserves 30 of 90 parliamentary mandates for the underrepresented gender, and 41 per cent of candidates in November 2025 were women (ODIHR, December 2025). No ethnic group is excluded by law from the vote or from office, and minority deputies sit in parliament.

Evidence against:

  • The citizens who objected to a border deal were counted as criminals. The Kempir-Abad reservoir agreement with Uzbekistan, negotiated in secret, transferred expanded rights over a reservoir in the Uzbek-populated south; when activists, politicians and human-rights defenders formed a committee on 22 October 2022 whose stated demand was that the negotiations be held openly, more than two dozen were arrested the next day and charged with preparing mass unrest and attempting to seize power. Some spent over 19 months in pretrial detention in deplorable conditions; the trial was classified and closed; the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention found the detention of five women defendants arbitrary (Opinion No. 61/2025). All were acquitted in June 2024 — after the count of their liberty had already been taken. One defendant, Rita Karasartova, was arrested again in April 2025 and sentenced in September 2025 to five years' probation in another classified case (CIVICUS Monitor; Human Rights Watch UPR submission, 2024; Wikipedia case records).
  • The Uzbek minority's institutional footprint keeps shrinking under laws he signed. Uzbek-language schooling has contracted sharply over the past decade — community reporting citing official statistics counts Uzbek-only schools falling from 133 in 2009 to one in 2025, a trend academic research attributes in part to state curriculum shifts emphasizing ethnic Kyrgyz identity — and the Constitutional Law on the State Language of July 2023 expanded Kyrgyz-proficiency requirements across public life, with 2025 draft amendments extending proficiency tests to MPs, judges, prosecutors and central-bank staff; MPs warned on the record that such requirements could narrow minority representation (Parliament Journal, May 2025; CESS 2019 education research). The trend predates him; the laws extending it are his.
  • Labor migrants — whose remittances averaged about a quarter of GDP across his term — count for less in the state's own success story each year: the World Bank records remittances' share of household income falling from 19 per cent in early 2022 to 6 per cent by late 2025, and notes poverty reduction was driven by earnings of existing workers "while job creation played a limited role" (World Bank, 2026). Extreme poverty rose in 2025, from 2.7 to 3.6 per cent, even as the headline rate fell (World Bank, October 2026).
  • His opponents' voters are counted in a deliberately narrowed field. ODIHR's 2025 mission found the restrictive environment "stifled candidate and voter engagement," linked low engagement to "fears of retribution for political activity," recorded significant financial disparities and numerous vote-buying allegations, and noted media refrained from covering candidates — limiting voters' ability to make an informed choice (ODIHR preliminary statement, 1 December 2025; final report, 14 August 2026). Turnout in the January 2021 election that founded his mandate was 39.16 per cent.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since October 2020, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Kumtor — kept, in full. Nationalizing the Kumtor gold mine was his signature demand from 2012 through the 2020 campaign. After parliament authorized external management in May 2021, a settlement signed 4 April 2022 gave Kyrgyzstan 100 per cent ownership of Kumtor Gold Company, recognized all revenues from 15 May 2021 as Kyrgyzstan's, and brought a $50 million payment, $11 million in dividends, and return of the $53 million reclamation fund, with all litigation in the US, Sweden and Canada ended (presidential address, 4 April 2022; Centerra Gold statements, April–July 2022). His words on signing: "Kumtor is owned completely by Kyrgyzstan."
  • The Tajik border — kept. He promised to settle the undelimited border; the State Border Treaty was signed in Bishkek on 13 March 2025 and the trilateral junction treaty at Khujand on 31 March 2025, welcomed by the UN Secretary-General, who commended the two leaders' "determination and political will" (UN News, 13 March 2025).
  • Growth and incomes — delivered on the numbers. The economic expansion and poverty reduction recorded under Test 4 broadly match the prosperity program on which he campaigned, whatever else is graded elsewhere.
  • The anti-corruption arrests he promised did occur: on taking power he stated Matraimov and other corrupt officials would be punished, and Matraimov was detained within days (OCCRP, October 2020). What the punishment turned out to be is on the other shelf.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The signed freedom pledge — reversed. On 23 December 2020 Japarov joined the "Agreement on rights and freedoms," pledging to guarantee freedom of speech, democracy, rule of law and human rights (Human Rights Watch letter to President Japarov, 24 February 2021). On election night he promised media there would be "no persecution" if they did not twist his words. The record since: the RSF index fell from 82nd (2020) to 146th (2026); Kloop liquidated and its publications declared extremist; Temirov deported; Temirov Live's director sentenced to six years; a "foreign representatives" law and a mandatory-registration media law signed over the profession's objections (Tests 2 and 3). On signing the foreign-representatives law in April 2024 he stated again that "there will be no persecution" of NGOs; the Venice Commission's documented warning is that the law itself risks stigmatising and eventually eliminating them — enforcement to date has been lighter than feared (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025), so this word stands under strain rather than wholly broken. The speech pledge does not: it was kept neither in letter nor in trend.
  • "Punished to the fullest extent" — inverted. His October 2020 formulation for Matraimov ended in a guilty plea, a $3,000 fine, lifted asset freezes, flight, a second detention ended by a $200 million payment, and a $1,150 fine on lesser charges (OCCRP, 2020–2024). The fullest extent of the law, in the flagship case of his anti-corruption promise, was a negotiated price.
  • The term — extended by interpretation. He was elected in January 2021 under a constitution permitting a single six-year term. The constitution adopted at his initiative in April 2021 permits two five-year terms. In February 2026 the Constitutional Court ruled his current term counts as his first under the new constitution; on 10 November 2026 he filed to run again, on a path to remain president until 2032 — twelve years after a revolution whose demand was an end to rulers who arrange to stay (Times of Central Asia, November 2026). No statement of his promising a single term is in the sources relied on here; the sequence is stated, and the electorate will grade it in January 2027.
  • The long arc, factually: the MP who campaigned against Kumtor's foreign owners in 2012, was convicted over a governor's hostage-taking in 2017 in a case he calls political, and was carried out of prison by a crowd in 2020, has governed as he campaigned on the questions of the mine and the border — and opposite to how he campaigned on the questions of speech, courts, and the length of his own stay. His word is reliable exactly where keeping it enlarged the seat, and elastic exactly where keeping it would have limited the seat.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: FAILS · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Sadyr Japarov delivered the two promises he had carried for a decade — Kumtor is wholly Kyrgyzstan's, and the Tajik border is settled by treaty — and presided over the fastest growth and poverty reduction in the country's recorded history. Against that record stands the method: a flagship corruption case settled for cash by the security service while the same service's investigations moved businesses toward the president's circle; a constitution rewritten to concentrate appointments, including judges, in the presidency; the leading investigative outlet liquidated and then declared extremist; journalists deported and imprisoned; and, in 2026, an open letter asking for an election answered with the dismissal and coup prosecution of the very ally who had run the machinery — convicted, in the end, of interfering with the electoral rights the letter had invoked. The flock is materially better off and counted in the economy. It is counted out of the argument, and the law that counts it prices its punishments. The seat gives by payment more than by law, grips the referees it rebuilt, and keeps its word where the word enlarged it.
