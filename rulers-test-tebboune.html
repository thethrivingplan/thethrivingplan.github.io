The Ruler's Test — Abdelmadjid Tebboune, President of Algeria

Subject: Abdelmadjid Tebboune — President of the People's Democratic Republic of Algeria since 12 December 2019, now in his second and constitutionally final term (to September 2029). The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: under Algeria's 2020 Constitution the president is not a ceremonial head of state but the executive itself — he appoints and dismisses the prime minister and government, chairs the Council of Ministers and the High Council of the Judiciary, is supreme commander of the armed forces and responsible for national defence, and governs extensively by decree. The prime minister (Sifi Ghrieb since September 2025, the third man Tebboune has placed in and removed from that office) serves at the president's pleasure and holds no independent electoral mandate. Tebboune, born 17 November 1945, is a career senior administrator — a former wali, minister, and briefly prime minister under his predecessor Abdelaziz Bouteflika (May–August 2017, dismissed after a public falling-out with the presidency's circle). He won the 12 December 2019 election with 58.13% on an official turnout of 39.93%, in a vote the Hirak protest movement boycotted; he brought the 2024 election forward from December to 7 September and was certified the winner with 84.30% by the Constitutional Court (see Test 3 for the dispute over the count). He is graded on the full tenure of just under seven years, with the heaviest weight on the second term, where the record is his alone. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Each test closes with the grade's reason in plain words and a ledger naming who his actions on that test helped and who they hurt. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is never itself a failure: carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the for-shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done — a second law for his own side (Test 1), gripping the referees (Test 2), counting out those who did not elect him (Test 4). Word-keeping and outcomes are graded as separate axes and printed independently; neither launders the other. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier says so and grades the best possible choice available in the moment; that is not held against him. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The anti-corruption drive that began with the Hirak's fall of the old order ran through courts and reached the very top of the previous establishment: two former prime ministers, Ahmed Ouyahia and Abdelmalek Sellal, were convicted and imprisoned, along with dozens of Bouteflika-era ministers and the regime's leading businessmen, in trials that began in 2019 and continued under Tebboune; the state recovered seized assets, and the car-assembly cases ended in fraud and money-laundering convictions of politically connected industrialists (BBC and wire reporting, 2019–2022; Transparency International country coverage). Whatever else it was, it was not a paper campaign.
  • The money-politics filter written into the electoral law in April 2026 (Article 200 of Organic Law 26-05, barring candidates linked to illicit business networks) was applied across the map, not only against the opposition: of the more than 3,700 candidacies the electoral authority (ANIE) barred before the 2 July 2026 legislative elections, roughly 450 came from the ruling FLN itself and some 300 from its RND ally, alongside 215 from the opposition MSP, and almost half of outgoing MPs were unable to stand again (ANIE acting president Karim Khelfane, via International IDEA and Middle East Women's Council election monitoring, July 2026).
  • The signature-buying prosecution had a documented factual core, whoever else it served: prosecutors stated that some 50 elected officials admitted receiving money for their sponsorship signatures and around ten intermediaries admitted collecting it, in the case that ended the presidential candidacies of businesswoman Saïda Neghza, former minister Belkacem Sahli, and Abdelhakim Hamadi (prosecution statements via BAMF briefing notes, 2025; wire reporting, May 2025).
  • The Constitutional Court's September 2024 recount cut the incumbent's own announced result by ten points and multiplied his opponents' votes (Test 3), and the corrected figures — worse for him — are the ones that stand in law.

Evidence against:

  • The president publicly announced a criminal sentence before the court delivered it. On 13 April 2025, addressing economic operators, Tebboune declared of the signature-buying file: "Those who bought signatures will be punished; the sentence will be ten years." On 26 May 2025 the court sentenced Neghza, Sahli, and Hamadi to exactly ten years each (reduced to four years on appeal on 9 July 2025). The Algerian rights organisation SHOAA documented the statement and the appeal hearing's procedural irregularities — including detention orders issued against previously free defendants during the session, before deliberation — and characterised the trial as part of a systematic restriction of the right to stand for office; the defendants and their lawyers dispute the process throughout (SHOAA statement, July 2025; BAMF Briefing Notes, January–June 2025). A sentence the president names in advance, delivered to the month, is the opposite of one law administered blind.
  • The 2024 ballot itself was filtered to three names before a vote was cast: of the sixteen candidacy files lodged, the Constitutional Court validated only Tebboune's and two others (Abdelaali Hassani Cherif of the MSP and Youcef Aouchiche of the FFS), rejecting the rest on signature counts and technical grounds that several rejected aspirants publicly disputed as opaque (Constitutional Court decisions, July–August 2024; contemporaneous reporting).
  • The law's sharpest instrument falls on critics, not on the connected. The June 2021 presidential ordinance expanding Penal Code Article 87 bis defined terrorism to include seeking to change the system of government by "unconstitutional means," and it has since been used against speech: the UN Special Rapporteur on human rights defenders warned that laws meant to prevent terrorism are instilling terror in defenders, and a figure of more than 215 prisoners of opinion was cited in the European Parliament in January 2025 (UN Special Rapporteur reporting, 2025; European Parliament, January 2025, via rights compilations). In 2026 alone, documented cases include a Kabyle activist sentenced to five years for Facebook posts and a journalist sentenced to three years for posts about public anger in Djanet (rights case compilations, 2026).
  • The establishment that seated him stands outside the law's reach, and his own family's file closed at his term's threshold. No figure of the serving military command — the institution that arbitrated his 2019 succession — has faced any comparable process, and the army's economic and political position is not justiciable in practice. His son Khaled Tebboune, arrested in June 2018 in the orbit of the Kamel Chikhi (701 kg Oran cocaine) investigations, was acquitted on 26 February 2020 — ten weeks into his father's presidency — by the Sidi M'hamed court in the separate property-powers case actually brought against him; no court established participation in the cocaine trafficking itself, and this dossier records the acquittal as a court act, not a wrongdoing. What is graded is the asymmetry of transparency: Algerian and Moroccan commentary across the spectrum records that the circumstances of the arrest, detention, and acquittal were never publicly explained, and domestic media cannot safely ask (Diplomatique and Al Massae analyses, November 2026). One law that cannot be questioned at the center is not one law.

Why: The drive against the old circle was real, and the 2026 candidacy filter cut his own camp too — but a president who announces a sentence before the verdict, a ballot pre-filtered to three names, a terrorism article that lands on Facebook posts, and an inner establishment (and family file) beyond question add up to two laws: one for those the system prosecutes, one for those it protects. Helped: The treasury and the public, where genuinely stolen assets were recovered and a corrupt former ruling circle was actually punished; voters in 2026, marginally, where Article 200 removed some money-bought candidacies from all parties. Hurt: Neghza, Sahli, and Hamadi and some 70 co-defendants, whose trial followed a presidentially announced sentence; the rejected 2024 aspirants and their voters; the hundreds prosecuted under Article 87 bis for expression, whose cases no anti-corruption rationale reaches.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The referees have, on documented occasions, ruled against the seat's immediate interest and been obeyed: the Constitutional Court's September 2024 recount reduced the president's own certified score from ANIE's announced 94.65% to 84.30% and raised both opponents' totals, and Tebboune was sworn in on the corrected figures (Constitutional Court, 14 September 2024). Opposition parties — MSP, FFS, the Workers' Party, the RCD — remain legal, campaign, and sit in the Assembly elected in July 2026, and that Assembly chose the country's first woman speaker, Khalida Boufedeche (IPU Parline; election results, July 2026).
  • The March 2026 constitutional revision, whatever its direction, was carried through the constitution's own procedure — a reasoned Constitutional Court opinion and a three-quarters vote of both chambers under Article 232 — and it did not touch the two-term presidential limit that binds Tebboune himself (ConstitutionNet analysis of Law No. 26-04, 2026). The limit stands; he leaves in 2029 under the rules as written.
  • Elections have been held on a regular calendar throughout his tenure (2021 and 2026 legislatives, 2024 presidential), and ANIE, the electoral authority created after the Hirak, still exists and publishes its decisions, including the contested 2026 exclusions, in the open.

Evidence against:

  • In March 2026 the referees were restructured in the seat's favour, by the seat. Parliament, meeting in congress, adopted twelve amendments (Law No. 26-04, 26 March 2026) presented as "technical": they removed the requirement to obtain the High Council of the Judiciary's approval for appointments to senior judicial posts — the president already chairs that council — and stripped out the categories (chamber appointees, magistrates' union representatives, the human-rights council president) that diluted executive control of its composition, re-centring it on the Supreme Court's prosecutor general, who answers to the executive; and they transferred the material and logistical preparation of elections from ANIE back to the administration, chiefly the Interior Ministry, reducing the Hirak-born electoral authority to supervision. Le Monde's assessment was that the revision "aims solely at strengthening the president's grip"; ARTICLE 19 and civil-society organisations record that civil society was not consulted and that the changes weaken countervailing institutions; the government characterises them as technical coherence measures — the dispute is named, and the texts themselves are not in dispute (Le Monde, 25 March 2026; International IDEA democracy tracker, March 2026; ARTICLE 19, 2026; ConstitutionNet, 2026).
  • The central bank's governorship turned over three times in eight months of 2026, each time by his decree: Salah Eddine Taleb, governor since May 2022, was dismissed on 4 January 2026 with no reason stated, his deputy Mouatassim Boudiaf installed as acting governor; Mohamed Lamine Lebbou was appointed governor by decree on 23 February 2026 and then moved sideways into the Finance Ministry in a reshuffle months later; Mohand Bourai took office as governor on 3 September 2026 (Central Banking, January 2026; Bank of Algeria confirmations via Algerian press, February and September 2026). A central bank whose head serves in months-long instalments at presidential pleasure is not an autonomous referee in any sense the test recognises.
  • Association and party life is licensed by the executive and revoked by it: the Algerian League for the Defence of Human Rights (LADDH), the country's oldest rights league, was dissolved by court order in January 2023 in proceedings brought by the government, which it was not properly notified of (Amnesty International and LADDH statements, 2023); the Democratic and Social Movement party was banned in February 2023, and its leader Fethi Ghares was sentenced again on 21 October 2025 — two years, provisionally free pending appeal — for "insulting the President" in remarks about revising the electoral and party laws (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025). A new organic law on parties (No. 26-08, 23 April 2026) adds dissolution for parties that fail to field candidates in two consecutive contests — participation made a condition of legal survival (Official Journal; International IDEA, April 2026).
  • The press operates inside the grip: Algeria stood 139th of 180 in RSF's 2024 World Press Freedom Index; the 2023 information law tightened licensing and foreign-funding prohibitions; journalist Ihsane El Kadi's outlets (Radio M, Maghreb Emergent) were sealed in December 2022 and El Kadi himself served nearly two years before a presidential pardon (Test 3); in November–December 2025 veteran journalist Saâd Bouakba was convicted (three years, suspended) over an interview discussing the FLN's independence-era funds, and his outlet's manager was convicted with him and the channel permanently closed (CIVICUS Monitor, December 2025; Associated Press, November 2024).
  • Above the civilian referees sits the one he cannot be said to hold at all — which is itself the finding. The army high command remains the system's arbiter: Tebboune is formally supreme commander and defence minister, yet the army chief of staff, General Saïd Chengriha, was elevated in November 2024 to minister delegate within the government, and every account of the system, friendly and hostile, treats the military establishment as a co-holder of the seat rather than an institution under its civilian control (Africa Research strategic assessment, April 2026; Le Monde, March 2026). The presidency grips the courts, the electoral machinery, and the central bank; the one institution that grips in return is the one that seated it.

Why: A referee that cut his score once in 2024 is real, and the term limit survived — but in 2026 he removed the judiciary council's last check on his appointments, took election logistics back into his own Interior Ministry, cycled three central bank governors in eight months, and governs over a dissolved rights league, a banned party, and a press ranked 139th. That is not holding the center; it is holding the referees. Helped: The presidency itself and the governing coalition, whose institutional path to 2029 now runs through referees it appoints, funds, and can restructure; the FLN–RND–Future Front bloc, which took 221 of 407 seats in July 2026 under the machinery as revised. Hurt: Judges whose promotion now needs no council approval beyond his; ANIE, reduced from operator to supervisor; the dissolved LADDH and banned MDS and their members; journalists and outlets closed or convicted; depositors and businesses priced by a central bank whose leadership changed three times in a year.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? And when another option became available, did he at least consider it openly?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clearest street pushback of his tenure was answered, once, with real concessions of form. Facing the Hirak's second anniversary in February 2021, Tebboune went on national television, called the movement "the blessed Hirak" that "saved Algeria," pardoned detained activists (the Justice Ministry recorded 59 releases, including journalist Khaled Drareni), dissolved the National Assembly a year early, and called legislative elections explicitly to "open the doors of parliament to young people" (presidential address, 18 February 2021; Amnesty International, February 2021; North Africa Journal, February 2021). The adjustment did not concede the Hirak's systemic demands — the against-shelf carries that — but the pardons, the dissolution, and the early ballot were open, named responses to named pressure.
  • He accepted a correction applied to himself. When ANIE's provisional 2024 figures (94.65% for him; turnout stated near 48%) were denounced for "major inconsistencies" in an unprecedented joint statement by all three campaigns — including his own — the Constitutional Court recounted, cut his share to 84.30%, raised his opponents' totals several-fold, and certified turnout at 46.10%; analysts' independent estimates put real participation nearer 23%, a figure the authorities dispute. Tebboune took the oath on the court's corrected numbers without challenging them (Constitutional Court, 14 September 2024; Associated Press, September 2024).
  • When another option became available in the Sansal file, he considered it and took it, openly. Franco-Algerian writer Boualem Sansal, arrested in November 2024 and sentenced on 27 March 2025 to five years for "undermining national unity" over remarks on Algeria's borders, was pardoned by Tebboune on 12 November 2025 after a humanitarian appeal by German President Frank-Walter Steinmeier; the presidency announced the German mediation and its humanitarian grounds (age, health) in its own statement, and Sansal was transferred to Berlin and then France (presidency statement via EuSEE/Hivos, November 2025; Associated Press, November 2025). A door offered by a third party, considered in the open, and walked through.
  • The same pattern appears in economic diplomacy: after Algeria's BRICS application was passed over in August 2023, Tebboune publicly closed that file rather than campaigning against the refusal, and Algeria instead took the available seat at the BRICS New Development Bank, its membership approved in 2025 (Algerian presidency statements, 2023–2025). And the mass clemency machinery has been used repeatedly and publicly: some 4,000 prisoners released on 1 November 2024 including El Kadi and Hirak poet Mohamed Tadjadit, and a further 5,539 granted clemency by decree of 31 October 2026 (Associated Press, November 2024; Algerian presidency via Rupture, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Hirak's actual demand — systemic change, a civilian state not arbitrated by the military — was never adjusted toward at all; it was answered with law. After the February 2021 concessions of form, the movement's resumed marches were banned and dispersed, the June 2021 ordinance widened Article 87 bis to cover advocacy of unconstitutional change, MAK and Rachad were designated terrorist organisations (May 2021), and prosecutions of activists, journalists, and unionists continued every year through 2026 — trade unionist Ali Mammeri's long sentence (15 years at first instance, CIVICUS Monitor, 2025) upheld on appeal in February 2026; opposition leader Ghares convicted again in October 2025 for criticising the president (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025–2026; Amnesty International reporting, 2021–2025). The wobble was absorbed, then criminalised.
  • The parallel door to Sansal's stayed shut, and the difference is the choice. French sports journalist Christophe Gleizes — arrested in May 2024 while reporting on the JS Kabylie football club, sentenced on 29 June 2025 to seven years for "glorifying terrorism" over contacts with a club official linked years earlier to MAK, the sentence upheld on appeal on 3 December 2025 — withdrew his cassation appeal in May 2026, clearing the path to a presidential pardon; his name was not among the 5,539 in the 31 October 2026 clemency decree, and as of mid-November 2026, past 500 days of detention, no pardon had been announced (Court of Appeal reporting, December 2025; Rupture, November 2026; Afrikactus, November 2026). The option that was considered for the writer with a German president's letter was not, on the record, considered for the journalist without one. France and Algeria dispute the case's character — Paris calls the detention arbitrary; Algiers calls it an ordinary counter-terrorism conviction — and both positions are recorded here.
  • Institutional pushback was answered by restructuring the institutions: when the referees' design (an independent ANIE running elections, a judiciary council with a say in senior appointments) produced friction, the March 2026 amendments moved the powers rather than the policy (Test 2). And moral-panic pushback was answered by widening the scaffold: at the opening of the 2025–26 judicial year Tebboune pledged application of the death penalty for child-abduction and school drug-trafficking cases — a pledge a court president publicly cited in October 2025 — and on 1 October 2026 the Assembly adopted penal-code amendments broadening the death penalty's scope, in a country that has not executed since 1993 and where courts continue, in practice, to convert death sentences to life terms (CIVICUS Monitor, October 2025; ARTICLE 19, 2026). No execution is documented as carried out by grading; the direction of the answer is nonetheless recorded.

Why: He adjusts where adjustment costs him little and occasionally where it costs him face — pardons, an early dissolution, accepting his own score cut, the Sansal door taken in the open. He does not adjust on the one question the decade put to him — who arbitrates Algeria — and where the machinery itself pushed back, he rebuilt the machinery. Tactical openness, structural refusal: the two shelves are both true, and the grade sits between them. Helped: The 59 activists freed in February 2021 and the thousands freed in the 2024 and 2026 clemencies, El Kadi, Tadjadit, and Sansal among them; German and French diplomacy, which got its humanitarian case answered; Algeria's treasury, marginally, by the NDB door taken instead of a grievance kept. Hurt: The Hirak's constituency as a whole, whose central demand was legislated against rather than negotiated; Christophe Gleizes, for whom the same pardon door was not opened; activists and unionists prosecuted in every year of the tenure, for whom the February 2021 page never turned.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Kabyle, the protesters, the migrants at the border, women, the young and the poor of the interior and the south — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The social state he runs is genuinely broad in design and reaches the poor by category, not by connection: the unemployment allowance created in February 2022 — the Arab world's first general benefit for first-time jobseekers aged 19–40 — began at 13,000 dinars a month and was raised in steps to 18,000, with official figures claiming around two million beneficiaries; the guaranteed minimum wage rose from 18,000 to 20,000 dinars in 2020 with a further rise to 24,000 decided in 2025; civil-service pay rose by a cumulative 47% phased across 2023–2024; pensions were raised repeatedly, including 10% for the smallest pensions in 2025; maternity leave was extended to five months in 2025; and the state committed to full coverage of cancer care for the uninsured (Algerian presidency and embassy releases, 2025; Africa Research strategic assessment, April 2026).
  • Water was delivered to the coastal millions as a universal good: the five emergency desalination plants (300,000 m³/day each at El Tarf, Béjaïa, Boumerdès, Tipaza, and Oran) were built by Algerian firms and entered service in early 2025, inaugurated by Tebboune in person, raising desalinated water toward 42% of drinking-water supply for some 15 million people (presidency and Sonatrach statements, February–March 2025; ADE–ADC contracts, October 2026).
  • The interior and the south appear in the investment record, not only in speeches: the Gara Djebilet iron-ore project in Tindouf, Saharan agricultural schemes around Timimoun with Italian and Qatari partners, and the Baladna dairy project are all sited in the historically neglected deep south (presidency statements, 2024–2026). Tamazight remains an official language and the Amazigh New Year a national holiday under his tenure.

Evidence against:

  • One population is counted out by convoy, at a scale that dwarfs every other file in this dossier. Algeria's expulsions of sub-Saharan migrants to the Niger border — people rounded up in cities, bused south, and left at "Point Zero" in the desert to walk some 15 km to Assamaka, without individual examination of their cases — rose every year of the second term: 26,031 people in 2023, 31,404 in 2024, at least 34,236 in 2025 (a record; the monitoring network Alarme Phone Sahara documented at least seven deaths in the 2025 convoys), and at least 30,277 more from January to early November 2026, including 941 people in a single operation on 6 November 2026. Human Rights Watch's 2025 documentation found mass expulsions lacking procedural guarantees; some of the expelled held valid documents or residence status, per the monitoring organisations; the IOM's parallel voluntary-return channel assisted some 9,500 people in 2025, a fraction of the convoy total. The Algerian government frames the operations as lawful border management against irregular migration; the counts, the method, and the deaths are documented by the receiving organisations and are not officially answered (Alarme Phone Sahara counts, 2024–2026; Human Rights Watch, 2025; ECRE/IOM reporting, 2025–2026).
  • A named region's political identity is prosecuted as terrorism. Kabylie — Amazigh, historically oppositional — saw its self-determination movement MAK designated a terrorist organisation in May 2021, a designation the US State Department's terrorism reporting assessed as more political than security-related, the movement not appearing to have committed acts meeting the US definition of terrorism; Amnesty International documented activists convicted, some initially sentenced to death after the August 2021 Kabylie fires, on affiliation allegations without tangible evidence tying them to the acts charged (US Department of State country reports; Amnesty International, 2021–2023). The prosecutions continued into 2026 (Test 1: five years for Facebook posts, April 2026). The fires and the lynching of activist Djamel Bensmail were real crimes with real victims; what is graded is a collective designation and affiliation-based convictions laid over a whole region's politics.
  • The protesters who made his presidency possible are counted as a security file: more than 215 prisoners of opinion on the European Parliament's January 2025 count, the rights league dissolved, a party banned, its leader re-convicted for insulting the president (Tests 1–2). Women are counted in declining numbers: representation in the Assembly fell to 25 of 407 seats (6.1%) in July 2026 — the lowest since the pre-quota era, down from 8.1% in 2021 and far below the roughly one-third elected under the old quota in 2012 — after the 2020 constitution replaced the quota with a list-parity rule whose seat outcomes his governments accepted without correction across two elections (IPU Parline; Middle East Women's Council monitoring, November 2026). Youth unemployment remains the system's open wound — official overall unemployment sits near 12% while youth joblessness is estimated around 30%, the gap the unemployment allowance cushions but does not close (ONS figures; Africa Research, April 2026).

Why: The transfers, the water, and the wages count almost every Algerian in — and then the convoys count one population out by the tens of thousands, into a desert, with deaths on the record, and a whole region's dissent is filed under terrorism. A count that includes everyone except the foreigner at the border and the Kabyle at the keyboard is not everyone. Helped: Some two million young jobseekers on the allowance rolls; minimum-wage workers and pensioners whose incomes rose in nominal terms year after year; the roughly 15 million coastal residents whose water security the five plants underwrite; southern wilayas named in the mining and agriculture programs. Hurt: The more than 95,000 migrants expelled to the Niger border across 2024–2026 on the monitors' counts, among them women, minors, and documented dead; Kabyle activists convicted on affiliation and expression; the Hirak generation, counted as defendants rather than citizens; women candidates, whose share of the Assembly fell to 6.1% on rules his governments wrote and kept.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 12 December 2019, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The social contract pledges — delivered, repeatedly, on schedule. The unemployment allowance promised in his program existed by February 2022 and was raised twice thereafter; the minimum wage rose in 2020 and again by decision of 2025; the phased public-sector pay settlement (+47% cumulative) was paid across 2023–2024 as announced; pension revaluations recurred annually. These were the promises his electorate could feel, and they were kept in the coin promised (presidency releases, 2020–2025; Africa Research, April 2026).
  • The desalination program — a dated infrastructure pledge, met. The five-plant emergency program was announced with named sites and a completion horizon; all five plants entered service in early 2025, built in roughly 26 months, and Tebboune inaugurated them personally in February–March 2025; a second program of six further plants followed (Sonatrach and presidency statements, 2022–2025).
  • The constitution — delivered in his first year, and its limit kept. He promised a new constitution early in the term; it was adopted by referendum on 1 November 2020 (approved by 66.8% of votes cast, on a turnout of 23.7% that this shelf records as the pledge's shadow). Its two-term presidential limit — the clause that ends his own tenure in 2029 — survived his own March 2026 revision untouched (Constitutional Court results, November 2020; ConstitutionNet, 2026).
  • Housing — machinery built, at scale. The AADL 3 rental-purchase program opened registrations in July 2024 and drew more than a million applications within days on official figures; distributions under the earlier AADL tranches continued and AADL 3 construction launched across multiple wilayas in 2025 (housing ministry statements, 2024–2025). Completions at the promised scale are not yet documented; the program exists and is moving, and the shelf records it as in motion, not kept.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The founding political promise — not kept. Tebboune took office presenting himself as the Hirak's partner: he called the movement blessed, promised a "new Algeria" answering its demands, and in 2020–2021 stated publicly that no journalist is imprisoned for an opinion in Algeria. The record since: the Hirak's organisations dissolved or banned, its activists prosecuted under a widened terrorism article in every year of the tenure, and journalists — El Kadi, Drareni, Bouakba, Gleizes among them — imprisoned for their work (Tests 1–3). The promise and the record face in opposite directions, and the distance between them is the largest single item on this test.
  • The durum wheat pledge — declared kept by the seller, unproven on the market. At his September 2024 inauguration Tebboune pledged self-sufficiency in durum wheat, barley, and corn by 2026. In July 2025 he announced Algeria was on track to durum self-sufficiency, and officials presented the 2025 harvest as covering durum needs; barley and corn self-sufficiency has not been declared or documented. Meanwhile the state cereals office continued to buy wheat abroad in bulk — trade reporting records a purchase of more than 700,000 tons in the tender closing 11 November 2026 — and Algeria remains one of the world's largest wheat importers, overwhelmingly in soft wheat (presidency statements, September 2024 and July 2025; OAIC tender reporting, November 2026). The durum claim is recorded as officially asserted and independently unverified; the pledge as a whole is, at its own deadline, at best one-third documented.
  • The round-number economic targets — missed or receding. A non-hydrocarbon export target of $13 billion announced for 2023 closed at roughly half that on official figures, and state-aligned reporting in late 2026 projected non-hydrocarbon exports of approximately $5 billion for the year — below the figure targeted for three years earlier; the $29 billion-by-2030 target recedes accordingly. The pledge that GDP would reach $400 billion by 2027 stands against IMF estimates putting GDP under $300 billion in 2025–26, with one year left (presidential statements, 2022–2024; IMF Article IV estimates, 2025; DZ Watch economic reporting, 2026).
  • The recovered-fortune pledge — a fraction delivered. Recovering the wealth looted under the old regime was a headline 2019 commitment, with official claims at times running to tens of billions of dollars; the documented recoveries — seized domestic assets, real estate, and company holdings from the convicted oligarchs — are real but modest against the claims, and no audited public accounting of the total has been published (court reporting, 2019–2022; Algerian official statements, various).
  • The trap he did not build (the no-good-choice clause, applied): Tebboune took office on 12 December 2019; within three months oil prices collapsed (Brent briefly under $20 in April 2020) and COVID-19 shut the economy, against reserves already drawn down to roughly $60 billion by the previous regime's spending. The options were austerity — cutting the imports and subsidies the poor eat by — or holding the social floor and spending the reserve. He chose the reserve: imports were compressed, subsidies and wages were maintained, no austerity budget was imposed, and reserves bottomed at roughly $42–46 billion in 2021 before the 2022 gas windfall rebuilt them toward $68 billion by 2023 (IMF and Bank of Algeria figures, 2020–2023). The dossier records the constraint itself, not a demerit: the rentier trap predates him by decades, he was publicly frank that reserves were falling, and the door he took was the least harmful one open to households at the time. What remains gradable — and is graded above — is what he did with the windfall years that followed.

Why: On concrete, dated, deliverable promises — benefits created, wages raised, plants built, a constitution delivered and its term limit respected — his word has been good, and the electorate that chose him on the social platform got the platform. On the tenure's founding political promise and its biggest round numbers — the new Algeria the Hirak was promised, $13 billion of non-oil exports, $400 billion of GDP, the recovered billions — the word was not kept, and the misses were re-dated or re-announced rather than owned.
