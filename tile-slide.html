How to play

What it is: a grid of tiles with one square empty. Tiles next to the gap can slide into it. You win when the tiles read in order — 1 across the top row, then the next row, and so on — with the gap in the bottom-right corner.

What it means: every slide moves the gap as much as the tile. Good solving is mostly gap-wrangling: putting the empty square where the next tile needs it, without wrecking tiles you have already placed.

What it could mean for you: start on 3 × 3 with a quick-practice shuffle, learn the row-by-row method below with the Coach on, then take on the standard 4 × 4. Your times and move counts are kept in your record so you can watch the method pay off.

The honesty rule, in plain words: half of all possible arrangements of this puzzle can never be solved — that is a proven mathematical fact, not a difficulty setting. So this page never shuffles by randomising the tiles. It starts from the solved board and scrambles it with real slides, which means a solution always exists: at worst, you could undo the scramble. The “par” shown above the board is that scramble length — the deal in front of you can always be finished in at most that many moves, and often in fewer.

Strategies — more than one honest way

Row by row (the standard method, and what the Coach teaches)

Finish the top row left to right, then the next row, and so on — until only the last two rows remain. Solve those last two rows in columns, one column at a time, left to right. Trade-off: it always works and is easy to hold in your head, but it is not the fastest — the last rows cost extra moves because you are protecting everything above them.

Why the last rows are solved in columns

With two rows left, there is no spare row to hide tiles in. If you try to finish the second-to-last row across, the final tile of that row has nowhere to go without breaking the row above. Working down each column instead treats the two tiles in a column as a pair: park the upper tile one square below its home with its partner beside it, then swing the pair up together. That swing is the endgame pair technique — the Coach walks you through it on your own board when you get there.

The last-tile-of-a-row pair

The same trick appears earlier: the final two tiles of any row are hard to place one at a time. Bring them together as a pair — one sitting under the other's home square — then rotate both into place in one short sequence. Placing them separately usually ends with the pair swapped, and a swapped pair in a finished row cannot be fixed inside that row.

Column by column (the mirror method)

Everything above, turned sideways: finish the left column top to bottom, then the next column, and solve the last two columns in rows. Trade-off: identical in power to row by row — pick whichever direction your eyes read the board better. Some players find the endgame pairs easier to see in columns; others in rows. Neither is “the real way.”

Fringe / layer solving (for the patient)

Solve the whole outer frame first, leaving a smaller puzzle in the middle, then solve that. Trade-off: elegant on 5 × 5, where it shrinks a big board fast — but the frame's last tiles need the same pair technique, and one mistake in the frame is expensive to repair. Row by row is the safer first method; fringe is a good second language.

The story Story / history

The craze. The sliding-tile puzzle appeared in 1879, made by Noyes Chapman, a postmaster in Canastota, New York. Within months — through the winter of 1879–80 — it swept Boston and then the world: students solved it in lectures, drivers were forbidden from solving it on duty, and it was denounced from pulpits as a work of the devil. Few toys have ever spread faster.

The proof. In 1879, mathematicians William Johnson and William Story proved the fact that still defines this puzzle: exactly half of all possible arrangements can never be solved, no matter how long you slide. Whether an arrangement is solvable depends on a parity count — the number of tile swaps hidden in the layout, together with where the gap sits. Change one pair of tiles and you cross from the solvable half into the impossible half.

The famous impossible challenge. The puzzle's most famous story is the “14–15” challenge, popularised by puzzle-maker Sam Loyd, who offered a $1,000 prize for solving a board that differed from solved in just one way: tiles 14 and 15 swapped. People worked on it for years. It could never be done — that single swap puts the board in the unsolvable half Johnson and Story had already described. Loyd also claimed, wrongly, to have invented the puzzle himself; the record credits Chapman. We tell it here as what it is: a story about a prize that mathematics had already made unwinnable.

Why this page deals only solvable boards: a game that can secretly hand you an impossible board is not a hard game, it is a rigged one. Every shuffle here is made by sliding tiles from the solved state, so every board you will ever see on this page sits in the solvable half — guaranteed by construction, and stated plainly so you never have to take it on trust.
