The Spectrum Spectacles

Not one magic lens — a key-ring you wear. Every band of light gets its own catcher, a translator re-keys it into the one window your eye owns (about 380–750 nm), and a dial on the bridge chooses which layer you read. Spin the dial fast enough and the layers stop taking turns: they blend, the way Newton’s disc blends the spectrum into white.

Start with the simulation — that’s the face. Everything else is layered underneath it: the idea, the drawing, every part down to the screw, the pathway, and the limits. Nothing is gone. It’s just down a layer, and then another.

LAYER 1 — THE IDEA

The design in four thoughts

Everything else on this sheet hangs off these four. Each one answers a wall that stopped every earlier attempt at a see-everything lens — and each one opens one layer down for the full reasoning.

THOUGHT 01

Many eyes, not one lens

No single material passes the whole line — the wall is physics, not craftsmanship. So the Spectacles don’t try. Each band gets its own catcher. A key-ring, not a key.

ONE LAYER DOWN

Ordinary glass blocks ultraviolet on one side and swallows infrared on the other. The Spectacles copy the mantis shrimp, which runs twelve-plus channels instead of one better eye: quartz for UV, germanium for thermal infrared, antennas for radio, and scintillator counters — which count individual events rather than form images — for the X-ray and gamma end.

THOUGHT 02

A translator, not a window — and the key is printed on the frame

Nothing outside the visible band can be “looked through.” It is caught, decoded, and re-drawn inside the eye’s window — false color, honestly worn, with the key printed on the frame.

ONE LAYER DOWN

The band and the mapping are printed on the frame itself, in the wearer’s view at all times, so a translation can never pass itself off as the thing itself. A guess in a photograph’s clothes is what happens when the label falls off; this device is built so the label can’t.

THOUGHT 03

The lens is liquid, because the original was

Glass is a liquid that got frozen mid-pour. The eye never used it — the eye’s soft lens morphs. The Spectacles copy the original, not the imitation. The lens stays in the frame; the water inside it becomes the dial.

ONE LAYER DOWN

The eye is a ball of water whose lens is squeezed fatter or flatter by a muscle. The Spectacles’ version is an electrowetting cell — a sealed droplet of water and oil whose curve changes with voltage. No moving parts, nothing to swap. Change the liquid’s shape and tuning, and the key changes with it.

THOUGHT 04

Any band — but not all bands at once, unless you spin

The window is narrow and the spectrum is endless; pour everything in at once and the layers paint over each other into mush. So the wearer chooses, one layer at a time — or turns the dial fast and blends them. Slow for the parts, fast for the whole.

ONE LAYER DOWN

Spun fast, the eye does what it does with Newton’s disc and every DLP projector: it fuses the separate bands into one blended whole. The choosing stays the wearer’s, every time — the device never picks the layer for them.

1Frame Two rings and a bridge holding every other part in alignment in front of the eyes; carries the printed key on the brow bar. Titanium wire and plate, or glass-filled nylon for the prototype run. CNC-bent rings, Ø 56 mm outside; bridge width fitted to the wearer (standard 18–22 mm); brow bar etched, not stickered, so the key can’t peel off. Titanium stock from any metals supplier; prototype frames 3D-printed from free CAD files, then cast or machined.
2Liquid lens cell ×2 The morphing lens. A sealed droplet whose curve — and so its focus and tuning — changes with voltage. This is the part that “morphs”; it replaces lens-swapping entirely. Two immiscible liquids (water and oil), a hydrophobic-coated cell, two transparent electrodes, fused-silica windows (quartz where the UV path needs it — plain glass would eat the UV before it lands). Electrowetting cell, Ø 52 mm aperture, sealed against evaporation; driven 0–60 V AC from the translator board; no moving parts, nothing to wear out. Electrowetting lenses are commercial parts today (sold for barcode scanners and industrial cameras) — buy the cell, don’t reinvent it; silica windows from optical suppliers.
3Sensor ring The many eyes: one catcher per band, seated in the rim of each ring — UV photodiode, visible sensor, thermal microbolometer for infrared, a stub antenna feed for the radio/microwave end, and a scintillator counter seated in the brow bar for the X-ray and gamma end, which counts individual events instead of forming an image. Silicon (visible), silicon-carbide or gallium-nitride (UV), vanadium-oxide microbolometer array (thermal IR), germanium window over the IR catcher, copper antenna trace, and a scintillator crystal (sodium-iodide or a modern equivalent) coupled to a photodiode for the counter. Each catcher looks through its own window material — quartz for UV, germanium for IR, polymer for visible — because no one window passes them all. Ring-mounted, factory-aligned to the lens axis. The counter needs no window and no alignment; it is shielded, brow-bar mounted, and reports counts, never pictures. UV photodiodes and microbolometer modules are catalog parts from sensor distributors; germanium windows from infrared-optics suppliers; scintillator counter modules from radiation-detector suppliers.
4Translator The key itself: a processor in the temple arm that takes each catcher’s raw band and re-draws it inside the visible window — assigning the false colors, holding the mapping, and driving the liquid cells and the display layer. A low-power system-on-chip, a micro-display or transparent waveguide per eye, and the mapping tables in firmware. Firmware carries one labeled mapping per band (thermal palette, UV violet-white, and so on); switching the dial switches the table, nothing else. Mappings are published with the design — a key anyone can inspect. Commodity phone-class chips and micro-displays from electronics distributors; firmware is part of this blueprint, free to build from.
5Hub dial The wearer’s choice, as a physical control on the bridge: click from band to band, or push past the last click into Spin, where rotation speed sets the blend. Knurled aluminum wheel, detent ring, rotary encoder. Ø 26 mm, seven detents (one per band) plus the spin zone; encoder read by the translator. Operable by touch alone — the wearer never has to look at it. Encoder and wheel are stock parts; the detent ring is machined with the frame.
6Power cell A rechargeable cell in the temple tip, counterweighting the front of the frame; charges over the same universal connector the rest of the family uses. Lithium-polymer pouch cell, ≈ 400 mAh per side. Sized for a day of intermittent band-switching; the thermal catcher is the hungry part, so IR mode carries a duty cycle and says so on the frame. Stock pouch cells from battery distributors; connector is the universal one, below.
7Record bridge The imprint-to-record bridge: every decode — band, mapping, timestamp, image — written to a file the wearer owns, so a seeing can be checked later by someone who wasn’t there. Flash storage on the translator board; a small record indicator in the lens corner, visible to the wearer whenever recording runs. Recording is never silent: the indicator stands whenever the bridge is open, and the file carries the key it was decoded with, so the record can’t outrun its label. Commodity flash; the file format is documented in this blueprint, readable by any computer.
8Universal fastener Every screw in the device — one drive, everywhere, so one driver opens all of it. Sizes scale by load; the interface never changes. Stainless micro-screws, M1.2–M2, single standard drive. Named on the sheet because the fastener is part of the design: a device nobody can open is a device nobody can check. Any fastener supplier, by the standard size — deliberately nothing proprietary.

LAYER 5 The limits, printed on the sheet What the device cannot do — stated in the same ink as what it can.

A blueprint by this method states what the thing does — and states its walls in the same ink.

  • It shows decodes, never the world “as it really looks.” Every band outside the visible arrives as a translation. True ones, if the keys are honest — but translations all the same. The crow doesn’t see a white crow either; it sees crow, in crow. There is no view from no key at all.
  • False color is honest only while it’s labeled. That is why the band and mapping stand printed on the frame, in the wearer’s view, and inside every recorded file. Remove the label and the same image becomes the crow post’s mistake: a guess wearing a photograph’s clothes.
  • Any band, not all bands at once. Simultaneously means the blend — and the blend trades the separations for the whole. Slow dial for layers, fast dial for white. The wearer chooses, every time; the device never chooses for them.
  • The eye never morphs. The world gets re-keyed into the eye, never the other way around. That condition — as long as your eye could see it — is not a flaw in the design. It is the design.
  • The radio and gamma ends are hear-and-count, not look. At those wavelengths there is no image-forming in any lens-like sense; the Spectacles render them as pattern and brightness mapped from antenna and counter data, and the frame’s key says exactly that when those bands are selected.

The Spectrum Spectacles — blueprint and simulation. A design on paper, not a built device; the simulation shows translations of a scene, drawn by hand, not sensor data. Born from a night’s chain: Franklin’s layered lenses, the crow’s fourth channel, a spinning two-ring trainer with a dial at its hub, Newton’s disc, and the liquid the eye was made of. There’s a pathway to get there. This sheet is the map of it so far.
