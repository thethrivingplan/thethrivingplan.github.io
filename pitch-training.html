Match the Note

Hear the target. Sing it. Keep your trace inside the green band until the hold bar fills — about a second and a half of steady, in-tune singing. Wobbling through the window does not count; holding it does. That steadiness is the skill.

Why it works

Your ear hears the target, your brain compares it with what your voice is doing, and your voice corrects — dozens of times a second. At first the corrections are slow and overshoot. Practice makes the loop faster and finer. You are not “getting a better voice”; you are training a feedback loop, like balancing a broom on your palm.

Climb the Ladder — a rising scale

Eight rungs: C – D – E – F – G – A – B – C. Match the glowing rung to climb to the next. Reach the top and the ladder restarts a step higher.

Rung 1 of 8. Hear the target, sing it, hold it.

Tip

Scales feel easy until the top two rungs — the voice wants to flatten as it climbs. Think the note before you sing it: hear it in your head first, then let the voice go to the sound you already heard. Singers call this “aiming”.

Sing the Interval

The page plays a home note, then a second note some distance away. Your job: sing the second note. Use “Hear the pair” as often as you like.

Interval: hidden until you match it — your ears first, names second.

Why intervals

A melody is nothing but intervals in a row. Singers who can hear and produce a fifth or a third on demand can learn songs by ear, find harmonies, and stop depending on a piano to feed them every note.

Reading the names

Voice families are rough neighbourhoods, not boxes: Bass (about E2–E4), Baritone (A2–A4), Tenor (C3–C5), Alto (F3–F5), Mezzo-soprano (A3–A5), Soprano (C4–C6). Most untrained voices span about an octave and a half; trained voices often reach two octaves or more. Where your voice sounds best (your tessitura) matters more than the extremes — the extremes just tell you where the fence is.

Learn — the knowledge under the game

What pitch actually is — Tap to open / close

What it is: pitch is how high or low a sound is, and physically it is speed of vibration — frequency, measured in Hertz (Hz). A string or vocal fold vibrating 220 times a second gives A3 at standard tuning. Double the frequency and you get the same note one octave higher; halve it, one octave lower.

What it means: the octave is divided into 12 equal semitones. Each semitone is divided into 100 cents. Being 50 cents off means you are halfway to the next note — plainly out. Within about ±10 cents sounds locked-in to most ears; ±25 cents is a fair practice window for a beginner, which is the green band on the scope.

What it could mean for you: “sharp” = too high, your frequency is above the target. “Flat” = too low. When the page says “18 cents flat”, it means: nudge up, less than a fifth of the way to the next note. Small corrections, not leaps.

Breath and placement — the basics, at the knowledge level — Tap to open / close

Breath: your voice is a wind instrument. Pitch stays steady when air flows steady. Breathe low — belly and ribs expanding, shoulders still — and let the air out slowly, as if fogging a mirror gently. Most pitch sagging (going flat at the end of a note) is simply running out of air pressure.

Placement: where the sound feels like it resonates. Humming with lips closed, feeling a buzz in the lips and front teeth, is “forward” placement — bright and easy to tune. A swallowed, throaty sound sits back and is harder to control. Try this: hum the target note until your lips tingle, then open into an “ah” keeping the same feeling.

Posture: stand or sit tall, head level — looking down or up bends the tube the air travels through and drags pitch with it. None of this is talent. It is plumbing, and plumbing can be learned.

This page teaches knowledge, not lessons in person: if singing ever hurts, stop — pain is information, and a real voice teacher beats any page for fixing strain.

Why matching improves — the ear–voice loop — Tap to open / close

Nobody sings in tune by having a “tuned voice”. They sing in tune because a loop runs constantly: ear hears → brain compares → voice adjusts. Studies of singers consistently find that pitch-matching accuracy improves with short, regular practice, because the loop gets faster and the comparison gets finer — the same way a darts player’s arm learns, the system learns.

Two honest consequences. First: if you cannot yet hear whether you are sharp or flat, that is the first thing training fixes — the verdict line on this page is a stand-in for that hearing until your own arrives. Second: progress is usually quick at the start (the window feels huge within a couple of weeks of ten-minute sessions) and then slower and finer. Both phases are normal.

440 vs 432 — what the reference switch does — Tap to open / close

“Concert pitch” is an agreement about where A sits. Today’s standard is A = 440 Hz. A = 432 Hz tunes everything about 32 cents lower — a slightly warmer, softer feel that many players prefer, and the tuning Tristan’s music is recorded at. Neither is “correct” in nature; what matters is that everyone in the room uses the same one. Train at 432 if you sing along with those records, 440 for almost everything else.

⚠ Honest limits — read this once

This is a practice mirror, not a measurement instrument. Phone and laptop microphones, room echo, a TV in the background, a fan, singing quietly or far from the mic — all of these move the reading, sometimes by a lot. Vibrato (the natural wobble in a sung note) makes the trace dance; read the middle of the dance, not the edges. If the page and your ears disagree in a quiet room at a steady volume, believe the disagreement is worth one more try — not that either of you is broken. And the mic is off until you turn it on, every time.
