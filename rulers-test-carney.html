The Ruler's Test — Mark Carney, Prime Minister of Canada

Subject: Mark Carney, Prime Minister of Canada since March 14, 2025 (Liberal leadership winner March 9, 2025; led the Liberals to a minority government in the April 28, 2025 federal election; the government reached a House of Commons majority in April 2026 through five opposition floor-crossings and three byelection wins — Reuters, April 13, 2026). Before politics he was Governor of the Bank of Canada (2008–2013) and Governor of the Bank of England (2013–2020), and a senior executive of Brookfield Asset Management. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Tenure at grading: about 20 months — shorter than most subjects in this series, and noted where it limits a grade. Method: Both shelves are shown for every test — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, provinces and insiders; no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He submitted himself to the standard ethics machinery and it has been used, repeatedly, in public view. On becoming prime minister he placed his assets in a blind trust and agreed a formal conflict-of-interest screen under s. 29 of the Conflict of Interest Act with Ethics Commissioner Konrad von Finckenstein, covering Brookfield Asset Management, Brookfield Corporation, Stripe Inc. and companies they own or control, administered by his chief of staff and the Clerk of the Privy Council (Ethics Commissioner public registry, July 2025 filing). The Privy Council Office stated in August 2026 that the screen had been applied in 25 situations (PCO statement, August 2026, as reported).
  • No ethics ruling has been made against him, and no documented decision exists in this record in which he participated to Brookfield's benefit — a point conceded even by critical coverage of the screen (Canadian Mining Report review of the record, November 15, 2026). The Ethics Commissioner defended the screen's design to MPs as a preventive measure of a kind the Federal Court of Appeal has approved (Ethics Commissioner testimony, December 8, 2025).
  • His provincial dealing crosses party lines on the record: a landmark energy MOU with Alberta's United Conservative Premier Danielle Smith (November 27, 2025), a one-project-one-review cooperation agreement with Ontario's Progressive Conservative government, and internal-trade and major-projects agreements pursued with governments of differing stripes (Government of Canada announcements, 2025–26). The federation's conservative premiers got deals, not freezes.
  • The rules he legislated bind his own cabinet: after a Conservative amendment, designated major projects remain subject to the Conflict of Interest Act and the Lobbying Act — closing a route by which cabinet members could have benefited from fast-tracked projects (House of Commons committee amendments to Bill C-5, June 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The conflict itself is structural and continuing. Carney retains Brookfield stock options and deferred share units inside the blind trust, plus carried interest from Brookfield Global Transition Fund I; the Ethics Commissioner told MPs plainly: "It is clear that Mr. Carney's future compensation is tied to the success of Brookfield" (Ethics Commissioner testimony, December 8, 2025; Brookfield COO testimony to the ethics committee, November 24, 2025). A blind trust hides management; it does not sever the interest — he knows the largest thing in it.
  • The screen's perimeter is incomplete on its own record. Annex A publicly lists 103 entities; Brookfield's COO told the ethics committee the firm owns over 2,000 businesses across its funds, and no public list of the remainder has been published (ethics committee testimony, November 24, 2025). The screen also contains a wide exception: Carney may participate in decisions "of general application" unless a listed company's interest is disproportionately affected — the category that covers most national economic policy (Conflict of Interest Act screen, July 2025).
  • The administering office has resisted showing its work. The Information Commissioner ordered the Privy Council Office to stop delaying and disclose records on how the screen has been enforced, with a deadline of November 18, 2026; a separate request about his two self-imposed screens of March–July 2025 remained undisclosed eleven months after a promised release date (Information Commissioner ruling and Democracy Watch filings, November 13, 2026). Democracy Watch's characterization of the screens as "loophole-filled smokescreens" is an advocacy group's characterization, labeled as such; the disclosure delays are documented fact. In November 2026 the Bloc Québécois leader told the House the screen had been triggered 29 times, a dozen since June 2026 — a figure the government did not dispute while insisting the rules are followed (House of Commons debate, November 18, 2026).
  • The House ethics committee in April 2026 called for the law to be changed to require a prime minister to sell such investments outright rather than trust-and-screen them (House ethics committee report, April 2026) — Parliament's own committee judging the standard he meets to be below the standard that should apply to his seat.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do provinces, Parliament, the courts, the Bank of Canada, and media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His signature centralizing law was passed by Parliament, not decreed: Bill C-5 (the One Canadian Economy Act, including the Building Canada Act) went through both chambers with Conservative support, was amended in committee (transparency of approval conditions; limits on bypass powers; Criminal Code and Hazardous Products Act excluded from exemption), and received royal assent June 26, 2025 (Parliamentary record, June 2025). The override power exists because a legislature voted it, with a five-year horizon on designations.
  • The Bank of Canada — his own former institution, where his preferences might be presumed strongest — has been left alone: no documented instance in this record of the government pressuring, directing, or publicly attacking the Bank over interest-rate decisions during his tenure.
  • The courts have not been attacked or defied. When First Nations filed Federal Court applications in autumn 2026 to quash the Pacific Link designation for inadequate consultation, the government proceeded by defending the designation in court — the wobble answered through the checking institution, not around it (Federal Court filings, October–November 2026).
  • Provinces were negotiated with rather than overridden: the Alberta MOU is a signed agreement with a premier who had campaigned against Ottawa, and when British Columbia and coastal First Nations held the north-coast tanker ban line, Ottawa kept the ban and the project was re-routed south (see Test 3) — a provincial "no" that stood (Canada–Alberta MOU, November 27, 2025; Coastal First Nations statements, 2026).
  • Media: no documented instance in this record of his government punishing, defunding mid-mandate, or singling out critical outlets; critical coverage of his ethics screen, housing record, and trade concessions has run continuously in national outlets throughout his tenure.

Evidence against:

  • The Building Canada Act is, by design, a grip: once cabinet designates a project "in the national interest," federal findings under other statutes are deemed made, a single ministerial authorization replaces all other federal permits, and cabinet holds broad power to exempt the project from other laws (Building Canada Act, 2025). The designation criteria are discretionary and non-mandatory, with no measurable threshold (legal analyses of the Act, Woodward & Company LLP, 2025). Parliament granted the grip; the grip is nonetheless his government's to hold.
  • Parliament's own scrutiny was curtailed in the granting: the government used a procedural motion (June 16, 2025) severely limiting debate on C-5 at each stage, passing a law of this consequence in roughly three weeks (House of Commons record, June 2025). Senators who opposed it, most citing Indigenous rights, saw every delaying amendment voted down (Senate record, June 26, 2025).
  • The minority Parliament the voters chose in April 2025 was converted into a majority without an election. Between November 2025 and April 2026 five opposition MPs crossed the floor to the Liberals — four Conservatives and one New Democrat — after private meetings with senior government figures, and three byelection wins on April 13, 2026 took the Liberals to 173 of 343 seats (Reuters, April 13, 2026; House of Commons seat records). Floor-crossing is legal and each crosser gave public reasons; the documented pattern — a year-end CBC interview in which Carney, asked if he was running an active recruitment campaign, answered evasively that "a spectrum of MPs are enticed by his brand of governing" — supports the opposition's characterization of an organized recruitment effort (CBC year-end interview, December 2025; Conservative leader's statements, 2026). The characterization "manufactured majority" is the critics'; the seat arithmetic is fact. The constraint the electorate imposed was removed by acquisition, not persuasion of voters.
  • Decision-making is unusually concentrated in his own office's machinery: a new Major Projects Office reporting into the centre, an agenda run through cabinet designation, and a governing style his own first-year record describes as centrally sequenced announcements from the PMO (Government of Canada, Major Projects Office, 2025–26).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, provinces, Indigenous nations, markets, or events push back — including the trade war with the U.S. — does he adjust openly, or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clearest adjustment: the pipeline. The November 2025 Alberta MOU contemplated a north-coast route requiring a tanker-ban exemption. British Columbia's government and Coastal First Nations refused — formally, in a signed North Coast Protection Declaration (November 5, 2025) — and Carney had personally assured coastal nations his government would seek their free, prior and informed consent (Haida Nation open letter recording the assurance, April 22, 2026). Ottawa adjusted: the tanker ban was kept whole, Alberta's July 2026 application routed the project (Pacific Link) south toward Delta instead, and the October 1, 2026 designation proceeded on that basis. The pushback changed the project; that is the wobble answered, at real political cost with Alberta.
  • In the trade war he has done both halves of adjusting in the open: he conceded where he judged concession bought room (below), and in August 2026 he walked away from a near-complete U.S. deal, stating the terms crossed Canadian red lines — sovereignty, French-language protections, and the auto industry — accepting Trump's retaliatory 50% tariffs on roughly $20 billion of goods rather than signing (AP, August–November 2026). By late October 2026 he reported talks resuming because, in his words, "the U.S. is coming closer to our position," while frankly conceding he could not be sure Trump would honor any signature — "written in pencil" was his own earlier phrase, repeated to him and not withdrawn (AP, October 26 and November 1, 2026).
  • His Davos address (January 20, 2026) was an open admission against his own class's interest: that the rules-based order was partly a "fiction" the strongest exempted themselves from, that Canada had participated in the ritual knowingly, and that the rupture had to be named rather than managed with euphemism (World Economic Forum address, "Principled and Pragmatic," January 20, 2026). Whatever its strategy, it is the opposite of denial as rhetoric.
  • On Indigenous pushback to C-5 itself, he responded to the first wave publicly and substantively — affirming free, prior and informed consent as central to the law's operation and committing that no project would proceed without the support of affected First Nations (prime ministerial statements, June 2025; repeated to coastal nations, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The concessions ledger runs against the campaign's "elbows up" posture, and each retreat was presented as tactics rather than acknowledged as retreat: Canada scrapped its digital-services tax on U.S. tech firms (June 2025) to restart talks; lifted most retaliatory tariffs on CUSMA-compliant U.S. goods (September 2025); halted streaming regulations Washington opposed; and in 2026 agreed to hand half the net toll revenue of the fully Canadian-financed Gordie Howe Bridge to a U.S.-controlled fund for its first 15 years, with a U.S. say over some toll changes (Wall Street Journal/Times review of the concessions, 2026). Sixteen months in, the Journal's assessment — concessions accumulating, tariffs not lifted — is a critic's summary, but the individual concessions are documented facts, and nearly 70% of Canadians told Abacus Data they wanted Ottawa to hold firm even at economic cost (Abacus Data polling, 2026).
  • The Indigenous wobble on C-5 was answered with speed, not adjustment, at the moment it was raised: Indigenous leaders asked for the bill to be slowed; the Senate passed it unamended within the month; Sen. Paul Prosper, quoting First Nations leaders calling the bill "not reconciliation" but "a betrayal of it," led the opposition in the chamber (Senate record, June 26, 2025). Grand Chief Alvin Fiddler of the Nishnawbe Aski Nation publicly asked the Governor General to intervene to slow passage (June 2025). The adjustments came later, on the pipeline route — not on the law itself, which stands as passed.
  • The Pacific Link designation (October 1, 2026) proceeded while the consent question was unresolved: Cold Lake First Nations applied to Federal Court on October 7, 2026 saying it was not consulted, and on November 2 nine B.C. First Nations announced their own applications to quash the designation for inadequate consultation; the Union of BC Indian Chiefs and the BC Assembly of First Nations say consultation was rushed and the designation overrides their rights, while at least one oilsands-region chief welcomes the offered equity stake (Federal Court filings and First Nations statements, October–November 2026). Whether consultation was legally adequate is now before the court — CONTESTED, undecided. The designation was not paused pending the answer.
  • On housing numbers and fiscal projections (Test 5), the government's response to its own watchdogs' findings has been redefinition rather than acknowledgment: the Parliamentary Budget Officer reports the operating-balance target will be missed and calls the capital/operating definitions "contradictory"; the government's reply disputes the definitions rather than the arithmetic (PBO reports and Finance Department responses, 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Indigenous peoples, Quebec, Alberta and the energy sector, immigrants and asylum seekers, the young and priced-out — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Alberta and the energy sector — counted out of federal favour for a decade — were counted in, in writing: the November 27, 2025 MOU commits Ottawa to a privately financed bitumen pipeline to the B.C. coast as a national-interest project, drops the federal oil-and-gas emissions cap, suspends the Clean Electricity Regulations in Alberta, and pairs the pipeline with the Pathways carbon-capture project and a $130/tonne industrial carbon price (Canada–Alberta MOU, November 27, 2025). Whatever its other costs, a whole region's central industry was brought inside the national project by negotiation with its own elected government.
  • Indigenous peoples are inside the structure of the new project economy, not only its consultation queue: the MOU framework contemplates Indigenous co-ownership of the pipeline, the Pacific Link designation offers Indigenous communities at least a 10% stake, and the federal Indigenous loan-guarantee program was doubled to $10 billion to let nations buy equity (Canada–Alberta MOU, 2025; Pacific Link designation terms, October 2026). Some nations have taken the offer up in principle — an oilsands-region chief publicly welcomed the equity stake (First Nations statements, 2026).
  • Quebec was counted in where it is most often counted out — in the trade war's red lines: Carney stated in August 2026 that he rejected U.S. terms partly because they threatened French-language protections, and supply management for Quebec's dairy sector has been held as a stated non-negotiable in the talks (AP, August–October 2026).
  • The immigration restraint he imposed was paired with a stated floor, not a closure: permanent-resident admissions stabilized at 380,000 a year (64% economic class), with the temporary-resident share targeted below 5% of population by end of 2027 — and by July 2026 he could report asylum seekers down a third, temporary workers down a half, student arrivals down two-thirds from their peaks (IRCC levels plans; prime ministerial statement, July 29, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Coastal First Nations were counted out of the decision that most affects them. The nations of the north and central coast — rights and title holders whose 2010 declaration bans crude-oil tankers in their waters — learned of the Ottawa–Alberta pipeline discussions from the press, said so in writing, and maintain absolute opposition: "No offer of equity or ownership will change our position, and no proponent is acceptable to us" (Coastal First Nations statements, November 19, 2025 and May 15, 2026). The route change (Test 3) spared their coast; but the designation system that can name a project in the national interest over a nation's stated refusal is the standing fact, and nine B.C. nations are now in Federal Court over consultation on the southern route (Federal Court filings, November 2, 2026). Equity offered is not consent obtained; on this record the count includes Indigenous peoples as partners where they agree and as litigants where they don't.
  • Asylum seekers were counted down by law. Bill C-12 (royal assent March 26, 2026) bars most claims made more than a year after arrival and tightens the land-border rule to 14 days — provisions refugee advocates and legal groups criticized as denying protection to people who delay filing out of fear, ignorance, or counsel's advice (Bill C-12, 2026; advocates' published criticisms, 2025–26 — characterizations, labeled as such). The backlog context is real — roughly 252,000 claims awaited decision in September 2026 (Immigration and Refugee Board figures) — but the instrument chosen closes the door on a category rather than deciding its claims faster.
  • The young and priced-out are, on the numbers, still outside. Housing starts ran at an annualized ~244,558 through October 2026 — down 3% year-to-date and under half the roughly 500,000-a-year pace he campaigned on — while youth unemployment reached 13.7% in October 2026 (CMHC housing-starts release, November 2026; Statistics Canada, October 2026). CMHC's fall 2026 supply report warns the current pace leaves the market up to 238,000 homes short of restored affordability by 2036. A generation's exclusion is measured in those two numbers, whatever the programs announced.
  • Temporary residents already in Canada — students and workers who built lives under the old rules — absorbed the adjustment: new temporary arrivals capped at 385,000 for 2026 (from ~674,000 in 2025), study permits at ~155,000, the parents-and-grandparents stream fully suspended for 2026 (IRCC levels plan, 2026–2028). The restraint was the policy; the people inside the old system paid for the correction of decisions they did not make.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record, with dates. His pre-politics record as a central banker is background, graded only where he made public commitments.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (where his word held):

  • The carbon tax: his first act as prime minister, March 14, 2025, was the directive ending the consumer carbon tax effective April 1, 2025, with the April rebate paid — the campaign's central promise, kept on the day he took office (prime ministerial directive and order in council, March 14, 2025). He simultaneously kept the less popular half of the promise: the industrial carbon price stayed, and the Alberta MOU raises it toward $130/tonne — he had said throughout the campaign he would keep and strengthen industrial pricing while ending the consumer levy, and both halves happened.
  • Taxes and affordability: the middle-class tax cut was implemented in withholding from July 1, 2025 and enacted in Bill C-4; the GST was waived on many newly built homes for first-time buyers, as platformed (Bill C-4, 2025; Budget 2025).
  • Defence: he promised to reach NATO's 2% target years ahead of the old schedule; on March 26, 2026 he announced Canada had achieved it, with over $63 billion spent — the largest year-over-year increase in generations — and NATO's 2026 reporting put Canada's core defence expenditure at 2.13% of GDP for 2026 (PMO announcement, March 26, 2026; NATO defence expenditure report, 2026). At The Hague (June 25, 2025) he signed the 5%-by-2035 pledge (3.5% core + 1.5% related) he had foreshadowed. Caveat on the shelf: the PBO notes the headline 2.01% figure is NATO's unaudited estimate and reads ~1.92% recomputed against actual fiscal-year GDP — the target was reached on NATO's measure, which is the measure the commitment was made in (PBO analysis, 2026).
  • Immigration: the platform promised temporary residents below 5% of population by end of 2027 and permanent admissions below 1%; the levels plans hold permanent admissions at 380,000, and the flows fell sharply (Test 4). Temporary residents stood at 6.7% of population on July 1, 2026 — not yet at target, on the trajectory the PBO projects will just meet it (Statistics Canada, July 2026; PBO projection, 2026).
  • Internal trade: he promised to eliminate federal barriers to interprovincial trade by Canada Day 2025; Part 1 of C-5 (the Free Trade and Labour Mobility in Canada Act) was law by June 26, 2025, with federal barriers removed on that schedule — the provincial half of the bargain proceeding by agreement thereafter (One Canadian Economy Act, 2025).

Evidence against (where his word moved):

  • Housing — the largest single gap. The campaign's headline promise (March 31, 2025) was to double homebuilding to roughly 500,000 homes a year, through a new $13-billion agency, Build Canada Homes. The record: starts at ~244,558 annualized through October 2026, down 3% on the year (CMHC, November 2026); the Parliamentary Budget Officer found (December 2, 2025) that BCH will add about 26,000 units over five years — roughly 5,200 a year — with no government plan set out for the rest of the 500,000 (PBO report, December 2, 2025). And in Budget 2026 (November 2026) the doubling target simply disappeared from the document, replaced by a plan to triple non-market construction to 24,000 starts a year; the housing minister's office, asked by The Canadian Press whether the doubling goal was abandoned, pointed to a six-month-old statement rather than answer (The Canadian Press via Building Canada, November 2026). The promise was not announced as broken; it was edited out.
  • Fiscal discipline: he campaigned against Trudeau's spending ("too much"), promising to spend less, shrink the deficit-to-GDP ratio, keep debt-to-GDP falling, and balance the operating budget by 2028–29. The record: a $66.9-billion deficit in 2025–26 and ~$65 billion projected for 2026–27; the PBO projects the deficit-to-GDP ratio rising to 2.0% in 2027–28 before declining, debt-to-GDP rising from 41.1% to 42.6% by 2030–31, and the operating-balance target missed by a year — under definitions the PBO calls unclear and contradictory (PBO reports, 2026; Budget 2025 and 2026 figures). The government disputes the PBO's definitions and says it will balance the operating budget a year early (Finance Minister's statements, September–November 2026) — CONTESTED as to measurement; the rising debt-to-GDP path in the government's own budget documents is not contested.
  • The U.S. relationship: he won the 2025 election on "elbows up" — no bad deal, leverage kept. The concessions ledger (Test 3) — digital tax scrapped, retaliation lifted, bridge revenue shared — was accumulated before any tariff relief was secured, and as of November 2026 no deal exists; his own November 1, 2026 statement claims only "the concept" of one (AP, November 1, 2026). His defenders note he also refused a deal in August on stated red lines; both are true, and the campaign promise was relief, not posture.
  • Major projects consent: in June 2025 he committed that Indigenous consultation and free, prior and informed consent were "central" to C-5 and that no project would proceed without affected First Nations' support; in October–November 2026 the Pacific Link designation stands while nine B.C. nations and Cold Lake First Nations litigate that they were not adequately consulted (prime ministerial statements, June 2025; Federal Court filings, October–November 2026). The courts will decide the legal question; the political promise and the litigation coexist on the record.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Twenty months in, Carney's file is the most institutionally intact in this series and the least settled: he kept the machinery of one law running — a real ethics screen, applied in public, with no finding against him and no documented decision for his old firm — while retaining a financial interest that machinery has to keep stepping around, and an office that resisted showing the screen's work until the Information Commissioner ordered it shown. He centralized real power in cabinet through the Building Canada Act and converted a minority into a majority by recruitment rather than election, yet he has left the central bank, the courts, and the media un-attacked, negotiated with provinces instead of overriding them, and changed a pipeline's route when a province and its First Nations held their line. He answered the trade war's wobble in both directions — concessions his critics call surrender and a walk-away his supporters call the red lines holding — and he named the world's rupture at Davos more honestly than any peer. What keeps his word at mixed rather than holds is the distance between two campaign numbers and the record: 500,000 homes a year promised, ~245,000 being built, the target quietly absent from his second budget; and fiscal discipline promised while debt-to-GDP rises on his own documents. No test could be failed on this record; none could be held, either. The tenure is young, and the grades are correctable in the open as the record grows.
