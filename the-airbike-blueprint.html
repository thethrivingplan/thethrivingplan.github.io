The Loop Drive, sat on — a bike that lifts on its own water

The
Airbike

A bike you sit on, lifted and driven by the Loop Drive water loop. The spine tank feeds the pump, the pump and Glide Chamber feed the Distributor, the Distributor feeds four vectorable nozzle clusters under the body — and the water comes back. Your lean is the steering. There are no jets, no fuel, and nothing hot: the machine in the video that sparked this sheet burns kerosene through turbines and costs $880,000 on preorder. This one runs on water and a battery, and refills from a lake, a river, or a hose.

§1

How it lifts

Water does the lifting. The rider does the choosing.

The pump draws from the spine tank and pushes the water through the Glide Chamber, which shapes the stream smooth and directed. The Distributor splits the flow across four nozzle clusters under the body. Pointed down, the jets lift the bike on the same water-jet lift that carries hydroflight riders today — that mechanism is proven, and it wears the badge. Vectored, the same jets lean the thrust and the bike travels.

The honest trade, printed at the same size as the promise: water lift is a low ride. This bike skims — inches to a few feet over water or ground, travelling at a brisk run, not a highway speed. It does not do what the kerosene jet bike does, and this sheet does not claim it does. What it does instead: no fuel to buy or spill, no hot exhaust, nothing spinning exposed, a refill anywhere there is water, and a failure that ends in a splash or a settle instead of a fire. The rider sits astride it like a motorcycle, feet on pegs, hands on the bar — a posture every rider already knows.

§2

The two forms

One bike, two ways to feed the loop.

Lake form — the open loop

Over water, the loop opens: the intake skirt under the body draws from the lake, river, or sea it skims, and the spent water returns straight down. The tank becomes a buffer instead of the whole supply, so the ride lasts as long as the battery does. This is the long form — the tourer. If the power dies over water, the sealed body floats and the bike becomes a small boat you sit on while you paddle, drift, or get towed in.

Anywhere form — the closed loop

Over land, the loop closes: the spine tank carries the supply and the catch skirt under the body catches the jetted water and funnels it home through the filter. The ride is low hops and glides over ground — a field, a beach above the waterline, a flooded street — measured by the tank and the battery together. It rides low because the skirt has to stay near the ground to catch; that is the shape of the form, stated plainly.

§3

Distributor & controls

Body first, hand second, menu never.

Weight-shift is the primary control. Lean forward, the Distributor answers with forward vector; lean back, it brakes and settles; lean to a side, it turns. The rider's body is the stick. The handlebar carries the rest: a twist effort dial (how hard the loop works), the mode switch, and the GO / settle control. Foot-peg pressure trims the fine balance, the way a rider's feet already talk to a bike.

Effort Control runs two ways. MANUAL: the rider's lean and peg pressure meter the vanes directly. AUTOMATED: the balance assist holds the bike level against gusts and wobbles — with instant human takeback: any deliberate bar input, any hard lean, and the machine is the rider's again in the same instant. The assist proposes; the rider disposes.

The dead-man line clips at the rider's belt. If the rider comes off, the line pulls, the loop spools down, and the bike settles straight onto its skirt and skids instead of flying on alone. It is jetski-proven practice, fitted from the first build, not an accessory.

§4

Tank, water & the soft fail

Five layers. A failure has to beat every one of them.

The spine tank runs along the body's centre line, low in the frame, so its weight is ballast that steadies the bike instead of topweight that fights it. The controller never spends the last fifth of the tank — the reserve is the settle-down supply, held by rule, not by the rider remembering.

The soft-fail stack, in order: (1) the reserve fifth of the tank, always there for the descent; (2) the catch skirt traps a cushion of water and air under the body on the way down; (3) the sealed, buoyant body — over water it floats, over land its belly is the crumple surface; (4) the splayed landing skids flex and spread the load; (5) the reserve cell runs the whole settle sequence with the main pack dead. Five layers, an odd count, no tie. And the working fluid is water: the worst spill this machine can have is getting wet.

§5

Parts — down to the screw

Every part carries its five answers. One drive opens all of it.

Body shell & frame

What it isBody shell & frame — carbon-composite bike body, sealed and buoyant, saddle and peg mounts moulded in

What it's forCarries the rider and every system; the flotation layer of the soft fail

How to get itMoulded composite shell over a foam core; saddle and bars are standard motorcycle fitments. Tools: mould, clamps, the one universal driver — composite shop, or a makeshift mould for the test rig

What it costsCarbon body-kit class ~$600–1,200 (found 28 Nov 2026)

Why it is what it isSealed and buoyant because Layer 3 of the fail stack is the body itself. Carbon because the tank's water is weight enough without a heavy shell

Spine Tank

What it isSpine Tank — sealed reservoir along the body's spine, dump valve, level sensor, centre hatch

What it's forBuffers the loop: feeds the pump, receives the return, carries the anywhere form's whole supply

How to get itRotomolded HDPE, food-grade seal; sensor press-fit; valve at the lowest point. Tools: hole saw, the one universal driver

What it costs20-gal class rotomolded / RV fresh-water tanks list ~$185–230 (found 27 Nov 2026)

Why it is what it isLow and centred along the spine, its weight is ballast. 20-gal class because it is a standard size any marine or RV supplier stocks

Pump

What it isPump — high-flow brushless electric pump, marine-rated, the Loop Drive's thrust circuit

What it's forMoves tank water through the Glide Chamber to the Distributor — the onboard pump that replaces the jetski and the jet turbine alike

How to get itBolted to the frame on universal-screw mounts, below the tank so it primes by gravity. Tools: universal driver

What it costsElectric water-jet / thruster class units list ~$900–1,400 (jet-thruster kits as found 27 Nov 2026)

Why it is what it isBrushless and marine-rated because it lives wet and runs long. Sized for flow, not pressure — lift is flow

Glide Chamber

What it isGlide Chamber — shaped duct between pump and Distributor

What it's forShapes the stream smooth and directed, so the water does part of the work (LD-01)

How to get itMolded polymer or carbon composite, two halves bonded, bore polished. Tools: mold from Fig. 1 profile, clamps — composite shop, or makeshift mold for the test rig

What it costsMolded polymer ~$120–300 in materials (found 27 Nov 2026)

Why it is what it isA wing for water: the shape multiplies what the push is worth, so a smaller pump lifts the same rider

Distributor

What it isDistributor — valve block + controller; splits flow across the nozzles, meters effort, runs balance assist

What it's forDirection and stability live here: the split is the steering answer to the rider's lean

How to get itProportional valves plumbed in line; board in a sealed box; tilt / height / wind sensors. Tools: universal driver, laptop for calibration (borrow or library machine is fine)

What it costsValves + controller + drone-class sensor kit ~$350–700 (found 27 Nov 2026)

Why it is what it isOne decider for flow keeps the four nozzle clusters honest with each other — four independent pumps would fight

Nozzle clusters ×4 + vanes + guards

What it isNozzle clusters ×4 + vanes + guards — vectorable nozzle clusters under the body, trim vane in each, guard grille on each

What it's forThe output: jets point down for lift, vector for travel; vanes trim; guards keep everything out of the jet

How to get itStainless / polymer nozzles on ball-joint mounts, threaded into Distributor outlets; guards on universal screws. Tools: universal driver, thread seal tape

What it costsNozzles + vanes + guards, set of four ~$240–480 (marine jet suppliers, found 27 Nov 2026)

Why it is what it isFour, at the corners of the belly: the widest stable base, and opposite-corner balancing kills tilt. Guards are a working backup — never deleted

Catch skirt + intake

What it isCatch skirt + intake — wide flexible skirt under the body, funnel neck to the tank return; in lake form the intake face opens to the water below

What it's forCatches the jetted water over hard surfaces and funnels it home, closing the loop onboard; over water, feeds the loop from the lake

How to get itHeavy marine vinyl / Hypalon-class fabric over a flexible hoop; bolts on and off on universal-screw mounts. Tools: universal driver, fabric shears, marine adhesive

What it costsFabric + hoop + fittings ~$150–280 (found 27 Nov 2026)

Why it is what it isThe skirt is both the recycling system and Layer 2 of the fail stack — one part, two jobs, either of which earns its place alone

Return / catch lines + filter + dump valve

What it isReturn / catch lines + filter + dump valve — hose from catch and intake back through filter to tank

What it's forCloses the loop: spent water comes home instead of being spent; the filter keeps grit out of the pump

How to get itFlexible marine hose, standard diameters, hose-clamped under the body. Tools: universal driver, clamps

What it costsHose + filter + valve + clamps ~$50–110 (any marine / hardware supplier)

Why it is what it isStandard diameters only — a hose you can buy anywhere is a hose you can fix anywhere

Saddle, bars & controls set

What it isSaddle, bars & controls set — motorcycle saddle, handlebar with twist effort dial and mode switch, GO / settle control, foot pegs, dead-man line + kill switch at the belt clip

What it's forThe rider's whole interface: posture, lean, effort, mode, and the line that stops it all

How to get itStandard motorcycle fitments on universal-screw mounts; controls wired through the sealed body gland. Tools: universal driver, multimeter (borrow)

What it costsSaddle + bars + controls + line & switch ~$180–360 (found 27–28 Nov 2026)

Why it is what it isA bike posture because riders already know it — nothing new to learn at the moment it matters. The dead-man line is jetski-proven practice, fitted from the first build

Landing skids

What it isLanding skids — two splayed flex skids under the body, replaceable pads

What it's forGround contact: parking, the last of the landing, Layer 4 of the fail stack

How to get itAluminium tube on flex mounts, universal screws throughout. Tools: universal driver, tube bender (rental counter or borrow)

What it costsTube stock + pads + mounts ~$60–120 (hardware / marine suppliers)

Why it is what it isSplayed wide like the jet bike's legs, but sprung: they give, so the landing doesn't have to be perfect to be soft

Power pack + reserve cell

What it isPower pack + reserve cell — sealed Li-ion main pack, one standard connector; small reserve cell for the settle sequence

What it's forMain pack runs pump and controller; reserve runs Layer 5 when the main is flat

How to get itSlides into the body bay, latched; reserve cell sealed beside the Distributor. Tools: universal driver only

What it costsMain pack (e-bike / EV class) ~$400–800; reserve cell ~$40–80 (found 27 Nov 2026)

Why it is what it isOne standard connector, standard pack sizes — charge anywhere, swap anywhere. The reserve exists because the decider layer must answer with the main pack dead

Fasteners

What it isFasteners — THE UNIVERSAL SCREW — one drive opens every panel, mount, guard, and cover on the bike

What it's forSo one driver builds, services, and rescues the whole machine

How to get itStainless; sizes scale by load, the drive never changes. Tool: the one universal driver — hardware store, ~$10–15, or any tool library

What it costsFull-bike fastener set ~$30–60

Why it is what it isFifty screwdrivers is fifty ways to be stuck. One drive, everywhere, always

§6

Hitting GO

So covered that just hitting go works.

Before the rider twists the effort dial, the bike has already answered for itself: tank full and the reserve fifth sealed by the controller; skirt fitted for the form — intake open over water, catch closed over land; dead-man line clipped at the belt; Distributor self-test green across all four clusters; guards on. The rider's one act is the choice — the 5 — and the machine carries 1 through 4. If any check fails, GO does not arm. That is not a limitation; that is the design keeping its word.
