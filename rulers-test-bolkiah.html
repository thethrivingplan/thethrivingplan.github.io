The Ruler's Test — Hassanal Bolkiah, Sultan of Brunei

Subject: Sultan Haji Hassanal Bolkiah Mu'izzaddin Waddaulah — Sultan and Yang Di-Pertuan of Brunei Darussalam since 5 October 1967, when his father, Sultan Omar Ali Saifuddien III, abdicated in his favour. The seat graded is the sultanate, and the reason is stated plainly: in Brunei the Sultan holds executive power absolutely. He is simultaneously head of state, Prime Minister (since independence from Britain on 1 January 1984), Minister of Defence and commander-in-chief, and Minister of Finance and Economy; he held the foreign-affairs portfolio himself from 2015 until June 2026, and he is head of the official religion. There is no elected counterweight to grade instead: no national elections have been held since 1962, and the Legislative Council is appointed by him and consultative only. The premise was verified for November 2026 and no correction is needed: the CIA World Leaders listing updated 5 November 2026 still names Hassanal Bolkiah as both Sultan and Prime Minister, and Britannica's country facts, updated 23 November 2026, name him head of state and government (CIA World Leaders, 5 November 2026; Britannica, updated 23 November 2026). Born 15 July 1946, he turned 80 in July 2026 and is the world's longest-reigning current monarch. His mandate is hereditary succession under the Malay Islamic Monarchy (Melayu Islam Beraja, MIB) national philosophy, continued under a state of emergency first declared in 1962 — before his reign — and renewed throughout it; he has never faced an electorate, so under the mandate rule he is graded on the holding, and on his own stated word — his royal addresses (titah), the Wawasan Brunei 2035 vision he launched, and the commitments his government has made in his name — never on the absence of a platform he never ran on. He is graded on a reign of 59 years, not a single term, and the file's depth reflects that: the record is long, and where it is thin — the finances of the Brunei Investment Agency (BIA) and of the royal household are not publicly audited — the thinness is stated where it matters. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test, printed in full: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them, and conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Every test closes with the reason for its grade in plain words and a ledger naming who was helped and who was hurt. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes: a kept word that hurt people earns its Test 5 credit and carries its hurt in the ledger in full, and a broken word that helped people is recorded the same way. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Day-to-day public-sector corruption is, by the region's standard measure, low. Brunei scored 63 of 100 in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025 (31st of 182, released February 2026), second in Southeast Asia only to Singapore (84), up from 60 the last time it was scored in 2020 (Transparency International, CPI 2025). Freedom House's 2023 assessment scored Brunei 3 of 4 on whether safeguards against official corruption are strong and effective (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2023).
  • The one great corruption case of the reign was pursued against the Sultan's own brother. After the 1997 Asian financial crisis, the Sultan ordered external audits of the BIA, which his younger brother Prince Jefri Bolkiah had chaired as finance minister (1986–1997); the audits alleged some £8 billion (about USD 14–15 billion in contemporary reporting) had been misappropriated through the BIA and Prince Jefri's Amedeo group of companies — an ALLEGATION the state then litigated for a decade. In the 2000 settlement Prince Jefri agreed to return assets valued at about £3 billion; the Privy Council in London ruled for the BIA against him on the procedural and enforcement issues in 2007 ([2007] UKPC 62); contempt proceedings against him were dropped in 2006 after asset transfers, and he later returned to Brunei and to ceremonial royal life. The state did not simply bury its own family's case (Privy Council judgment, 8 November 2007; contemporaneous Reuters and UK press reporting, 2006).
  • The ordinary criminal law is enforced against officials as well as subjects, including inside the coercive apparatus: the US State Department's 2026 Trafficking in Persons report records that the government investigated and detained four immigration officials for allegedly using counterfeit and official stamps to facilitate immigration violations — recorded here as an investigation of an ALLEGATION, not a verdict (US State Department, TIP Report 2026).
  • One welfare law genuinely does run for every citizen alike (Test 4): free healthcare and education, no personal income tax, and heavy subsidies on fuel, electricity, water, rice and sugar are not rationed by loyalty, faction, or region (Brunei country risk reporting, March 2025; fiscal reporting on the 2025/2026 budget).

Evidence against:

  • At the source itself there are two laws, written into the constitution. Article 84B, inserted by the 2004 amendments the Sultan himself approved, provides that the Sultan "can do no wrong in either his personal or any official capacity" and "shall not be liable to any proceedings whatsoever in any court" for anything done or omitted during or after his reign (Constitution of Brunei Darussalam, Article 84B, as amended 2004). The ruler is not merely unprosecuted in practice; he is unprosecutable by law, while every subject beneath him is fully exposed. Test 1 grades the source, and at the source the exemption is total.
  • The state's money and the family's money have never been publicly separated. The Sultan is finance minister, the BIA — the sovereign investment vehicle — publishes no audited accounts, and no public accounting distinguishes state assets from royal assets; the Delaware Court of Chancery, in litigation arising from the Jefri feud, described the BIA as an agency the Sultan "solely dominates and controls" (Delaware Court of Chancery, Zaman v. Derbyshire, memorandum opinion, 23 May 2008). Estimates of his personal fortune — USD 30 billion in much reporting, USD 50 billion in a 2026 ranking cited in reference works — are printed here as estimates only, because no audited figure exists; that no audited figure exists is itself the finding (reference and press estimates, 2026).
  • The Jefri case, the strongest item on the for shelf, also cuts the other way on its ending: it was resolved by settlement, asset return, and dropped contempt proceedings — never by a criminal verdict against the prince, who resumed his place in the royal family — while caning is mandatory for 42 ordinary criminal offences, including immigration violations, and is commonly carried out against ordinary offenders (US State Department human rights reporting; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2011, summarising State Department reporting). Restitution for a prince; the cane for an immigration offender. That is a second law operating inside the reign's largest case.
  • The June 2026 cabinet reshuffle distributed the state's senior offices inside the family by appointment. On 4 June 2026 the Sultan appointed his son Prince Abdul Mateen foreign minister — a portfolio he had held himself since 2015 — and his son Prince Abdul Malik to his first cabinet post, as Minister in the Prime Minister's Office; Crown Prince Al-Muhtadee Billah retained the senior minister post at the Prime Minister's Office, and the Sultan kept the premiership, defence, and finance for himself (Reuters, 4 June 2026). Whatever the princes' individual qualifications — Prince Abdul Mateen holds degrees from King's College London and SOAS and a military commission — no non-family candidate could compete for these posts, because no one outside the palace is eligible for them at all.
  • Family status itself is held and removed by personal decree: in August 2026 the Sultan stripped his daughter-in-law, Pengiran Raabi'atul Adawiyyah, wife of Prince Abdul Malik, of all her titles, stating her conduct was "unbecoming" of a member of the royal family — a personal ruling, with no published process or appeal (Brunei Country Risk Report, October 2026).

Why this grade: The ordinary machinery of Bruneian law is comparatively clean, and the state once sued its own prince — but Test 1 grades the source, and at the source the law is written in two tiers: a constitution that declares the ruler incapable of wrong and beyond any court, an unaudited fusion of state and royal wealth, restitution-without-verdict for a royal offender against mandatory caning for common ones, and senior office distributed within one family. One law for everyone below the throne; no law at all above it. That is the definition of the failure this test names.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — ordinary citizens, who live under a low-corruption administration by regional measures (CPI 2025); the state treasury, which recovered billions in assets in the BIA settlement. Hurt — every subject who can be prosecuted, caned, or detained under laws from which one man is constitutionally exempt; taxpayers and citizens as owners of the BIA's assets, whose holdings have never been publicly audited; non-royal Bruneians of ability, for whom the top offices of their own country are closed by birth.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The courts are not shown, on the documented record, taking orders in ordinary cases. Freedom House records that although all senior judges are appointed by the Sultan and the constitution does not specifically provide for judicial independence, "the court system operates without government interference" in practice, and the courts "appear to act independently when handling civil matters" (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024; Brunei Country Risk Report, October 2026). Final appeal lies to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London, outside the Sultan's own court structure, and it ruled on the merits in the BIA–Jefri litigation.
  • The appointed Legislative Council does perform a real, if limited, scrutiny function: it formally passes the state budget, holds question-and-answer sessions with ministers, and elicits written answers that enter the public record — including, in 2025–2026, answers disclosing that 82,013 foreign workers were employed in the private sector, that 1,111 business names and 720 companies closed between 2021 and 2025, and that the government keeps no integrated database of citizens working overseas (Legislative Council written answers, August 2025, March 2026 and August 2026, as reported).
  • The monetary authority operates a long-standing, rules-based arrangement — the Brunei dollar's one-to-one interchangeability with the Singapore dollar under the 1967 Currency Interchangeability Agreement — which has held across the whole reign and constrains discretionary monetary manipulation by any finance minister, including this one.
  • No journalist, judge, or official is documented in the record assembled here as having been imprisoned for a single act of institutional independence during the recent period; the grip described below works structurally, through appointment, licensing, and law, rather than through a documented purge.

Evidence against:

  • Every referee is his appointment. All senior judges are appointed by the Sultan (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024). The Legislative Council's members are all appointed by him and hold their seats at his pleasure; the council was suspended entirely from 1984 to 2004 and exists in its present form by his proclamation (Constitution of Brunei Darussalam; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2023). There is no electoral commission to keep autonomous, because there are no national elections: none have been held since 1962, and Freedom House scores the questions "was the head of government elected through free and fair elections" and "were legislative representatives elected" at 0 of 4 each (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2023 and 2026).
  • The state of emergency under which he rules has run, unbroken, since 1962 — renewed throughout his 59-year reign. Under it, the Sultan may make "any Orders whatsoever which he considers desirable in the public interest," including modifying or suspending any written law (Constitution, Article 83(3)). A referee structure that exists only while an emergency proclamation — renewed by the player himself — says so is not held at arm's length; it is held in the hand.
  • The prosecution and the central financial referees sit inside his own portfolios: he is Prime Minister (to whom the Attorney General's Chambers answer), Minister of Finance and Economy, and Minister of Defence. The authority that would investigate the finance ministry is subordinate to the finance minister.
  • The media are gripped by law and by ownership. The government may close a newspaper without prior notice or showing cause; journalists face fines and up to three years' imprisonment for reporting deemed "false and malicious"; the country's only television station is state-run; the main English-language daily, the Borneo Bulletin, is controlled by the Sultan's family, and its journalists practise self-censorship (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024; US State Department, Brunei 2024 Human Rights Report). The Brunei Times closed abruptly in 2016, allegedly after complaints from the Saudi embassy over its hajj coverage — an ALLEGATION about the cause, recorded as such; the closure itself is documented (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022).
  • Expression itself has no constitutional protection: there is no provision for freedom of speech in the constitution or law; the Sedition Act makes it an offence to challenge the royal family's authority or "the standing or prominence" of the MIB philosophy; online communications are monitored and an informant system watches for dissent (US State Department, Brunei 2024 Human Rights Report; CIVICUS Monitor, 2025; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024). In 2019 a former civil servant became the first person convicted under the Sedition Act, over statements criticising the government's halal certification policy; he subsequently fled the country (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2022). The individual is not named here: he was a private citizen below the public eye, and his conviction is documented — the naming rule's exception does not require naming him to record the fact.
  • Detention without trial remains available to the executive: persons detained under the Internal Security Act lack due process rights including the presumption of innocence (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024).
  • The aggregate measures state the result plainly: Freedom House rates Brunei "Not Free" at 27 of 100 in Freedom in the World 2026 — political rights 7 of 40, civil liberties 20 of 60, unchanged from the prior year (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026). CIVICUS rates Brunei's civic space "repressed" (CIVICUS Monitor, August 2025).

Why this grade: A ruler holds without gripping when the referees could rule against him and survive it. In Brunei no referee is positioned to do so: judges, legislators, prosecutors, the finance ministry, and the licensing power over every newspaper trace back to one man's appointment, operating under an emergency he renews himself. The honest mitigation — courts left alone in ordinary civil work, a council that asks real questions — describes a grip that does not need to squeeze daily, not a grip that has been released.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the palace itself, which has governed for 59 years without an institutional veto; litigants in ordinary civil and commercial disputes, whose courts function without documented interference. Hurt — journalists and editors, who work under closure-without-cause and self-censor as a condition of working at all; citizens, who have no elected representative, no protected speech, and no court that can hear a case against the head of the state; anyone detained under the Internal Security Act without the presumption of innocence.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, when another option became available, he at least considered it openly: considering it and declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it at all is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clearest wobble of the reign was answered with a real, public adjustment. When the final phase of the Syariah Penal Code Order (SPCO) took effect on 3 April 2019 — prescribing death by stoning for adultery and for same-sex acts between men, and amputation for theft — the international pushback was immediate: condemnation from the United Nations and Western governments, and a boycott campaign against the Dorchester Collection hotels owned by the BIA, led publicly by George Clooney and joined by Elton John and Ellen DeGeneres, among others. On 5 May 2019, in a televised address at the start of Ramadan, the Sultan answered in person: he acknowledged "many questions and misperceptions" about the code, and announced that Brunei's de facto moratorium on the death penalty — in place for cases under the common law — "will also be applied to cases under the [Syariah Penal Code]" (Sultan's Ramadan address, 5 May 2019, as reported by Reuters, AFP and the New York Times, May 2019). The moratorium has held: no execution has been carried out in Brunei since 1957, and the US State Department confirms the 2019 de facto moratorium remained in place in its recent reporting (US State Department, International Religious Freedom reporting on Brunei). Another option became available — non-enforcement — and he took it, openly, in his own voice.
  • The same pattern, slower, preceded it: the SPCO was announced in 2013 and its harshest phases were repeatedly delayed after the first wave of international criticism in 2014, with full implementation deferred five years to 2019 — delay as a form of partial adjustment, recorded as such (contemporaneous reporting, 2014–2019).
  • On economic wobbles he has adjusted policy rather than denying the problem. After the 2014–2016 oil price collapse and again through the weak-growth years of the 2020s, the government's stated answer was diversification: the Economic Blueprint under Wawasan Brunei 2035, five priority sectors (downstream oil and gas, food, ICT, tourism, services), and, in June 2026, a cabinet restructure creating three coordinating minister posts for national security, the economy, and social policy, with the Second Minister of Finance and Economy stating publicly in August 2025, "We must accelerate efforts toward economic diversification, particularly by reducing our dependence on the oil and gas sector" (Reuters, 4 June 2026; AMRO, 2026; Department of Councils of State reporting, August 2025).
  • The June 2026 reshuffle is itself an answer to a wobble — succession and capacity questions around an 80-year-old ruler who had undergone knee replacement surgery in January 2026 and made limited public appearances — answered openly, on television, with named appointments and four-year terms rather than silence (Reuters, 4 June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The 2019 adjustment stopped exactly at the point of maximum pressure and no further. The moratorium is a personal declaration, revocable at any time by the same authority that made it; the stoning, amputation, and whipping provisions remain in force in law, and the Sultan's address defended the code's merit while extending the moratorium — "once these [misperceptions] have been cleared, the merit of the law will be evident" (Sultan's address, 5 May 2019, as reported). Rights organisations' assessment at the time — that the move was "all about trying to abate the international pressure" (Phil Robertson, Human Rights Watch, May 2019) — is recorded as an interested critic's characterisation, not a finding; the documented facts are the extension, the defence, and the retention, and they are mixed facts.
  • On the domestic wobbles — the ones with no foreign boycott behind them — the record shows refusal to look. At Brunei's fourth Universal Periodic Review (review November 2024, response adopted March 2025), the government declined to support recommendations to ratify the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, to protect peaceful assembly, and to establish an independent national human rights institution meeting the Paris Principles (UN Human Rights Council, UPR fourth cycle, 2024–2025; CIVICUS Monitor, August 2025). These were options presented formally, on the record, by other states; they were not considered-and-declined with stated reasons in any published response — they were simply not supported. Under this test's rule, refusing to look is the against-evidence, and it is documented.
  • The foundational wobble of the state — the 1962 emergency — has never been answered at all. Sixty-four years on, and fifty-nine into this reign, the emergency proclamation is still renewed (the Attorney General's Chambers' own listing records Proclamations of Emergency continuing through S 16/2026) rather than lifted, debated to a conclusion, or replaced with ordinary law (Attorney General's Chambers, constitutional instruments listing, 2026). The 2004–2005 opening on elected Legislative Council seats (Test 5) was likewise never revisited after it stalled: no date set, no reasons published, no alternative offered.
  • The no-good-choice clause was considered for this test and does not apply. The situations above were not traps with only harmful doors: the SPCO was the Sultan's own announced project (2013), the emergency is renewed by his own signature, and the UPR recommendations cost nothing to consider. Where a ruler builds the situation himself, the clause's protection does not attach — its mechanics grade the situation's origin as part of the record, and the origin here is the seat itself.

Why this grade: He has demonstrably bent when the pushback was loud, foreign, and expensive — the 2019 moratorium extension was a genuine, personal, public adjustment, and it has held for seven years. He has just as demonstrably refused to look when the pushback was quiet, domestic, or procedural — UPR recommendations declined without stated reasons, an emergency renewed into its seventh decade, an elections opening left to die in silence. Both halves are documented; neither cancels the other.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — every person who would face capital charges under the SPCO, above all those accused of adultery or same-sex acts, who live under a law that is not enforced against their lives; Brunei's hotel and investment interests, spared a widening boycott. Hurt — the same accused persons, in a different register: the provisions remain law, revocable protection is not repeal, and whipping and amputation were never covered by the moratorium at all; domestic reform advocates and the states that made UPR recommendations, whose offered options were declined without engagement.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the minorities, the stateless, the foreign workforce, women, and those whose lives the law disapproves of — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • For citizens, the counting is real and unusually complete. The oil and gas revenue the Sultan's state controls has been distributed to citizens as a comprehensive welfare settlement maintained across the whole reign: basic medical and dental care at no charge (a nominal BND 1 registration fee in current reporting), free education through university with state scholarships and allowances, no personal income tax and no sales or capital-gains tax, heavily subsidised fuel (RON 97 at about BND 0.53 per litre), electricity, water, rice and sugar, and housing through the National Housing Scheme on subsidised rent-to-own terms (Brunei country risk reporting, March 2025; fiscal reporting on the 2025/2026 budget; Brunei social-policy reporting, 2026). Citizens needing treatment unavailable in Brunei have been flown abroad at state expense.
  • The welfare reaches minorities who are citizens: non-Muslim citizens practise their faiths in registered churches and temples, and the US State Department records non-Muslim and minority-Muslim reports of no significant change in their ability to practise since the SPCO's full implementation in 2019 (US State Department, International Religious Freedom reporting on Brunei). Ethnic Chinese, Iban, Dusun and other communities are present in public and commercial life; village-level councils are elected, though candidates are government-vetted (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024).
  • Some movement on the stateless file is documented: 1,975 stateless persons gained citizenship between 2017 and 2021, and UNHCR has welcomed the government's cooperation on statistics and naturalisation in earlier cycles (Statelessness Encyclopedia Asia Pacific, 2023, citing UNHCR Global Trends).
  • Women's presence at the top of government, while entirely by his appointment, has grown on his watch: the first woman cabinet minister, Hajah Romaizah, was appointed in June 2022, and the June 2026 reshuffle brought the number of women in cabinet to four, the most in Brunei's history (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2023; June 2026 reshuffle reporting).

Evidence against:

  • A whole population born in the country is counted out of citizenship by the law's design. Brunei's Nationality Act grants nationality by operation of law on the basis of race — membership of the "indigenous groups of the Malay race" or of groups listed in the Act's First Schedule. The largest stateless group is people of Chinese descent, many from families resident for generations (UNHCR submission to the UPR; UN Human Rights Council compilation, A/HRC/WG.6/47/BRN/2, August 2024). The counts conflict and are printed separately, as sourced: Brunei reported 20,863 stateless persons to UNHCR for 2022 — a figure unchanged across 2019–2022 — while a US State Department report in 2012 suggested the true figure could be as high as 150,000, and the State Department's 2022 report states that recent statistics on the stateless population are unreliable (Statelessness Encyclopedia Asia Pacific, 2023, citing UNHCR Global Trends and US State Department reports). On either figure, stateless permanent residents cannot hold a Brunei passport, cannot own land, property, or a business, and are excluded from fully subsidised healthcare and higher education; some who pass the required Malay language and culture tests still wait five to ten years without a grant (Statelessness Encyclopedia Asia Pacific, 2023). From 2025, free healthcare ceased to apply to certain non-citizens and expatriates, who must now pay or insure (Brunei social-policy reporting, 2026).
  • The nationality law discriminates by gender on its face: a Bruneian man's children receive citizenship automatically, while a Bruneian woman's children require a registration process to be recognised; women are likewise restricted in conferring nationality on foreign husbands where men are not. Brunei maintains its reservation to Article 9(2) of CEDAW — the equal-nationality-rights article — and UNHCR recommends withdrawing it (UN Human Rights Council compilation, August 2024; Statelessness Encyclopedia Asia Pacific, 2023).
  • The foreign workforce that builds and services the country — 82,013 foreign workers in the private sector alone on the government's own August 2026 Legislative Council answer, and around 100,000 in total on the US State Department's 2025 Trafficking in Persons account — is counted as labour, not as people with claims. The State Department records that these workers, in domestic work, construction, services and agriculture, often face passport confiscation, wage withholding, and high recruitment fees; Brunei was placed on the Tier 2 Watch List in the 2025 TIP Report, with no convictions under its anti-trafficking law for eight consecutive years at that point, victims formally identified only when a prosecution followed, and some potential victims penalised for immigration or sex-work offences committed as a result of being trafficked. The 2026 TIP Report records improvement — upgrade to Tier 2, the first anti-trafficking convictions since 2016, 22 cases investigated — and both reports are printed here because both are the record (US State Department, TIP Reports 2025 and 2026).
  • Religious counting stops at the state's list. The government bans faiths it deems "deviant," including the Ahmadiyya Muslim Community, the Baha'i Faith, and Jehovah's Witnesses; non-Muslims may not proselytise; public celebration of other religions is restricted — the government interprets the SPCO as prohibiting public Christmas displays, and Chinese New Year lion dances are confined to temples, community facilities, and residences (US State Department, International Religious Freedom reporting and Brunei 2024 Human Rights Report). Converts from Islam face meetings with religious counsellors arranged by the Ministry of Religious Affairs, and leaving Islam is punishable under the SPCO (Open Doors, World Watch List 2026).
  • Those whose intimate lives the code disapproves of are counted as offenders: consensual same-sex acts carry death by stoning in the SPCO's text (unenforced under the 2019 moratorium) and whipping in force; women convicted of lesbian sex face whipping. The moratorium covers executions only (SPCO, 2013; US State Department and contemporaneous reporting, 2019).
  • Youth are counted in the welfare and discounted in the economy: the youth unemployment rate was 18.23% in 2025 (ILO-modelled series), and the government's own Labour Force Survey 2025 puts unemployment among 18-to-24-year-olds at about 18%, with 55.2% of the unemployed searching for more than twelve months (Department of Economic Planning and Statistics, Labour Force Survey 2025; FRED/ILO series, 2025).

Why this grade: The for shelf is not token — for the roughly four-fifths of residents who are citizens, this is one of the most complete welfare countings on earth, and it has lasted six decades. But the test asks whether everyone is counted, and in Brunei the exclusions are not oversights; they are written into the nationality law's racial schedule, the CEDAW reservation, the banned-faiths list, the SPCO's offences, and the labour regime for a hundred thousand foreign workers. A count that stops, by law, at the citizen line — and within it, at the Malay-Muslim line — fails this test even when what it does inside the line is generous.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Bruneian citizens of all ethnicities, who receive free healthcare and education, subsidised food, fuel and housing, and pay no income tax; the 1,975 stateless residents naturalised between 2017 and 2021; trafficking victims reached by the improved 2026 enforcement. Hurt — stateless permanent residents, predominantly of Chinese descent, born in Brunei and denied passports, land, and full subsidies across generations; Bruneian women, whose nationality passes to their children and husbands on lesser terms than men's; the foreign workforce of roughly 100,000, exposed to passport confiscation and fee-charging with, until 2026, no trafficking convictions in eight years; members of banned faiths and converts from Islam; gay and lesbian Bruneians, who live under whipping provisions in force and capital provisions in suspension.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Royal addresses, the Wawasan Brunei 2035 vision, and public commitments vs. the record, with dates. He never stood for election, so under the mandate rule there is no platform to grade — only the word he actually gave, in titah, in law, and through his government.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The welfare settlement — kept for six decades. The standing promise of the reign, restated across his titah and embodied in every budget, is that oil wealth reaches citizens as free services and low living costs. It has been kept continuously: free healthcare and education, no personal income tax, and heavy subsidies stand in the 2025/2026 budget, in which oil and gas still supplied about 75% of projected revenue of BND 3.26 billion (fiscal reporting on the 2025/2026 budget; Brunei country risk reporting, March 2025). This is crossing 1 in its purest form: word kept, and it helped people.
  • The moratorium word — kept since given. On 5 May 2019 he stated the death-penalty moratorium would extend to SPCO cases. Seven years on, no execution has occurred — none has occurred since 1957 — and the US State Department confirms the moratorium remains in place (US State Department reporting; May 2019 address as reported). The crossing here is double, and both axes are recorded independently: against the letter of his own 2013 commitment to implement the code in full, the non-enforcement is a departure from his word — and it helped people, sparing every person who would otherwise face a capital SPCO charge (crossing 3). The keeping of the 2019 word itself is crossing 1.
  • Wawasan Brunei 2035 — in motion, measurably, on the diversification limb. The vision he launched in 2007–2008 promises by 2035 an educated, highly skilled people, a top-ten quality of life, and "a dynamic and sustainable economy" with GDP per capita among the world's top ten (Wawasan Brunei 2035; WTO Trade Policy Review, WT/TPR/G/464, October 2024). With nine years left: non-oil-and-gas exports rose from 11% of total exports in 2017 to more than 60% in 2025; GDP per capita remains among the world's highest (about USD 32,235 nominal in 2025 on World Bank figures; about USD 77,000 at PPP in 2024 on IMF figures — two different measures, printed separately) (AMRO, 2026; AERI Investment Guide 2026, citing World Bank and IMF data). The machinery — Economic Blueprint, Digital Economy Masterplan, Twelfth National Development Plan — exists and is staffed, most recently by the June 2026 coordinating ministers.
  • The SPCO itself — a word kept that hurt people, recorded as such. In October 2013 he announced the sharia penal code as his project, and despite five years of delay he did bring it fully into force on 3 April 2019. The keeping earns its place on this shelf; the hurt is not laundered by the keeping — it is carried in full in Test 4's ledger: whipping in force for same-sex acts and other offences, capital provisions suspended but unrepealed (crossing 2) (SPCO, 2013; contemporaneous reporting, 2013–2019).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The elected-council opening — the reign's clearest unkept political word. In 2004 the Sultan reconvened the Legislative Council after twenty years and announced that the next parliament would include elected seats; the council approved a constitutional amendment expanding it to 45 seats, 15 of them elected, and in 2004 he announced that for the next parliament 15 of 20 seats would be elected (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2007 and 2009; Legislative Council history). In September 2005 he dissolved that council and convened a new, appointed one. Twenty-one years later: no election date has ever been set, no direct legislative election has been held since 1962, and the council remains wholly appointed. This is crossing 4: the word was broken, and the hurt is the continuing absence of any elected voice at national level (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2026).
  • Wawasan's economy limb — receding on its own terms. The vision's test is a dynamic, sustainable economy, not merely a rich one. The record: oil and gas still account for around 90% of total exports on May 2026 country-risk figures and about 75% of government revenue in the 2025/2026 budget; much of the celebrated rise in "non-oil" exports is downstream petrochemicals — hydrocarbons one step processed, as AMRO itself notes; real GDP growth was 4.2% in 2024, 0.7% in 2025, and is projected at about 1.9% in 2026 (IMF World Economic Outlook, October 2026, as reported); youth unemployment stands at about 18% (AMRO, 2026; Department of Economic Planning and Statistics, 2025; country risk reporting, May 2026). The 2035 deadline has not arrived, so this is recorded as receding, not yet broken — but on the current trajectory the "dynamic and sustainable" limb, as distinct from the "rich" limb, will not be met.
  • The anti-trafficking word — kept late. Brunei's Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act dates from 2019; the US State Department records no convictions under the anti-trafficking law for eight consecutive years through its 2025 report, before the first convictions since 2016 arrived in the 2026 reporting period (US State Department, TIP Reports 2025 and 2026). A law on the books for eight years without a conviction is a commitment in suspension; the 2026 convictions move it toward kept, and both facts stand.
  • Not documented: no instance was found in the record assembled here of the Sultan making a dated, personal public promise on the stateless-nationality question — in either direction. Under the two-axes rule this is recorded as not documented, not filled in: his governments have naturalised individuals (Test 4) and maintained the racial schedule in law, but a personal word given or broken on the file itself is not in evidence, and this dossier does not invent one.

Why this grade: His word divides cleanly by subject. Where the promise is material — welfare, subsidies, no executions — it has been kept for decades and can be checked in every budget. Where the promise is political — elected seats announced in 2004 — it was quietly abandoned without ever being withdrawn, and where the promise is transformational — a dynamic, diversified economy by 2035 — the trajectory, nine years out, points the other way. All four crossings of the two axes occur in this one reign and are named above: kept-and-helped (the welfare settlement, the moratorium), kept-and-hurt (the SPCO implemented as announced), departed-from-and-helped (the code's capital provisions left unenforced), and broken-and-hurt (the elected council that never met).

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — citizens, by a welfare word kept across six decades and two oil shocks; persons spared capital exposure under the SPCO by the 2019 word; exporters and workers in the new downstream and services sectors. Hurt — citizens again, in their capacity as political subjects, by the 2004 elections word that produced no election in twenty-one years; young Bruneians, 18% of whom are unemployed inside an economy whose transformation was promised to them; trafficking victims during the eight years the 2019 Act went unconvicted under.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Hassanal Bolkiah has held one seat for 59 years with no election, no opposition, and no court that can hear a case against him, and the dossier's grades follow from where that power was and was not restrained by his own hand. On one law, the failure is constitutional, not anecdotal: Article 84B declares that he can do no wrong and is liable to no proceedings, the state and royal fortunes have never been publicly separated or audited, and the reign's biggest corruption case ended in restitution for a prince while ordinary offenders face mandatory caning — helped: citizens under a comparatively clean administration (CPI 63 of 100, 2025); hurt: every subject who lives under laws the lawgiver is exempt from. On gripping, the failure is structural: judges, legislators, prosecutors, the finance portfolio, and newspaper licensing all trace to his appointment under an emergency he has renewed since 1967, mitigated only by courts left alone in ordinary civil work and a council that asks real questions — hurt: journalists, detainees without presumption of innocence, and citizens with no elected voice, in a country Freedom House scores 27 of 100, Not Free. On the wobble, the record is genuinely two-sided: he answered the 2019 international backlash in person and extended the death-penalty moratorium to the sharia code — a real adjustment that has held for seven years — while declining, without stated reasons, the UPR's offered options on civil rights, assembly, and a human-rights institution, and renewing a 1962 emergency into its seventh decade; the no-good-choice clause does not protect him here, because the traps in question were of his own making. On counting everyone, the generosity inside the citizen line — free healthcare and education, no income tax, deep subsidies, kept for six decades — does not reach the people the law itself counts out: a stateless population reported at 20,863 by the government to UNHCR for 2022 (with a US estimate as high as 150,000 printed separately, not blended), mostly of Chinese descent and barred from passports, land, and full subsidies; women, whose nationality passes on lesser terms under a maintained CEDAW reservation; some 100,000 foreign workers exposed to passport confiscation and recruitment fees; banned faiths; and gay and lesbian Bruneians under whipping provisions in force. On his word, the material promises were kept and the political one was not: welfare and the moratorium stand in every budget and in a zero-execution record stretching back to 1957, while the elected Legislative Council seats announced in 2004 have produced no election in twenty-one years, and Wawasan Brunei 2035's diversified-economy limb — oil still near 90% of exports, youth unemployment near 18%, growth of 0.7% in 2025 — is receding with nine years to run. The open files that will move these grades are named: whether the moratorium is ever converted into repeal, whether the Nationality Act's racial schedule is amended, whether an election date for the council is ever set, and whether the June 2026 cabinet — his three sons now holding the senior-minister, prime minister's office, and foreign-affairs portfolios under four-year terms to 2030 — governs as a preparation for a broader settlement or as the consolidation of the family's grip. The succession, when it comes, will pass to Crown Prince Al-Muhtadee Billah by hereditary right; nothing in the present record suggests it will pass through the hands of the counted or the counted-out alike.
