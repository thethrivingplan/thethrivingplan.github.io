Ten Runs

Ten phases of sets and runs. One phase at a time: build exactly the phase in front of you, lay it down, hit extra cards onto phases already on the table, then shed your whole hand. First to finish all ten phases with the lowest score wins.

Homage, said out loud. Ten Runs borrows a proven shape — the phase-climbing card game families have played for decades: ten contracts, one at a time, wilds, skips, and points for whatever is left in your hand. It works, so we kept what works, paid homage here, and expanded it: our own name, our own card art, our own tuned phase list below, a teaching dial that explains the why, and bots whose real skill differences are printed honestly — no rubber-banding, no hidden help.

The ten phases — our list, tuned by us

This is Ten Runs' own list, tuned by us — not the classic contract order with a new coat of paint. It opens fast: a short set and a short run together in Phase 1, so you learn both shapes in the first round. Colour arrives early (Phase 3) instead of near the end, the long pure runs are spaced between set climbs rather than stacked in a row, and the last three phases are the real wall — a quintet with a run, a ten-card run, then the two biggest sets in the game back to back. A set is cards of one number. A run is cards in counting order, any colours mixed. A colour phase is any numbers, all one colour.

Card points when a round ends

Cards left in your hand cost: 1–9 = 5 points each, 10–12 = 10 points each, Skip = 15, Wild = 25. Only players who laid down their phase move up to the next one; everyone else plays the same phase again next round.

Why chasing your phase beats hoarding

What it is: every round, exactly one contract matters — the phase printed beside your name. Cards that do not build toward it are not an investment; they are future points against you.

What it means: a pretty pair for a phase you are not on cannot be laid down, cannot be hit, and costs 5–10 points each when someone goes out. Meanwhile the player who went all-in on their own phase advances, and advancing is the only way the game ends in your favour. Hoarding also hides information from yourself: you stop counting what you actually need, so you pass up discards that would finish you.

What it could mean for you: the one exception is the Wild. A Wild is worth holding only while it plausibly completes this phase — because at 25 points it is the most expensive card in the game to be caught holding. The teaching dial will tell you, in the moment, when your hand can lay down and when a card you love is just a points risk. That is the whole strategy of Ten Runs in one line: build the phase in front of you, shed everything else.

The bots — honestly different, stated plainly

Bot-honesty statement (read this before you trust a difficulty label): these bots do not cheat and do not quietly adjust. No bot sees your hand, the deck order, or any hidden card. The differences below are the whole difference — real decision rules, printed here, exactly as coded:

Wren — Learner (easiest). Draws from the deck or discard pile by coin flip. Only notices a lay-down about half the time, even when holding one. Hits only by accident (roughly 1 time in 3 when a hit exists). Discards a random card. Wren is a warm-up and a confidence builder, and is labelled that way on purpose.
Milo — Steady (middle). Always lays down when a lay-down is in hand, and takes a discard that completes the phase. Hits whenever a hit is available. Discards the highest-point card that is not part of the phase being built. Milo never bluffs and never holds a Wild past the phase it completes.
Ada — Sharp (hardest). Everything Milo does, plus: chooses the lay-down that spends the fewest Wilds, takes a discard that merely advances the phase (not only one that completes it), remembers the number another player just picked up from the discard pile — a card taken is a want shown — and avoids discarding that same number for the next player to grab, and discards the card that is both high-point and least useful. Ada is genuinely harder for those stated reasons — not because she peeks.

Rules are settings — change them, the game obeys

Hitting allowed
When on, laid-down players may add cards to any phase on the table. When off, your hand only shrinks by discarding.
Skip cards in the deck
When on, four Skips are shuffled in. Discarding a Skip makes the next player lose their turn.
Wilds cost 25 points
The classic price. Turn off to make a caught Wild cost 15 instead — friendlier for new players.
