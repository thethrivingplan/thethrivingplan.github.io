The Ruler's Test — Tarique Rahman, Prime Minister of Bangladesh

Subject: Tarique Rahman — Prime Minister of Bangladesh since 17 February 2026, and chairman of the Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP). The 13th general election was held on 12 February 2026, after eighteen months of interim government under Muhammad Yunus that followed the July 2024 uprising and the fall of Sheikh Hasina's Awami League government. The BNP won 209 of 300 directly elected seats (212 with allies, a two-thirds majority); the Jamaat-e-Islami-led eleven-party alliance won 77 and forms the opposition. Rahman, son of former president Ziaur Rahman and former prime minister Khaleda Zia, returned to Bangladesh on 25 December 2025 after seventeen years of self-imposed exile in London. He is graded on his nine months in office, plus his prior public record where it bears on the tests; where the file is thin, or where a condition was created by the interim government and he inherited it, that is stated plainly — what is graded is his own act: continuation, escalation, or reversal. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The accountability process his government runs is anchored in a documented atrocity record, not invented charges. The UN Human Rights Office's fact-finding report of 12 February 2025 found reasonable grounds to believe the former government and its security apparatus, with Awami League-linked violent elements, systematically committed serious violations between 1 July and 15 August 2024, estimating as many as 1,400 people killed, most shot by security forces (OHCHR, February 2025). The International Crimes Tribunal (ICT) convicted former prime minister Sheikh Hasina of crimes against humanity on three counts and sentenced her to death in absentia on 17 November 2025, with a life sentence on a separate charge; former home minister Asaduzzaman Khan Kamal also received a death sentence, and the former inspector general of police, who turned state witness, received five years (ICT verdict, 17 November 2025; ISAS analysis, November 2025). Hasina, from exile in India, calls the tribunal "rigged" and the verdicts "biased and politically motivated" — her position is on the record and printed here.
  • His government completed the domestic legal process to seek Hasina's extradition under the 2013 treaty with India, rather than acting outside the law: Law Minister Md Asaduzzaman announced completion of the legal requirements in November 2026, leaving the decision with New Delhi; India has not complied, and the government publicly refused her a passport to return voluntarily, insisting on the treaty route (Law Ministry statements; ICT Chief Prosecutor Md Aminul Islam, November 2026).
  • The tribunal process continued against security officials as well as politicians under the post-uprising framework: charges were filed in October 2025 against 28 people over enforced disappearances under the former government, on the work of a commission that received over 1,850 complaints (Human Rights Watch, World Report 2026).
  • His own legal history cuts in his favor on one point: every conviction against him was overturned by appellate courts before he held power, under the interim government, on stated grounds. Between 2007 and 2024, 84 cases were filed against him and he was convicted in five, all in absentia or under governments of his opponents — including a life sentence in the 2004 grenade-attack case (an attack on an Awami League rally that killed 24 people). The High Court acquitted him and all co-accused in that case on 1 December 2024, citing procedural irregularities in the trial and recommending a fresh investigation; the Appellate Division acquitted him in the money-laundering case on 6 March 2025; the Zia Orphanage Trust conviction was overturned on 15 January 2025; the illegal-wealth conviction on 28 May 2025 (High Court and Appellate Division records, 2024–2025). The BNP's position throughout was that the cases were politically motivated; that the convictions were handed down in absentia by courts of a government OHCHR later found to have directed a lethal crackdown is documented context for that position. Critics allege the post-August 2024 courts were captured in his favor — that is an ALLEGATION, contested, and no court finding supports it.

Evidence against:

  • One law does not currently reach the ruling party's own street level. Transparency International Bangladesh's survey work found 41 percent of respondents believed extortion had worsened since the Awami League left power, and TIB's executive director publicly described the failure to control land-grabbing and extortion — around bus terminals, markets, footpaths and sand quarries — as "an ominous sign" (TIB, 27 September 2026; ISAS Brief No. 1430, 14 October 2026). The party's corrective acts were disciplinary, not legal: after a Prothom Alo investigation, the BNP expelled a Dhaka ward president on 29 September 2026 and dissolved three Dhaka committees and expelled fourteen more leaders on 30 September 2026 — party punishment, with no documented prosecutions of the same men (ISAS, October 2026).
  • A week after taking office, his government replaced the ICT's chief prosecutor: Md Aminul Islam was appointed on 23 February 2026, replacing Muhammad Tajul Islam (Parliament Library news clipping, June 2026). Replacing the prosecutor of the tribunal trying the former ruling party, days into a new government, is a source-level act whatever its administrative justification.
  • The tribunal's output under his government continued in the same direction and at the same contested standard: on 15 September 2026 seven aides of Sheikh Hasina were sentenced to death in absentia for their roles in the 2024 crackdown (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). Human Rights Watch stated in July 2026 that the authorities were failing to uphold international legal standards at the ICT (reported by CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). Trials in absentia ending in death sentences are the sharpest form of the one-law question: the crimes charged are documented by OHCHR; the process by which absent defendants are condemned is criticized by the leading international rights monitor.
  • The case net around the former ruling party is very wide and largely inherited — and he has kept it wide. Transparency International Bangladesh counted 1,785 cases filed nationwide over the July uprising as of 19 November 2025, with an estimated 150,000 people made accused, and itself expressed concern at "indiscriminate filing" (TIB, 2 February 2026). As of June 2026, at least 192 Awami League-linked leaders and officials were in jail in the July cases, many held over a year with their cases still under investigation (New Age reporting compiled by the Parliament Library, 6 June 2026). Journalists detained since August–September 2024 on charges including protester killings remained awaiting trial in March 2026 — five of them, with more than 140 media workers facing cases RSF describes as unfounded (RSF via CIVICUS Monitor, March 2026). Those detentions began under the interim government; nine months into his government, they continue.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, media, the Election Commission, the central bank, and local governments keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The election that seated him was run by an Election Commission appointed under the interim government and was graded independently as sound: the EU Election Observation Mission called the 12 February 2026 polls "credible and competently managed," the first genuinely competitive parliamentary election since 2008, with the Commission working "professionally" and some 770,000 expatriate voters enfranchised; turnout was 59.44 percent (EU EOM Final Report, 2026). The mission's criticisms — inconsistent enforcement of campaign rules, campaign-finance opacity, near-absence of women candidates — are process criticisms, printed in its 19 recommendations.
  • The opposition exists in the open and uses the institutions: Jamaat-e-Islami's leader sits as Leader of the Opposition; the student-born National Citizen Party (NCP) publicly conditions cooperation with the government and criticizes the prime minister by name without documented reprisal (NCP statements, November 2026). The press publishes investigations that bite the ruling party — the Prothom Alo extortion investigation of September 2026 was followed within days by party expulsions, not by action against the newspaper.
  • Courts visibly process challenges to his government through ordinary channels: a writ challenging the central bank governor's removal and replacement was heard and summarily dismissed by a High Court bench on 6 September 2026 — a ruling for the government, reached by a court, in public (The Business Standard, September 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The central bank's autonomy was overridden in his first eight days. Governor Ahsan H. Mansur — a former IMF economist appointed in August 2024 with a contract running to August 2028, and the architect of the banking-sector cleanup — had his remaining tenure cancelled by gazette on 25 February 2026, the same day a protest by central bank employees over transfer orders roiled the building; he learned of his removal from television news, two days after being assured by the finance minister that banking reforms would continue (Reuters, 25 February 2026; The Business Standard, February 2026). His replacement, Md Mostaqur Rahman, is a garment exporter and cost accountant who served on the BNP's own election steering committee — the first businessman to hold the post since 1972 — and legal experts questioned the appointment as a conflict-of-interest breach, since he regulates an industry he exports from (The Business Standard, February–March 2026). The government's stated reason is an administrative reshuffle to implement its agenda (Finance Minister Amir Khosru Mahmud Chowdhury, February 2026); the new governor has publicly committed not to bow to political pressure. The act itself — a mid-contract removal of a central bank governor by notification, replaced by a member of the ruling party's election team — is the grip fact, whatever follows.
  • The presidency passed to his own party: in August 2026 parliament elected Mirza Fakhrul Islam Alamgir, the BNP's secretary general, as the 23rd President (255 votes to 88; sworn in 21 August 2026). Lawful and by vote — and it means the head of state, the head of government, the central bank governor and the ICT chief prosecutor all arrived at their seats through his party or his government's appointment within his first six months.
  • Speech law is being rebuilt in a direction watchdogs warn against. His government has proposed amendments to the cyber security law that Transparency International Bangladesh says define "rumour," "misinformation" and "insulting to the state" so as to invite targeted misuse, with penalties up to five years for defamation and ten for spreading rumours; the Committee to Protect Journalists warns the draft could let authorities block journalists' work and deregister outlets (TIB and CPJ via CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The restrictive-law inheritance was a stated grievance of the uprising; the direction of his draft is toward new restriction, not repeal.
  • Expression cases continued under his government, with his party as the most frequent complainant against journalists: the Human Rights Support Society documented 111 people facing expression-related violations in January–October 2026, 66 arrested or detained; 62 faced action over criticism of political leaders, 22 of them over criticism of the prime minister; of cases against journalists where the complainant was identified, BNP and affiliated organisations were linked to the largest share (HRSS report, 18 November 2026). Bangladesh fell to 152nd in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index (RSF, April 2026) — a ranking compiled largely over the interim period, printed here as trajectory, not solely as his record.
  • Institutional reform laws arrived late and diluted: the National Human Rights Commission and enforced-disappearance ordinances of the interim period were not enacted by his government for months, and when laws were passed, civil society objected that provisions fell short of international standards (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, protesters, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He answered the garment workers' wobble through the wage mechanism rather than only with police. When unions invoked the 2026 Labour Act amendment (which shortens the wage-revision interval from five years to three) and protests built from July 2026 onward, the government formed a Minimum Wage Board on 1 November 2026, the day after the first road blockades; the board has since negotiated in the open, with employers' and workers' offers converging from Tk 15,000/Tk 32,000 toward Tk 15,700/Tk 22,000 at the entry grade as of 24 November 2026 (Prothom Alo; The Business Standard, 24 November 2026; Clean Clothes Campaign monitoring, November 2026).
  • He answered exposure of his own party's extortion with visible discipline within days (the September 2026 expulsions, Test 1), and after the November 2026 Narail violence he publicly instructed party leaders to avoid provocation, cooperate with law enforcement and leave action against miscreants to the agencies (party meeting reports, 15–16 November 2026).
  • He opened dialogue with the uprising's other heirs rather than freezing them out: on 23 November 2026 he met the NCP leadership at the Secretariat under a national-unity initiative; the NCP set hard public conditions, and the Prime Minister's Office described the meeting as part of uniting the forces of the July uprising (The Business Standard, 23 November 2026).
  • Delivery began fast where he had promised it: the Family Card pilot launched on 10 March 2026, within a month of taking office, and a Farmer Card pre-pilot reached 22,065 marginal farmers in eleven upazilas (government programme records; promise tracking, November 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The clearest wobble of his term — the referendum — was answered by routing around it. Voters approved the July National Charter in the 12 February 2026 referendum by about 68 percent, and Article 8 of the Charter's Implementation Order required MPs to take a second oath, in the same ceremony, as members of a Constitution Reform Council. On 17 February 2026 BNP MPs took only the parliamentary oath; the government's position is that the existing constitution provides no such council oath and the Chief Election Commissioner had no authority to administer it (BNP standing committee member Salahuddin Ahmed, February 2026). The government instead formed a parliamentary constitution amendment committee of its own majority — which the opposition parties boycott and the NCP calls a "one-party Constitution Amendment Committee" obstructing the referendum's mandate, demanding its dissolution as a condition of cooperation (NCP, 22 November 2026). The legal argument is a real one, made by lawyers on both sides; the effect is that the reform pen stayed in the ruling party's hand, and nine months on, the charter's structural items (a 100-member upper house, a ten-year prime-ministerial limit) remain in committee with a January 2027 target (The Daily Star, 7 October 2026).
  • The wage wobble was answered late, after force had been used. Protests ran from August 2026; the board came only on 1 November, after strikes began; through November, police fired tear gas and sound grenades at road blockades, hundreds of factories closed at the peak, and union leaders were detained on 17 November 2026, drawing release calls from international labour and brand bodies (Reuters, 17 and 24 November 2026; Clean Clothes Campaign; Asia Floor Wage Alliance, November 2026). Some detained leaders later received bail. As of grading, no new wage has been set: the 2023 entry minimum of Tk 12,500 still stands against a worker demand that began at Tk 30,000.
  • On law and order — his stated first priority — the government's public line has leaned toward denial: ministers argue current violence is "not historically exceptional," while the NCP's convener told him to his face on 23 November 2026 that law and order had deteriorated over the seven to eight months since the election and that the government lacked a firm position (ISAS, October 2026; The Business Standard, 23 November 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Awami League voters, Hindus and other minorities, Rohingya refugees, garment workers, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • His stated principle, in his first address to the nation on 18 February 2026, was the count itself: "whether you voted for the BNP and gave it the mandate to form this government, or chose not to, or did not vote at all, you all hold equal claim to this government," and the country a safe homeland for "Muslims, Hindus, Buddhists, Christians — regardless of political belief, religion, ethnicity" (first televised address, 18 February 2026). He repeated the anti-revenge line publicly in March 2026: "there should be no revenge, vengeance, or unnecessary disputes" (Independence Award ceremony speech, March 2026).
  • His parliamentary opponents are fully counted: the Jamaat-led alliance sits as a functioning opposition with its leader recognized, and the NCP — six seats, and the party most critical of his charter handling — is received at the Secretariat by the prime minister himself (February–November 2026).
  • The Rohingya remain counted, on the same terms as under his predecessors: Bangladesh continues to host over 1.2 million, and Rahman personally carried the file to the UN on 23 September 2026, proposing five measures for "early, safe, voluntary, dignified and sustainable" repatriation and insisting return must be voluntary and rights-protected (PM's statement, UN high-level side event, 23 September 2026). No forced returns are documented under his government. In November 2026 the International Court of Justice ruled that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya — a judgment his government welcomed as vindication of the sheltering policy (ICJ judgment, November 2026).
  • Garment workers — some four million, mostly women — are inside the count's machinery: a wage board sits, negotiates publicly, and operates under a labour law his parliament amended to shorten the revision cycle (Test 3).

Evidence against:

  • The largest single bloc of citizens is counted out of politics altogether. The Awami League — which governed for fifteen years and, whatever its record, is the political home of a very large share of the electorate — had its activities banned by the interim government in May 2025 and its registration suspended; it was barred from the February 2026 ballot, its symbol absent. The EU mission chief, while calling the election credible, expressly declined to call it fully participatory on turnout alone, noting that participation means no relevant group is excluded (EU EOM, February 2026). Rahman inherited the ban; his government has maintained it — the ban runs, by its terms, until the ICT trials are complete, with no end date — and gone further in rhetoric: on 21 November 2026 a state minister said the government and opposition would, if necessary, amend the constitution to ban the party permanently (State Minister Mir Shahe Alam, 21 November 2026). Ordinary AL supporters sit inside the case net described in Test 1: an estimated 150,000 accused in July-uprising cases (TIB), post-election violence in which at least 10 people were killed and 497 injured between 13 February and 31 March 2026 (Odhikar monitoring), and — in November 2026 — retaliatory arson after two BNP leaders were killed in Narail district: at least 40 houses of Awami League-linked families damaged or burned on 20 November (The Daily Star, 22 November 2026), with residents and some outlets alleging Hindu households were among the targets and abductions of women alleged — ALLEGATIONs the district police dispute, stating the targeted locality had no Hindu families and the targets were AL-affiliated; the government's public response emphasized vigilance against "provocation" and blamed fugitive AL leaders for destabilization, and no independent investigation has been announced as of grading.
  • Minorities remain exposed, on counts that are contested in scale but not in existence. The Bangladesh Hindu Buddhist Christian Unity Council documented 257–261 incidents of communal violence in January–June 2026, including 44 deaths and 62 attacks on places of worship, and assessed that killings and temple attacks rose against 2025 even as total incidents fell (BHBCOP report, 26 July 2026). The government's methodological counter-position, stated by the interim government and not repudiated since, is that police examination classifies most reported incidents as political or private disputes rather than communal — of 645 incidents examined for 2025, 71 were assessed as communal (police figures cited January 2026). Independent monitors land between the two: Ain o Salish Kendra recorded 26 incidents with eight deaths in January–May 2026 (ASK tables compiled in GCDG Working Paper 121, September 2026). The council also continues to demand the release of Hindu leader Chinmoy Krishna Das, imprisoned since November 2024 on a sedition charge after organizing a minority-rights rally; his detention began under the interim government and continues under this one (BHBCOP, July 2026). The OHCHR's February 2025 report had already documented that in the uprising's aftermath Hindus, Ahmadiyya Muslims and indigenous people in the Chittagong Hill Tracts were attacked by mobs, with some BNP and Jamaat supporters among those involved in revenge violence — and no evidence available to OHCHR that such attacks were orchestrated by party leadership. That sentence is the honest state of the record in both directions: the violence is documented; central direction is not.
  • Women were counted thinly at the election that seated him: the EU mission recorded that women candidates were "almost absent," a failure of political will across parties, his included (EU EOM Final Report, 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record of nine months in office, with dates; his longer public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The manifesto was specific, and its flagship items started on schedule. The BNP manifesto, unveiled 6 February 2026 under the slogan "Bangladesh First," carried 51 pledges. The Family Card — Tk 2,500 a month to low-income households, issued in women's names — began as a pilot on 10 March 2026, less than a month after he took office, with full rollout from 16 August 2026 toward 4.1 million families in 2026–27 (manifesto; programme records; The Daily Star, 9 August 2026). The Farmer Card is in pre-pilot. He has publicly committed to delivering the pledges "one by one... at any cost," while asking patience on timing (Family Card launch address, 2026).
  • A small promise with a clean record: in his first address he announced BNP MPs would forgo duty-free car imports and government land plots — a renunciation of perks by his own side, made on 18 February 2026 and not reversed on the record since.
  • The election itself was a kept word of the transition he benefited from and supported: the BNP had demanded elections by December 2025; the February 2026 date was set after his June 2025 London meeting with Yunus, the vote happened on that date, and power transferred on schedule (2025–2026 record).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The July Charter is the largest gap between signature and delivery. The BNP signed the charter in October 2025 and campaigned on implementing it; the referendum endorsed it by roughly 68 percent; nine months into the term, the reform-council oath has not been taken by his MPs, the work sits in a majority-controlled committee the opposition boycotts, and the NCP — his fellow heirs of the uprising — publicly charges that refusing the referendum's mechanism "amounted to breaking commitments made to the July Uprising" (NCP, 22 November 2026). His party's answer — implementation "exactly as it was signed," through parliament, respecting its recorded dissents — is a lawyer's reading of the promise, and it is graded as such: the word is being interpreted, not simply kept.
  • The manifesto's employment and health numbers are not moving at their promised speed: the campaign pledged 10 million jobs within eighteen months — nine months in, no official progress count supports the trajectory; the pledge to recruit 100,000 health workers had, as of late October 2026, reached the stage of planned posts with recruitment not yet begun (promise tracking against Prothom Alo and Daily Star reporting, October–November 2026).
  • His first-priority promise — law and order — is the one his critics now quote back at him: the NCP told him directly that order had deteriorated over his first seven to eight months (23 November 2026), TIB's survey records public belief that extortion worsened after the AL's fall, and mob violence continued through his term on the monitors' counts (ASK recorded 89 mob-attack deaths in January–May 2026, following 198 in 2025 — the 2025 figure spanning the interim government and his first months). His November 2026 answer has been instructions to his own party to stay vigilant and cooperate with police — an adjustment of tone, not yet a change in the numbers.
  • The long arc, factually: Rahman was the most controversial figure of his mother's 2001–2006 government, with critics alleging he ran a parallel power centre at Hawa Bhaban; a leaked US diplomatic cable of that era described him as a "symbol of kleptocratic government" — a diplomat's characterization, printed as such. He was arrested in the 2007–2008 military-backed caretaker period, released on parole in 2008, and lived in London until December 2025. The corruption and violence ALLEGATIONs of that era were litigated as the cases in Test 1: filed by successor regimes, convicted in absentia, and every conviction overturned on appeal in 2024–2025. His 2026 campaign word was a break with that past — "Bangladesh First," no politics of vengeance, rule of law over party. Nine months in, the break is real in his public language and partial in his government's practice, and the remaining term is where this grade will be decided.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Tarique Rahman came to power through the cleanest election Bangladesh has held since 2008, at the head of a two-thirds majority, saying the words the count requires — that the government belongs equally to those who did not vote for him. Nine months in, the institutions around him are neither captured nor untouched: the opposition sits and speaks, the press investigates his own party and is answered with expulsions rather than raids, and the courts process challenges in the open — while the central bank governor was removed by gazette eight days in, the tribunal's prosecutor was replaced within the week, the presidency passed to his party's secretary general, and his government drafts speech laws the watchdogs warn against. The accountability he runs against the fallen regime rests on a UN-documented atrocity record, and it also runs through in-absentia death sentences, a prosecutor he appointed, and a case net an estimated 150,000 wide. His failures concentrate where the tests always find them: the fallen party's voters are counted out of politics entirely, with the ban he inherited now spoken of by his own ministers as possibly permanent, and the minorities his first address promised a safe homeland still bury their dead on contested counts his government answers with classification disputes. The file is nine months old. The wage board sits this week; the charter committee reports by January; the record is correctable, and so are the grades.
