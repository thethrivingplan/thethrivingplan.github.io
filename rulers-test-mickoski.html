The Ruler's Test — Hristijan Mickoski, Prime Minister of North Macedonia

Subject: Hristijan Mickoski — Prime Minister of North Macedonia since 23 June 2024, when the Assembly elected his government of VMRO-DPMNE, the VLEN coalition of Albanian parties and ZNAM by 77 votes to 22. He remains in office in November 2026; the next parliamentary elections are due by 2028. His coalition's standing was reinforced at the local elections of 19 October and 2 November 2025, in which VMRO-DPMNE won 55 of 81 mayoral posts, including Skopje, VLEN won 10, the opposition SDSM 6, the DUI-led National Alliance for Integration 4, ZNAM 1 and the Roma Union 1 (State Election Commission results; OSCE/ODIHR Final Report, 7 April 2026). ODIHR assessed those elections as competitive but held in a sharply polarised atmosphere, and warned of misuse of public resources and allegations of voter pressure and intimidation. A cabinet reshuffle was approved on 13 July 2026 after opposition MPs walked out. He is graded on the record since June 2024. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The prosecution reached the top of the previous government, across party and ethnic lines. Former First Deputy Prime Minister Artan Grubi (DUI) was named a suspect in December 2024 in the State Lottery case — prosecutors allege decisions and contracts in 2023 caused more than €8 million in damage to the state budget through unlawful appropriation (an ALLEGATION; Grubi denied wrongdoing and said he was abroad on a private visit). After 14 months outside the country, he was detained at the Blace border crossing on 23 February 2026 and faces proceedings (Interior Ministry and Public Prosecutor's Office statements, December 2024 – February 2026). Former Prime Minister Dimitar Kovačevski (SDSM) has also been under investigation, as documented by International IDEA's Democracy Tracker.
  • In the Kočani fire case (Test 1's largest file, below), the indictment of 13 June 2025 charged 34 persons and three legal entities — club owners and their families, inspectors, security guards, former mayors of Kočani, former economy ministers and state-institution employees — with "grave acts against public security," one defendant additionally charged with soliciting a bribe for forged licences (Chief State Prosecutor Ljupčo Kocevski, 13 June 2025). The sitting VMRO-DPMNE mayor of Kočani resigned in March 2025 and was among those detained in the investigation's first wave — enforcement reached his own party's local officeholder, not only predecessors.
  • Eleven earlier suspects in the Kočani investigation, including two former economy ministers, were not charged for lack of evidence (prosecution statement, June 2025) — a documented instance of the file narrowing by evidence rather than expanding by politics.
  • After the fire, the government ordered nationwide inspections: of 50 entertainment venues checked, only 22 held valid licences, and dozens were closed (government statements, March 2025) — the licensing rule applied across the sector, not only to the implicated club.

Evidence against:

  • The Kočani accountability stopped, on the documented record, below the level of the officeholders serving on the night of the fire — and the victims' families have marched against exactly that line for over a year. The fire at the Pulse nightclub on 16 March 2025 killed 62 people (the toll in the prosecution's indictment; early counts were 59, rising as injured victims died) and injured around 200. Families' weekly "March of the Angels" protests accuse the prosecution of selective justice: the current Minister of Economy, the current director of the State Market Inspectorate, and the police officers who approved and executed that night's security plan were not, they say, included among the accused as the law required; 13 Kočani police officers were arrested in June 2025, but the families' demand that serving senior officials answer has not been met (families' public statements at the Kočani protests, 2025; contemporaneous reporting). The main opposition SDSM demands the resignation of Interior Minister Panče Toshkovski, on whose watch the tragedy happened; Mickoski has kept him in post throughout. Whether the exclusion of serving officials reflects the evidence or their office is CONTESTED — the families and the opposition allege protection; the prosecution states the indictment follows the evidence.
  • The European Commission's verdict on the anti-corruption record is flat: in its 4 November 2026 report it found "no progress in the fight against corruption, which the Commission calls 'an issue of serious concern'" (European Policy Centre analysis of the 2026 Enlargement Package, 10 November 2026; Commission 2026 Rule of Law country chapter, 17 July 2026, noting delays, infrequent final convictions and limited resources as obstacles to a track record in high-level corruption). A government elected on an uncompromising anti-corruption pledge has, two years in, no documented high-level corruption convictions to show.
  • The January 2026 amendments to the Criminal Code narrowed the scope of the abuse-of-official-position offence in public procurement, leaving other serious manipulations unaddressed; the Commission found they "fail to adequately address negative consequences from 2023 changes and contribute to legal uncertainty" (Commission 2026 Report, 4 November 2026). Mickoski's defence — that the changes were a temporary measure required by a Constitutional Court ruling to prevent mass amnesty or legal anarchy, and that a new Criminal Code has been submitted to Brussels — is on the record and printed here; the narrowing's practical beneficiaries include former officials facing abuse-of-office exposure, which is why the opposition contests it (Mickoski statements, November 2026).
  • Grubi's return produced a one-law question that remains open. After 14 months as a fugitive, he received 30 days' house detention rather than pre-trial detention — the prosecution citing his surrender and fears for his safety in prison. SDSM alleges this was "part of a deal between the DUI and VMRO" and asks "what the price is" (SDSM statement, 23 February 2026) — an ALLEGATION by an interested party, unproven; VMRO-DPMNE itself said it "understood feelings of public outrage over this mild measure." The disparity between the treatment of the returned fugitive deputy prime minister and ordinary defendants is documented fact; its cause is not established.
  • The Alta Banka file raises an unanswered licensing question. The National Bank, under Governor Trajko Slaveski (Test 2), approved Serbia's Alta Banka's acquisition of Stopanska Banka AD Bitola; by autumn 2026 the Serbian bank was in crisis, clients queued outside the Bitola branch, and SDSM leader Venko Filipče demanded Slaveski's resignation and criminal investigation, alleging the bank was "linked to criminally acquired money" and had been allowed entry contrary to warnings attributed to the Austrian financial supervisor (SDSM statements, November 2026 — ALLEGATIONs, denied by Slaveski, who maintains the procedures were lawful and within the central bank's remit). Mickoski publicly attributed the affair to an "imported" case and blamed the opposition for spreading panic (November 2026). No Macedonian authority has documented unlawful conduct on the Macedonian side; the question of who approved what, and on what checks, remains publicly unanswered.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the prosecution, the judicial councils, the media regulator, the electoral authority and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The judiciary's governing law was rebuilt with the Venice Commission, not against it. Parliament adopted a new Law on the Judicial Council on 29 December 2025, which the European Commission assessed as "largely addressing the Venice Commission recommendations"; the draft had been sent to the Venice Commission by the Justice Minister in April 2025, its June 2025 opinion (CDL-AD(2025)026) was reviewed by the drafting working group and incorporated, and a follow-up compliance review was sent to the Commission (Ministry of Justice Reform and Growth Facility reporting, 2025; Commission 2026 Report). New laws on the Public Prosecutor's Office and the Council of Public Prosecutors were likewise prepared with a Venice Commission joint opinion (CDL-AD(2025)036, 14 October 2025), and parliament adopted amendments strengthening the prosecution service's autonomy (Commission 2026 Rule of Law chapter).
  • The Constitutional Court ruled against the ruling majority's convenience and was obeyed: in May 2025 it repealed provisions on the number of supporting signatures required of independent candidates, against the framework the majority had legislated (ODIHR Final Report on the 2025 local elections, 7 April 2026). In October 2024 the same court abolished the "Ethnic Balancer" quota mechanism — a decision Mickoski's party welcomed, but one delivered by the court on its own reasoning, not by the government (International IDEA Democracy Tracker, October 2024).
  • Elections remain administered competitively: ODIHR found the October–November 2025 local elections competitive, the State Election Commission reported no major disruptions, and the opposition's wins — including Skopje's Centar municipality — stood (ODIHR, 2026; SEC statements, October 2025).
  • The public broadcaster's classification moved toward independence, not away: the State Media Monitor reclassified MRT in 2026 as "Independent State Funded and State Managed," up from "State-Controlled" (2022–2025); MRT adopted a new statute with safeguards for editors' independence on 5 March 2026, and a new director was appointed by its Programme Council on 23 April 2026 (State Media Monitor, October 2026 review). North Macedonia's RSF World Press Freedom Index position moved only slightly — 45th of 180 in 2026 (score 69.49) against 42nd in 2025 (70.44) — within the region's normal range, with no journalists killed or detained (RSF Index 2025, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The chief prosecutor was forced out mid-term under a government-driven dismissal procedure that the European Commission says "exposed a risk of politicisation and shortcomings in the dismissal rules" (Commission 2026 Rule of Law chapter, 17 July 2026). The documented sequence: Chief State Prosecutor Ljupčo Kocevski, appointed by parliament in early 2024 for a six-year term, led the Kočani investigation; from February 2025 the Justice Ministry proposed his dismissal, citing scandals, dysfunction, breaches of ethics and an improperly made acting appointment — grounds Kocevski contested, defending his record and his work programme (Justice Ministry proposal and Kocevski's responses, February 2025). The ruling party held repeated press conferences against him, accusing him of protecting judicial corruption and of alignment with SDSM (VMRO-DPMNE statements, 2025). On 17 December 2025 Kocevski submitted an irrevocable resignation, citing violations of the rule of law. The Council of Public Prosecutors then appointed an acting head; prosecutors elected Iskra Hadži Vasileva head of the organised-crime and corruption office on 28 October 2026, and Nenad Saveski appears as State Public Prosecutor in November 2026 reporting. The institutions continued to function; the independence cost of the episode is the Commission's documented finding, not only the opposition's claim.
  • Judicial independence is perceived to be falling, and the judiciary itself has protested official rhetoric. The Commission records that perceived judicial independence fell in 2026 to 21% of the public and 19% of companies rating it fairly or very good (from 28% and 26% in 2025), that "interference and pressure from other State branches continue to raise serious concerns," and that the Judicial Council, the Supreme Court and the Association of Judges jointly issued a public statement against "persistent, unsubstantiated critiques of the judiciary" by officials, arguing they undermine independence and the separation of powers (Commission 2026 Rule of Law chapter). The judiciary's autonomy is also "undermined by insufficient budgetary allocation," and the shortage of judges and prosecutors has worsened with no new candidates in initial training (same source).
  • The central bank governorship went to a senior figure of the ruling party. Trajko Slaveski — a member of VMRO-DPMNE's Executive Committee and president of the Skopje City Council on the party's list, and a former finance minister (2006–2009) — was nominated by President Siljanovska-Davkova and took office as National Bank governor on 30 May 2025, resigning his party posts on appointment. The appointment was lawful and the bank's statutory independence is unchanged; the documented criticism is that no consensus was sought for a post whose holder then approved the contested Alta Banka acquisition (Test 1) within his first year (presidential nomination, May 2025; SDK.mk commentary and SDSM statements, 2025–2026). The criticism is an argument about the appointment's character; no document establishes that the governor takes instructions.
  • Media pressure is documented at the level of climate rather than capture: RSF records that "government officials tend to have poor and demeaning attitudes towards journalists," that the two largest parties run parallel media systems under their political and economic influence, that the public broadcaster "lacks editorial and financial independence" in its assessment, and that 16 new defamation and insult lawsuits were filed in 2025 with no anti-SLAPP mechanism (RSF country file, updated 7 May 2026; AJM register via State Media Monitor). The February 2024 amendments re-authorising state advertising in private media — adopted just before this government took office and retained by it — are flagged by RSF as raising influence-peddling concerns.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, prosecutors, the EU, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Kočani fire — the gravest wobble of his tenure — was answered first with ownership language and systemic action. On the day of the fire Mickoski called it a "mass murder" caused by "money, bribes, and corruption," and vowed that those responsible would be prosecuted "regardless of who they are, from which institution, from what level, from which party, or profession they come" (statement on X, 16 March 2025; OCCRP reporting). The government declared seven days of national mourning, ordered the nationwide licensing inspections that closed dozens of venues (Test 1), and Mickoski pledged to reopen past cases of the same pattern, naming the 2021 Tetovo Covid-hospital fire (International IDEA Democracy Tracker, March 2025).
  • On the Albanian bar-exam dispute, pushback produced process rather than refusal. After Albanian law students protested from April 2026 — broadening into demonstrations in Skopje in May 2026 demanding the jurisprudence exam in Albanian — Mickoski said the government was "working on a solution in line with Venice Commission recommendations," formed a committee of constitutional experts, and described a "dual challenge" of mother-tongue rights and legal uniformity (International IDEA, May 2026; EUalive reporting, April 2026). When coalition partner ZNAM threatened in November 2026 to quit the government over the judicial-exam language question, he confirmed talks with its leader and announced a coalition leaders' meeting in December, presenting a two-part exam model and an urgent Venice Commission opinion request (Mickoski statements, November 2026).
  • He has adjusted instruments when the Constitutional Court required it: his stated reason for the January 2026 Criminal Code amendments was compliance with a Constitutional Court ruling to avert mass amnesty or legal anarchy — contested by the Commission on the amendments' substance (Test 1), but the adjustment itself was framed and defended publicly rather than denied (Mickoski statements, November 2026).
  • Electoral defeat at the margins was accepted without contest: the 2025 local elections' opposition wins, including repeat votes ordered in four municipalities where turnout fell below the legal threshold (re-run 11 January 2026), proceeded under the rules (ODIHR Final Report, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the EU's central demand he has not adjusted in two and a half years — and says so. The Commission's November 2026 report records that "no attempts were made" to adopt the constitutional amendments during the reporting period (Commission 2026 Report; EPC analysis, 10 November 2026). His position is stated at full strength in Test 5; here it is graded as a wobble response: confronted by the Council conclusions of July 2022, by Commissioner Kos's repeated statement that the amendment "is an obligation" (23 October 2026), and by his own local-election landslide removing any domestic obstacle of mandate, he answered with a counter-condition — amendments with effect deferred until accession, plus guarantees against further bilateral demands — which Bulgaria's foreign minister Velislava Petrova in October 2026 called "unacceptable," rejecting renegotiation. Dialogue, the Commission notes, "has stalled."
  • He publicly disputes the referee's findings rather than answering them. Responding to the Commission's November 2026 criticism of the Criminal Code amendments, Mickoski "expressed disagreement with the European Commission," disputing its findings on legal uncertainty (CLSTR/EU reporting, November 2026). On the name question, after the Commission recorded that government members disregard the Prespa Agreement by not using the constitutional name, his response (15 December 2025) was that "we brought back" the name — pointing to opposition figures who also say "Macedonia" (Metla.mk, 5 November 2026, recounting the exchange; Greek Foreign Ministry reaction, 17 December 2025).
  • The Kocevski affair shows the opposite of adjustment: when the chief prosecutor's work displeased the government, the response across 2025 was a dismissal procedure and a party press campaign, ending in the prosecutor's resignation (Test 2) — pushback from within the justice system was treated as the problem.
  • On Kočani, the adjustment stopped where the families' central demand begins: a year and a half of weekly marches demanding that serving officials be included in the accountability file has been met with the prosecution's evidence formula and no political consequence for the interior minister (Test 1). The trial of the 34 indicted opened in November 2025 amid thousands marching in Skopje demanding justice and blaming corruption (Independent, 15 November 2025); the wobble is still being answered, and the answer so far is that the line holds where it was drawn.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Albanians, Roma, Turks, Bulgarians, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Albanian community is inside his government, not outside it. The VLEN coalition of Albanian parties has been a governing partner since June 2024, holding ministries and a deputy premiership; VLEN won 10 mayoral posts in the 2025 local elections, including Tetovo, and the Roma Union won Šuto Orizari and a Turkish-community party won a municipality — minority communities hold executive office at both levels under his coalition (SEC results, October–November 2025). Practical cooperation with Albania's government continued, including the joint "One Stop Shop" border system promoted with Prime Minister Rama in November 2026.
  • The pension rise was universal in design: the promised linear increase of 5,000 denars was paid to all pensioners in two steps (September 2024 and March 2025), a flat amount that proportionally favours the smallest pensions; average net wages grew 7.6% nominally in December 2025 (3.3% in real terms), and unemployment fell to 11.4% in Q4 2025 (Ministry of Finance monthly data, February 2026). Growth of 3.5% in 2025 (IMF Article IV concluding statement, 2026) was driven substantially by these transfers and public investment — the flock's floor rose, whoever they voted for.
  • The 2025 local elections seated everyone who won: ODIHR found them competitive, the opposition governs where it won, and repeat elections were held where the turnout rule voided results rather than the results being overridden (ODIHR, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Albanian electorate's plurality choice was counted out of government. In the May 2024 parliamentary elections, the DUI-led European Front won more Albanian votes and more seats (19) than VLEN (14); the unwritten post-2001 convention was that the winner of the Albanian vote joins the government. Mickoski formed his government with VLEN instead. DUI leader Ali Ahmeti and DUI have maintained since that the government lacks legitimacy among Albanians because the community's expressed plurality was overridden — a claim about the convention, CONTESTED by the government, which answers that coalition choice is the prime minister's constitutional prerogative and that DUI's two decades in office were themselves the problem voters punished. The documented fact underneath the dispute: the larger Albanian vote went to the party left outside.
  • The Bulgarian community is counted out of the constitution by his stated choice. The July 2022 Council conclusions — the agreed price for opening the first negotiating cluster — require naming Bulgarians among the constitution's communities, as Albanians, Turks, Vlachs, Roma and others already are. Mickoski refuses adoption without guarantees and reciprocity (Test 5), so the roughly 3,500 citizens who declared Bulgarian identity in the 2021 census remain the one named community unlisted. His reasoning — that Bulgaria does not recognise a Macedonian minority, that some two dozen European Court of Human Rights rulings on Macedonian associations in Bulgaria are unimplemented, and that a people asked to amend its constitution repeatedly deserves to know the demand is final — is stated at full strength here and in Test 5; the exclusion itself is nonetheless the operative fact.
  • The equitable-representation mechanism was abolished and, two years on, not replaced. The Constitutional Court abolished the "Ethnic Balancer" quota mechanism on 9 October 2024; ODIHR's April 2026 report records "concerns over equitable representation in public institutions" as a live backdrop, and Albanian analysts note that no new fair-representation law has been enacted (ODIHR Final Report, 2026; Fairpress analysis, 2026). The court made the decision; the vacuum since is the government's.
  • The bar/jurisprudence exam remains Macedonian-only, against documented minority protest. Albanian law graduates — who may study law in Albanian — must pass the professional exam in Macedonian; the Justice Ministry maintains the framework does not allow otherwise; the Language Inspectorate has warned the Ministry and the Ombudsperson has proposed amendments, leaving the ministry's position legally contested by the state's own oversight bodies (International IDEA, May 2026). Students protested through spring 2026 under the frame of institutional discrimination; the government's expert-committee process (Test 3) had produced no change by November 2026, and coalition partner ZNAM simultaneously pressed from the other direction. Meanwhile the Constitutional Court's review of the 2018 Law on the Use of Languages — pending since before this government — remained undecided in November 2026, the court awaiting its final judge (Alsat/FrontPages.eu, 14 November 2026); on 7 October 2026 the court declined to review a ministry regulation published only in Macedonian, a decision critics argue treats bilingual-publication breaches as administrative rather than constitutional (decision UZ.19/2026, as analysed October 2026).
  • The poor are counted in transfers and counted out in structure: the IMF's 2026 Article IV mission warns that the growth he delivers rests on ad-hoc wage and pension increases and expansionary fiscal policy — deficits of 3.9–4.5% of GDP against a 3% fiscal rule — with consolidation still unspecified, and recommends restoring the link between contributions and benefits (IMF, March 2026). The Commission adds that mid-year budget rebalancing shifted funds from capital expenditure to current spending (Commission ERP assessment, April 2026). This is a sustainability finding, not an exclusion finding; it is recorded here because the flock's floor is being raised on borrowing the same flock must repay.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since June 2024, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Pensions — kept, on schedule. The 2024 campaign's signature social promise, a linear 5,000-denar increase for all pensioners, was delivered in two documented steps — 2,500 denars in September 2024 and 2,500 in March 2025 — and pension spending drove the documented ~25% ad-hoc pension rise of 2024 (German Economic Team Economic Monitor, January 2026; government statements, 2024–2025).
  • No constitutional changes "under dictate" — kept in its own terms. He campaigned on refusing constitutional amendments imposed as a bilateral condition without guarantees, and in office he has not adopted them (Tests 3–4). Whatever the cost graded elsewhere, the refusal is the promise as he made it, kept. His proposed formula — adopt now, take effect on accession, with guarantees against further bilateral vetoes and reciprocity on Macedonian-minority rights — was restated consistently from January 2026 ("rights for Macedonians in Bulgaria and guarantees for Macedonian identity and language") through the November 2026 EU Enlargement Forum in Brussels (State Media Monitor, October 2026; CLSTR reporting, November 2026).
  • The Hungarian loan — terms as stated. He announced in July 2024, agreed with Viktor Orbán on the margins of the NATO summit in Washington (11 July 2024), a €1 billion credit from Hungary's Eximbank; parliament authorised the borrowing by special law on 17 September 2024 and the agreement was signed on 8 October 2024 — €988 million in two tranches at 3.25% fixed interest over 15 years with a three-year grace period, used in part to repay a maturing ~€500 million eurobond and to lend on to domestic business (European Western Balkans reporting; MKNews Hub summary, October 2026). The instrument exists on the terms he announced.
  • Full CFSP alignment and the reform-facility track — kept. North Macedonia remains fully aligned with the EU's Common Foreign and Security Policy, and by June 2026 had drawn €142.1 million under the Reform and Growth Facility against completed reform steps (Commission 2026 Report; EPC analysis, November 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The uncompromising fight against corruption — the record runs the other way. The 2024 campaign's central pledge was an uncompromising battle with corruption and no impunity for the previous government's officials. Two and a half years in: the Commission finds "no progress in the fight against corruption," an "issue of serious concern," with no track record of final high-level convictions (Commission 2026 Report). The flagship case against Grubi stands at house detention and an unproven deal ALLEGATION (Test 1); the Criminal Code amendments narrowed the main abuse-of-office offence (Test 1); and the chief prosecutor who was to deliver the accountability drive exited mid-term under government pressure (Test 2).
  • The EU path — stalled at the gate he promised to pass. He promised European integration as a strategic goal pursued on merit; the first negotiating cluster remains unopened, no roadmaps were extended to North Macedonia in the November 2026 package (they went to Albania, Moldova, Montenegro and Ukraine), and the Commission repeats "no progress" more than thirty times across the 2026 report (Metla.mk, 5 November 2026; Commission 2026 Report). His answer — that the blockage is Bulgaria's bilateral condition, not his reforms — is his stated position and is graded in Tests 3–4; the outcome against the promise of forward movement is documented either way.
  • The name — his word moved. In May 2024, as prime minister designate, he said he acknowledged the reality of the legal name but would use "Macedonia" in his own public appearances as a personal right, inviting Greece to sue at the International Court of Justice if it believed Prespa breached (Euractiv reporting, 16–17 May 2024). By 15 December 2025 he was claiming the government had "in practice restored" and "brought back" the name — a claim about state practice, not personal usage — drawing a formal Greek Foreign Ministry response two days later that there are "no 'facts on the ground' in international treaties" (BalkanView and Metla.mk reporting, December 2025). The Commission's 2026 report records that government members and senior officials "on occasions disregard the [Prespa] Agreement, including by not using the country's constitutional name, which risks affecting good neighbourly relations." Domestically and in all state documents the constitutional name stands; the drift from "my personal right" to "we brought it back" is the movement graded here.
  • The Hungarian loan's transparency — contested, unresolved. The loan was negotiated leader-to-leader without a competitive process, authorised by a special law rather than the ordinary borrowing framework — criticised at adoption by SDSM as non-transparent borrowing from a political ally (parliamentary debate, September 2024). In 2026 the file acquired a second life: Hungary's new government under Péter Magyar announced on 24 July 2026 a review of four Eximbank transactions including this one, and Hungarian police opened a criminal investigation on 1 September 2026 into suspected abuse of office and mismanagement at Eximbank — concerning the Hungarian side's approvals, not Macedonian spending (bne IntelliNews; EUalive reporting, 2026). Mickoski told parliament on 29 October 2026 that the State Audit Office had confirmed the money's intended use and that after six months of announced scandals there was "not even a 'K' of crime"; the audit report itself has not been published, and its publicly known portion covers a €250 million segment (MKNews Hub, October 2026). The opposition's stronger claims — documents linking the government to Vučić and Orbán networks — have not been published and remain ALLEGATIONs. What is documented: a billion-euro obligation taken on by announcement, defended by an unpublished audit.
  • Judicial reform by dissolution — quietly replaced. On the 2024 campaign trail he pledged to dissolve the Judicial Council and the Council of Public Prosecutors as captured bodies — a pledge the EU and the Venice Commission treated as a threat to the independence he now cites. In office he did not dissolve them; the councils were reformed by law instead (Test 2). The substitution was the better act and the broken letter of the promise at once; it is recorded as both.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Hristijan Mickoski governs a country whose institutions still answer back — a Constitutional Court that repeals his majority's provisions, prosecutors who elected their own organised-crime chief in October 2026, an opposition that wins cities and holds them, a public broadcaster reclassified upward by independent monitors — and his government has done real parts of the formal work: judicial-council and prosecution laws rebuilt with the Venice Commission, a delivered pension promise, full alignment with EU foreign policy, an election machine that wins under ODIHR's eye. Against that stands the other half of the record, documented by the same referees: a chief prosecutor forced out mid-term under a procedure the Commission calls a politicisation risk; a judiciary publicly protesting official pressure while its perceived independence falls; an anti-corruption pledge the Commission grades "no progress… serious concern"; a Kočani accountability line that stops, on the families' documented account, below the serving officials; a billion-euro loan defended by an audit no one may read; and two doors kept shut by his own hand — the Bulgarian community's line in the constitution and the Albanian plurality's seat in his government — each defended with reasons stated in this dossier at full strength. He is neither the captor of his state's institutions nor their neutral steward: he works them hard where they obstruct him, reforms them where the referees watch, and keeps his clearest promises — the pensions, the refusal, the name — exactly where his voters can see them.

Free. Always free.
