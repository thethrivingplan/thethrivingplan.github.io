The Ruler's Test — Yamandú Orsi, President of Uruguay

Subject: Yamandú Orsi — President of Uruguay since 1 March 2025. A history teacher and former two-term Intendente (governor) of Canelones department (2015–2024), he won the 24 November 2024 runoff for the Frente Amplio with 52.0% of the vote against Álvaro Delgado of the Partido Nacional. His coalition holds a majority in the Senate (16 of 30 seats, plus the vice president's chair) but not in the Chamber of Representatives (48 of 99), so every law of his term has required negotiation. He is graded on roughly twenty months in office — a partial record, and the thinness of the file is stated where it matters. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The oversight machinery reached the president himself, and he submitted to it. After press analysis of his sworn asset declarations revealed in May 2026 that he had bought a Hyundai Santa Fe at a large discount days before taking office (detailed on the against shelf), the Junta de Transparencia y Ética Pública (JUTEP) — the state ethics board — took up six anonymous complaints, declared the case a priority because of "the institutional relevance of the person denounced," and pursued it through 2026, including a second request for patrimonial information from the Presidency in late October 2026 (JUTEP president Ana María Ferraris, via Canal 10 and press reporting, June–October 2026). Orsi did not block the process; on 1 June 2026 he published a video apology, announced he would donate the vehicle to the public education administration (ANEP) — done on 2 June, accepted unanimously by ANEP's board ten days later — and stated on the record that if an oversight body found he had erred, he would pay any difference and accept the consequences (Presidency video, 1 June 2026; El Observador, June 2026).
  • The audit court ruled against his own government's flagship land purchase. In 2025 the Tribunal de Cuentas (TCR) formally observed the National Colonization Institute's (INC) US$32.5 million purchase of the María Dolores estate in Florida, finding among other things that the spending exceeded the agency's budget allocation and that a direct purchase under the Colonization Law required four of five board votes, where only three had been cast. The purchase proceeded under the constitutional reiteration mechanism, but the observation stands on the public record, a Chamber of Representatives investigative commission examined the operation for three months, and on 11 November 2026 the Chamber voted — with votes from five parties — to remit the commission's reports to the Fiscalía (public prosecutor) for examination (TCR resolution as reported by El Observador, 2025; Chamber records, November 2026). Whether the purchase involved irregularities is now a question before prosecutors; no finding has been made, and the government's position that the operation complied with the law is recorded on the against shelf where it belongs.
  • No prosecution, investigation, or documented enforcement action against an opponent has been attributed to his direction, and no ally of his has been documented as shielded from one. The corruption file reaching his circle in twenty months is, on the documented record, thin — it consists principally of the vehicle purchase and the INC purchase, both graded here — and the dossier says so rather than inflating it.
  • The outcome measure stayed at the region's top: Uruguay scored 73 on Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2025, ranked 17th of 182 and the cleanest in Latin America (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released 10 February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The vehicle discount was a favor at the source, accepted by the ruler personally. On 21 February 2025, a week before his inauguration, the president-elect bought a new Hyundai Santa Fe for about US$54,000 against a market value of roughly US$79,000–87,000 — a discount of about US$25,000 from the dealer — paying in part with two vehicles, one of them a Renault that had been donated to the Frente Amplio campaign; Orsi's position is that the Renault was a personal donation to him as a candidate, a characterization the opposition disputes (El País and Radio Carve reporting from the JUTEP sworn declarations, May 2026; Búsqueda, June 2026). A former JUTEP president's assessment, on the record, is that accepting the discount was a mistake though not a violation of the law; the opposition's assessment is that the explanations were insufficient and raised further questions. The underlying conduct — a five-figure personal benefit from a private seller on the eve of taking power, disclosed only through journalists' reading of his asset declarations fifteen months later — is the kind of exception at the source this test measures, whatever its final legal characterization, which JUTEP had not issued at grading.
  • The ethics board that judges him is not built to be believed. JUTEP's three-member board is party-composed — two members aligned with the governing coalition and one with the opposition — and its rulings are non-binding. Opposition legislators stated on the record in 2026 that they had lost confidence in the body's impartiality and credibility, pointing to the pace of its handling of the president's case against its speed in others; an opposition local official stated that the governing-party majority voted in September 2026 to archive the complaint while the opposition member abstained — a characterization contested by the board's continued information requests in October 2026 (El Telégrafo, October 2026; Montevideo Portal, November 2026). The composition predates Orsi and applies to every government; but under him, as under his predecessors, the referee of the president's ethics is a body his own coalition can outvote, and that structural fact is part of the one-law record.
  • The INC purchase was pushed through on party votes against the audit court's reading of the agency's own law. The María Dolores purchase — 4,404 hectares for US$32.5 million, announced on 14 May 2025 during the wake of former president José Mujica and framed by the government as a tribute to rural workers — was approved by the INC board with only the governing-party members' votes, without, on the opposition's documented account, prior technical studies justifying the price or the dairy-colony purpose; the TCR's observation that the Colonization Law required four of five votes for a direct purchase is a finding of the state's own audit court, not an opposition claim (El País, May 2025; TCR, 2025). The government's answer — that the purchase followed the applicable norms and used available colonization funds, with Economy Minister Gabriel Oddone conceding publicly that the episode showed "things to improve" in how such investments are made — is recorded; the concession itself confirms the procedure was not clean. A politically symbolic purchase for the governing movement's heartland, executed over the audit court's objection, is favoritism in form even if prosecutors ultimately find no crime.
  • The outcome measure slipped: the CPI score fell from 76 (2024, rank 13th) to 73 (2025, rank 17th) in his first year — a small move, in the wrong direction (Transparency International, CPI 2024 and 2025).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, the prosecution, the audit bodies, media, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • The transfer of power itself was the region's model, and he has governed inside the same rules that produced it. The Electoral Court completed its final count on 28 November 2024 and proclaimed the Orsi–Cosse ticket on 4 December; the transition ran through a joint process with the outgoing administration from 27 November 2024, and on 1 March 2025 outgoing president Luis Lacalle Pou handed Orsi the presidential sash in Plaza Independencia, marking forty years of uninterrupted democracy and the ninth government since 1985 (Presidency of the Republic, 1 March 2025). No institution's role in that process was contested by the winner.
  • The audit court ruled against his government and was neither punished nor packed: the TCR's observation of the INC purchase (Test 1) was issued by a court whose majority was appointed under previous governments, its FA-aligned members dissenting — and the government answered through the constitutional reiteration channel and a parliamentary commission, not by attacking the court's standing (El Observador, 2025).
  • The legislature he does not control has repeatedly rewritten his bills, and he has taken the losses. Holding 48 of 99 deputies, his government passed its five-year budget and its 2026 budget review (Rendición de Cuentas) only by negotiating: the opposition bloc refused the Rendición, and the government bought the two votes of Cabildo Abierto with a written agreement, signed by Orsi personally, accepting that party's conditions — payment of the new child benefit by debit card only, and a rise in the benefit reduction for school non-attendance from 20% to 40% — and accepting modifications throughout the text; key articles passed 50–49 (Chamber of Representatives records and press reporting, August 2026). His competitiveness bill passed the Senate with support of all three main parties and still awaited the lower house at grading; his early-retirement bill was sent to a special commission of all parties (El Observador, November 2026). A president who must sign written concessions to a two-seat party to pass a budget is not gripping the legislature.
  • The central bank has run its own policy in public, including against the political convenience of the moment. Under president Guillermo Tolosa, the BCU held its inflation-targeting framework, reported inflation of 3.65% for 2025 — below its 4.5% target midpoint — and in January 2026 cut the policy rate by an unusually large 100 basis points to 6.5%, with Tolosa stating publicly that exchange-rate operations by the Economy Ministry were for the ministry to explain and that the bank's decisions answer "exclusively to its price-stability mandate" (BCU Copom statements, 2025–2026; Forbes Uruguay, January 2026). No public pressure on the bank by the president is documented. His government has proposed the opposite of capture in fiscal oversight: a new fiscal framework law (Law 20,446, December 2025) with a net-debt anchor of 65% of GDP and a proposal to grant the fiscal council greater autonomy precisely to insulate fiscal discipline from political pressure (Ministry of Economy and Finance, 2026; Fitch Ratings, 10 September 2026).
  • The prosecution he inherited is headed by an acting chief he did not choose and cannot remove. The Fiscalía General has been led on an acting basis by Mónica Ferrero since 2021 — a prosecutor who has the confidence of the traditional opposition parties — because appointing a titular Fiscal General requires a three-fifths Senate consensus that has eluded three governments. Orsi's government declared completing the consensus appointments (Fiscalía, Tribunal de Cuentas, Electoral Court, Supreme Court vacancies) a priority at the start of the term and was still negotiating them, exploring formulas the opposition might accept, in late 2026 — the prosecutor's office meanwhile processed appointments through open merit competitions with Senate consent in the ordinary way (El País/GDA reporting, 2026; Presidency legal filings, October–November 2026). A grip would have forced the appointment; the documented record is an unresolved negotiation.
  • Media operate without documented interference: the stories that damaged him most — the vehicle discount, the INC purchase, the homicide figures — were broken and sustained by El País, El Observador, Búsqueda, Radio Carve and la diaria, none of which has reported state retaliation; opposition parties hold departmental governments, including most of the country's interior, and campaign openly against him.

Evidence against:

  • The consensus appointments are still unmade twenty months in. The Fiscalía General remains under an acting head, and the Tribunal de Cuentas and Electoral Court remained incompletely composed at grading, because the cross-party agreements his government promised at the start of the term have not closed. This is incompleteness, not capture — the vacancies weaken the referees by leaving them interim — and it is recorded here as the test's open seam rather than as a grip.
  • The one referee that did judge him personally, JUTEP, is party-composed and outvotable by his coalition (Test 1). The design predates him and he did not create it; but he has not reformed it either, and his own case is the standing illustration of its weakness.
  • Where he does hold a majority — the Senate — his coalition has used it, including to sustain the INC purchase's political defense and to pass his bills on party votes before lower-house negotiation. That is ordinary majority use, documented here for completeness; no procedural abuse of the Senate is on the record.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Congress, unions, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His largest personal wobble was answered with an apology and a surrender of the object. Within a week of the vehicle story breaking, Orsi recorded a public apology video (1 June 2026), explained the purchase, donated the vehicle to public education the next day, and committed in advance to accept an oversight body's finding and pay any difference (Presidency, 1–2 June 2026). Whatever the underlying fault (Test 1), the response was concession, not denial.
  • On the inherited water contract, he adjusted the method to keep the goal lawful. His coalition had promised to cancel the previous government's Neptuno/Arazatí drinking-water project; breaking the contract signed on 23 January 2025, days before the handover, would have cost the state roughly US$100 million in termination penalties plus litigation. The government instead negotiated for months with the Aguas de Montevideo consortium and on 18 July 2025 reached agreement: Arazatí was left without effect, the same consortium will build a treatment plant at Aguas Corrientes and associated works instead, the contract value fell from US$364 million to US$213 million, and the government revived the long-shelved Casupá dam — the largest in the country — with CAF financing of US$130 million approved on 10 October 2025 (Secretariat of the Presidency and Environment Ministry, July 2025; CAF, October 2025). The campaign goal was met by renegotiation rather than rupture.
  • He has absorbed legislative defeats by concession rather than by decree or veto war: the Rendición de Cuentas passed only after he personally signed written concessions to Cabildo Abierto (Test 2), and the 2025–2029 budget law was modified in negotiation with his own coalition's factions and, in the Senate, with all-party support for parts of the package.
  • He has acknowledged the public verdict on his management in words: after his approval fell through the first half of 2026, Orsi said publicly in May 2026 that "if there are people who are not very satisfied, it is because something is not going well — you have to know how to correct course" (press reporting, May 2026).
  • His government accepted the electorate's 2024 plebiscite verdicts as binding on its room to move — including the rejection of the night-raids amendment his opponents wanted and of the pension plebiscite discussed in Test 5 — and has not attempted to govern around either result.

Evidence against:

  • The adjustment to his own coalition's central demand never came. Orsi convened a "Diálogo Social" on social security in 2025; when its work concluded, the government declined to incorporate the recommendation the union movement considered central — a state administrator for individual pension savings — keeping the private AFAP funds and proposing instead to let them invest more abroad. PIT-CNT president Marcelo Abdala's verdict was that "agreements are made to be kept"; the central called the decision a breach that damaged the dialogue's credibility and struck over it, in a partial general strike on 11 August 2026 — the third or fourth general strike of his term, following earlier ones on 12 August and 29 October 2025 (PIT-CNT statements and press reporting, July–August 2026). The government's defense — that the dialogue was consultative, not binding, and that the 2024 plebiscite's rejection freed it from the unions' program — is recorded; but a ruler who convenes a dialogue and then sets aside its headline product has answered the wobble by outwaiting it.
  • On security, the wobble was answered late. Public security was the voters' top concern when he was elected and his own program named it a "lighthouse" priority with fourteen measures in March 2025; the formal National Public Security Plan was presented only on 26 March 2026, more than a year into the term, after a 2025 national dialogue — and homicides rose through 2026 regardless (Test 5). Analysts across the coverage describe a government slow to respond on the issue the public ranks first; the plan's most visible early measure, patrolling with twelve US-donated Army armored vehicles, was debated inside his own coalition, with Orsi himself noting the legal framework for their use still needed resolving (Inter-American Dialogue, July–November 2026; Sociedad Media, November 2026).
  • On the INC purchase, the answer to the audit court's observation and a three-month parliamentary investigation was defense, not correction: the government maintained the purchase was lawful and a policy success, its economy minister conceding only that procedures could improve, while the file went to prosecutors unresolved (Test 1).
  • His approval collapse — from the mid-40s at inauguration to 24% approval against 57% disapproval in Cifra's October 2026 survey, the lowest second-year rating of any Uruguayan president since 2000, with Opción Consultores measuring 20% approval in November — is itself a wobble he has so far answered with acknowledgment rather than with changes of personnel or program on any documented scale (Cifra, October 2026; Opción, November 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — the poor and their children, Afro-Uruguayans, the rural interior, prisoners, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The headline count improved on his watch, and it improved most where his program aimed. Income poverty fell from 17.3% of persons in 2024 to 16.6% in 2025, and the first-semester 2026 reading fell further to 14.8%, with indigence at 1.2% in that reading against 1.7% for 2025 (National Statistics Institute, INE, 2026). Poverty among children under six fell from 32.2% to 29.1% in 2025 (INE via Ministry of Economy exposition, 2026). The labor count moved with it: unemployment fell from 8.2% to about 7.5% in 2025, real wages rose 2.3%, and inflation closed 2025 at 3.65%, the lowest in two decades — gains that accrue disproportionately to wage-earners and the poor (INE; BCU; press compilations, 2026).
  • The term's largest social reform was built for the worst-counted group and passed almost unanimously. The 2026 budget review created a unified transfer system for childhood and adolescence — merging family allowances, the Uruguay Social Card and the Crianza bonus — projected by the government to raise average benefits for ages zero to three by more than 80% and to cut poverty among the covered population by about 25%; its core received 97 of 99 votes in the Chamber of Representatives, with only the two Identidad Soberana deputies against (Chamber records and Presidency presentation, August 2026). It begins in 2027 with children born from 2025.
  • His water policy counts the metropolitan majority's basic need as a right to be secured in public hands: the cancellation of the Arazatí project — whose critics across academia and civil society had challenged its cost, its environmental basis, and its private-finance structure — and the revival of the Casupá dam are designed to guarantee drinking water for the metropolitan area to 2045, after the 2023 drought had exposed the system's fragility (Environment Ministry, 2025).
  • His opponents' voters were negotiated with, not governed against: the Rendición agreement paid Cabildo Abierto's price in the open; the EU–Mercosur agreement — the previous government's trade priority as much as his — was ratified by Uruguay first among Mercosur members, on 26 February 2026; and opposition parties continue to govern most departments and to sit in every institution (Parliament records, February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The children are still counted last in outcomes, whatever the program design. In 2025, 29.1% of children under six, 27.3% of those aged six to twelve, and 26.5% of adolescents lived in income poverty, against 6.0% of those over 65; four in ten children and adolescents live in households in monetary or multidimensional poverty — a structure the INE itself calls the persistent "infantilization of poverty," and the government's own figures show the under-six rate's 2025 fall was not statistically significant (INE, 2026). A president whose coalition named early childhood its first priority ends his second year with nearly one in three small children poor.
  • Afro-Uruguayans are counted at nearly double the rate of exclusion: poverty among Afro-descendant Uruguayans was 28.7% in 2025 against 14.9% among white Uruguayans (INE, 2026). No documented program of his government is aimed at closing that specific gap, and it did not close.
  • Indigence — the very bottom — rose in his first year, from 1.5% of persons in 2024 to 1.7% in 2025, even as overall poverty fell; the partial 2026 reading (1.2%) suggests recovery, but at grading the full-year record shows the poorest counted worse (INE, 2026). The 2026 budget review added roughly US$31 million for homelessness, childhood, education and security — a sum the unions and the opposition alike documented as small against the street population it addresses.
  • The lethal count is concentrated in the poor neighborhoods of the capital and rose on his watch: of 195 homicides in the first half of 2026, 114 were in Montevideo and 30 in Canelones, concentrated in districts such as Villa García and Flor de Maroñas; 74.9% involved firearms; and the prison system that holds the counted-out at the highest rate in South America — about 450 prisoners per 100,000 inhabitants, with documented overcrowding — remains the state's main answer where the killings occur (Interior Ministry statistics unit, July 2026; budget exposition, 2025).
  • The price of the Cabildo Abierto deal was paid by poor families: the written agreement raised the sanction for school non-attendance against the new child benefit from a 20% to a 40% reduction — a condition the government's critics, including within the education sector, documented as punishing the households least able to comply (agreement text and Chamber debate, August 2026).
  • Rural and northern Uruguay remains the other side of the average: poverty in Montevideo (18.7%) runs above the interior's (15.3%) in the 2025 reading, but multidimensional poverty in the rural north runs far above the urban south — about 30% against 7% on the measures cited in regional reporting — and the colonization policy that was supposed to be his rural instrument spent its first year in the courts and commissions of the María Dolores file (Test 1).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since March 2025, with dates; stated positions held or traded.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Water — kept, by renegotiation. The Frente Amplio program promised not to proceed with Neptuno/Arazatí and to secure supply through Casupá. Arazatí was cancelled by agreement on 18 July 2025, at a saving the government puts above US$150 million against the original structure, and Casupá has financing approved (CAF, 10 October 2025) and tendering under way (Test 3).
  • Pensions architecture — kept to his stated position. Orsi campaigned against the PIT-CNT plebiscite that would have abolished the AFAP funds and constitutionalized retirement at 60; the plebiscite failed on 27 October 2024 with about 39–41% voting yes. In office he held exactly the position he had campaigned on: the AFAPs stay ("it is not in our spirit to eliminate or statize the AFAPs," Orsi, April 2026), and in October 2026 his government sent Parliament a bill creating an early-retirement path at 60 with 30 years of contributions at a reduced replacement rate (36%), with a supplement for the lowest pensions — the product of the Diálogo Social, at an estimated cost of up to 0.16% of GDP (Economy Ministry and press reporting, October 2026). His union critics call the bill a continuation of his predecessor's 2023 reform (Law 20,130), which it is — and which is also what he told voters he would do.
  • Institutional and trade continuity — kept. He promised a government of agreements; the record shows a ratified EU–Mercosur agreement (26 February 2026, Uruguay first in the bloc), a competitiveness bill advanced with all-party Senate support, a new fiscal framework law with a debt anchor (Law 20,446, December 2025), and an IMF Article IV consultation (October 2025) that welcomed the upgraded fiscal and monetary frameworks. Growth and fiscal promises were framed modestly and the framework has been kept even where the numbers slipped (below).
  • Childhood priority — in motion, late. The unified child transfer (Test 4) is the concrete form of his campaign's first "lighthouse," legislated in August 2026 for a 2027 start.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Security — the central promise is unmet at twenty months. Orsi campaigned on community policing, attacking criminal economies and money laundering, and on reducing the violence that made security the electorate's first concern; his March 2025 program listed fourteen security measures among sixty-three priorities, and independent analysis of the 2025 budget law found several of them announced in the motives but not funded in the articles. The outcome: 369 homicides in 2025, slightly below 2024's 382 — then a reversal: 195 in the first half of 2026, up 6.6% year-on-year and the highest first half since 2015, and 312 in January–September 2026, up 13%, the highest since the 2018 peak (Interior Ministry, January and July 2026; press compilations, November 2026). Robberies and thefts fell throughout (−10.5% and −8.3% in 2025; −4.0% and −7.5% in the first half of 2026), and the government presents the plan as evidence-based and serious; but on the measure he asked to be judged by — lethal violence — the word is not kept. In Opción's November 2026 survey, 73% rated his security management negatively.
  • "No new taxes" — broken in form, contested in substance. Orsi campaigned explicitly against raising taxes. His 2025–2029 budget law raised taxes on low-value (de minimis) imports, raised the minimum real-estate investment for tax residency to roughly US$2 million, and introduced a 12% tax on foreign-source income for residents declining the standard tax holiday; the 2026 budget review added a tax on electric cars valued above US$19,000 (budget law and Rendición records, 2025–2026). The opposition's charge that this breaks the pledge is on the record; Economy Minister Oddone's rejection — that the government has not raised the broad tax burden and that these are targeted corrections — is also on the record. The dispute is named as contested; the literal campaign formulation was broader than what was delivered.
  • The wealth tax — refused to his own side. PIT-CNT proposed a 1% surcharge on the wealthiest 1% (about 25,000 people) to fund social spending; Oddone ruled it out ("not for this term of government") and Orsi confirmed the refusal (press reporting, 2026). A refusal is a kept position toward the markets and a broken expectation toward his own movement; it is recorded on this shelf because his coalition's program had kept the door open.
  • Growth and the deficit — the modest promises slipped. He inherited a central-government deficit of 3.2% of GDP in 2024 (IMF Article IV, October 2025); it widened to 3.7% in 2025 (4.2% excluding extraordinary social-security inflows), growth slowed to 1.8% in 2025 from 3.3% in 2024, and the 2026 growth estimate was cut from 2.2% in the budget law to 1.6% — with Fitch forecasting 1% — while the consolidation path was back-ended and slightly weakened (deficit targets eased by 0.1% of GDP a year) in the June 2026 budget review bill (MEF, June 2026; Fitch, 10 September 2026). The framework was kept; the trajectory promised inside it was not.
  • Police numbers — not verifiable as kept. Opposition campaigning has charged that his promise of 2,000 additional police has not been delivered; the budget analysis above found the security priorities only partially funded. The dossier records the charge and the partial-funding finding; it does not record a government figure demonstrating the promise kept.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: HOLDS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Yamandú Orsi is the first subject of this series whose grip test is passed outright, and the reason is structural before it is personal: he governs a country whose referees were never his to capture — an audit court that observed his own government's land purchase, an electoral court that proclaimed him and would proclaim his opponents, a central bank that lectures his ministry in public, a prosecutor's office still headed by an acting chief the opposition trusts — and on the documented record he has not tried to capture them, absorbing instead the daily costs of a legislature he does not control, down to signing written concessions to a two-seat party to pass a budget. His failures are of the ordinary democratic kind, and they are real: a five-figure personal discount accepted on the eve of power and disclosed only by journalists; a symbolic land purchase pushed through on party votes over the audit court's objection; a dialogue with his own union movement whose headline product he set aside; a child-poverty structure that has barely moved; and above all the promise on which he was elected — security — where lethal violence at twenty months is higher than when he started and his approval has fallen to the lowest second-year level in a quarter-century. The file is twenty months of a sixty-month term. What would move these grades is already visible: JUTEP's final word and the prosecutors' on María Dolores, the 2027 start of the child transfer, and whether the March 2026 security plan bends the homicide line it was written to bend.
