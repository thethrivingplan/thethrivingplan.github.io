The Ruler's Test — Horta Inta-A Na Man, Transitional President of Guinea-Bissau

Subject: Major-General Horta Inta-A Na Man (his name is also rendered Horta N'Tam and Horta Inta-a) — Transitional President of the Republic of Guinea-Bissau since 27 November 2025. The seat graded is the transitional presidency, and how it is held must be stated exactly. Horta, an army general presented by the military command as the army's chief of staff and described by AFP as a close associate of President Umaro Sissoco Embaló from the presidency's security circle, was installed by the High Military Command for the Restoration of National Security and Public Order — the body of officers, announced on state television by Brigadier General Dinis Incanha, head of the Military Office of the Presidency, that seized power on 26 November 2025. The seizure came one day before the National Electoral Commission (CNE) was due to announce the results of the presidential and legislative elections held on 23 November 2025, in which both the incumbent, Embaló, and the opposition candidate Fernando Dias da Costa had claimed victory. The High Military Command suspended the electoral process, the constitution, and the institutions, closed the borders, imposed a curfew, and arrested Embaló, Dias's backer Domingos Simões Pereira, the armed forces chief of general staff, the interior minister, and — on an international monitor's account — the head of the electoral commission. Horta governs through the High Military Command and a government headed since 28 November 2025 by Prime Minister Ilídio Vieira Té, who was Embaló's finance minister and the campaign director of Embaló's Madem G15 party in the interrupted election. Two things about this seat are unsettled on the documented record, and the dossier says so rather than settling them. First, who won the 23 November 2025 election is unknowable: on 2 December 2025 the CNE announced that armed men in balaclavas had confiscated its computers and destroyed the tally sheets and servers, leaving it unable ever to publish the results. Second, whether the takeover was staged with Embaló's collusion — alleged at the time by Dias, by the Popular Front civil-society coalition, by Senegalese Prime Minister Ousmane Sonko (who called it a "sham"), and by former Nigerian President Goodluck Jonathan, the ECOWAS election-observation head, who called it a "ceremonial coup" — remains an ALLEGATION, disputed by no less than the junta's own conduct in October 2026, when it blocked Embaló's return to the country, disarmed his presidential guard, and dismissed his remaining allies. No confirmed evidence of a prior arrangement appears in the record assembled here. Embaló (president 27 February 2020 – 26 November 2025) appears as context where the record Horta inherited bears directly on a test: Embaló had dissolved the opposition-controlled National People's Assembly on 4 December 2023 and never restored it; postponed the elections due in late 2024 by roughly a year; remained in office past both contested readings of his term's end (27 February 2025 on the opposition's reading; 4 September 2025 on the Supreme Court's ruling of 3 February 2025); threatened an ECOWAS mission with expulsion in March 2025, after which it left; and presided over a Supreme Court that disqualified the PAI Terra Ranka coalition on 25 September 2025 and Pereira personally on 14 October 2025, barring the main opposition party, the PAIGC, from the presidential race. Horta is graded on the twelve months since 27 November 2025. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. The mandate rule is stated for this seat in its exact form: Horta holds no electoral mandate, and none is graded against him as such — a transitional seat is not failed for lacking an election. What is graded is the holding, and on Test 5 the word he himself gave in place of a mandate: the one-year transition and the elections that were to end it. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them, and contested counts are printed separately and never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Releases did happen, in tranches, and one release ran against the seat's own prosecution. Six detained opposition figures were released on 23 December 2025. On 30 January 2026 Pereira was released from prison — though placed immediately under house arrest — and the army commander Jorgito Biague told AFP that "all those detained in connection with the coup have been released," with Pereira "the only one under house arrest" (AFP via Telegraph Nigeria, 31 January 2026). In July 2026, after Pereira had been returned to prison by a military court, a military investigating judge released him on medical grounds and he travelled to Portugal for treatment — a judicial decision taken against the direction of the military prosecution that had detained him (Reuters, 24 July 2026; Afrikactus, July 2026).
  • Discipline by decree fell on the seat's own founding camp as well as on its opponents. In October 2026 the transitional authorities dismissed Interior Minister Mamasaliu Embaló and Fisheries Minister Virgínia Correia (Decree 22/2026, 6 October 2026), dismissed the tax director Malam Sissé, removed the mayor of Bissau, disarmed presidential guards loyal to the ousted president, and dismissed Lieutenant-General Sadji Fati, Embaló's former defence minister and military adviser, from the head of the presidency's civil cabinet (AFP reporting, October 2026). Whatever else these removals were (Test 2), they were not a law that exempts the insider.
  • The seat's stated first priority was a law-enforcement one: Horta publicly made combating drug trafficking a top priority of the transition, in a country the UN Office on Drugs and Crime described in 2008 as "Africa's first narco-state" and where a 2025 Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime report found the cocaine market "more profitable than at any point in the country's history" (Genocide Watch country report, September 2026, citing GI-TOC 2025). No documented major interdiction or prosecution under that priority appears in the record assembled here; it is recorded as a stated priority, not a result.

Evidence against:

  • The detentions of the tenure map onto the seat's critics. The UN human rights office counted at least 18 people arbitrarily arrested in the aftermath of the takeover, including government officials, opposition figures, and magistrates (OHCHR, via Civicus Monitor, 2026). At the spontaneous protests of 27 November 2025 demanding the release of the election results, at least 50 people were detained on the Guinean Human Rights League's (LGDH) count, released two days later, some allegedly beaten in detention (LGDH, via Civicus Monitor). The two counts are printed separately, as the house rule requires; both describe arrests of people whose offence was opposition.
  • The Pereira file is the limiting case of one law for one man. Detained at the takeover in November 2025; released to house arrest on 30 January 2026 on suspicion of economic crimes; returned to prison on 10 July 2026 by a military court, now accused of a role in an alleged coup attempt of October 2025 — an allegation his family rejects and for which no trial date had been set; released again on 23 July 2026 for 90 days of medical treatment in Portugal, barred from political activity while abroad (Reuters, February and July 2026; Afrikactus, July 2026). The charges changed as the custody continued; the custody is the constant.
  • A regional court has now ruled on the detentions, and the state lost. On 5 October 2026 the ECOWAS Community Court of Justice held Guinea-Bissau liable for violations of the fundamental rights of five detained opposition-linked figures — Pereira; the PAIGC figures Octávio Lopes and Augusto Nansambe; the PRS leader Roberto Mbes'ba; and the elected legislator Marciano Indi — and awarded each 30 million CFA francs in compensation, as reported by the applicants' counsel (Law and Society Magazine, October 2026, reporting judgment ECW/CCJ/APP/05/26). No documented compliance with the judgment appears in the record assembled here.
  • The violence file runs one way and its investigations do not appear. On 31 March 2026 the body of rapper and civil-society activist Vigário Luís Balanta, spokesman of the Movimento Revolucionário Pó di Terra and an organiser of protests demanding constitutional order, was found in Ndam, some 30 km from Bissau, reportedly with signs of extreme violence; civil-society groups accused the transitional military authorities or military-linked forces of orchestrating the killing — an ALLEGATION, recorded as such — and the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights demanded a prompt, thorough, and impartial investigation (OHCHR, 2 April 2026; DW, April 2026). No investigation outcome appears in the record assembled here. Around the same file: the lawyer and commentator Augusto Nansambe was reportedly abducted on 28 December 2025; a Pó di Terra member, Iaia Carraque, was abducted by four armed men on 12 January 2026; a movement coordinator was assaulted at his home on 27 December 2025 and a MADEM-G15 movement leader beaten at his home on 21 December 2025 (LGDH and OCCRP reporting, via Civicus Monitor). None of these attacks is attributed to the authorities by a court; none is documented as solved. A young man, Luis Bedam, died after a beating allegedly by military officers, and the protests that followed on 7 January 2026 were dispersed with tear gas and, reportedly, live ammunition (RFI, via Civicus Monitor).
  • The founding act of the tenure was itself outside any one law: the ballots of a national election were seized and destroyed by armed men, and no one has been documented as charged for it. The CNE's own statement of 2 December 2025 records the destruction; the record assembled here contains no prosecution of the destroyers — the single most consequential crime against the law of the tenure, unanswered.

Why this grade: A seat that gives by one law must run the same law over its critics, its friends, and itself. This record runs detention, military courts, and shifting charges over the opposition's leader; raids and abductions over activists, none documented as investigated to a conclusion; a regional court's liability judgment unanswered; and the destruction of a national count unpunished — while its releases, though real, arrived in tranches under outside pressure and its inward discipline was dismissal by decree without stated reasons or process. The law here has an address, and it is the seat. FAILS. Helped: the detainees released in the December 2025 and January 2026 tranches, and Pereira's family, who got him to treatment in Portugal; the officers and officials promoted in the October 2026 reshuffle, including the nine officers reported promoted in the transition's ranks. Hurt: Pereira, Lopes, Nansambe, Mbes'ba, and Indi, detained in violation of their rights on the ECOWAS Court's ruling; Balanta, dead with his killing uninvestigated on this record, and his movement; the LGDH staff beaten at the Casa dos Direitos; every arrested protester of November 2025 – April 2026.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Two referees outside his reach still blew their whistles. The ECOWAS Court of Justice ruled against the state on 5 October 2026 (Test 1) — a referee the seat cannot grip, credited here the way this series credits the referees a ruler leaves untouched because they sit outside his jurisdiction. Domestically, the military investigating judge who released Pereira on medical grounds in July 2026 decided against the military prosecution's position (Reuters, 24 July 2026).
  • The media blackout was not made permanent. Private outlets suspended on 26 November 2025 gradually resumed within three days, and when some ten private radio stations were suspended on 31 March 2026 over unpaid licence fees, they were allowed back on air the same day while negotiations with the Private Radio Forum continued (RFI, via Civicus Monitor). The grip on the airwaves loosened twice; both loosenings are on this shelf at full strength.

Evidence against:

  • The electoral referee was not gripped but destroyed as a counting body. Soldiers were present at the CNE during the takeover; its head was arrested on a monitor's account; and on 2 December 2025 the commission itself announced that balaclava-clad armed men had confiscated computers from 45 staff and destroyed all tally sheets except Bissau's and the main results server (CNE statement via France 24 and Al Jazeera, 2 December 2025). An electoral commission that cannot count is not an independent referee; it is a room. The transitional charter then mandated new electoral officials — the referee reconstituted by the seat it referees (International IDEA country tracker, December 2025).
  • The judiciary's own oversight was abolished by the seat within eight days. On 4 December 2025 the High Military Command dissolved the Superior Council of the Judiciary and the following day appointed a new public prosecutor with expanded powers (International IDEA country tracker, December 2025). Local reporting in October 2026 also recorded the dismissal of the prosecutor general and of the president of the Court of Accounts — recorded here as reported, single-source local reporting, not independently confirmed in the record assembled here (Bissau-Guinean local reporting, October 2026).
  • The legislature is the seat's own creation. The National People's Assembly had already been dissolved by Embaló in December 2023 — an inheritance, stated as such — but the transition did not restore or elect one: the charter created a 65-member National Transition Council, including senior military officers, appointed under the High Military Command, which legislates in the Assembly's place — among other acts adopting the Law on Digital Integrity of 20 May 2026, a law against "false information" on the internet administered by a new digital verification commission (International IDEA; Civicus Monitor, 2026).
  • The media's suspension was the takeover's first act and speech control its standing rule. On the evening of 26 November 2025 Incanha announced that "all bodies of social communication" were suspended "until further notice"; soldiers had interrupted Radio Sol Mansi's broadcast by force, and only the state broadcaster remained on air carrying the military's announcements (RSF and CPJ reporting, November 2025). On 9 December 2025 the High Military Command threatened outlets with closure for disseminating calls to civil disobedience; on 9 January 2026 it banned "unauthorised" press conferences and prohibited anyone from making "unauthorised" public statements, warning defiers they would be "severely reprimanded" (DW and RFI reporting, December 2025 – January 2026). On referendum day, 30 August 2026, police detained two Capital FM journalists and a sound technician at the count; they were released after some hours (MFWA and LGDH, via Civicus Monitor).
  • The rights infrastructure was raided in person. Rapid Intervention Police invaded the Casa dos Direitos — the consortium housing the LGDH — on 22 December 2025 after a vigil for political prisoners, beating two staff members, and returned the next day to seal it; on 7 February 2026 dozens of heavily armed, hooded officers emptied the building again while the European Union's ambassador was visiting it, with no explanation given (RFI and LGDH, via Civicus Monitor).

Why this grade: Test 2 asks whether the referees keep their autonomy. Here the electoral commission's count was physically destroyed, the judiciary's council dissolved and its prosecutor replaced by the seat within days, the legislature replaced by an appointed council, and public speech placed under a permission regime — while the two documented loosenings (radios back on air; a military judge's release order) are exceptions inside a structure the seat owns. The referees that still function independently are the ones seated abroad. FAILS. Helped: the High Military Command and the transitional presidency, which now appoint or constitute every domestic referee; the state broadcaster, left alone on the air at the founding moment. Hurt: the CNE and its 45 staff whose computers were confiscated; the dissolved Superior Council of the Judiciary and the magistrates arrested at the takeover; private radio and its listeners; the LGDH and the Casa dos Direitos consortium; every citizen whose public statement now requires an authorisation that does not exist as a procedure.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The sharpest external pushback was engaged, not refused at the door. ECOWAS suspended Guinea-Bissau on 27 November 2025 and the AU Peace and Security Council suspended it from all Union activities on 28 November 2025, both demanding the electoral process be completed and detainees released (ECOWAS MSC communiqué, 27 November 2025; AU PSC communiqué of the 1315th meeting, 28 November 2025). The junta received the ECOWAS high-level mission led by Sierra Leone's President Julius Maada Bio in Bissau in December 2025 and talks continued (AP, 2 December 2025); it negotiated Embaló's release and departure to Senegal within a day rather than holding him; and it answered the detainee demand in tranches — six released on 23 December 2025, Pereira moved to house arrest on 30 January 2026 (Reuters and AFP reporting, December 2025 – January 2026).
  • The election delay of October 2026 was announced in the open, with reasons, by the referees' and the government's own mouths — not denied or decreed in silence. On 8 October 2026 the CNE's vice-president told reporters the conditions to organise elections by 6 December would not be met, and Prime Minister Té stated publicly that "the conditions for holding credible elections do not exist" (Reuters, 8 October 2026; The Gambia Journal, 9 October 2026). The narrow October decision itself is graded under the no-good-choice clause below: by then the candidacy deadline of 6 October had passed with no candidates registered, so the options actually open were a ballot with no candidates — a vote in name only — or an open delay. The delay was the least-harm door of that pair, and it was taken openly. What the clause does not cover is the trap's origin, which is graded where it belongs: the conditions failed inside a year of bans, detentions, and timetable management by the seat itself (Tests 1, 2, and 5).
  • When another option became available, one was at least formally entertained. ECOWAS designated Senegal's President Faye as mediator at its July 2026 summit in Freetown, and the transition engaged that mediation channel; ECOWAS's communiqué of 8 October 2026 supports the mediation and calls for wide consultations on the electoral calendar (ECOWAS Commission communiqué, 8 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The core demand of both regional bodies was refused, and refused in substance rather than in words. The AU Peace and Security Council's position was that restoring constitutional order required completing the November 2025 electoral process — not negotiations or interim arrangements — and called on the military to step aside and finalise the results (AU PSC, 28 November 2025, as summarised in Amani Africa's PSC programme note, March 2026). The results were never finalised; the tallies no longer existed. The seat answered the referees' peripheral demands (detainees, access) and declined the central one (the count) — the pattern this test exists to catch.
  • The street's pushback was answered with prohibition and force. Protests, strikes, and any activity deemed a "threat to peace and stability" were banned from the takeover onward; the planned nationwide protests of 31 December 2025 were prevented by saturation deployment; the protests after Balanta's funeral on 3 April 2026 were dispersed with tear gas and batons and dozens were arrested (Reuters; Africanews and DW, via Civicus Monitor).
  • The pushback from inside his own founding camp was answered by purge, not adjustment. When Embaló announced on 28 September 2026 that he would return to contest the December election, the High Military Command blocked his return on 1 October citing security conditions, then disarmed his guard and dismissed his allies through October (AFP reporting, October 2026). Whatever the merits against Embaló personally, a former head of state was barred from his own country by decree, without a court.
  • The most recent other option has no documented answer. On 6 November 2026 a transition agreement and national reconciliation pact — a time-bound transition, an independent electoral calendar, an end to political persecution, security-force reform — was publicly proposed by Father Augusto Mutna Tambá and circulated by civil society (proposal of 6 November 2026). It is recent, and it may yet be answered; no documented response by the transitional authorities appears in the record assembled here, and the absence is recorded as an absence, not a refusal.

Why this grade: The seat answers pushback the way a negotiator answers a siege: it concedes what costs little, in the open, on a schedule set by the pressure — detainees released in tranches, missions received, a delay announced with stated reasons — and it does not concede the thing the pushback is about. The count was never restored, the street was banned rather than answered, and the one pushback from its own camp was settled by disarmament and dismissal. More than refusal across the board; far less than answering. MIXED. Helped: ECOWAS and AU mediation, which kept a channel and a mediator in place; the detainees whose releases the pressure bought; Embaló personally, whose negotiated exit spared him the cell his own opponents received. Hurt: the voters of 23 November 2025, whose demand — count our ballots — was the one never answered; protesters met by bans, tear gas, and batons; Embaló, barred from returning to his own country by the men who removed him.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition, the regions, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • A national vote was held under the transition and its ballots were counted and published. The constitutional referendum of 30 August 2026 produced CNE provisional results announced on 1 September 2026 — Yes 383,116 (70.42%), No 160,954 (29.58%), 572,712 votes cast from 914,428 registered voters, turnout 62.63% (CNE provisional results, 1 September 2026). The count was published with regional breakdowns, and the "no" campaign's 30% stands in the official record unedited.
  • The government seated was mostly civilian — 22 ministers and five secretaries of state under Té, with five military officers in the security portfolios (Ecofin Agency, 1 December 2025) — and in February 2026 Horta wrote to ECOWAS offering an "inclusive" transitional government: three portfolios to the PAIGC and three to the PRS, with all political prisoners to be released (Reuters, 2 February 2026). The offer was real, and it is on this shelf; that Pereira and Dias refused offers to join the transitional government (30 January 2026) is also on this shelf — the refusal was theirs to make. The cabinet nonetheless remained, on AP's account of its installation, drawn mostly from allies of the ousted president, and no PAIGC or PRS minister appears in the cabinet as listed in mid-2026 (AP, 29 November 2025; CIA World Leaders listing, updated 4 June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The largest act of counting-out in this series' recent record is this seat's founding fact: an entire national electorate voted on 23 November 2025 and its ballots were destroyed before they were counted. Roughly 900,000 registered voters cast ballots whose tally sheets were confiscated and burned through — the CNE's word is destroyed — by armed men never documented as identified or charged (CNE statement, 2 December 2025). Every other exclusion in this dossier is an exclusion of a party or a person; this one is the exclusion of the count itself.
  • The referendum's count is contested, and the two counts are printed separately, never blended. Against the CNE's official turnout of 62.63%, the opposition coalitions PAI–Terra Ranka and API–Cabas Garandi and the Firkidja di Pubis movement claimed nationwide participation was below 10%; Reuters witnesses described polling stations in Bissau as largely empty, and some residents said they had not known the referendum was being held; the PAIGC, which boycotted, called the vote a "sham" and a "second coup d'état in less than a year" (Reuters, 1 September 2026; The Gambia Journal, September 2026; DW, September 2026). The dossier does not reconcile these figures; it records that the seat's one counted vote is a vote half the political country says did not happen as announced. The constitution that vote adopted concentrates power in the presidency — the president may appoint and dismiss the prime minister and government and dissolve parliament in a grave crisis — while cutting the Assembly from 102 seats to 65 and the constituencies from 29 to 12: fewer seats, counting fewer places, under a stronger single seat (BBC and Reuters reporting, September 2026).
  • The counted-out have names and categories. The PAIGC — the movement that won independence — was excluded from the 2025 presidential race by Embaló's court, then offered three portfolios by the men who kept it excluded, then watched its leader cycle through prison, house arrest, military prison, and medical exile (Tests 1 and 5). Protesters were counted as a security problem (Test 3). The activist Balanta was counted, in the end, as a body in Ndam (Test 1).
  • The poor are counted in the statistics and not in the transition's documented acts. Guinea-Bissau's cashew crop supplies roughly 90% of export earnings and income to some 80% of the rural population; over a fifth of the population is undernourished and 28% of children under five are stunted (2025 Global Hunger Index, via Genocide Watch, September 2026). All development-partner programming was disrupted or suspended after the takeover (ARIES Consult strategic report, April 2026), and a 2026 IMF review warned that coming austerity would fall first on vulnerable groups. These are environment as much as acts, and the shelf records them as such — but no documented transitional programme answering them appears in the record assembled here, in a year whose documented energies went to charters, referendums, and reshuffles.

Why this grade: A ruler counts everyone when the accounting includes the people who did not choose him. This seat began by un-counting an entire electorate, governs a political map from which the historic opposition is absent, held its one affirmative vote under a boycott with a turnout its opponents dispute by fifty points, and used that vote to shrink the legislature and strengthen the single seat. The inclusive offer and the published referendum figures are real, and they do not carry the shelf against the destroyed count. FAILS. Helped: the voters whose referendum ballots were counted and published, including the 160,954 "no" voters whose dissent stands in the official record; the civilian ministers and the Embaló-camp networks seated in the first cabinet. Hurt: the roughly 900,000 registered voters of November 2025 whose ballots were destroyed uncounted; the PAIGC and its voters, excluded from race, government, and count alike; rural cashew households under suspended development programmes; the protesters and activists counted as threats.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

The transition's own stated word — the charter, the decrees, the pledges — vs. the record since 27 November 2025, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The ineligibility bar — kept to date. The transitional charter, adopted in the first days of December 2025 and published on 9 December, bars both Horta and Prime Minister Té from participating in the post-transition elections (transitional charter, December 2025). As of this grading neither man has declared a candidacy, and the one candidacy the authorities moved against in 2026 was not Horta's but Embaló's (Test 3). This is the transition's most consequential self-limiting word, and it stands — recorded here so the record can grade it again if it moves.
  • The one-year frame was, for most of the year, worked as stated. The charter foresaw elections at the end of a one-year transition; on 21–22 January 2026 a decree set presidential and legislative elections for 6 December 2026 — days after the transition's first anniversary — declaring that "all the conditions for organising free, fair and transparent elections have been met" (High Military Command decree, 22 January 2026, via Africanews). A constitutional referendum to lay the legal framework was then actually held, on 30 August 2026, on the road the authorities had announced. The timetable was real paper, publicly set, and publicly followed until October 2026.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The founding word — elections ending the transition — was abandoned without a successor date. On 8 October 2026 the prime minister announced the 6 December elections could not be held; no new date was set then or by this grading, and the candidacy deadline of 6 October had already passed with no candidates registered (Reuters, 8 October 2026). The one-year transition proclaimed on 27 November 2025 therefore reaches its anniversary with the seat still held, the exit unscheduled, and the January decree's declaration — all conditions met — reversed by the same government's October declaration that the conditions do not exist. A word reversed by its own speaker, with no replacement word given, is the definition this test grades.
  • The prisoner-release pledge was broken by the seat's own hand. In his February 2026 letter to ECOWAS, Horta pledged that all political prisoners would be released (Reuters, 2 February 2026). In July 2026 the seat's military court returned the most prominent of those prisoners, Pereira, to prison on a new allegation (Test 1). The pledge and the re-imprisonment are both the seat's acts; the second cancels the first.
  • The inclusive-government word was offered in a form its addressees could not take, and then dropped. The February 2026 offer of three portfolios each to the PAIGC and PRS was made while the PAIGC's leader was under house arrest on the seat's own charges and its candidate's exclusion stood unremedied; both parties refused participation, and no inclusive government was formed (Reuters, 2 February 2026; refusal reported 30 January 2026). An offer designed to be refused is recorded as an offer, not as a kept word.
  • The stated anti-trafficking priority has no documented yield. The takeover's own justification — announced by Incanha on 26 November 2025 — was the discovery of a plot by politicians and a "well-known drug lord" to manipulate the election results; Horta then made fighting trafficking a stated top priority. Twelve months on, the record assembled here contains no documented prosecution of the alleged plot, no named drug lord charged, and no major interdiction attributed to the transition — while GI-TOC's assessment of the trade's profitability stands unrebutted. The justification that founded the seat remains, on this record, an assertion: an ALLEGATION the seat made and has not documented.

The four crossings, named (the two axes graded independently): 1. Kept his word and it helped: the charter's ineligibility bar — written, published, and so far honored; it is the one structural promise that made the transition's end conceivable, and it helped everyone asked to believe in that end. 2. Kept his word and it hurt: the takeover's announced programme of 26 November 2025 — suspension of the electoral process and of the institutions — was carried out exactly as announced. The keeping is not in doubt; the hurt is the destroyed count and the suspended constitution. 3. Went against his word and it helped: the High Military Command's first announcement claimed rule "until further notice," without limit; within two weeks the seat published a charter with a one-year limit and an ineligibility bar — a retreat from its own open-ended claim that helped, because it gave ECOWAS, the AU, and Guinea-Bissau's citizens a date and a limit to hold the seat to. The detainee releases of December 2025 – January 2026 likewise reversed the detention posture of the first days, to the detainees' benefit. 4. Broke his word and it hurt: the February 2026 pledge that all political prisoners would be released, broken by Pereira's re-imprisonment in July 2026; and the January 2026 decree's assurance that all conditions for free and fair elections were met, reversed in October 2026 when the elections were abandoned with no new date — the break that leaves the transition, at its first anniversary, without an end.

Why this grade: The word that founded this seat was not a platform — he had none, and the mandate rule does not grade him for that — it was a duration: one year, then elections, and neither he nor his prime minister on the ballot. The duration's machinery was genuinely built (charter, decree, referendum), and the ineligibility bar still stands. But at the anniversary the elections do not exist, no date replaces them, the prisoner pledge lies broken by his own military court, and the founding justification remains undocumented. A transition whose end-date word fails at the moment it falls due has failed the test the seat itself set. FAILS. Helped: the future field of candidates, by the ineligibility bar that still excludes the two most powerful men of the transition; ECOWAS and the AU, given a charter and a date to negotiate against for most of 2026. Hurt: Pereira, re-imprisoned under a broken pledge; every citizen and candidate who organised their year around 6 December 2026; the PAIGC and PRS, offered inclusion on terms that required blessing their own exclusion.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Horta Inta-A Na Man holds Guinea-Bissau the way the seat was taken: by command. Twelve months after the High Military Command stopped an election count the day before its announcement, the documented record is of a transition that built real machinery — a charter with a one-year limit and an ineligibility bar, an election date set by decree, a referendum held and published — and then, at the moment the machinery was to deliver its purpose, let the date collapse without a successor. On one law, the record is detentions mapped onto the opposition, a regional court's liability judgment for five of those detentions, an activist dead with his alleged killing uninvestigated on this record, and the destroyers of a national count uncharged. On gripping, every domestic referee is the seat's creature — the electoral commission reconstituted after its count was destroyed, the judiciary's council dissolved in the first week, the legislature an appointed council, public speech under an authorisation regime — and the referees that still rule against the seat sit abroad. On the wobble, the seat negotiated, received missions, released detainees in tranches under pressure, and announced its election delay openly with stated reasons — while refusing the one demand every pushback shared, the count, and answering the street with bans and batons. On counting, the founding act un-counted an entire electorate, and the one vote since was held under boycott with a turnout disputed by fifty points and used to shrink the legislature and strengthen the presidency. On his word, the crossings are all four present and named in Test 5: the bar kept and helping; the suspension kept and hurting; the open-ended claim withdrawn for a charter, against his word and helping; the prisoner pledge and the election date broken and hurting. Two questions the record cannot answer are left open on purpose, because this Test grades evidence: who won on 23 November 2025 — the ballots that would say are destroyed — and whether the takeover was staged with the man it removed, an ALLEGATION the October 2026 purge of that man's allies complicates but does not settle. The open files that will move these grades are named: a new election date and whether it is kept; compliance with the ECOWAS Court judgment of 5 October 2026; an investigation, or none, into the killing of Vigário Luís Balanta; and whether the ineligibility bar survives contact with an actual election.
