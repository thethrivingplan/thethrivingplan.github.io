First Lines — a tattoo practice simulator

Learn the knowledge and line discipline behind real tattooing, and practise it on drawn practice skin — never on a person. The page models needle depth as you draw: too shallow and the line heals faint, in the window and it holds, too deep and it blows out. Every grade is explained by the skin theory underneath it.

Everything here is original — the name, the practice designs, the words. The designs are simple line art drawn for this page, for practice skin only.

Safety and the law — read this first, at full size

  • Sanitation is the craft's foundation, not an extra. Tattooing breaks skin, so it can carry bloodborne pathogens — hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, and skin infections. Real practice means: a new single-use needle for every client, opened in front of them and dropped straight into a sharps container after; gloves; barrier film over the machine, clip cord, and work surfaces; surfaces disinfected between clients; ink poured into single-use caps, never back into the bottle; and formal bloodborne-pathogen training. Anything that touches blood is either single-use or sterilised in an autoclave. There are no shortcuts around this — it is the first skill, learned before line work.
  • Tattooing people requires training, an apprenticeship, and usually a licence. The rules vary by city, state, and country — many places require a licence, a registered premises, a minimum age for clients, and inspected sanitation. Working through a real apprenticeship under an experienced, licensed tattooist is how the craft is actually learned.
  • Practice belongs on practice skin and fruit — silicone practice skins, oranges, bananas, grapefruit — never on yourself, never on a friend, never on anyone "just to try." Untrained work on real skin scars people and spreads infection.
  • This game teaches knowledge and line discipline only. It qualifies nobody. It cannot teach the feel of real skin, real sanitation under pressure, or judgement about a real person's body. The only thing left after this page is the real apprenticeship — that is deliberate, and it is the honest limit of what a page can carry.

The practice skin

Pick a level, then draw on the skin. Tap a level button to choose it. In Trace mode the guide line is printed on the skin — follow it. In Freehand mode the guide fades to a whisper and you draw from the small reference, the way you'd work from a stencil you've already studied.

Depth is the game. Your needle depth comes from pressure and speed together: press harder and move slower and the needle sits deeper; press light and move fast and it rides shallow. If your pen or touch gives real pressure, the page uses it. If not, use the Pressure slider below — that is the plain fallback, and it works fine. Watch the depth gauge while you draw. The depth numbers are a teaching model, not a measurement of any real machine.

The ladder is a ramp, on purpose. Each rung adds exactly one new demand and nothing else: straight lines ask for even speed and depth; curves add changing direction; corners add turning without dwelling; the whole design adds endurance and working order; whip shading adds a stroke that changes as it travels. That ordering is how the craft is taught — master the demand in front of you before the next one is stacked on it. The Ramp setting below lets you choose the guided order or an open bench.

Skin layers — why depth is everything

A tattoo is not drawn on skin. It is placed inside it, and which layer receives the ink decides whether the work lasts a month or a lifetime. Tap a depth to see what the needle does there.

In the window (the dermis). The dermis barely renews itself, and its collagen mesh traps the ink particles. Immune cells called macrophages grab the pigment and, being long-lived and going nowhere, hold it in place for decades. This is the only layer where a tattoo is permanent and crisp — which is why the whole craft is aimed at a band only a millimetre or two deep.

Why too shallow fades

The epidermis is a conveyor belt: new cells are born at its base and push upward, shedding from the surface roughly every month. Ink parked there rides the belt out of the body. A shallow line can look perfect on the day and heal patchy and grey within weeks — the ink was never in the building, only on the escalator.

Why too deep blows out

Below the dermis sits fat — loose, soft, with no mesh to hold anything. Ink pushed into it migrates sideways along the loose tissue, and the line heals with a blurry halo: a blowout. Blowouts are permanent and cannot be pulled back out. Depth errors downward are forever; depth errors upward merely fade. That asymmetry is why good tattooists would rather re-touch a faint line than apologise for a blown one.

Needles, machines & ink

Needle groupings — what each bundle is for Tap to open

A tattoo "needle" is a bundle of fine pins soldered to a bar (or sealed in a cartridge). The grouping — how many pins, arranged how — decides what the bundle does well. The number is the pin count; the letters are the arrangement.

GroupingArrangementWhat it is for — and why
Round Liner (RL) — e.g. 3RL, 5RL, 9RLPins pulled into a tight round point Line work. A tight bundle deposits ink in a narrow track and the pins support each other, so the line stays crisp. Small counts (3RL) draw fine lines but carry little ink and punish shaky hands — every wobble shows. Large counts (9RL+) lay bold lines and carry more ink per pass.
Round Shader (RS)Round, but the pins sit slightly spread Soft shading and small fills. The spread bundle covers more skin per pass and lays ink less densely, which is exactly what a soft gradient wants — and exactly wrong for a crisp line.
FlatOne straight row of pins Bold one-pass lines and solid colour in strokes. The row acts like a chisel: strong coverage along the stroke, weaker across it, so it rewards drawing with the row.
Magnum (M1 / M2)Two rows of pins, woven (M1) or stacked (M2) Shading and packing colour over larger areas. Many pins spread the work, so large areas fill with fewer passes — and fewer passes means less trauma to the skin. Stacked magnums pack denser; woven magnums run softer.
Curved / Round Magnum (CM / RM)Two rows with the outer pins shorter — an arc Soft-edged shading. The arc means the edges of the bundle touch lighter than the middle, so gradients melt out with no hard edge. The same arc makes it a poor choice where a hard edge is the point.

The strategy trade-off: a smaller grouping gives control and fine detail but covers slowly and shows every tremor; a larger grouping covers fast and forgives small shakes but cannot turn tight corners or draw fine lines. Choosing the grouping is choosing which mistakes you can afford.

Machine types — coil, rotary, pen Tap to open
MachineHow it worksStrengthsCosts & trade-offs
CoilTwo electromagnetic coils snap an armature bar down and a spring throws it back — the classic buzzing machine, tuned by springs, gaps, and capacitor. A hard, punchy hit that drives big liner groupings and lays bold lines; endlessly tunable by a tattooist who knows the machine. Heavy, loud, and the tuning is a skill of its own — a badly tuned coil chews skin. Vibration tires the hand over long sessions.
RotaryAn electric motor turns a cam that pushes the needle in a smooth, even cycle. Consistent, quiet, light; the same even hit suits shading and colour packing; less to tune. The hit is softer and more uniform — some artists find bold lines need more care, and there is less of the coil's "give" feedback through the hand.
PenA rotary motor inside a pen-shaped body, using sealed cartridges. The grip feels like drawing, which shortens the learning curve for line control; cartridges swap groupings in seconds and are single-use by design. Cartridges cost more per session; the pen's light weight removes the ballast some artists use to steady a line. Wireless pens add battery weight and one more thing to run flat.

Voltage and hand speed are one decision, not two. The machine cycles the needle so many times per second; your hand moves the skin past it. Move too slowly for the voltage and each patch of skin takes hit after hit — overworked, chewed skin. Move too fast and the needle skips, leaving a dotted, faint line. The pair must match: that matching is most of what "a good line" physically is.

Ink & colour basics Tap to open

Tattoo ink is pigment — solid coloured particles — suspended in a carrier liquid (typically water, alcohol, and glycerin or similar) that gets the particles into the skin and then leaves. Traditional black is carbon-based. What stays in the dermis is the particle, and size is destiny: particles too small would be carried away by the immune system; tattoo pigments are large enough that a macrophage can swallow one but cannot digest or move it. A tattoo lasts because the ink is, quite literally, too big to remove.

  • You see healed colour through the epidermis, like looking through a tinted window. The skin's own tone sits over every colour, so the same ink reads differently on different skin — pale yellows and pastels can nearly vanish on deeper skin tones, while black and strong contrast hold on everyone. Honest tattooists design with the client's skin tone, not against it.
  • Contrast outlives colour. Over years, fine detail softens and light colours fade fastest. Designs built on strong dark-versus-light structure age gracefully; designs built on subtle colour differences blur into each other. This is the whole theory behind "bold will hold."
  • Sun is ink's enemy. Ultraviolet light breaks pigment particles down into smaller pieces — small enough for the immune system to finally carry away. Faded old tattoos are, in part, sun-eaten.
  • Fresh ink colour lies. On the day, ink also sits in the epidermis and in irritated, reddened skin, so fresh work looks bolder and brighter than it will heal. Judging a tattoo before it has healed (two to four weeks) is judging a draft.

Know-how — the discipline behind the line

Line discipline — one confident pass Tap to open

Skin is not paper. Every pass of the needle is a small injury, and skin answers injury with swelling, bleeding, and toughness. A line laid in one confident pass at the right depth heals crisp. A line scratched in with three timid re-passes is three injuries stacked on one track: the skin swells, the stencil blurs under plasma, later passes land in damaged tissue that holds ink unevenly, and the heal comes out scarred and patchy. That is why the game counts your re-passes against you — going back over a line is sometimes necessary in real work, but it is never free.

Two honest strategies, with their trade-offs:

  • Commit: pull the full line in one pass at matched speed and depth. Best heal, cleanest result — and no eraser. A wobble is in the skin. This is the strategy the levels train.
  • Build: lay a first pass slightly light, then strengthen weak sections once. Forgiving for beginners and for skin that takes ink unevenly — but every extra pass is extra trauma, and "slightly light" easily becomes "three scratchy passes." Building works only with the discipline to stop.

Pull lines toward your body where you can, in sections your hand can travel without re-gripping, and breathe out through the pull — the same reason marksmen fire on the exhale: a moving ribcage is a moving hand.

Stretching the skin — why your other hand matters Tap to open

Loose skin gives under the needle: it bounces, drags, and lets the line wander, and the needle's depth varies as the surface moves. Stretched taut — usually a three-point stretch between thumb, finger, and the side of the hand — the skin becomes a firm, flat drum: the needle enters cleanly, depth stays even, and the line goes where it is aimed. Stretch also shows the tattooist the skin's natural grain, because a design must sit with how the skin moves on the body, not against it. In the game, unstretched skin adds drift to your stroke; stretched skin holds still. Try the same curve both ways and read your consistency score — that difference is the lesson.

Whip shading — and why direction is the whole trick Tap to open

Whip shading lays a gradient with a flicking stroke: the needle enters the skin at the dark end of the gradient and whips out — accelerating and lifting — so the dots of ink start dense and thin out to nothing. The taper comes from physics, not magic: as the stroke speeds up and the needle rides shallower, less ink lands per millimetre. Whip away from the dark area, always in the direction the gradient should fade. Whipping the wrong way piles ink at the light end and buries the gradient. Level 5 grades exactly this: stroke direction and taper. The alternative, pendulum shading, swings the needle back and forth in an arc for a softer, more even field — gentler on skin over large areas, but slower to build a strong gradient and easier to overwork if you keep swinging over the same patch.

Aftercare — the principles and their reasons Tap to open

A fresh tattoo is an open wound with ink in it, and the heal decides the final quality as much as the needle work did. The standard principles, each with its reason:

  • Keep it clean, gently. Wash with clean hands and mild soap — the wound is open to bacteria for the first days, and infection in the dermis destroys ink and scars.
  • Moisturise thin, not thick. A thin film keeps the surface supple so it doesn't crack; a thick suffocating layer traps moisture and bacteria and can draw ink out. The skin needs to breathe to close.
  • Never pick or scratch. Flaking skin and scabs are still attached to healing dermis; pulling them pulls ink particles out with them, leaving pale gaps. Itching means healing — pat, don't scratch.
  • No soaking, no sun while healing. Long soaks soften scabs off early (same ink loss as picking) and pools and baths are bacterial soups. Sun burns healing skin fast and starts breaking pigment down before the tattoo has even settled.
  • After it heals: sunscreen is the maintenance. For the UV reason above — every unprotected summer spends a little of the tattoo.

This is general knowledge about how tattoos heal, printed so the reasoning is visible. Real aftercare instructions come from the licensed tattooist who did the work and from local health guidance — follow those over any page, including this one.

Variant — not the standard rule: Open bench removes the order and lets you start on any rung. The guided ramp is the standard because each rung adds exactly one new demand; jumping ahead stacks demands you haven't isolated yet.

Steady hand — hand fatigue

With fatigue on, a long piece drains your steadiness and wobble grows into the line, the way a real hand tires — and real skin tires too: it swells over a long session and stops taking ink cleanly, which is one reason big pieces are split across sittings. The Break button rests your hand back to full. Attempts made with fatigue on say so in your record, with the steadiness you finished on.
