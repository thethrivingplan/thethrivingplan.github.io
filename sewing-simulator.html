Honest limits, at full size

This page teaches knowledge and planning — it cannot teach your hands. The feel of fabric feeding, the weight of scissors, the moment a needle starts to bend: those come only from real fabric and a real machine. The real door is a real sewing session — a borrowed machine, a library makerspace, a class, a friend's kitchen table. Use this page to arrive knowing why everything works, so your real practice time teaches your hands instead of your confusion.

Safety, plainly: a sewing machine needle moves fast enough to pierce a fingernail — fingers stay behind the presser foot, never in front of the needle, and the machine is off (or the handwheel locked) while you thread it. Rotary cutters are razor discs: blade closed the second a cut ends, always cut away from your body, and treat a dropped cutter like a dropped knife. Pins and needles live in a pincushion, never in clothing, carpet, or a mouth — a lost needle is found before anyone walks barefoot. This simulator has blunt pixels; the real tools do not.

What's at stake here — and it isn't money

There is no money anywhere in this game: no coins, no shop, no selling your work. Sewing's real stakes were never money. The stake is trust. You start as the new pair of hands. Every piece that comes back holding earns you the next, harder piece — a neighbour's cloth, a bag that has to carry weight, a favourite garment somebody can't replace. Work that fails sends trust the other way, exactly like real mending. The reward is usefulness: the finished thing itself, and what it now does — stated plainly on every grade card, from your actual grades.

    Strength is this page's honest model: stitch type sets the ceiling, needle and tension matches scale it, and your line and spacing quality finish it. A real seam's strength also depends on thread, fabric wear, and washing — things only real cloth can tell you.

    The four stitches — and why each one works Tap to open · tap again to close

    Running stitch — the sprinter

    The needle dives down and surfaces a stitch-length later, over and over, so the thread shows as dashes on top and dashes underneath, never both at once. Why it works: each dash lies flat and carries load only along its own length; nothing crosses or locks anything. Why it's weak: cut or wear through one dash and the thread can ladder out along the whole seam, because every stitch depends on the thread staying unbroken — there's no knot anywhere. Real uses: basting (temporary holding, meant to be pulled out), gathering fabric into ruffles (the dashes slide, which is exactly what gathering needs), quick tacking, and visible decorative sashiko-style work where the dash pattern is the point.

    Backstitch — the lock

    Each new stitch goes back to the end of the previous one before stepping forward, so on top the thread forms one unbroken line and underneath each stitch overlaps its neighbour. Why it locks: every stitch is anchored by the one before it — the thread doubles back through the same hole, so a break in one place can't unzip the seam; the damage stays local. The trade-off: it uses roughly twice the thread and takes longer per inch. Real uses: any seam that must hold under stress by hand — repairs, tote straps, garment seams where no machine is available. It is the strongest common hand stitch, and it is what a machine's straight stitch imitates: a machine locks differently (two threads looped together, below), but the goal is the same — no single point of failure.

    Zigzag — the stretcher

    The needle swings side to side as the fabric feeds, laying a row of diagonal stitches. Why it works: the diagonals act like a row of tiny springs — a knit can stretch and the stitches give instead of snapping, where a straight stitch would pop. The swing also straddles a raw fabric edge, wrapping it so the edge fibres can't fray out. The trade-off: weaker in pure pull along the seam than a straight or back stitch, uses more thread, and on thin fabric a dense zigzag can tunnel and pucker it. Real uses: sewing knits and stretch fabric, finishing raw edges, appliqué (satin stitch is just a zigzag with the length turned nearly to zero), and bar tacks — short, dense zigzags that reinforce pocket corners and belt loops.

    Blind hem — the invisible one

    The hem is folded and the needle takes a long glide inside the fold, then catches just one or two threads of the main fabric, over and over. Why it's invisible: the long glides hide inside the fold where nobody sees them, and the tiny catch is too small to show as more than a pinprick dimple on the outside. Why it's delicate: each catch holds by a thread or two of fabric — it's a hem, not a structural seam, and a hard tug on one catch can pull the whole hem down like a zipper. Real uses: trouser and skirt hems, curtains, any edge where a visible stitch line would spoil the look. Done by hand for control, or by machine with a blind-hem foot that folds as it feeds.

    How a machine stitch actually locks

    A machine uses two threads. The needle thread dives through the fabric carrying a loop; below, a rotating hook catches that loop and wraps it around the bobbin thread; then the take-up lever pulls everything snug. The two threads interlock inside the fabric layers — that's the lockstitch. It can't unravel from one end the way a single-thread chain can, which is why nearly every home machine is built around it. This is also the whole basis of tension theory, below: a good stitch is the one where the interlock lands in the middle of the fabric, hidden and balanced.

    Seam allowances and fabric grain — the invisible architecture Tap to open · tap again to close

    Why seam allowances exist

    The seam allowance is the strip of fabric between your stitch line and the cut edge — most patterns use ⅝ inch (15 mm) for garments, ¼ inch (6 mm) for quilting. It exists for four reasons. Strength: stitches too close to a cut edge tear out through it, because there isn't enough fabric — and enough woven threads — on the edge side to hold the load. Fray room: woven fabric unravels from a cut edge; the allowance is the sacrificial zone that can fray a little without ever reaching the stitches. Fitting: a generous allowance is fabric you can let out later; a seam sewn at ¼ inch can never be loosened. Accuracy: the allowance gives the presser foot and feed dogs something to grip, and gives you a guide edge to steer by — most sewers steer by watching the fabric edge against a guide, not by watching the needle. In this game, your guide line sits a fixed distance from the cloth's edge for exactly that reason.

    Grain: straight, cross, and bias

    Woven fabric is a grid: lengthwise warp threads run along the roll, crosswise weft threads run across it. The straight grain (along the warp) barely stretches — warp threads are held taut on the loom and are the stronger, stabler direction. The cross grain gives a little. At 45° between them lies the bias, where the grid can shear like a trellis — bias-cut fabric drapes, flows, and stretches noticeably even though no thread in it is elastic. Why a garment hangs differently: a skirt cut on the straight grain hangs in crisp folds and keeps its length; the same skirt cut on the bias clings, swirls, and grows longer as the grid shears under its own weight (bias garments are hung for a day before hemming, so the growth happens before the hem is levelled, not after). Pattern pieces carry a grainline arrow because rotating a piece even a few degrees off-grain makes trouser legs twist and hems flare unevenly forever — the fabric's grid, not the sewing, decides. Knits are different: a knit is loops, not a grid; it stretches across its width far more than along its length, which is why T-shirt patterns align the stretch around the body.

    Which stitch for which job — the pairing logic

    Match the stitch to the load and the fabric, the way you'd match a sauce to a pasta shape: structural seam in woven fabric → straight/backstitch (maximum hold, no stretch needed because the fabric has none). Seam in a knit → zigzag or a stretch stitch (the seam must stretch as far as the fabric or it pops the first time the garment moves). Raw edge → zigzag over the edge, or a French seam that encloses it (fraying is the enemy, so wrap or enclose). Hem that must not show → blind hem (invisibility beats strength here because a hem carries almost no load). Temporary hold → running stitch / basting (weakness is the feature: it must come out cleanly). When a job fails in real sewing, the post-mortem is almost always a pairing failure — strong stitch, wrong fabric behaviour — not a sewing failure.

    The machine — parts, threading, and why the path matters Tap to open · tap again to close
    1. Spool pin. Holds the thread spool. The thread must unwind freely — a catching spool jerks the tension with every snag.
    2. Thread guide. First waypoint; it aims the thread into the tension discs at the right angle so the discs grip evenly.
    3. Tension discs. Two spring-loaded plates the thread passes between. The dial squeezes or releases them — this is the top half of tension theory.
    4. Take-up lever. The arm that rises and falls with every stitch. It pulls the stitch tight at the top of its stroke — and if you thread the machine with the lever down and miss its eye, the thread has no pull, so the first stitches explode into a nest underneath. Always thread with the lever fully up.
    5. Needle. Carries the thread through the fabric. Its eye sits just above the point; the long groove down the front protects the thread from friction as it passes through the cloth.
    6. Presser foot. Holds the fabric against the feed dogs. Its pressure is a quiet variable: too little and fabric skates, too much and stretchy fabric gets stretched as it sews.
    7. Feed dogs. The toothed bars under the plate. They rise, grip the fabric, pull it back one stitch length, and drop — that cycle is the machine's pace, and stitch length is simply how far they travel per cycle. You steer; they feed. Pulling the fabric through yourself fights them and bends needles.
    8. Handwheel. Turns the whole mechanism by hand — always toward you, because turning it backward tangles the loop timing the lockstitch depends on.
    9. Stitch dial. Sets stitch length/width. Longer stitches = fewer holes and weaker per inch but faster and easier to unpick; shorter = stronger and neater, more holes in the fabric.
    10. Bobbin (under the plate, gold cover in the diagram). The small spool that supplies the bottom thread of the lockstitch.

    Threading, in order, with the why of each step

    Presser foot up first — lifting the foot opens the tension discs, so the thread can seat between them instead of riding on top (threading with the foot down is the single most common cause of "my machine is broken": the thread never enters the discs, tension is zero, and the underside becomes a bird's nest). Then: spool → guide → down to the tension discs → up through the take-up lever's eye (lever at its highest, so the eye is reachable and the thread gets its full pull) → down the front guide → through the needle eye, front to back or as your machine directs. Leave a tail of thread pulled to the back, so the first stitch has something to hold instead of being sucked down into the bobbin area.

    Tension theory and troubleshooting — reading what the cloth tells you Tap to open · tap again to close

    The theory: a tug-of-war that should end in a draw

    Every lockstitch is a tug-of-war between the top thread (held back by the tension discs) and the bobbin thread (held back by the bobbin case spring). When the take-up lever pulls the stitch snug, the two threads interlock wherever their pulls balance. Balanced: the interlock hides in the middle of the fabric — the stitch looks the same top and bottom, and the seam can flex without either thread taking the whole load. Top tension too high (or bobbin too loose): the top thread wins, the interlock is dragged to the top surface, bobbin thread shows as dots on top, and the fabric puckers along the seam because the winning thread is effectively shorter than the fabric it sews. Top tension too low: the bobbin wins, loops of top thread hang underneath, and the seam feels loose and grins open under strain. Thick fabric needs the balance point to travel further, fine fabric needs a gentler pull overall — which is why tension is set per fabric, and why you test on a scrap of the same fabric, folded to the same number of layers, before touching the real piece.

    What you seeWhat it meansThe fix — and why
    Puckering along the seamThread is pulling shorter than the fabric — tension too high, or stitch length too short for the fabricLower the tension a step, or lengthen the stitch. Short stitches pack more thread pulls into each inch; on light fabric that alone can pucker it.
    Loose loops / a nest underneathTop thread has almost no tension — usually it never entered the tension discsRe-thread with the presser foot UP and the take-up lever at the top. Changing the dial won't help a thread that isn't in the discs at all.
    Bobbin thread dots showing on topTop tension winning the tug-of-warLower top tension one step at a time, testing on scrap. Small steps: the balance point is narrow.
    Top thread visible underneathBobbin side winningRaise top tension a step. (Bobbin tension exists too, but it's set with a tiny screw and is the last resort, not the first.)
    Skipped stitchesThe hook missed the needle's loop — needle bent, wrong needle for the fabric, or feeding too fast through thicknessFit a fresh, correct needle first (a knit needs a ballpoint that slips between loops instead of spearing them, which is what makes the loop form wrong). Slow down through thick seams.
    Thread keeps snappingToo much friction or pull somewhere: high tension, a burr on the needle eye, old thread, or the thread path snaggingRe-thread fully, change the needle, then lower tension. Needles are consumables — a needle that's sewn for hours is dull and damages both thread and fabric.
    Seam "grins" open when pulledStitches too long for the load, or tension so low the interlock sits on the surfaceShorten the stitch (more interlocks per inch share the load) and check the balance on scrap.

    Golden rule of troubleshooting: change one thing, sew a test line on scrap, read it, repeat. Changing two things at once destroys the evidence — you learn that something worked and nothing about why.

    Needle numbers, decoded

    Needle sizes are written like 80/12: the first number is the shaft diameter in hundredths of a millimetre (80 = 0.8 mm, European), the second is the American size for the same needle. Bigger number = thicker, stronger needle for heavier fabric. A needle that's too fine for denim flexes and snaps; too coarse for silk, it punches holes the fabric can't close. Needles are the cheapest consumable in sewing and the cause of a wildly disproportionate share of "machine problems" — changing the needle is step one of almost every real diagnosis.

    Thread, briefly

    All-purpose polyester thread is the workhorse: strong, slightly elastic, and forgiving. Cotton thread is weaker and is chosen for quilting, where it should age and soften with cotton fabric. The rule of pairing: thread should be the same weight or lighter than the fabric expects — heavy thread in fine fabric acts like rope through tissue, and the seam fails at the holes. In this game, thread is a given; in real sewing, it's the third leg of the match.

    Strategies — more than one way, with the trade-offs printed Tap to open · tap again to close

    Holding fabric before you sew it

    Pins, perpendicular to the seam: fast and precise; the machine can sew over perpendicular pins slowly (though hitting one can break a needle — many sewers pull each pin just before the foot reaches it). Pins can distort knits and leave holes in silk and leather. Clips: no holes, great for thick layers and knits, but bulky near the needle and they can't hold a curve as precisely. Hand-basting (long running stitches): the slowest and the most control — nothing shifts, nothing to hit, and it holds slippery or matched-pattern seams that pins let creep. The trade is time now versus unpicking later; on a repair or a slippery fabric, basting usually wins on total time.

    Securing the ends of a seam

    A machine seam is locked in the middle but naked at both ends — the first and last stitches can pull out. Backtack (sew backward 3–4 stitches over the seam's ends): quick, standard, slightly bulky. Knot the thread tails by hand: neatest finish, slowest, best where the seam end will show. By hand with backstitch: the lock is built into every stitch, so ends just need a small knot or a few stitches worked back over themselves. Skipping end-securing entirely is the classic beginner failure: the seam passes inspection and unzips in the first wash.

    Speed strategy

    Slow: maximum steering accuracy, and on thick fabric it gives the needle time to form each loop — the fix for skipped stitches. Costs time and can make long seams wander if your attention fades. Fast: on a long straight seam, momentum and the feed dogs actually help the line stay true — but every wobble is amplified, corrections come too late, and thick or slippery fabric punishes you. Most real sewers sew slow at the start, the end, and every curve, and open up only on the safe straights — exactly the rhythm this game's speed setting lets you practise.

    When it goes wrong: the seam ripper is a tool, not a verdict

    Every experienced sewer unpicks constantly. The strategy: stop early (a wandering line caught at two inches costs two inches of unpicking; caught at twenty, it costs the seam), rip from the bobbin-thread side where the loops are visible, and re-sew once with the lesson in hand. Pressing matters too: press every seam flat with an iron before judging it — half of all "bad seams" are good seams that were never pressed, because pressing sets the interlock into the fabric and melts the fibres' memory of being handled.
