The Ruler's Test — Luis Abinader, President of the Dominican Republic

Subject: Luis Rodolfo Abinader Corona — President of the Dominican Republic since 16 August 2020, of the Modern Revolutionary Party (PRM). He won the July 2020 election on an anti-corruption platform of "change" (el cambio), ending sixteen years of Dominican Liberation Party (PLD) government, and was re-elected in the first round on 19 May 2024 with 57.44% of the vote. As of November 2026 he is in the second half of his second term, which ends 16 August 2028; under the constitutional reform he himself promoted in October 2024 he is permanently barred from running again, and an open contest to succeed him is under way inside his own party. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His founding act was to hand the prosecution power to someone with no loyalty to him. On 16 August 2020, the day of his inauguration, Abinader appointed Miriam Germán Brito — a former Supreme Court judge who had famously clashed with the previous attorney general and belonged to no party — as Attorney General, with career prosecutor Yeni Berenice Reynoso as her deputy for persecution. The PRM's stated commitment, made before taking office, was "zero impunity, sanction for corruption, no clean slate," applied to "past, present and future" corruption (PRM statements, August 2020; inauguration-day appointments, 16 August 2020).
  • Under that prosecution service, the law reached the summit of the previous ruling circle and stayed there through verdict. In Operación Antipulpo, a court convicted Juan Alexis Medina Sánchez — brother of former president Danilo Medina — of bribery and money laundering and sentenced him to seven years in prison, with confiscation of the network's companies and assets; the prosecution announced it would appeal the acquittals of two co-defendants, including another Medina sibling (court sentence, August 2025; Public Ministry statements, August 2025). The former attorney general himself, Jean Alain Rodríguez, was arrested in Operación Medusa in June 2021 and, after years of preventive detention and house arrest, is on trial for an alleged diversion of more than RD$6,000 million — the charges are ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated, and the trial remained procedurally stalled by defense recusals as of November 2026 (court reporting, November 2026).
  • The law also reached his own party's elected officials. Rosa Amalia Pilarte, a sitting PRM deputy, was prosecuted by the Public Ministry and convicted by the Supreme Court's Second Chamber on 28 May 2024 of laundering drug-trafficking proceeds, sentenced to five years; the full court ratified the conviction on 29 August 2025 and she was taken into custody in September 2025 to serve it, with confiscation of assets valued in the billions of pesos (Supreme Court sentences SCJ-SS-24-0592 and SCJ-PL25-00009; Public Ministry, September 2025). Prosecutors also linked two other PRM congressmen to the Operación Falcón drug-trafficking network — those links remain ALLEGATIONs (InSight Crime, 2023).
  • The largest corruption case of his own administration was opened from inside it. In the SeNaSa case — an alleged fraud against the state health insurer that prosecutors estimate above RD$15,000 million — Abinader ordered an internal review in November 2024, publicly confirmed the investigation on 9 September 2025, and the Presidency delivered its irregularities file to the Attorney General days later. He had already dismissed SeNaSa's director, Santiago Hazim — his own appointee — in August 2025; Hazim was arrested in December 2025 under Operación Cobra and held in preventive detention, with dozens more arrested in a second phase in September 2026. The fraud itself is an ALLEGATION against Hazim and the co-defendants, unadjudicated (presidential statements, September 2025; Public Ministry and court reporting, December 2025–November 2026).
  • When scandal touched his cabinet's edge early, the official fell: his Minister of the Presidency, Lisandro Macarrulla, left the government in July 2022 after his son was charged in Operación Medusa (the son later reached a plea agreement with prosecutors), and his Minister of Youth took leave in October 2020 and resigned after her sworn asset declaration was questioned, though no charges resulted (government announcements and press reporting, 2020–2022).

Evidence against:

  • For most of his tenure, the hammer fell overwhelmingly on one side. The mega-cases of 2020–2024 — Antipulpo, Coral and Coral 5G, Medusa, Calamar — targeted the Medina-era circle almost exclusively, and the PLD's standing charge, repeated by its leaders throughout, is that the prosecution service functions as an instrument of political persecution. That charge is an interested party's characterization, but the distribution of cases in the first term gives it a factual basis that the Senasa case, arriving only in his fifth year, only partly answers (case records, 2020–2026; PLD statements).
  • The biggest opposition case also showed the limits of the prosecution's evidence. In Operación Calamar, after prosecutors sought trial in March 2026 against former ministers Gonzalo Castillo, Donald Guerrero and José Ramón Peralta over an alleged RD$19,000–41,000 million fraud (figures vary by filing), the instructing judge ruled in May 2026 that the evidence was insufficient and dismissed the charges against Castillo and Peralta, sending other defendants to trial; the Public Ministry appealed, and the appeal was still pending in November 2026. The defendants maintain the case was political lawfare; prosecutors maintain the evidence was misjudged. Both positions are on the record; the dismissal is a court finding, not an allegation (court reporting, May–November 2026).
  • Danilo Medina himself was never charged, while his brother, his sister (acquitted), his ministers and his attorney general were — a pattern critics across the spectrum read in opposite directions: as restraint toward a former president, or as prosecution that stops exactly at the line of maximum political cost. No document establishes either motive; the pattern itself is the documented fact.
  • The SeNaSa file cuts both ways. The alleged scheme operated for years inside his own government's flagship social institution, through audits and controls his administration owned, before the presidency's internal review caught it in late 2024 — detection and prosecution were his; so was the supervisory failure that let the alleged fraud reach tens of billions of pesos (Public Ministry filings, 2025–2026; opposition statements).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral authority, the prosecution, the central bank, and the media keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: HOLDS

Evidence for:

  • He used his re-election mandate to give away appointment power, permanently. The constitutional reform promulgated on 27 October 2024 transferred appointment of the Attorney General from the president to the National Council of the Judiciary, with a fixed term and removal protections — a change whose stated purpose was to guarantee the prosecutor's independence, and which took effect against his own office. The first attorney general chosen under the new system, Yeni Berenice Reynoso, was selected unanimously by the Council on 21 February 2025 and sworn in on 24 February 2025, succeeding Germán Brito, who served out her full tenure unremoved (constitutional reform text, October 2024; National Council of the Judiciary, February 2025).
  • The same reform locked the presidency itself: the two-term limit was moved into the constitution's unamendable provisions, sitting officials were barred from benefiting from constitutional changes made in their own term, and a transitory clause provides that the president elected in May 2024 — Abinader — can never run again (Constitution, Articles 124, 268 and tenth transitory provision, as amended 27 October 2024).
  • Courts rule against his government's interests and are obeyed: the instructing judge's May 2026 dismissal of the Calamar charges against the opposition's most prominent defendants was accepted through the ordinary appeal channel, with no retaliation against the judge documented; the Constitutional Court is seized of multiple unconstitutionality actions against his new Penal Code, including on its abortion articles, and its proceedings run in the open (court records, 2026).
  • The electoral authority administered the 19 May 2024 election that re-elected him, and the result was accepted by the losers within hours — the PLD candidate conceded on election night — under OAS and EU observation that reported no outcome-determining irregularities (JCE results; observer mission statements, May 2024).
  • The central bank's autonomy was left intact in practice: Abinader retained Héctor Valdez Albizu, governor since 2004 under presidents of both parties, and monetary policy through the 2021–2023 inflation shock and the 2024–2025 easing cycle was set by the bank's board without documented executive interference (Central Bank records, 2020–2026).
  • Media operate freely enough to wound him: the SeNaSa scandal was exposed and pursued by Dominican newspapers and digital outlets; critical coverage of blackouts, deportations and the succession runs daily in Listín Diario, Diario Libre, Acento and others, and his government holds a weekly open press conference, La Semanal, at which he takes unscripted questions (Dominican press, 2023–2026).

Evidence against:

  • The structural capacity for grip is large and growing. After the 2024 elections his PRM holds overwhelming majorities in both chambers of Congress, and through Congress and the National Council of the Judiciary — where the executive's weight is constitutional, not incidental — his coalition shapes appointments to the Constitutional Court, the Supreme Court, the electoral boards and the Chamber of Accounts. The November 2026 renewal of Supreme Court seats proceeded under his presidency of the Council; legal-sector commentary praised the picks, but the pipeline itself concentrates appointment power in the governing coalition (Congress and CNM records, 2024–2026; legal commentary, November 2026).
  • His constitutional reform was passed at speed by that majority with limited public consultation; civil-society organizations criticized the absence of a broader national dialogue, a criticism recorded by International IDEA's democracy tracker even as it noted the reform's self-limiting content (International IDEA, October 2024).
  • His government proposed a new media regulator with sanctioning power. The draft Organic Law on Freedom of Expression and Audiovisual Media, deposited in the Senate on 2 May 2025, would create an Instituto Nacional de Comunicación (INACOM) empowered to fine media outlets and communicators; critics in the press and opposition warned the body could become an instrument of pressure. In fairness, the draft was written by a plural expert commission created in 2022 that included the head of the newspaper publishers' association and the journalists' guild president, the publishers' association publicly backed it, and as of November 2026 it had not been enacted — the grip exists as a pending bill, not as law (draft law and commission records, 2022–2025; press debate, 2025).
  • The deportation machinery documented under Test 4 operates with due-process deficits — collective expulsions without individual hearings, per the UN and Amnesty International — which is a failure of legal restraint by the executive even where it is not a capture of an institution.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, Congress, business, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His clearest wobble is fiscal, and he answered it by retreat, twice. In October 2021, facing broad opposition, he announced there would be no fiscal reform. In October 2024, weeks after presenting his Fiscal Modernization bill — a centerpiece of his second term — he withdrew it entirely on 21–22 October 2024, stating it lacked the necessary consensus, although his party's congressional supermajority could have passed it over the objections. Business and civil-society groups publicly praised the withdrawal as democratic responsiveness; credit analysts recorded the fiscal cost (presidential announcement, 21 October 2024; CHTA statement, 23 October 2024; market commentary, October 2024).
  • In the September 2023 canal crisis with Haiti, after closing the entire land border on 15 September 2023 over construction of a canal on the Masacre River, his government partially reopened trade corridors within a month as Dominican border merchants and exporters absorbed losses — escalation, then adjustment (Reuters, September–October 2023).
  • He has offered himself as mediator rather than imposer in the stalled labor-code reform, publicly volunteering in 2026 to broker agreement between business and labor rather than legislate over either (press reporting, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • On the defining pushback of his second term — the international response to his deportation campaign — he has never adjusted. The UN refugee agency's call to halt forced returns, Amnesty International's open letter of April 2024 and urgent actions of October 2024 and April 2025, IACHR concern, Haiti's protests, and US and European criticism were answered not with modification but with escalation: on 6 April 2025 his government announced fifteen further restrictive measures, including the hospital protocol documented under Test 4, and deportations rose through 2025 to the highest levels on record (Amnesty International, 2024–2025; DGM figures, 2025).
  • His government's posture toward documented abuse findings is denial at the top. Amnesty International records that "high-ranking officials have denied the findings" of its deportation documentation; the migration director maintained in 2026 that he was unaware of reported effects such as home births by women avoiding hospitals, and the government's public line throughout has been that operations follow "strict protocols" of dignity — a claim the UN and rights monitors' case documentation contradicts in specific instances (Amnesty International, October 2024; AP reporting, 2025–2026).
  • The fiscal retreat, read the other way, is also a record of not spending capital: the reform his own economists deemed necessary was abandoned in 2021 and again in 2024 rather than negotiated, leaving the structural deficit — roughly 3% of GDP — and the electricity sector's losses unaddressed, a choice rating agencies and the IMF have repeatedly flagged (IMF Article IV and market commentary, 2024–2025).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Haitian migrants, Dominicans of Haitian descent, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • For the Dominican flock, the counting-in is real and measured. Official monetary poverty fell from 23.0% in 2023 to 19.0% in 2024 and 17.3% in 2025 — the lowest in the official series — with extreme poverty down to 2.2% (ONE / Interinstitutional Technical Committee on Poverty, 2025 and March 2026 bulletins). Tourism set records under his tourism minister: 11,676,901 visitors in 2025, the highest annual figure in the country's history (Ministry of Tourism, January 2026). Growth averaged among the region's highest over his tenure (5.0% in 2024, moderating to 2.1% in 2025 per the World Bank), cash transfers reached some 1.3 million poor households, and the homicide rate remained among the lowest in Latin America (World Bank country reporting, 2024–2026).
  • His stated rationale on Haiti is entitled to be heard at full strength, and it is not frivolous: Haiti's state collapsed into gang rule after 2021; the Dominican Republic, sharing the island, already hosted an estimated half-million Haitians; Haitian patients and students place documented strain on border-province services; and Abinader warned the UN repeatedly that the international community was abandoning Haiti — "We warned at the United Nations that either it and all the countries that had committed themselves act responsibly in Haiti, or we will" (October 2024), and "There is no Dominican solution to the Haitian crisis… Haiti is drowning while an important part of the international community watches passively from the shore" (AP, February 2025). His government supported the UN-backed security mission for Haiti and provided it logistical support.
  • His opponents' voters are counted in the ordinary way: the opposition governs municipalities it won, its media publish freely, and its 2024 concession was met with no reprisal machinery (Test 2).

Evidence against:

  • One population is counted out, by policy and at scale. On 2 October 2024 Abinader announced the deportation of up to 10,000 Haitians per week. The documented results: more than 276,000 deportations in 2024 (authorities' figures), and in 2025 between 265,215 forced returns recorded by the IOM and 379,553 claimed by the migration directorate — the discrepancy between the two counts is itself part of the record. Amnesty International, the UN and the IACHR characterize the operations as collective expulsions carried out with racial profiling — detention by skin color — and without individual due process, in violation of Dominican and international law (Amnesty International, October 2024 and April 2025; IOM forced-returns factsheet, 2025; DGM, January 2026).
  • The expulsion reaches the legally protected and the Dominican-born. UNICEF recorded 1,099 unaccompanied children deported in 2024 (AP, February 2025). The UN condemned the deportation of some 900 pregnant or breastfeeding women in a matter of months. Amnesty International and the UN document cases of Dominicans of Haitian descent — including stateless Dominicans left paperless by the 2013 Constitutional Court ruling — detained and expelled or placed at risk of expulsion from their own country (Amnesty International, 2024–2025; UN statements, 2025).
  • In April 2025 the counting-out was extended into the maternity ward. Under the hospital protocol in force from 21 April 2025, public hospitals must verify foreign patients' documents and notify migration authorities; undocumented patients are deported after treatment — mothers detained, on the migration director's own account, 72 hours after a natural birth and seven days after a caesarean. Monitors document women avoiding hospitals and delivering at home in consequence; the government defends the protocol as necessary to control costs and guarantee Dominicans' rights (government announcements, 6 April 2025; Amnesty International report, Health without stigma, 2025; AP and press reporting, 2025–2026).
  • The wall states the policy in concrete: of the roughly 160–164 km perimeter fence announced in February 2022, the Migration Policy Observatory's October 2026 report counts 54 km completed, with further sections under construction — the flock inside is to be secured, in the government's framing, precisely by keeping the neighboring flock out (Migration Policy Observatory, fourth report, 29 October 2026).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since August 2020, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • An independent prosecutor — kept, then constitutionalized. The central institutional promise of his 2020 "change" campaign, made by a candidate who had marched in the Green March anti-impunity protests, was delivered on inauguration day 2020 with the Germán Brito appointment, and entrenched beyond his own tenure by the October 2024 reform moving the appointment to the National Council of the Judiciary (Tests 1 and 2).
  • No third term — kept, in the strongest available form. Abinader said throughout that he would not seek to remain; in October 2024 he converted the statement into an unamendable constitutional clause plus a transitory provision naming his own situation, and through 2025–2026 he has repeated that he will not pursue a third term while his party's succession contest proceeds openly without him — five declared PRM hopefuls remained in the race as of November 2026 (constitutional reform, October 2024; Listín Diario reporting via press, November 2026).
  • A modern penal code — delivered. Replacing the 1884 code was a decades-old promise of Dominican politics; Abinader promulgated the new Penal Code (Law 74-25) in early August 2025, and it entered into force in August 2026, adding offenses such as femicide, contract killing, forced disappearance and crimes against humanity (Law 74-25, August 2025). Its content is contested — women's organizations challenge its near-total abortion ban before the Constitutional Court, and civic groups criticize retained defamation offenses — but the promise to enact a code was kept (Constitutional Court filings, 2026; Participación Ciudadana, July 2026).
  • Economic stewardship — substantially kept. He promised stability and growth; the record shows post-pandemic recovery, record tourism, inflation returned to the central bank's target band, and poverty at series lows (Test 4), though growth slowed to 2.1% in 2025 (World Bank, 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Fiscal reform — promised twice, abandoned twice. A tax/fiscal reform featured in his program from 2020; he renounced it in October 2021 and withdrew it again in October 2024 (Test 3). The fiscal consolidation he promised investors and the IMF remains undone, and public debt stays above pre-pandemic levels (IMF and World Bank reporting, 2024–2026).
  • "Zero impunity" — breached from inside. The slogan of his rise was tested by SeNaSa: the largest alleged corruption scheme of his tenure operated for years inside his own government's health insurer before his internal review caught it (Test 1). The prosecution that followed was also his; the promise as stated — zero impunity, including "present" corruption — was not kept in the years the alleged fraud ran undetected.
  • The border wall — far behind its announced schedule. Announced in February 2022 with a first phase expected within about nine months and some 164 km planned, the fence stood at 54 completed kilometers in October 2026, with completion of current sections projected into 2027 (presidential announcement, February 2022; Migration Policy Observatory, October 2026).
  • Electricity — the unkept everyday promise. He pledged to fix the distribution sector's losses and end the blackout regime; blackouts persisted through his tenure, including a nationwide grid failure in November 2025, and blackouts head the opposition's case against him in the 2028 cycle (AP reporting, November 2025; opposition statements, 2026).

The long arc, factually: Abinader, a businessman from one of the country's prominent Lebanese-Dominican families, lost the 2016 presidential election, marched with the Green March in 2017, and won in 2020 as the institutional-change candidate. In office his word has been most reliable exactly where it binds himself — the prosecutor he could not control, the re-election he made impossible — and least reliable where it required spending political capital against resistance: taxes, electricity, and the fiscal bill. On Haiti, his word and his record match each other; it is the promise's content, not its keeping, that Test 4 grades.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: HOLDS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Luis Abinader is the rare subject of this series who weakened his own seat on purpose: he appointed a prosecutor he did not control, let her successors be chosen by a council instead of by himself, wrote the two-term limit into the constitution's unamendable core with a clause that names him, and then stood aside while his party fought over who replaces him. The courts that dismissed his prosecutors' biggest opposition case, the newspapers that exposed his health insurer's collapse, and the attorney general now pursuing his own appointees all operate in the open, and none has been punished for it. Against that record stand two heavy entries. The law's reach, though real in both directions, fell for years overwhelmingly on his predecessors' circle, and the largest alleged fraud of his tenure grew for years inside his own flagship institution before his government caught it. And one whole population is counted out of his flock by design: the deportation campaign against Haitians — collective, racially profiled on the monitors' documented record, reaching into maternity wards and sweeping up children, pregnant women and stateless Dominicans of Haitian descent — is the most extensive exclusion program in this series outside an authoritarian state, maintained without adjustment in the face of every appeal. The seat, on this evidence, holds without gripping; the flock it counts is drawn with a border through it.
