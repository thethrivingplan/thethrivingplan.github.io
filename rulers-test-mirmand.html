The Ruler's Test — Christophe Mirmand, Minister of State of Monaco

Subject: Christophe Jacques-Pierre Mirmand — Minister of State of Monaco since 21 July 2025. The seat graded is the Minister of State, head of the Prince's Government, and the framing is stated plainly because it shapes every test: under Monaco's Constitution of 17 December 1962, executive power sits with the Sovereign Prince — Prince Albert II, head of state since 2005 — who appoints and can dismiss the Minister of State, holds the exclusive right to initiate legislation, promulgates laws, and to whom the government is responsible. The Minister of State is the Prince's representative: he directs the executive services, commands the police, and presides over the Council of Government, but he is not elected, holds no electoral mandate of his own, and is not constitutionally answerable to the elected National Council (24 members, elected by Monegasque nationals only). Mirmand, born 22 July 1961 in Constantine, French Algeria, is a French senior civil servant and former prefect (Alpes-Maritimes, Corsica, Brittany, Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur) who was serving as chief of staff to France's Overseas Minister when Prince Albert II appointed him by Sovereign Ordinance No. 11.290 of 2 July 2025 (Government of Monaco, gouv.mc, July 2025; German Federal Foreign Office country page, 2025). The Prince's role is therefore graded inside this file wherever it bears on a test — above all Test 2 — rather than in a separate dossier. The timing of the tenure is stated plainly, per this series' handling of short and interrupted records: Mirmand's predecessor Didier Guillaume took office on 2 September 2024 and died in office on 17 January 2025, four months in, after hospitalisation on 10 January 2025 (Monaco Life, January 2025). Isabelle Berro-Amadeï, Minister of External Relations and Cooperation — the first woman and second Monegasque national to hold the seat — served as acting Minister of State from 10 January to 21 July 2025. Between Guillaume's death and Mirmand's appointment there was a further turn the record must carry: on 4 June 2025 the Prince appointed Philippe Mettoux, a French magistrate and anti-corruption specialist, to take office on 4 July 2025; on 26 June 2025 Mettoux withdrew before taking office, stating that "negative and contrary forces are already at work to perpetuate the archaic practices of the past" and that he would not have "the essential levers" to carry out his mission — his own stated reason, recorded here as his claim, not a finding; the Prince's Government replied that it regretted "malicious remarks" in the press and on social networks aimed at damaging Monaco's image (Monaco Life, June 2025; Government of Monaco press release, 27 June 2025). Mirmand is graded on roughly sixteen months in office, from 21 July 2025 to this grading. Work begun under Berro-Amadeï's interim government — above all the anti-money-laundering file — is credited to the seat's record as context and is attributed to her where the dates require it; Mirmand is graded on what he carried, completed, or changed. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: The ethic of the Test, printed verbatim: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Conflicting counts are printed as separate sourced figures, never blended. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. Governing on the platform a ruler was appointed or elected to carry out is not itself a failure; the Test grades the holding, never the platform. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as two independent axes and are never allowed to launder each other. Where the record shows a situation with no good choice, the dossier says so and grades the choice actually available in the moment, not a hindsight door. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The anti-money-laundering enforcement that defined this seat's record was applied, on the documented outcome, to the financial centre itself rather than only to outsiders. Monaco was placed on the FATF grey list on 28 June 2024, with an action plan covering six strands: understanding of money-laundering risk linked to tax fraud committed abroad, outbound requests to identify and seize criminal assets, sanctions for AML breaches and for breaches of beneficial-ownership requirements, resourcing of the financial intelligence unit, quality and timeliness of suspicious-transaction reporting, and judicial efficiency and penalties (FATF statement, June 2024, via 99 Avocats summary, 2026). Under the government Mirmand heads from July 2025, that plan was carried to completion: the FATF concluded in June 2026 that Monaco had substantially completed it, assessors visited in September 2026, and on 30 October 2026 the FATF removed Monaco from the grey list, its president singling out Monaco's increased seizures of property suspected to derive from criminal activities (FATF plenary outcome via Government of Monaco press release, 30 October 2026; Monaco Life, 30 October 2026). The government states that sanctions against professionals who fail AML obligations and against beneficial-ownership breaches became "significantly stronger," that staffing at the Monaco Financial Security Authority (AMSF) — the combined supervisor and financial intelligence unit — quadrupled and will reach 80 by the end of 2026, and that mutual legal assistance requests sent abroad rose on a sustained basis (Government of Monaco, 30 October 2026). Sanctions that bite on the Principality's own banks, fiduciaries, and company agents are the one-law test applied to insiders, and on this file the documented direction is toward applying them.
  • The integrity machinery aimed at the top of the executive was kept moving on his watch, building on steps begun under the interim government. GRECO's Fifth Round evaluation of Monaco (adopted 21 June 2024, published 24 July 2024) commended early measures — an ethics officer, an ethics committee, risk mapping, a draft whistleblower law — and the Steering Committee created by Sovereign Ordinance No. 11.092 of 18 February 2025, chaired under Berro-Amadeï, was charged with building a full anti-corruption strategy implementing GRECO's recommendations, with a first progress report due by the end of 2025 (GRECO Evaluation Report on Monaco, 2024; Monaco Life, 31 March 2025). No documented act by Mirmand's government abolishing, defunding, or shelving that committee appears in the record assembled here; the file is recorded as continuing, with its delivery still to be graded when the reports are public.
  • In the October 2026 budget confrontation (Tests 3 and 4), the government accepted a rule that applies to its own hiring: senior civil-service posts will now be advertised systematically, so that Monegasque candidates can apply, and a dedicated contact in the Minister of State's office will report annually to the National Council on how the national-priority rule is applied (Monaco Life, 16 October 2026). A published rule with a reporting duty is a move toward one law stated in advance rather than discretion exercised case by case.

Evidence against:

  • The source of the seat is itself an exception to one law. The Minister of State is chosen by the Prince alone, traditionally from senior French officials; there is no election, no confirmation vote, and no published criteria against which Mirmand — or any candidate — was measured (Constitution of 1962; Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2023, on the traditional French-candidate practice). The Mettoux episode sits directly on this point: a man appointed on 4 June 2025 to lead a transparency mission withdrew on 26 June 2025 saying he lacked the levers and that forces were working "to perpetuate the archaic practices of the past." That is Mettoux's ALLEGATION about unnamed forces, not a finding against any named person, and the government disputed the surrounding commentary as malicious (Monaco Life, June 2025). But the documented fact underneath the allegation is not disputed by either side: the appointment system allowed a reform mandate to collapse in twenty-two days with no public accounting of what levers were withheld or by whom.
  • GRECO's central one-law finding remains open on the record: the rules do not reach the top. GRECO recommended that transparency and integrity measures be made applicable to the Sovereign Prince himself in his executive functions — contacts with third parties, gifts, conflicts of interest, use of public funds for the Sovereign household — and that the Prince recuse himself from decisions involving personal interests; it also found that the ethics and asset-declaration rules did not cover the people who work most closely with ministers, the Secretary of State for Justice, or the Prince's advisers, that there were no rules governing dealings with lobbyists, and no functioning whistleblower protection system (GRECO, 2024; eucrim summary, 2024). As of this grading, no documented measure extending those rules to the Prince's executive functions appears in the record assembled here. A system in which the most powerful executive actor stands outside the integrity rules is, by this Test's definition, two laws — however well the lower law is enforced.
  • The Rybolovlev affair's tail shows what the older dispensation cost, and its ending cuts both ways. The facts established by courts: Monaco's Director of Judicial Services, Philippe Narmino, resigned in September 2017 hours after Le Monde published extracts suggesting he had received gifts from resident billionaire Dmitry Rybolovlev and stayed at his Swiss residence while Rybolovlev's fraud case against art dealer Yves Bouvier was live; in December 2019 Monaco's Court of Appeal dismissed the charges against Bouvier, finding the investigation had been "conducted in a biased and unfair way"; in June 2024 the European Court of Human Rights held that the extraction and use of data from the phone of Rybolovlev's lawyer violated Article 8 of the European Convention; and on 27 February 2025 — under Berro-Amadeï's interim government, before Mirmand took office — Monaco's Court of Appeal annulled the 2017 influence-peddling and corruption proceedings against Rybolovlev, his lawyer, and the officials charged alongside them, because the case rested solely on that unlawfully obtained evidence (Monaco Life, 28 February 2025; Artnet News, 2025). The annulment is the courts applying one law against the state's own investigators — recorded on the for shelf of Test 2 — but for Test 1 its other face stands: a decade-long file in which a wealthy resident's proximity to the justice system was documented by the courts themselves ended with no verdict on the underlying influence allegations in either direction. The allegations against the officials charged were never proven; they are recorded here as ALLEGATION, annulled proceedings, not guilt and not exoneration on the merits.
  • The FATF exit is not yet matched in Brussels. The European Commission added Monaco to the EU list of high-risk third countries by Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/1184 of 10 June 2025, in force 5 August 2025, and as of this grading Monaco remains on that EU list until the Commission adopts a new regulation — so EU banks continue to apply enhanced due diligence to Monaco business even after the FATF removal (European Commission, June 2025; Petrini analysis, October 2026). The two listings are printed separately because they are separate sourced facts; the FATF's verdict does not erase the EU's.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because both movements are documented at the same time. On enforcement, the government Mirmand heads finished a two-year action plan whose sanctions, seizures, and supervision fell on Monaco's own financial professionals, and the external referee — the FATF — certified the result on 30 October 2026. On the source, the system's deepest exception is untouched: the Prince performs executive functions outside the integrity rules GRECO asked to have extended to him, the head of government is appointed with no published criteria, and the one appointee who said publicly that the levers for reform were being withheld was answered with a communiqué about malicious remarks rather than a public accounting. One law is being built for the financial centre; it does not yet run to the top of the state.

Helped: Compliant banks, fiduciaries, and businesses in Monaco, whose cross-border transfers and correspondent relationships were slowed for twenty-eight months by the grey listing and are eased by its removal; Monegasque job applicants, who gain advertised senior posts and an annual accounting of the priority rule; the public record itself, which gained a completed, externally verified action plan. Hurt: Professionals and firms sanctioned or subjected to quadrupled supervision under the AMSF regime — hurt in the ordinary way enforcement hurts, recorded without judgment on individual cases, none of which are documented here by name; residents and businesses still carrying EU enhanced due diligence after the FATF exit; and, on the older file, every party to the Rybolovlev proceedings — the dealer whose prosecution Monaco's own court found biased, and the officials whose annulled charges leave their names attached to allegations a court never tested.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The courts ruled against the state's own case, at the highest cost to the state, and the ruling stood. The February 2025 annulment of the Rybolovlev proceedings (Test 1) was delivered by Monaco's Court of Appeal on the request of the investigating judges and with the agreement of the General Prosecutor's Office, following the European Court of Human Rights' June 2024 judgment against Monaco's investigative methods (Monaco Life, 28 February 2025). A judiciary that voids its own prosecution service's flagship corruption file on rights grounds — and a prosecution that accepts it — is a documented instance of the referee ruling against the house. It predates Mirmand by five months and is credited to the institutions, not to him; nothing in his sixteen months documents him reversing, punishing, or circumventing it.
  • The elected National Council exercised real leverage over his government and was not dissolved, packed, or bypassed. In October 2026 its president suspended a budget sitting — a rare step — in protest at the Minister of State's position, and a week later the government returned with concessions and the budget passed by vote, 13 in favour, 2 against, 5 abstentions (Monaco Life, 16 October 2026). The Prince holds the constitutional power to dissolve the Council; no documented use or threat of it appears in this file.
  • The AML supervisor was built up as a specialist body rather than folded into the ministry: the AMSF combines the financial intelligence unit and the supervisor, its staffing quadrupled toward 80 by the end of 2026, and from March 2025 — under the interim government — it has been led by a former director of France's financial intelligence unit, TRACFIN, appointed for that expertise (Monaco Life, October 2026; Government of Monaco, 30 October 2026). Critical and independent-leaning media operate openly: Monaco Life reported the Mettoux withdrawal, the government's "malicious remarks" rebuttal, and the October 2026 confrontation in terms sharply critical of the government, and international coverage of Monaco's governance disputes circulates without documented interference in the record assembled here.

Evidence against:

  • The constitutional structure is itself a grip, and it is the Prince's. Executive power is vested in the Prince; the government is responsible to him alone; he appoints and dismisses the Minister of State and the government councillors, holds the exclusive right of legislative initiative, promulgates the laws, can dissolve the National Council, and justice is dispensed in his name by judges appointed under sovereign ordinance (Constitution of 1962; Freedom House, 2023; Council of Europe materials, 2026). Mirmand stated the structure aloud, in the chamber, on 7 October 2026: opening the amended-budget debate, he reminded councillors that the Government takes its strategic priorities, and the confidence to carry them out, from the Sovereign Prince alone, and that it is not constitutionally answerable to the National Council (Monaco Life, 16 October 2026). That statement is constitutionally accurate — and it is the grip, described by the man holding the government's end of it. Test 2 grades the holding: a head of government who answers upward only, to an unelected appointer, holds the referees' board in one hand by design, whatever his personal restraint.
  • The Minister of State also commands the police — the Directorate of Public Safety is directed under his authority — and GRECO documented the soft spots exactly there: transparency in the appointment of senior police officers and integrity screening of appointees still needed strengthening, the asset- and interest-declaration system for at-risk posts lacked effective scrutiny, and particular attention was required for the police's power to issue residence permits, which carry Monaco's advantageous tax status (GRECO, 2024). A residence permit in Monaco is an economic prize; the documented record shows the issuing power sitting inside the executive the Minister of State directs, with the safeguards GRECO asked for not yet documented as complete.
  • The judiciary's independence carries a documented historical wound that the current structure has not been shown to have healed: the Narmino resignation of 2017 (Test 1) established, on the courts' own later findings, that the Bouvier investigation was conducted in a biased and unfair way with the complainant's lawyer participating in investigative strategy (Court of Appeal findings via ARTnews, December 2019). GRECO's 2024 round on top executive functions and Mirmand's own June 2026 address to the Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe both point to judicial-independence reforms as work Monaco itself describes as ongoing — Mirmand cited "reforms Monaco had set in motion since its accession to the Council of Europe in 2004" to strengthen judicial independence and combat corruption and money laundering (Council of Europe, 22 June 2026). Work described by its own head of government as still in motion is not yet a referee beyond reach.
  • The Mettoux withdrawal is, on its face, a Test 2 event whatever its truth: the Prince's chosen reformer stated he could not obtain "the essential levers" — an ALLEGATION about unnamed forces, disputed in tone by the government, with no documented independent inquiry, published explanation, or institutional follow-up in the record assembled here. A system that cannot or will not explain why its own appointee walked away leaves the question of who actually holds the levers unanswered on the public record.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because the institutions demonstrably bite and the hand above them demonstrably holds. The courts annulled the state's own case; the Council suspended a sitting and extracted concessions; the supervisor was professionalised. But all of it operates inside a constitution in which the Prince appoints the government, initiates all legislation, can dissolve the elected chamber, and is the government's only source of confidence — a structure Mirmand did not create, did not loosen, and in October 2026 invoked expressly against the elected chamber's claim to oversight. He holds without any documented personal act of gripping; the seat he holds is a grip by design, and the Prince's part of that design is graded here as the framing of this dossier requires.

Helped: The National Council and its electorate, whose budget leverage proved real in October 2026; defendants and complainants before Monaco's courts, whose rights the Court of Appeal enforced against the prosecution in the Rybolovlev annulment; the AMSF's professional staff and the financial centre's compliant majority, who gain a credible referee. Hurt: No named person is documented as punished by Mirmand for institutional independence — that absence is part of the record. Structurally hurt: Monegasque voters, whose elected chamber can be told, correctly, that the government does not answer to it; and the public's ability to know who holds power, left unserved by the unexplained Mettoux collapse.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clearest wobble of his tenure was answered, within a week, by considering another option that he had first refused — which is exactly what this Test asks. On 7 October 2026, opening the debate on the 2026 amended budget, Mirmand rejected the National Council's two central demands — a joint commission on national-priority employment, which he refused on separation-of-powers grounds, and a faster pace of state housing — and reminded the chamber that the government answers to the Prince alone. Council President Thomas Brezzo suspended the sitting, stating that Mirmand's response had significantly damaged relations between the government and the Assembly "both in its substance and tone," and warning of "a total impasse" (Monaco Life, October 2026). When the debate resumed, the government had shifted: the joint commission it had rejected would be created; senior civil-service posts would be advertised systematically; a named contact in the Minister of State's office would report annually on national priority; and on housing the government recommitted to regular construction toward housing 75% of Monegasques in the state sector. The budget passed on 15 October 2026, and Brezzo framed the outcome as neither side backing down: "The Assembly did not win against the Government, and the Government did not give way to the Assembly. It is Monaco that is moving forward" (Monaco Life, 16 October 2026). Considering the other option and moving, with the movement on the public record, is for-shelf evidence under this Test's rule — the initial refusal is recorded on the against shelf below, and the two are not merged.
  • His first budget encounter, a year earlier, showed the same pattern without the rupture: presenting the revised 2025 budget on 7 October 2025 — a swing from a projected €88.6 million deficit to an €86 million surplus on property-related VAT — he urged caution about volatile revenue in the chamber, proposed a legal mechanism to make registration of private sale agreements mandatory so fiscal income could be forecast honestly, and the session closed in what Brezzo called a welcome "convergence of views" across housing, transport, and the Fontvieille redevelopment (Monaco Life, October 2025).
  • The largest external pushback of the period — the FATF process itself — was met by completing the demanded reforms rather than denouncing the referee: after the June 2026 finding that the action plan was substantially completed and the September 2026 on-site visit, Mirmand's public line at the 30 October 2026 removal was that it was not a finish line: "We do not see this as a finish line: what matters now is what Monaco delivers over the long term. The reforms will therefore continue as practices evolve" (Government of Monaco, 30 October 2026).
  • On figures, he answered challenge with checkable numbers rather than denial. When the Council claimed more than 50 qualified Monegasque candidates were turned away from public-sector jobs each year, the government disputed the count openly and specifically: the 2025 applications concerned 31 individuals, several of whom had applied more than once, and 16 of whom were subsequently recruited — two conflicting counts, printed here as two sourced figures, the Council's "more than 50 applications" and the government's "31 individuals, 16 recruited," never blended (Monaco Life, October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The October 2026 adjustment came only after the wobble had been made as public and costly as the Council could make it. The first response to pushback was a constitutional lecture and a refusal of the joint commission on stated principle; the principle was then set aside within a week, which leaves the documented sequence as: deny, be suspended, concede. The Council's own statement records that the damage was "in substance as in tone" (Monaco Life, October 2026). A ruler who adjusts only at the point of impasse passes the letter of this Test — he did adjust, openly — and the shelf records the cost of getting him there.
  • On the housing numbers at the heart of that dispute, the government's answer to the Council's demand of roughly 100 new state homes a year was a statistical defence, not a plan change: Mirmand argued from IMSEE figures — Monegasque population growth averaging 93 people a year between 2015 and 2024, roughly 50 new households a year — that about 40 new apartments a year would suffice to house 80% of Monegasques in the state sector, while the Council replied that the government was returning to the same reliance on projections it had challenged in 2018, before the Prince announced the National Housing Plan in 2019 (Monaco Life, October 2026). And the delivery record underneath the argument is stark: no new state-owned apartments are due to be delivered in 2026 or 2027, with the first Bel Air tower expected in January 2028 after geotechnical delays, the gap to be bridged by around 90 homes at the next allocation round through accelerated renovations (Monaco Life, 16 October 2026). Explanation was offered; the trajectory the elected chamber asked to change was defended, not revised.
  • The Mettoux wobble — the loudest pushback against the system in his succession line — was answered by the government he now heads with dismissal rather than engagement: the official response regretted "malicious remarks" damaging Monaco's image, and no documented point-by-point answer to the claim that a Minister of State lacks the levers to reform appears in the record (Government of Monaco, 27 June 2025; Monaco Life, June 2025). That response predates Mirmand's arrival by three weeks and belongs to the interim government's record; it is entered here because Mirmand, in sixteen months since, has not reopened or answered it either.

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because the tenure contains one complete, documented cycle of each kind. Pushed by the elected chamber to the edge of impasse, he considered the option he had refused, adopted much of it in public, and got his budget passed with the other side claiming shared victory — that is answering the wobble. Pushed on housing delivery and on the Mettoux questions, the same government answered with statistics and silence respectively. The pattern is a ruler who adjusts when an institution with a vote forces the issue, and explains when it cannot.

Helped: The National Council, whose suspension tactic produced a joint commission, advertised senior posts, and an annual report it did not have before; Monegasque jobseekers facing career difficulty, the commission's intended beneficiaries; budget transparency, which gained a proposed mandatory-registration mechanism for private property sales. Hurt: Monegasque households waiting for state housing, for whom the defended trajectory still delivers no new state apartment in 2026 or 2027; and institutional trust in the first week of October 2026, damaged — on the Council's own wording — by the government's opening tone, a cost the eventual settlement repaired only in part.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the Monegasque nationals, the foreign residents who are most of the population, the cross-border workers who staff the economy, and the less wealthy — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • For the community the system is built to count, the counting is concrete, funded, and delivered on his watch. The National Housing Plan for Monegasques, announced by the Prince in 2019, had invested €1.5 billion and built 633 new state-owned homes by the end of 2024; Phase 2, presented to the National Council in June 2025 under the interim government, schedules regular deliveries to 2029 — Résidence Héméra Block A (50 homes), Bel Air (197), Larvotto Supérieur (35), La Luciole (27), Hector Otto (76–90) — toward housing nearly three quarters of Monegasque nationals in the state sector (Government of Monaco, June 2025). Under Mirmand, the State Housing Commission chaired at the Ministry of State allocated 156 state-owned homes on 6 March 2026 — 49 new Héméra Block A homes and 107 renovated apartments — with more than two-thirds of applicants whose households had grown receiving an offer matching their situation and 60% of applicants not yet housed in the Principality receiving an offer; the government states more than 1,300 properties have been allocated to Monegasque households since 2019 (Government of Monaco, March 2026).
  • The allocation system itself is being reformed toward the applicants rather than the administration: a new methodology, presented to the National Council and due to be adopted before the end of 2026, aims to increase the frequency of allocation rounds and give greater consideration to applicants' wishes, with the plan to be re-based every five years on IMSEE demographic studies (Government of Monaco, June 2025). The October 2026 settlement added the employment side: a joint commission for Monegasques facing career difficulties and systematic advertising of senior posts (Monaco Life, 16 October 2026).
  • The wider resident population is counted in the state's own statistics and services even where it is not counted at the ballot box: IMSEE's census counts all 38,857 residents across 144 nationalities (IMSEE, census at 31 December 2025), and the government's public services — police, fire and emergency services, whose readiness Mirmand made his first-day priority in July 2025 — serve residents and the daily working population without a nationality test (Government of Monaco, July 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The arithmetic of Monaco is that the counted are a minority of the people governed. IMSEE counted 38,857 residents at 31 December 2025, of whom 9,333 — 24.0% — are Monegasque nationals; the rest are French (8,270), Italian (7,559), British (3,081), and 140 other nationalities (IMSEE, 2025 census). Only Monegasque nationals may vote or stand for the National Council; the roughly three quarters of residents who are not nationals have no vote in any Monegasque election (IPU Parline; Freedom House, 2023). The Minister of State who governs all of them is appointed by the Prince; no resident of any nationality elects him. That is the constitutional design Mirmand serves rather than a policy he chose — the mandate rule means the design itself is not his demerit — but Test 4 grades the accounting as it stands under his government, and in that accounting the majority of the governed are counted for services and statistics and counted out of every decision.
  • The people who staff the economy are counted out of its housing and its politics alike. Monaco's employment total is 65,117 employees (IMSEE, 2025) against 38,857 residents; the large majority of private-sector employees live in France and commute — IMSEE-derived figures put about 81.5% of private-sector employees resident in France and only about 10.1% living in Monaco (IMSEE data via 2025 census reporting). These workers are subject to Monegasque labour law, policed by Monegasque authorities, and dependent on the transport and housing decisions of a government in whose selection they have no part; the national-priority rule expressly ranks them last in hiring, behind nationals, residents, and previously employed residents of the neighbouring French communes. No documented measure of Mirmand's government addressed cross-border workers' representation, housing, or transport costs as a constituency in its own right in the record assembled here; transport investment (rail frequency, the La Brasca park-and-ride) benefits them as users and is recorded as such, not as counting.
  • Inside the counted community, the delivery gap falls on those waiting now. No new state-owned apartments are due in 2026 or 2027 (Test 3), and the private market that non-nationals and waiting nationals alike must use is priced beyond ordinary incomes: IMSEE's Real Estate Observatory put the average price at €57,569 per square metre in 2025, with rents rising 9.1% in 2025 (IMSEE, February 2026). State housing is reserved to nationals, so a long-resident non-national household in housing need has no door in the system at all — a fact of the design, stated because this Test asks who is counted, and under Mirmand's government the design was administered, defended on statistical grounds, and not proposed for change.
  • The fiscal settlement counts one group preferentially by structure: Monaco levies no income tax on residents (French nationals excepted, under the 1963 Franco-Monegasque convention), and the 2026 amended budget balanced at a €5.9 million surplus with no borrowing and no income tax, its revenue resting heavily on property VAT and state-domain income (Monaco Life, 16 October 2026). The beneficiaries of that structure are disproportionately the wealthy residents the system attracts; the cost is carried in the constrained investment budget — €107 million of investment spending removed in the amended budget, €104 million of it deferred rather than saved, on the Finance Committee's own sceptical reading (Monaco Life, 16 October 2026).

Why: The grade lands at MIXED because the two halves of the ledger are both fully documented and neither cancels the other. For Monegasque nationals — a small, genuinely exposed minority in their own country, which is the honest ground of the protective design — his government allocated homes at pace, reformed allocation toward applicants' wishes, and conceded new employment machinery. For everyone else who lives or works under his government — three quarters of residents and the tens of thousands who commute in — the accounting stops at services and statistics: no vote, no state housing, last place in hiring, and no documented proposal from his government to change any of it. He counts the counted very well. The Test asks about everyone.

Helped: Monegasque households on the housing list — 156 allocations in March 2026 alone, including growing families and first-time housed applicants; Monegasque jobseekers, who gain the joint commission and advertised senior posts; all residents, who receive police, fire, and emergency services without a nationality test. Hurt: Non-national long-term residents — about 29,500 people — who fund and obey the state without a vote or a housing door; cross-border workers, roughly four fifths of the private workforce, who carry Monaco's economy while ranking last in its hiring law and living outside its housing system entirely; Monegasque families whose need falls in the 2026–2027 delivery gap, waiting on renovations while new towers stand delayed.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Appointment commitments and public statements vs. the record since 21 July 2025, with dates; his predecessors' completed words stated as context.

GRADE: NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The FATF exit — the seat's standing commitment, completed on his watch. The commitment was made by the Prince's Government in June 2024, before Mirmand: to implement the FATF action plan and remove Monaco from the grey list on the agreed timetable, with Monaco's report due by January 2026 and a decision expected at the June 2026 plenary (Government of Monaco press release, 28 June 2024, via 99 Avocats summary). The outcome ran close to that timetable and is documented at each step: FATF's preliminary conclusion of substantial completion in June 2026, the on-site visit in September 2026, and removal on 30 October 2026 (Government of Monaco, 30 October 2026). Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE.

Christophe Mirmand is a prefect sent to run a principality: appointed by Prince Albert II on 2 July 2025 after his designated predecessor Philippe Mettoux withdrew claiming he would not be given the levers to reform — an allegation never publicly answered — and sworn in on 21 July 2025 to head a government that answers, by constitution and by his own statement in the chamber, to the Prince alone. Sixteen months on, the record is genuinely two-sided on every test that can be graded. On one law, his government finished the FATF action plan and took Monaco off the grey list on 30 October 2026 with sanctions and seizures applied to its own financial centre — while the integrity rules GRECO wrote in 2024 still stop below the Prince who performs executive functions, and Monaco remains on the EU's high-risk list. On gripping, the courts voided the state's own flagship case and the elected Council suspended his budget debate and won concessions — inside a structure where the Prince appoints the government, initiates all law, and can dissolve the chamber that pushed him. On the wobble, he answered the one institution that could force the issue, first with a constitutional refusal and then, within a week, with most of what it asked — and answered the housing-delivery challenge with statistics and the Mettoux questions with silence. On counting everyone, he counts the counted superbly: 156 state homes allocated in a single March 2026 round and new employment machinery for the 9,333 Monegasque nationals — while the roughly 29,500 non-national residents and the tens of thousands of cross-border workers who staff the economy remain, by a design he administers and has not proposed to change, without a vote, a housing door, or first claim on a job. On his word, the file is honestly too young to grade: the one great promise attached to his seat was made by others and kept on his watch, and his own dated pledges fall due in 2026–2029. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the EU follows the FATF off the list, whether GRECO's rules are ever extended to the top of the executive, whether the joint commission and annual report materialise in 2027, and whether a single new state apartment is delivered before Bel Air in January 2028.
