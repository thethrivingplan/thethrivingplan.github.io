Part one · the foundation

Skating

Hockey is a skating game that happens to involve a puck. A player with an average shot and great feet gets chances; a player with a great shot and average feet watches chances go by. Skating is taught first and deepest here because every later section quietly assumes it. The honest order of learning: balance on edges → forward stride → stops on both sides → crossovers → backwards and transitions. Skipping ahead is the most common way beginners stall.

Edges — what an edge actually is Tap to open / close

A skate blade is not flat. It is ground with a shallow U-shaped groove — the hollow — running its length, which leaves two edges: an inside edge (the side toward your other foot) and an outside edge (the side away). At any moment you are standing on one edge, the other, or — briefly, in a glide — nearly flat between them.

Why it matters: an edge is a grip tool. Lean the blade over and the edge bites the ice, letting you push against it and turn on it. Hold the blade flat and it slides — that is glide. All skating is choosing, moment by moment, between bite (push, turn, stop) and glide (carry speed for free). A deeper hollow bites harder and turns sharper but drags more and catches more; a shallower hollow glides faster and releases easier but feels slippery in turns. Neither is "better" — it is a trade-off between grip and glide, and most recreational skaters are well served by a middle hollow.

The feel to find: standing still, roll each ankle slowly in and out. The moment the blade tips and "catches" is the edge engaging. On the move, long one-foot glides on a gentle curve teach each edge separately: curve left on your right foot and you are on its inside edge; curve left on your left foot and you are on its outside edge.

Common fault — "ankle bending" blamed on the skater alone. Skates that fold inward at the ankle are usually a fit and stiffness story first: boots too big, too soft for the skater's weight and level, or laced loosely through the top eyelets. A beginner in the wrong boot will look like they have weak ankles no matter how hard they try. What really hasn't Shaming the skater before checking the boot. Check fit first — heel locked, toes brushing the cap, support matched to the skater — then coach the ankle.

What works One-foot glide drills and deliberate edge rolls at slow speed. Edges are learned by feel at walking pace long before they survive at game speed.

Coach note When your practice record shows skating work, watch for this: edge comfort on the weak side always lags the strong side. Log which side you trained — the honest pattern is almost always one-sided practice.

The forward stride — where speed actually comes from Tap to open / close

The stride has three parts: load (glide foot under you, knee bent), push (drive the other leg out and back against the inside edge until the leg is fully extended and the toe is the last thing to leave the ice), and recover (bring the pushing foot back under your body, low, ready to glide). Repeat on the other side.

Why knee bend makes speed: a bent knee is a loaded spring with range. The deeper your comfortable bend, the longer the track your leg has to push along — and push length, not push frequency, is where most speed lives. Skating tall shortens every push before it starts.

Why full extension matters: the last part of the push, out through the toe, is free distance on every stride. Skaters who recover early — pulling the foot back before the leg straightens — pay full effort for three-quarter strides. Multiply that by every stride in a shift and it is the difference between arriving and arriving late.

Why the push goes out-and-back, not just back: you can only push against the ice in the direction the edge faces. A stride is a series of angled pushes against inside edges, like climbing a ladder laid sideways. Pushing straight back on a flat blade just slips.

Common faults: standing tall between strides ("skating on stilts"); short, choppy, fast steps that look busy and go nowhere — the "running on ice" stride; and swinging the recovering foot wide and high, which wastes the time it should spend gliding. What really hasn't "Move your feet faster" as the first fix for a slow skater. Cadence on top of a short stride just makes a short stride happen more often. Length first, then quickness.

What works Slow, exaggerated full strides — bend, push to a straight leg, hold the glide, count to two — before any speed work. The glide hold exposes every shortcut your stride is taking.

Coach note Film from directly behind at ice level (see Drills). From behind, an incomplete push is unmistakable: the toe leaves the ice while the knee is still bent. Fix one side at a time.

Crossovers — turning without slowing down Tap to open / close

To turn left at speed: lean into the turn so both skates sit on edges (right foot on its inside edge, left foot on its outside edge), then step the right foot over the left, plant it on its inside edge, and push underneath with the left foot's outside edge. Each crossover is two pushes, which is why a good turn can actually gain speed.

Why the lean comes first: a turn is controlled falling. Your edges can only hold a turn your balance has already committed to; skaters who stay upright and step across anyway get a wide, skidding turn or a caught edge. Shoulders and hips point where you are going, knees bent, weight over the balls of the feet.

Common fault: crossing over only on the comfortable side and doing wide, upright turns the other way. Games do not ask which side you prefer. What works Circles both directions, every session, at a pace slow enough to feel both pushes. The weak side catches up through reps, not through wanting it to.

Stops — the hockey stop, on both sides Tap to open / close

Glide on two feet, knees bent. Rotate your hips and shoulders so both skates turn sideways across your direction of travel, roll onto the edges that face the slide (inside edge of the front skate, outside edge of the back skate), and let the blades shave the ice as you sit your weight down and back slightly. More edge angle and more weight = a faster, spray-throwing stop; less = a long, gentle one. That dial is the skill.

Why sideways works: a blade slides freely along its length and bites across it. Turning the blades across your travel turns two gliding skates into two brakes, and the shaved ice is the friction doing its work. This is also why a stop is really an edge skill, not a courage skill.

Common faults: stopping on one side only (games approach from both); standing tall, which puts weight over the toes and makes the blades catch and chatter instead of shave; and learning stops at full speed first. What works Start at a jog, one side, to a full stop, then the other side — same session, every session, until both sides are boring. Boring is the goal: a stop you must think about is not yet yours.

See it done: the "Watch It Done" shelf below carries a full hockey-stop tutorial, verified for this page.

Backwards skating & transitions Tap to open / close

Backwards propulsion comes from C-cuts: weight on one foot, the other pushes out in a C shape against its inside edge and recovers under you — alternating, with knees bent and chest up, so you can see the play. Backwards crossovers add the second push and real speed on turns, the same mechanics as forward crossovers mirrored.

Transitions (turning from forward to backwards without losing speed) are a weight shift, not a jump: unweight, open the hips toward the new direction, pivot on the balls of the feet — the front of the blades — and land already bent and gliding. Done on the toes of the blade with knees soft, it is a swivel; done flat-footed and tall, it is a stumble.

Why defenders live here: defending is skating backwards while reading forwards. A defender who cannot skate backwards at the attacker's speed cannot control the gap (see the Thinking Game) and is reduced to turning and chasing — the most expensive way to defend there is.

Common fault: sitting back on the heels going backwards, which puts the weight behind the blades and makes every correction a near-fall. Weight stays over the balls of the feet in both directions.

Part two

Stick & Puck

The stick is a lever, a sensor, and a passing lane all at once. Grip: top hand at the very end of the shaft (it is the control hand — it rolls the blade open and closed), bottom hand sliding lower for passing and shooting, higher and looser for handling. Blade flat on the ice, puck cradled in the middle of the blade.

Stickhandling with your head up — how it is actually built Tap to open / close

Nobody learns head-up handling by being shouted at to look up. The skill is built in a honest order:

  • Feel first. Stationary, eyes wherever they need to be, move the puck side to side with soft, cupping touches — blade rolling over the puck's edges, never slapping it. You are teaching your hands what the puck's position feels like through the shaft.
  • Then glance, don't stare. Moving slowly, look up, glance down only to check, look up again. The glances get rarer as the feel gets truer.
  • Then peripheral vision. At real skill, the puck lives at the bottom edge of your vision while your eyes read the play — defenders, open ice, a teammate's stick. That is the actual end state: not "never looking," but not needing to look.

Why the order matters: vision is your scarcest resource in a game. Every second spent watching the puck is a second not spent seeing the hit coming, the lane opening, or the pass that was there. But eyes-up handling on top of unbuilt feel just loses the puck slower. Feel is the foundation; eyes are what the foundation buys.

The named myth: What really hasn't "Keep your head down on the puck" taught as a habit. Looking down to find the puck is a beginner's stage, honestly passed through — as a permanent habit it blinds you to the whole game and, in checking leagues, puts your head where it must never be. The opposite slogan fails too: demanding head-up play before the feel exists produces turnovers, not vision.

What to try Handle a ball or puck while naming things you can see around the room — a teammate drill version: a partner holds up fingers, you call the number without looking down. If the puck keeps escaping, the honest answer is more feel reps, not more willpower.

Passing — forehand, backhand, and why the saucer exists Tap to open / close

Forehand pass: puck starts toward the heel of the blade, sweeps across it as you push through, and leaves off the toe with a small follow-through pointed at the target. The sweep is not decoration — it keeps the puck flat and spinning gently, which keeps it lying flat on arrival instead of bouncing.

Backhand pass: the mirror image on the blade's back side. Weaker and less accurate for almost everyone — which is exactly why it must be practised: the puck will be on your backhand in games whether you trained it or not.

Saucer pass: the puck is deliberately lifted, tilted, and set flying flat like a saucer, so it sails over an obstacle — a defender's stick, a sprawled player — and lands flat to be received.

Why it exists: the flat passing lane is the one defenders are paid to close. A pass along the ice into a stick is a turnover; the same pass floated a few inches up arrives clean. The trade-off is real: a saucer is slower and harder to land softly, so it is a tool for blocked lanes, not a default.

Common faults: passing to where a teammate is instead of where they will be — lead them; telegraphing by staring at the target for a full second; and the "hope pass" up the middle of your own zone, the highest-risk pass in the sport. What works Aim at the receiver's stick blade, at a firmness they can handle — a pass is a delivery, judged at the receiving end.

Receiving — soft hands, and why hard hands bounce pucks away Tap to open / close

A pass arrives carrying speed. If your blade is a rigid wall, the puck hits it and rebounds — physics, not bad luck. Receiving is cushioning: as the puck arrives, let the blade give a little, travelling a few inches in the puck's own direction, so the puck's energy is absorbed over distance instead of in one instant. Think catching an egg versus catching it with a board.

Why "soft hands" is literal: a relaxed bottom hand and a slightly loose top-hand grip let the stick flex and move on impact. A white-knuckle grip locks the whole lever rigid. The same softness is what good stickhandlers have — it is one skill wearing two hats. Also: present your blade early, angled to deflect the puck down onto the ice, and meet passes in front of your body where your hands have room to give.

Common fault: stabbing at the puck as it arrives — adding your motion to its speed — then blaming the pass. Some passes are genuinely bad; the honest check is whether pucks bounce off you from every passer. Then it is the hands.

Part three

Shooting

Every shot is the same physics wearing different clothes: your weight moves, the stick bends and unbends, and the puck leaves before the goalie finishes reading it. First the shared engine, then the four shots.

The shared engine — weight transfer and stick flex, in plain words

Weight transfer: shots are powered from the ice up. You load your weight onto the leg nearer the puck (for most shots), drive down and through, and finish with your weight moving toward the target. Arm-only shots are slow for the same reason a punch thrown without the hips is slow: the big muscles never got involved.

Stick flex: a modern stick is a spring. As you shoot, you press the shaft down into the ice so it bows — storing energy like a drawn bow — and as the blade sweeps through, the shaft straightens and snaps the puck forward, adding the spring's energy to your own. That is why shooters plant the blade slightly behind the puck and lean on the shaft: no bend, no bonus.

The flex number: a stick's flex rating is, roughly, how many pounds of force it takes to bend that shaft one inch. A 75-flex needs about 75 lb; a 100-flex about 100 lb. The honest rule: if you cannot bend it, it cannot help you — a too-stiff stick returns nothing and forces arm-shooting; a too-soft stick feels whippy and inaccurate at full effort. Lighter and younger players generally need lower numbers. It is a matching problem, not a toughness contest. What really hasn't Buying the stiffest stick on the rack as a badge of strength.

Coach note If your record shows shooting work piling up with the same note twice, change one variable only — puck position, or weight transfer, or target — so the note can say what actually changed.

Wrist shot — the bread-and-butter shot Tap to open / close

Puck beside you, slightly behind your front foot. Weight loads onto the puck-side leg; hands pull the puck in a short sweep — top hand pulls back and across, bottom hand pushes through — the blade rolls from heel to toe, and the puck spins off the toe as your weight finishes over your front leg, stick pointing at the target.

Why it rules: it needs almost no wind-up, so it can leave from a stickhandle, off either foot, in traffic — the shot that exists in the most game situations. Its power is modest next to a slap shot; its availability is unmatched, and availability is what gets shots on net.

Common fault: scooping — lifting with the blade like a shovel, which floats the puck weakly. The lift comes from the blade's natural loft as it rolls through, not from digging under the puck.

Snap shot — power on a short fuse Tap to open / close

A hybrid: the blade pulls back a few inches off the puck — a mini wind-up — then snaps down and through, striking ice just behind the puck so the shaft loads and releases in one quick motion. Hands separate a little more than on a wrist shot; the whole thing happens in a blink.

Why it exists: it trades a little of the slap shot's power for a lot of its speed of release. From mid-range, it is the shot goalies say they read latest — there is almost no backswing to read.

Common fault: letting the mini wind-up grow into a slap shot's wind-up, losing the one thing the snap shot is for.

Slap shot — the big wind-up, honestly priced Tap to open / close

Hands wider, bottom hand lower. The stick rises behind you — no higher than it needs to — comes down to strike the ice a few inches behind the puck, the shaft bows hard against the ice, and the stored bend plus your full weight transfer drive through the puck. Follow through toward the target.

Why it is the hardest shot and the rarest good one: the wind-up is a telegram. Everyone on the ice, goalie first, sees it coming and has time to set, step out, or block. Its honest home: the point with time and a screen in front, or a one-timer where a pass supplies the timing. What really hasn't The giant wind-up as a habit from everywhere — it spends the shooter's best asset, surprise, to buy power the situation rarely needs.

Common fault: hitting only puck, no ice — no ice contact means no flex load, and the "slap" shot leaves slower than a good wrist shot.

Backhand — the neglected shot Tap to open / close

The wrist shot mirrored: puck on the blade's back side, weight loads and transfers the same way, blade sweeps and the puck leaves off the back of the blade. The blade's back is rounder and less lofted, so the shot is naturally flatter and harder to lift — which is why the in-close backhand roof shot is a genuine specialist skill.

Why goalies respect it anyway: it is the hardest shot to read. The mechanics are compact and the release point is unusual, so the goalie's pattern-matching fails. A merely decent backhand, released unexpectedly, beats a beautiful forehand the goalie saw coming.

Common fault: never practising it because it feels clumsy for the first hundred reps. It feels clumsy for everyone's first hundred reps. That is the price, and it is the same for all.

Release quickness & deception — beating the read, not the goalie

Goalies do not react to pucks so much as predict them, from your wind-up, your blade angle, your eyes, and your body's direction. Deception attacks the prediction:

  • Hide the release: shoot off the stickhandle with no extra motion — the less changes before the shot, the less there is to read.
  • Change the angle: pull the puck a few inches toward your body (or push it away) in the last moment before release. The goalie set for the old angle; the puck leaves from a new one. A few inches at the stick becomes a foot at the net.
  • Shoot off either foot: a shot that can leave while your weight is "wrong" arrives a half-beat before the goalie expects any shot at all.
  • Eyes sell, too: a glance at a passing option before shooting is a honest fake — the goalie must honour it. What to try Practise one deception until it is invisible; three half-learned fakes fool nobody.

Part four

The Thinking Game

Most of a game, you do not have the puck. What you do then — where you stand, what you take away, when you change — decides whether your skills ever get a chance to matter.

Hockey's real stakes: trust and ice time

There is no money on the line in most hockey, and the real currency was never money anyway. It is trust, paid out as ice time. Coaches hand the penalty kill, the last minute of a close game, and the defensive-zone faceoff to the players they trust — and that standing is earned by exactly the unglamorous skills on this page: the backcheck finished, the gap held, the puck got out along the wall. Nobody's highlight reel earns the last minute. Their habits do. Every drill below is, in the end, a way of becoming the player a coach can trust — and that your teammates can lean on.

Positioning without the puck Tap to open / close

With the puck (your team has it): be an option. Open players stand in lanes the puck carrier can actually see and reach — "support" means a pass that exists, at a distance that helps: too close crowds the play, too far makes the pass a hope. Move to stay an option; standing open for one second then drifting covered helps no one.

Without it: be a problem. Take away the most dangerous option first — in your own end, that is the front of your net and the middle of the ice; passes to the outside are the ones you can live with. The honest ordering: protect the middle, then the net-front, then everything else.

Why "stay between your man and your net" is only half of it: pure man-watching drags defenders out of position chasing one player. Modern defending is a blend: stay goal-side, yes, but in a shape that also guards the dangerous ice. When both honestly apply, guard the ice first and the man within it.

Gap control — the defender's whole job in one distance Tap to open / close

The gap is the distance between a backwards-skating defender and the puck carrier. Roughly a stick's length is the working gap in open ice: close enough that the carrier cannot wind up, shoot cleanly, or change direction for free; far enough that one move does not beat you clean.

Why it works — and why it helps your goalie: a tight, honest gap means the carrier's only shots are rushed, off-balance, or from the outside — your goalie faces fewer clean looks, which is the entire point of defence, stated in usefulness terms. A loose gap invites the carrier to use you as a screen and shoot through your legs. A gap that is too tight gets walked around. The skill is matching the attacker's speed backwards so the gap stays constant — which is why backwards skating is in Part One.

Trade-off, plainly: stepping up early to close the gap can end the play at the blue line — or get you beaten for a breakaway. The honest read is support: step up when a teammate is behind you to cover; hold and contain when you are the last one back.

Forecheck shapes — 2-1-2 vs 1-2-2, with trade-offs Tap to open / close

The forecheck is how a team pressures the puck in the attacking end to win it back. The numbers name where the three forwards stand: first number = forwards deep, then the next layer, then the last.

Faceoff basics Tap to open / close

The centre's job is not to "win a duel" in the abstract — it is to deliver the puck to a planned spot: straight back to a defenceman, back to a winger, or forward for a set play. Hands low and choked down for leverage, weight forward, eyes on the official's hand (not the puck in the air — the drop is faster than your reaction to the puck itself), and the first move is a short, violent pull or push, planned before the drop.

Why it is a team skill: a centre's faceoff work gives five teammates the puck instead of a chase — wingers who tie up their checks and defencemen who arrive on time turn a 50/50 drop into a planned possession. And why the centre sometimes should lose on purpose: in some set plays, directing the puck to a specific contested spot beats a clean win to nowhere. It is chess at a standstill.

Common fault: watching the puck drop from the official's hand. By the time it lands, the other centre has already moved. Watch the hand; react to its motion.

Shift discipline — your legs are a resource that drains

A shift is spent, not served. Legs skated hard are good for roughly a short, intense shift — after that, speed, edge control, and decisions all decay together. The player who stays out tired cannot backcheck, cannot close a gap, and turns one person's overstay into a goal against that the whole line pays for.

What works Change while you can still skate. The honest rule: come off when you are still able to sprint to the bench, ideally while your team has the puck moving up-ice, so the change costs nothing. Short shifts skated hard beat long shifts skated tired at every level of the sport — and changing on time is a team duty, not a personal preference: your fresh teammate's legs are the team's resource, and your tired ones are its liability. Managing your own energy honestly — spending it for the team, in full, then recovering — is a skill exactly like a crossover, and coaches trust the players who have it.

A contested one, both shelves at the same size: young players — specialise early, or play many sports?

The multi-sport shelf

Long-term athlete-development research consistently finds that most elite adult players played several sports as children and specialised later; early single-sport specialisation is associated with higher overuse-injury and burnout rates, and broad movement skills (running, jumping, throwing, other stick and ball games) transfer into skating balance, hand-eye skill, and game reading. On this shelf: childhood is for a wide base; the pyramid narrows later.

The early-focus shelf

The honest counterweight: ice time is genuinely scarce and expensive, skating is an unusual movement no land sport reproduces, and players who start skating very late rarely reach the highest levels — the base has to include real skating early, whatever else is in it. Some families also simply cannot do three sports' schedules. On this shelf: protect serious skating time early, even inside a multi-sport childhood.

The two shelves mostly agree more than they argue: skate early and often, and do not let one sport eat a child's whole year or whole body. What the evidence does not support is year-round single-sport hockey for young children as a shortcut — it is the one claim here that belongs on the "hasn't" shelf.

Part five · in brief, honestly scoped

Goalie Basics

A short, honest shelf — enough to understand the position and start in it, not a pretence that one section makes a goaltender. Goalie coaching is its own craft; a real goalie coach is this position's version of the real checks named all over this page.

Stance: feet a little wider than shoulders, knees bent, weight on the balls of the feet, chest up, glove and blocker in front of the body where they can be seen — a goalie who cannot see their own glove will not trust it. Skates on edges, ready to move in any direction: the stance is a loaded spring, same as a skater's.

Angles, in plain geometry: imagine lines from the puck to each goal post. Your job is to stand on the middle line of that narrowing wedge, far enough out to fill as much of it as you can. Stepping out ("challenging") shrinks the open net the shooter sees — try it yourself in the Angle Toy below — but every step out is a step you must travel back if the puck is passed across. Depth is a trade: more coverage now, more distance to cover next.

Butterfly: both knees drop, pads flare flat along the ice, sealing the bottom of the net edge to edge, stick covering the middle, chest tall, gloves up.

Butterfly buysButterfly costs
The entire bottom of the net, sealed at once — where most shots honestly go.The top of the net opens — a shot that rises beats a dropped goalie.
A stable, repeatable platform for rebounds and second shots.Mobility: from your knees, every lateral move is a push and a recovery; passes across are its hardest test.

What works Butterfly as a response to a close, low, committed shot — not as a default resting position from far out, where standing gives the same coverage with full mobility kept. What really hasn't Dropping early on every shot: it hands the shooter the top of the net for free and spends your mobility before the play declares itself.

Coach note If your record's goalie sessions cluster on butterfly work alone, the honest gap is usually movement — skating, in goalie skates, is this position's Part One too.

Practice toy · real geometry

The Angle Trainer

One end of a rink, drawn to scale: the net is 6 feet wide, the crease is real, and every line below is computed, not drawn for show. Drag the shooter (red) and the goalie (dark) with a finger or mouse. The pale wedge is every shot the shooter can see; the dark band inside it is the part the goalie's body covers. What is left pale at the net is genuinely open — from this puck, against this goalie position.

Drag = move. The maths: lines run from the puck to both posts (the shooter's whole world). The goalie is drawn 3.2 ft wide — a fair width for pads, body and gloves held in front — and the share of the net hidden behind that width, projected from the puck, is the covered share. Real limits, printed honestly: a real goalie also has height (this toy is top-down), moves, and reacts; treat the numbers as the geometry of the problem, not a prediction of goals.

Part six

Drills — Levelled, With Honest Transfer Notes

Every drill family below is a ramp, the way real coaching already works: Level 1 stationary or solo at walking pace → Level 2 moving, full length of the ice → Level 3 with a partner or light pressure → Level 4 game speed, decisions included. The rule of the ramp: move up only when the current level is clean — not lucky once, clean most reps — and drop back down without shame when it isn't. Dropping a level is not failure; it is how the ramp is supposed to be used.

Skating ramp Tap to open / close
  • Level 1: edge rolls standing at the boards; one-foot glides in a straight line, both feet, both edges.
  • Level 2: full-stride laps with a two-count glide hold; crossover circles both directions; hockey stops both sides from a jog.
  • Level 3: stops and starts on a whistle; transitions forward-to-backwards on a line, both ways; backwards crossovers around the circles.
  • Level 4: race-to-the-puck drills against a partner; stop, turn, and accelerate out — the stop becomes the start of the next play.

Transfer note: on-ice skating drills transfer almost one-to-one, because they are the skill. The honest gap is pressure: a stop that works alone at Level 2 must be re-earned at Level 4 with someone arriving.

Stick & shooting ramp Tap to open / close
  • Level 1: stationary handling — wide, narrow, toe drags; wrist shots at a target from a standstill, weight transfer exaggerated.
  • Level 2: handling while gliding; shots off the move, off either foot; give-and-go passes with a partner, forehand then backhand.
  • Level 3: handling around obstacles with eyes up (call out numbers a partner holds up); saucer passes over a stick laid on the ice; catch-and-shoot in one motion.
  • Level 4: shots off a pass at game pace; one-timers; small-area games where every skill must survive contact and hurry.

Transfer note: stationary shooting builds the shape of the shot honestly — most players' first real fix (weight transfer) is learned standing still. It does not build timing against a moving pass or a closing defender; that only exists at Levels 3–4.

Dryland, honestly separated

What dryland can build: strength and balance that skating uses (single-leg strength is the big one); stickhandling feel with a ball — genuinely transferable, because the hand skills are the same skill; shooting mechanics on a pad or against a net, including flex loading, if you use your real stick; fitness, which buys more good shifts.

What dryland cannot build: edges. There is no dryland substitute for a blade biting ice — not roller skates (similar, honestly useful for stride pattern, but the edge behaviour differs), not slide boards (a cousin of the stride, not the stride), not balance boards. Anyone who sells you off-ice edge training is selling you something else with edges' name on it. What really hasn't Also on this shelf: practising only what is already fun. The ramp exists because the weak side and the backhand are where the honest gains live.

Fatigue, on both shelves: What works Training tired is game-realistic conditioning — third periods are played tired, and fitness work at the end of a session teaches your body to function there. Film yourself — the real check

This page cannot see your stride, and neither can a mirror. Film is the honest middle ground between guessing and a coach:

  • Stride: phone at ice level, directly behind you, 10 full strides away from camera — incomplete extension and a wide recovery show plainly from behind.
  • Stops & crossovers: from the side, at hip height, far enough to keep your whole body in frame through the turn.
  • Shot: from the side for weight transfer (does your weight finish toward the target?) and from behind the net, safely off to the side, for release point.
  • Goalie: from directly behind the net, elevated if possible — angle errors are invisible from the side and obvious from behind.

Then the standing rule of this whole wing: filming from these angles and a real coach's eye are the real checks. Film shows you what happened; a coach shows you what to change first. Use both.
