The Ruler's Test — Kassym-Jomart Tokayev, President of Kazakhstan

Subject: Kassym-Jomart Tokayev — President of Kazakhstan since 20 March 2019, when he succeeded Nursultan Nazarbayev, who had ruled since independence and designated him as his successor. A former Soviet diplomat, UN Under-Secretary-General, foreign minister and prime minister under Nazarbayev, he was elected in his own right in June 2019 and re-elected in a snap election on 20 November 2022 with a reported 81.3% of the vote, under a constitutional amendment — his own proposal — limiting the presidency to a single, non-renewable seven-year term running to 2029. He remains in office in November 2026, now governing under a new Constitution adopted by referendum on 15 March 2026 and in force since 1 July 2026, which replaced the bicameral parliament with a unicameral Kurultai (first elected 23 August 2026) and created a vice-presidency, to which he appointed his longtime adviser Erlan Karin on 31 August 2026. On 7 July 2026 the Constitutional Court, ruling on his own request for interpretation, held that terms held under the previous constitution do not count against the new constitution's single-term limit — leaving him legally eligible to seek another seven-year term in 2029. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The law reached the summit of the old order. After January 2022, the state dismantled the legal architecture of the Nazarbayev era: parliament annulled the Law on the First President (Elbasy) in January 2023, stripping the former president's family of its statutory immunity and lifetime privileges; the June 2022 referendum had already removed the Elbasy status from the constitution; Nazarbayev's office was closed in June 2023 and the capital's name reverted from Nur-Sultan to Astana in September 2022 (Constitutional Court decision, January 2023; RFE/RL, June 2023).
  • Members of the former ruling family were convicted in open proceedings. Nazarbayev's nephew Qairat Satybaldy was arrested in March 2022 and sentenced in September 2022 to six years for embezzlement and abuse of power at Kazakhtelecom and the Transport Service Center, with property confiscated and his general's rank stripped; he repaid some 40 billion tenge in damages under a plea agreement (RFE/RL, September 2022; Times of Central Asia, June 2024). Former justice minister Marat Beketayev was convicted in July 2025 and sentenced to nine years; businessman Kairat Boranbayev, a former Nazarbayev in-law, was convicted in 2023 (Times of Central Asia reporting, 2025).
  • Asset recovery was legislated, not merely announced: the July 2023 Law on the Return of Illegally Acquired Assets created a register and recovery machinery aimed at persons holding assets above roughly $100 million whose legality they cannot demonstrate. Prosecutors report that by March 2026 recovery measures covered more than 1.3 trillion tenge, with about 1.076 trillion tenge (~$2.4 billion) returned; recovered funds have been publicly directed to schools and social infrastructure (Akorda, July 2023; Times of Central Asia, March 2026).
  • The outcome measure improved for most of his tenure: Kazakhstan's Corruption Perceptions Index score rose from 31 (2018) to a peak of 40 (2024), its best result (Transparency International CPI series).

Evidence against:

  • The dismantling stopped at the man at its center. Nazarbayev himself retains the general presidential immunity under the law on presidents, has never been charged with anything, and lives unprosecuted; his son-in-law Timur Kulibayev, among the country's richest men, lost posts in January 2022 but has faced no prosecution and retains his fortune. The law fell on the clan's expendable members, not on its head or its largest fortune (RFE/RL, June 2023; Times of Central Asia, 2024–2025).
  • Within the same family and the same events, outcomes diverged by proximity. Samat Abish — Nazarbayev's nephew and first deputy head of the National Security Committee (KNB) during the January 2022 events — was tried in a closed, classified proceeding and in February 2024 received an eight-year suspended sentence for exceeding his authority, having spent no time in custody; his brother Satybaldy got six years, and KNB chairman Karim Masimov got eighteen years for treason at a closed trial in April 2023 (RFE/RL, 20 March 2024; Astana City Court via Times of Central Asia). Whether Abish's leniency reflects the evidence or his bloodline is CONTESTED — the classification of his case file makes the question unanswerable from the public record, which is itself the point.
  • The same legal system that convicted the old clan imprisons the new opposition. The unregistered party Alga Kazakhstan had its registration refused 25 times between May 2022 and November 2024, on grounds its supporters documented as pretextual; its leader Marat Zhylanbayev was sentenced in November 2023 to seven years on extremism charges that Human Rights Watch calls politically motivated, and the UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention called in April 2025 for his immediate release. Zhanbolat Mamay, leader of the unregistered Democratic Party, received a six-year suspended sentence and a six-year ban on public activity in April 2023. Journalist Duman Mukhammedkarim was sentenced in August 2024 to seven years after interviewing an exiled opposition figure. Parliamentary Assembly of the Council of Europe members counted 49 political prisoners in April 2025; Tokayev has stated publicly that Kazakhstan has no political prisoners (PACE written declaration No. 804, 23 April 2025; CIVICUS Monitor, 2023; RFE/RL, January 2024).
  • Enforcement instruments built for the old elite were settled by negotiation where the defendant could pay: Satybaldy's prison term was replaced by restricted freedom in 2023 after he transferred assets to the state, and Boranbayev was released in October 2023 on the same basis — a documented pattern in which the price of liberty is restitution, available only to those with assets to return (Times of Central Asia, 2024–2025).
  • The outcome measure has turned: the CPI score fell from 40 (2024) to 38 (2025), rank 96th of 182, with Transparency International Kazakhstan attributing the decline in part to the non-transparent dissolution of the standalone Anti-Corruption Agency and the transfer of its functions to the KNB in June 2025 (Transparency International, CPI 2025, released 10 February 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the media, the electoral authority, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • Some referees were genuinely rebuilt. The Constitutional Court — abolished under Nazarbayev in 1995 — was restored by the June 2022 referendum and began work in January 2023, with citizens able to appeal to it directly; Kazakhstan completed abolition of the death penalty in 2022 (June 2022 referendum record; UN Committee Against Torture review, May 2023).
  • The formal barriers to political organization were lowered: the membership threshold for registering a party fell from 20,000 to 5,000 in 2022, and new parties were in fact registered afterward — Respublica in 2023 and Adilet in 2026 (Tokayev's reform address, 16 March 2022; ODIHR reporting).
  • Elections are professionally administered even by his critics' account: ODIHR found the 2022 presidential and 2023 parliamentary votes "efficiently prepared" and election day calm, and the August 2026 Kurultai election "technically well prepared" (ODIHR final reports, 14 July 2023; IEOM preliminary statement, 24 August 2026).
  • No central banker, judge, or election commissioner is documented to have been removed for ruling against him; the National Bank operates under a conventional inflation-targeting mandate and its leadership changes have followed ordinary process.

Evidence against:

  • The anti-corruption referee was folded into the security service. By decree of 30 June 2025 Tokayev dissolved the standalone Anti-Corruption Agency and transferred its functions to the National Security Committee — the same KNB whose leadership he had purged in 2022 — a move Transparency International Kazakhstan described as non-transparent and warned turns anti-corruption into "a PR campaign with no independent oversight" (presidential decree, 30 June 2025; Transparency International Kazakhstan, February 2026).
  • Media autonomy narrowed by statute and by practice. The mass media law he signed on 19 June 2024 imposed compulsory state registration on online publications and gave the government broad power to deny, suspend, or withdraw the accreditation of foreign media — powers Human Rights Watch, the International Press Institute, and SEEMO warned are structured for use against critical journalists; accreditation rules adopted under it were used within weeks against domestic journalists covering the government (one denial was reversed after public criticism; a journalists' court challenge was dismissed in November 2024). RFE/RL's Kazakh Service correspondents — more than 30 of them — faced accreditation difficulties in 2023–2024 before a negotiated settlement (HRW, 24 June 2024; CIVICUS/KIBHR monitoring, 2024). Kazakhstan fell to 149th of 180 in RSF's 2026 World Press Freedom Index, and RSF placed Tokayev personally on its 2026 "press freedom predators" list in the legal-persecution category (RSF, 2026).
  • The electoral gate is kept by registration, and the gate stays shut to opponents. ODIHR's consistent finding across 2022, 2023, and 2026 is that limits on fundamental freedoms "prevent some political groups from participating in elections": no genuinely opposition party has been registered under Tokayev, while pro-presidential parties register without documented difficulty (ODIHR final reports, July 2023; IEOM statement, August 2026).
  • The 2026 constitutional reset was executed on his timetable and removed remaining friction. The new constitution — drafted under a commission he created, published 12 February 2026, and adopted by referendum five weeks later — abolished the Senate, ended independent candidacies (candidates may be nominated only by parties, from their own members, under closed lists), and left most prior ODIHR recommendations unaddressed; the electoral laws were then "substantially revised close to the elections with limited public consultation." The August 2026 Kurultai election returned 110 of 145 seats to Adilet, the party formed in spring 2026 by merging his former ruling party Amanat into a new vehicle; ODIHR found all seven contesting parties supported the president's policies, that Adilet enjoyed "an undue advantage" blurring state and party, and that serious counting violations and discrepancies between observed and official turnout "raised questions about the integrity" of the count (IEOM Statement of Preliminary Findings and Conclusions, 24 August 2026; Central Election Commission results, 25 August 2026).
  • The Constitutional Court's most consequential act of 2026 was performed at his request and in his interest: on 7 July 2026, interpreting the new constitution on the president's own application, it held that office-holding under the 1995 constitution does not count toward the new single-term limit — clearing the only legal obstacle to his running again in 2029 (Constitutional Court Resolution No. 89-NP, 7 July 2026; Times of Central Asia, July 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the street, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Confronted with the first protests in January 2022, he conceded the trigger within days and in full: on 4–5 January 2022, as protests over the lifting of the liquefied-gas price cap spread from Zhanaozen, he restored price regulation (a cap of 50 tenge per litre for six months), accepted the resignation of Prime Minister Askar Mamin's entire government, and on 5 January removed Nazarbayev as chairman of the Security Council, taking the post himself (presidential addresses, 4–5 January 2022; contemporaneous reporting).
  • He followed the crisis with a reform package rather than only repression: the March 2022 reform address, the June 2022 constitutional referendum (approved by 77% on official figures), restoration of the Constitutional Court, reduction of presidential appointment powers in parliament, direct election of district and city akims, and the November 2022 amnesty law, which released or reduced the sentences of roughly 1,500 January-events participants (Tokayev address, 16 March 2022; amnesty law, 2 November 2022).
  • He has marked the dead publicly rather than erasing them: in December 2022 he unveiled a memorial to the January victims in Almaty, and the state acknowledges the official toll of 238 dead, with the Prosecutor General's office publishing the list of names (AP reporting, December 2022; Kazakh delegation to the UN Committee Against Torture, May 2023).

Evidence against:

  • The concession and the crackdown were simultaneous, and the crackdown was ordered in the language of extermination. On 7 January 2022 he announced he had "given the order to law enforcement agencies and the army to shoot to kill without warning," dismissed negotiations as "nonsense" ("What kind of talks can we hold with criminals and murderers?"), and on 5 January had already requested the Russian-led CSTO intervention that deployed some 2,000–2,500 foreign troops (televised address, 7 January 2022; CSTO deployment, 6–20 January 2022).
  • The factual claim used to justify that order was never substantiated and quietly abandoned by his own officials. On 7–8 January 2022 he asserted that Almaty alone had been attacked by "20,000 bandits"/"terrorists," some speaking non-Kazakh languages, in six waves; he deleted the tweet, produced no evidence then or since, and Kazakhstan's chief prosecutor later retracted the figure. No foreign terrorist organization behind the unrest has ever been documented; aside from roughly ten members of a banned domestic group, no religious or foreign network was identified (Tokayev statements, 7–8 January 2022; Chatham House, February 2022; RFE/RL, January 2024). Tokayev himself has never corrected the claim or apologized for it.
  • He refused the independent check. Calls by the European Parliament, the UN, and human rights organizations for an international investigation into the January events were rejected; the investigation remained in the hands of the same security apparatus that carried out the crackdown (Open Dialogue Foundation submission; AP reporting, January 2023).
  • Four years on, his position is closure without answers. On 5 January 2026 he told the newspaper Turkistan that "the main picture was restored long ago," that public commissions had brought clarity to every episode, and that those discussing the events get lost in details that do not matter — while, in the same weeks, the case into 20 deaths in Shymkent was closed and victims' families were still publicly asking who killed their relatives (Zakon.kz, January 2026; Shyndyq.kz review, 3 November 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — the January 2022 victims' families, ethnic minorities, the poor, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The January amnesty counted most participants back in: the November 2022 law dropped cases and expunged records for those convicted of lesser offenses and cut heavier sentences, covering about 1,500 people — though it expressly excluded those charged with organizing riots, extremism, treason, or torture (amnesty law, 2 November 2022; Caspian Policy Center briefing, January 2023).
  • Some security-force accountability exists on paper and occasionally in verdicts: prosecutors acknowledged six detainees died from torture; 29 law-enforcement officers were named suspects in torture cases in 2022, and convictions followed in a small number of cases — including six police officers sentenced to three years each in January 2025 (Prosecutor General's Office, November 2022; Kazakh delegation to the UN Committee Against Torture, May 2023; HRW, January 2025).
  • Ethnic minorities have not been singled out for exclusion under his rule: Kazakhstan's multiethnic order — including its large Russian-speaking population — has been maintained without the ethnic purges or language purges seen elsewhere in the region, and the state's official accounting of the January dead, 238, was eventually published by name.
  • Material counting-in has been a stated and funded program: recovered oligarch assets are publicly channeled to schools and social infrastructure (150 billion tenge to 89 schools over three years, per official reporting), and the 2026 State of the Nation address committed National Fund resources to social projects in 2026–2027 (Times of Central Asia, 2025–2026; presidential address, 28 October 2026).

Evidence against:

  • For the January dead, the accounting was never completed. Of 238 people killed — including bystanders and children — the overwhelming majority of deaths were never individually explained in public; investigations into civilian deaths were closed in case after case, and the state's own figures to the UN record exactly one completed sentence for a torture death. Human rights lawyer Tatiana Chernobil documented that of 190 torture complaints taken up by the Coalition Against Torture, 104 were dismissed; the Human Rights Commissioner reported roughly 80% of torture cases dismissed before court (AP reporting, January 2023; UN Committee Against Torture review, May 2023). More than 10,000 people were detained in January 2022; 1,396 had been convicted by January 2024 (Prosecutor General's Office).
  • The victims' families are still, in November 2026, the ones asking. Reviews compiled from court and media records on the fourth anniversary document families who have never been told the circumstances of their relatives' deaths, closed cases (including the 20 Shymkent deaths), and journalists who covered the events serving sentences or professional bans — while the president declares the picture complete (Shyndyq.kz, 3 November 2026).
  • His opponents' voters are counted out of the political arithmetic altogether. Because no opposition party can register (Test 2), the 2026 Kurultai election offered voters seven parties that all support the president; ODIHR concluded that political choice was "reduced by the lack of distinct political alternatives." Citizens who vote against the system have, on the documented record, no one to vote for (IEOM preliminary statement, 24 August 2026).
  • Peaceful dissenters are counted as criminals: the 49 political prisoners counted by PACE members in April 2025 include activists imprisoned for participating in peaceful rallies, supporting unregistered movements, or interviewing opposition figures — categories of people the state's accounting treats not as citizens with grievances but as extremists (PACE written declaration No. 804, 23 April 2025).

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises and stated positions vs. the record since March 2019, with dates.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The 2022 reform package was delivered in form. He promised constitutional reform after January 2022 and held the referendum on 5 June 2022; the Constitutional Court was restored, the death penalty fully abolished, the party-registration threshold lowered, the Elbasy provisions repealed, and the capital renamed back to Astana — each a specific, checkable commitment carried out (referendum record, 2022–2023).
  • The Nazarbayev dismantling was a stated course, pursued. His post-January 2022 pledge to end the old elite's monopolies produced the asset-recovery law and the return of roughly $2.4 billion in assets by official count (Test 1).
  • Economic headline, on his numbers: in his 28 October 2026 State of the Nation address he cited IMF projections of GDP approaching $400 billion in 2026 with per-capita GDP above $19,000, and committed dated infrastructure and social spending — figures presented as the record of his "Just Kazakhstan" program (presidential address, 28 October 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or reversed):

  • The single term — his signature word, reversed by his own hand. On 1 September 2022 Tokayev himself proposed limiting the presidency to "one term of seven years without the right to re-election," calling it essential to prevent "a monopoly on power"; the amendment was entrenched before the November 2022 election expressly so it would bind him, and after winning he said: "I believe that seven years will be enough… The most important thing is that there will be no monopoly on power in our country." On 3 January 2024 he went further, telling Egemen Qazaqstan that the rule that a president can be elected only once "will never change again," that the constitutional reform "was not aimed at further changing the Constitution," and denying rumors of a referendum to change it (State of the Nation address, 1 September 2022; Interfax, 27 September 2022; Egemen Qazaqstan interview, 3 January 2024).
  • What followed is documented step by step: in 2025 he set in motion a wholesale new constitution; its draft was published on 12 February 2026 and adopted by referendum on 15 March 2026 (official result: 87.15% in favor, turnout 73.12%); and on 7 July 2026 the Constitutional Court, on his own application, ruled that his service under the old constitution does not count — so the man who entrenched a single-term limit to bind himself is eligible to run in 2029 for a further seven years, which would extend his presidency to 2036, seventeen years after he first took the oath. He has not announced a candidacy; the reversal graded here is not a candidacy but the dismantling, by the promise-maker, of the promise's mechanism, after assuring the public it would never change (Central Referendum Commission, 17 March 2026; Constitutional Court Resolution No. 89-NP, 7 July 2026; Reuters, 31 August 2026).
  • "Listening State" and political pluralism — pledged, unbuilt. His 2019 and 2022 platforms promised a "listening state," dialogue with civil society, and political modernization; seven years on, ODIHR finds the same unimplemented recommendations on freedoms of expression, assembly, and association carried from report to report, RSF ranks the country 149th for press freedom, and the only parties permitted to contest elections support him (ODIHR final reports, 2023; IEOM statement, August 2026; RSF, 2026).
  • Accountability for January 2022 — pledged, declared complete instead. He promised that the January events would be fully investigated and the guilty punished regardless of position; the documented outcome (Tests 3 and 4) is closed cases, dismissed torture complaints, one completed sentence for a torture death, and a presidential statement in January 2026 that the picture was settled long ago.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Kassym-Jomart Tokayev's presidency divides into two documented halves. In the first, he did real things no predecessor had done: he broke the Nazarbayev family's legal supremacy, prosecuted its members, legislated the return of billions in assets, restored the Constitutional Court, abolished the death penalty, and conceded the grievance that filled the streets in January 2022 within days of its eruption. In the second half — the same record, continued — the law that reached the fallen clan never reached the clan's head or the new opposition's registration forms; the anti-corruption agency was folded into the security service; the press was registered, accredited, and ranked 149th in the world; and the January dead were counted as a number, 238, without being accounted for as persons, while their families' questions were answered by closure. The wobble grade is the honest split: he adjusted, genuinely, when the street moved in 2022 — and then spent four years declining to adjust on the questions that adjustment raised. But the word is where the record closes. A ruler who proposes a single-term limit as his own reform, entrenches it against himself, and then tells the nation it "will never change again" has made the clearest promise in this entire series. He then changed it — by referendum, by court ruling, by his own application — and stands eligible in 2029 to rule into a third decade. On the evidence assembled here, the seat gives one law to the defeated and another to the inconvenient, grips the referees it rebuilt, and keeps its word exactly as long as the word does not bind the keeper.
