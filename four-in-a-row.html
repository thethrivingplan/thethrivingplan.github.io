Four in a Row

Drop a disc, connect four of yours in a line — across, up-down, or diagonal — before the other side does. Thirty seconds to learn; the depth is in the threats you build and the ones you almost didn’t see.

Our own version of the proven, documented game Connect Four (Milton Bradley, 1974; mathematically solved by Victor Allis in 1988 — with perfect play the first player wins). Rules here stay true to that documented game; the name, art, and words are ours. The worst way to lose this game is to one threat you never saw — so the teaching layer below exists to make every threat visible, as much or as little as you want.

Teaching

Light names each threat the moment it forms. Coach also weighs your options before you move — and where more than one real strategy exists, it lays out the alternatives with their trade-offs, never just one way. A single answer appears only when there is genuinely one move that works (like a forced block). Off is silent.

Pro tool: the Threat highlighter button by the board lights up every one-move winning threat on the board — yours in green, theirs in red — so nothing wins unseen. Tap it on or off any time.

How pros think — more than one way to win

There is rarely one “right” strategy here. These are the real approaches strong players mix — each with its trade-off, in plain words. The Coach names which ones are live in your position.

StrategyHow it worksThe trade-off
Own the centreThe centre column sits in more possible winning lines than any other — 7 lines run through a centre disc. Strong players take it early and keep building around it.Everyone knows it. Fighting for the centre can leave the wings open, and a centre stack is easy to read.
Build two threats at onceOne threat gets blocked. A move that creates two winning threats at once can only be blocked in one place — the other wins next turn. This is the move the whole game bends toward.Setting it up takes quiet moves that don’t threaten anything yet — a sharp opponent may strike first while you build.
Block and buildWhen they threaten, don’t just plug the hole — block in the column that also grows your own line, so the same disc defends and attacks.Not always available. A plain block that builds nothing is still better than losing — defence first when the win is one move away.
Control the row aboveWatch the cell above a threat: dropping into a column can hand the other side the winning cell right on top of your disc. Pros check “what does my move let them play?” before every drop.Playing safe can mean passing up your own fastest threat. Sometimes the race is worth it — count who wins first.
Count the raceIf both sides have a threat building, the game becomes arithmetic: whose fourth disc lands first, and does either side have to spend a move blocking?Miscount by one and the “winning race” loses. When in doubt, the highlighter shows both sides’ threats at once.

Do the bot levels really differ? The receipts

Before this page shipped, the four levels played each other head-to-head in simulation — same engine, same code the bot uses against you, first move alternated so neither side got the first-drop edge every game. Results, exactly as they came out:

Match-up (10 games each)Result
Pro vs LearningPro 10 — Learning 0
Sharp vs LearningSharp 10 — Learning 0
Steady vs LearningSteady 10 — Learning 0
Pro vs SteadyPro 8 — Steady 1, 1 draw
Sharp vs SteadySharp 8 — Steady 2
Pro vs SharpPro 6 — Sharp 4

Every level beat every level below it — no name tags on the wrong strength. The closest fight is Pro vs Sharp, which is honest too: at five moves deep, Sharp already sees most traps; Pro’s edge is the double-threat discipline and never slipping.

How they differ under the hood: Learning searches 1 move deep and slips to a loose move ~55% of turns; Steady searches 3 deep, slips ~18%; Sharp searches 5 deep, slips ~4%, and takes double-threat moves; Pro searches 7 deep with no slips, takes and refuses double threats, and opens in the centre — the first move of the solved game’s winning strategy (Allis, 1988). Pro is labelled honestly: deep search plus solved-game principles, not the full precomputed solution.
