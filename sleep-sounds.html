    Binaural mode — one tone per ear

    Binaural mode plays a slightly different frequency into each ear. Your brain perceives the difference between them as a slow beat. It only works with headphones — through speakers both tones mix in the air and the effect is gone. What the evidence actually shows is written plainly in the Learn section below: interesting, studied, and not proven to do what it is often sold as doing.

    1 · Breathing Wind-Down About 4 minutes spoken · approx.

    A slow breathing settle. Breathe at whatever pace is comfortable — never force a breath to match the words.

    Find a comfortable position... and let your hands rest wherever they land. You do not need to sit up straight... and you do not need to do this perfectly. Just get a little more comfortable than you were a moment ago.

    When you are ready... let your eyes close... or let them rest on one soft, still spot. Take one easy breath in... and let it out slowly... like a quiet sigh. Again... breathe in... and let it fall away. There is nothing to hold onto in that breath. It came... it did its work... and it left.

    Now let the breathing find its own rhythm. You do not need to count... and you do not need to change anything. Notice the coolness as the air comes in... and the warmth as it leaves. In... and out. In... and out.

    If thoughts arrive — and they will — that is not a mistake. A thought is just your mind doing its evening rounds. Picture each one as a leaf set down on slow water. You do not chase it... and you do not push it away. You watch it drift... and you come back to the breath. In... and out.

    Let your shoulders drop a fraction. Let your jaw loosen. Let the small muscles around your eyes soften. Each breath out is a little permission to be heavier... quieter... less braced against the day.

    Stay here a little longer. Breathing in... breathing out. The day is finished asking things of you. For these next minutes... there is only the breath... coming... and going... and the quiet underneath it.

    When you feel ready... there is nothing you need to do next. Let the last breath out be the slowest one yet... and rest.

    2 · Body Scan About 5 minutes spoken · approx.

    Attention moved slowly from feet to crown, letting each part soften in turn.

    Lie back... or sit back... and let the surface underneath you do the holding. Feel its support along your back... your legs... your head. You can stop holding yourself up now. The bed... the chair... the floor will do it.

    Bring your attention to your feet. Notice whatever is there — warmth... coolness... the touch of a blanket... or nothing much at all. Nothing needs to change. Just notice... and as you breathe out... let your feet grow heavy and still.

    Move slowly up to your legs. Calves... knees... thighs. These muscles carried you through the whole day without being asked. Thank them the only way they understand: by letting them stop. Breathe out... and let them sink.

    Now your hips... and your belly. Let your belly be soft. Let it rise and fall with the breath like something floating. No holding in... no bracing. Just the slow tide of breathing.

    Your hands next... and your arms. Uncurl your fingers. Let your palms face however they want. Hands work all day and rest so rarely. Let them be completely done.

    Your shoulders... the great shelf where the day likes to sit. Breathe in... and on the breath out... let them drop away from your ears... down... and back. And again... down... and back.

    Finally your face. Smooth your forehead. Loosen your jaw — let your lips part a little if they want to. Soften around your eyes. The whole face... quiet.

    Now feel the whole body at once... heavy... warm... and held. Scan once more from feet to crown, and anywhere you find a last pocket of tension... breathe toward it... and let it go on the breath out. Stay in this heaviness as long as you like. There is nowhere else to be.

    3 · Sleep Descent About 5 minutes spoken · approx.

    A staircase down into sleep, for the very end of the night. If you fall asleep before it ends, it has done its job.

    Picture a wide, gentle staircase... in a quiet house... at the end of the day. The steps are broad and shallow... the railing is smooth under your hand... and the light is low and warm. You are at the top. There is no hurry at all.

    Take the first step down... and let the day get one step quieter. Another step... and the sounds of the house fade a little more. With each step... your body grows a fraction heavier... and your breathing grows a fraction slower.

    Step... down. The worries of the day cannot follow you here — they belong upstairs... in the daylight rooms... and they will keep until morning without you. Down here... there is only quiet.

    Step... down. Imagine the air getting softer... the light getting dimmer... the way the world feels just before snow. Your eyelids are heavy. Your thoughts are coming slower now... with more space between them.

    Step... down. Nearly at the bottom now. One more step... and you are in the deepest, quietest room of the house... where a bed is made and waiting... and the blankets are already warm.

    Let yourself be in that bed now. Feel the pillow take the weight of your head. Feel the blanket settle over you. The staircase is behind you... the day is behind you... and ahead of you there is only sleep... arriving in its own time... the way dusk arrives... gradually... and then all at once.

    You do not need to listen for the end of these words. Drift whenever you are ready. Good night.

    White, pink, and brown noise — what they actually are · Tap to open

    White noise gives every frequency the same amount of energy. It sounds like television static or a loud fan — bright and hissy. Because it covers a wide range evenly, it is good at masking other sounds: a door closing or a dog barking stands out less against it.

    Pink noise turns the high frequencies down and keeps more energy in the lows, which matches how human hearing works, so it sounds more balanced and softer than white noise — closer to steady rainfall or rustling leaves. Many people find white noise too sharp for sleep and pink noise easier to live with all night.

    Brown noise (also called red noise) turns the highs down much further still. What is left is a deep rumble — like a strong waterfall heard from a distance, or thunder that never quite arrives. People who find hiss distracting often settle on brown noise because there is almost no high end in it at all.

    None of the three is "best." They are three different shapes of the same static, and the right one is the one your ears stop noticing.

    What a binaural beat is — and what the evidence does and doesn't show · Tap to open

    What it is: play 220 Hz into the left ear and 226 Hz into the right, and you don't hear two tones — you hear one tone with a slow wobble, beating 6 times a second. The beat doesn't exist in the air; your brain builds it from the difference. That part is real and easy to verify with your own ears and headphones.

    What people use it for: different beat speeds are sold as sleep aids (slow beats), focus aids (faster beats), and more. You will find strong claims online.

    What the evidence shows, honestly: binaural beats have been studied in small trials for sleep, anxiety, attention, and pain. Results are mixed — some small studies find modest effects, others find none, and reviews generally call the evidence weak and inconsistent. There is no solid proof that a binaural beat puts your brain into a chosen state, and this page makes no medical claim for it. If a slow beat under a rain mix feels good to fall asleep to, that is a fine reason to use it — treat it as a sound you like, not a treatment.

    Why a fade beats a hard stop · Tap to open

    Your hearing stays on guard while you sleep — that's why a floorboard creak can wake you. A sound that cuts off suddenly is exactly the kind of change your ears are watching for, and the silence after it can feel louder than the sound did. A sound that fades slowly gives your hearing nothing sudden to react to: the level slides down below the room's own quiet, and by the time it's gone, you were already not hearing it. That's why this page's sleep timer fades everything out over several seconds instead of switching it off.

    How the mixer sounds are made — no recordings, honestly · Tap to open

    Every channel starts from one of two raw ingredients your browser computes on the spot: noise (random samples, shaped into white, pink, or brown) and tones (oscillators). Filters carve the noise into shapes — a low filter and a slow swell become ocean waves; a wandering filter becomes wind. The campfire's crackle and the storm's thunder are short bursts of shaped noise, fired at random intervals by the page, so no two minutes sound exactly alike — because they aren't a loop of anything.

    The honest limit: synthesis imitates. The rain here is filtered noise that behaves like rain, not a microphone in a storm. If a sound couldn't be made to honestly resemble itself this way, it didn't ship.

    Meditation and ASMR — what is actually known · Tap to open

    Guided meditation: slow breathing and body-scan exercises like the ones on this page are widely used as relaxation aids, and many people find them genuinely helpful for winding down. That is different from a treatment: this page claims nothing medical, and if sleep problems persist or worsen, a clinician — not a web page — is the right next step.

    ASMR: the tingling, calming response some people get from soft, close, repetitive sounds is real as a reported experience, but the research is early and mostly self-reported. Plenty of people feel nothing, and some find the sounds annoying. Both shelves are offered the same way: try them, keep what helps you, ignore what doesn't — no hype either way.

    The voices: the meditations and whispers are spoken by your own device's text-to-speech, so quality varies a lot by phone and browser. The scripts are printed in full so the words never depend on the voice.
