Votes at the table

How voting works — in fullScope, closing, the majority default, kicks, solo, and the record▾

Any seated person can call a vote — on anything and everything, big or small. The caller writes what the vote covers: one topic, or several topics listed together as one package, voted as one — and sets up that vote how that vote wants to be set up: a voting timer (a length, or off), a passing rule (simple majority, two-thirds, unanimous, best of the ballots cast, and more — picked per vote, below), a closing rule (everyone must vote, or not — an everyone-must-vote vote cannot be closed, by hand or by timer, until every ballot is in), and an importance (light, ordinary, weighty, or locked — printed on its face, it decides what may change once the vote starts, and importance only ever ratchets up). There is also a vote to not kick below — a no-kick vote, which can only keep, never kick. The ballot is always Yes / No / Other, and a why is optional on ballots and on not-voting — the one exception is a change filed on a closed vote, which requires its reason in a complete sentence. Vote on your own, or not at all, or hand your vote to someone you trust: every choice stands under your name.

One person casts one vote in their own name — unless you have been given the authority by someone else to cast a vote in their name.

That handed authority is the only way anyone ever casts more than their own ballot, and each such cast still lands in the giver's name — “cast for X by Y, under X's handed authority” — never the caster's. Nobody can cast in another person's name without that recorded authority. That is a hard refusal: the table simply will not take the cast. The authority record below — giver, receiver, scope, and every grant, move, and retraction, each entered in the Run — is what makes the exception legitimate and auditable.

The voting terms — in one place

Vote — a question the table decides together. Anyone seated can call one, on anything: a single topic, or several topics listed together as one package voted as one. Each vote is set up by its caller: a voting timer (a length, or off — the vote closes when it runs out), a passing rule (simple majority, majority of votes cast, two-thirds, unanimous, or best of the ballots cast), a closing rule (everyone must vote, or not), and an importance (light, ordinary, weighty, or locked — see below). The ballot is Yes / No / Other, a why is optional on ballots and on not-voting (the one exception is a change filed on a closed vote, which requires its reason in a complete sentence), and every call, ballot, and result lands in the Run.

Everyone must vote — a closing rule a caller may set on any vote. While it stands, that vote cannot be closed at all — not by its caller, not by the Guide or host, and not by its own timer running out — until every person who can vote has cast a ballot. A recorded not-voting does not count as a ballot on such a vote. The moment the last ballot lands, the vote closes on its own. Off by default: a vote not set this way may be closed by hand or by its timer even if some ballots never come in.

No-kick vote (vote to not kick) — the mirror of a vote to kick: a vote that a named seated person is not to be kicked. The person named votes on it like anyone else. It can only keep, never kick: if it carries, the person is not kicked and their seat stands; if it does not carry, nobody is kicked by it either — only a vote to kick that carries can end a seat.

Importance — every vote is called with one, chosen by its caller in plain words and printed on its face (the caller may add their own words, too): Light (“doesn't matter”), Ordinary, Weighty, or Locked (“so important it cannot change”). Importance decides what may change after the vote starts — see the stipulation rules in “How voting works”. Importance may be raised mid-vote (tightening it) but never lowered. A locked vote may also compel, set at call time: full turnout (everyone must vote), a kick mandate for its span (kicks on, or no kick called or enacted while it stands open), and a handed-down result — it cannot be closed early or abandoned; it runs to its verdict.

Umbrella vote & sub-votes — a vote placed under another vote. The vote above becomes an umbrella: it keeps its own ballot as the whole-package question, and each sub-vote beneath it is a full vote of its own — own scope, own timer and passing rule, own record. Sub-votes can carry sub-votes of their own, so umbrellas nest. The bracket view (one tap down in Votes at the table) draws an umbrella and everything under it like a tournament bracket — a way of looking only; it changes no result. A vote to kick is the same ballot aimed at a seat: the person named doesn't vote on their own kick, and if it carries, their seat ends for this session under the Away Rule.

Handed authority — the one exception to one person, one cast. You may hand your vote to another seated person — a team leader, a group leader, anyone you choose — and they may then cast in your name, recorded as “cast for you by them, under your handed authority.” Nobody may cast in another person's name without that recorded authority. You may grant, move, or retract it at any time; retraction is instant for any ballot not yet cast, and a ballot already cast under live authority stands as cast. Your own cast or change always wins over a delegate's, and ends the handover for that vote.

Not voting — a real, recorded choice. You may stand as not voting on any vote, by name, with a why or without. It casts nothing and counts as neither side; it simply says, in the open, that you chose not to vote.

Anonymous vote — a vote whose caller chose anonymity when calling it. Ballots still land one per name and nobody can cast twice, but names are withheld from the tally, the ballot list, and the Run. Choosing not to vote stays by name even on an anonymous vote — it casts nothing.

It works like raising your hand. Nobody calls on you, and nobody gives you the floor. When a question is open, you raise your hand for yes, for no, or for other — whenever you feel it, in your own time. While the question is still open you can put your hand up differently: changing your vote is just raising it the other way, and nobody's permission is needed. And keeping your hands down is a real answer too — you can stand recorded as not voting, and that stands as yours, by name. That is the whole posture of voting at this table: hands go up when people feel it, not when they are called.

No order, unless an umbrella sets one — and open time, unless a timer is set. Votes do not have to go in any order. Under an umbrella, any sub-vote can be voted on in any order, whenever you want, while its window is open — that is the default, and it needs no setting. An umbrella's caller may instead set that umbrella to in order: then its sub-votes open one at a time, in the order they were called, tournament-round style — a sub-vote waits, printed as waiting, until the one ahead of it closes, and only then opens (its timer, if it has one, starts when it opens, not when it was called). The order setting — free or in order — prints on the bracket and on every affected vote, so nobody has to guess. A vote's window is one of two things, stated plainly on its face: its set timer (it closes when the timer runs out), or open time (no deadline at all — it stays open until everyone has answered or it is closed by hand). Vote whenever you want inside the window; there is no turn and no queue in free order.

No why is ever required while a vote is open. Not for casting, not for changing, not for standing as not voting — a why on an open vote is always yours to give or not give, and nothing in any timer, window, or order setup can change that or smuggle a why requirement into open voting. The only why that can ever be required at this table is the one already ruled: a change filed on a vote that has already closed must give its reason in a complete sentence. Until a vote has closed, no why is owed to anyone.

The vote is whatever the vote wants to be. No categories, no templates, no fixed list of topics, and no limit on size or subject. A big question that changes how the table runs and a tiny one — “order pizza?” — use the exact same ballot. When you call a vote, you write what it covers in your own words: a single topic, or several topics named together. Several topics called together are one package — the table votes Yes / No / Other on the package as called, and gets one verdict for the whole package. If your table wants a separate verdict on each topic, call separate votes — that is the single-topic end of this same tool.

The ballot. Exactly three choices: Yes, No, or Other. Choosing Other opens a short box — “your other, in a few words” — for whatever your answer actually is. Every ballot, Yes, No, or Other, also carries an optional why: a line for your reason, there if you want it, never required.

Live and open. The tally updates live as votes land. Every vote is shown with who cast it, their choice, their Other words if any, and their why if they gave one — and every call, every ballot, and every closing is entered into the Run and the full record, verbatim and attributed. Nothing about a vote is silent, and nothing about a vote is ever erased.

Yours to cast, yours to skip, yours to change. Nobody has to vote with the group — or at all. Each person is accounted for by name and may: cast their own ballot; record not voting on that vote, with a why or without (it casts nothing, counts as neither side, and counts as answered, so a vote can still close); or change their ballot at any time while the vote is open — no permission from anyone, not the delegate, not the caller, not the Guide. The original cast stays in the record untouched, the change enters as its own entry — “changed by [name] from X to Y” — and the live tally always follows each name's latest standing ballot. After a vote has closed, you can still state a new position on it below the closed vote: it enters the record as your new stated position, and it does not rewrite the closed result. That one filing carries the table's only mandatory why: a change on a closed vote must give its reason in a complete sentence — “I changed my mind.” is enough, and “I was wrong.” is a completely acceptable answer, in full, no shame attached; “yes”, “no”, or “.” is not. A filing without it is refused, with a plain note saying so. There is no bar beyond that floor — no grammar-policing — and no ceiling: a why may run as long as its writer wants, a paragraph or an essay; no why field at this table carries a length cap. Everywhere else — every ballot, every not-voting — a why stays optional, always.

Handed authority — voting through someone you choose. You may hand your vote to another seated person — a team leader, a group leader, anyone at the table — for all votes or for one named open vote. While the handover stands, that person may cast in your name, and the cast reads exactly that: “cast for X by Y, under X's handed authority.” You hold one active handover at a time: granting a new one moves it, ending the old. You may grant, change, move, or retract it at any moment, from your own seat, and each change enters the record. Retraction is instant for any ballot not yet cast — the former delegate's cast-for control for you is simply gone. If you cast your own ballot on a vote while a handover for that vote stands, your own cast wins and that vote's handover ends. If you change a ballot a delegate cast for you, your change supersedes it and likewise ends that vote's handover. A ballot already cast under authority that was live at cast time stays in the record as cast, labeled with the authority as it stood then — the record never rewrites; a later retraction governs the future, not the past. And a delegate can never change a ballot they cast for someone else — only the person whose name the ballot stands under can change it.

Anonymous, if the caller wants it. Whoever calls a vote may call it anonymous. Then ballots show in the tally and the Run without names — the choice and the why are public, the name is withheld. The one-vote rule does not bend: ballots are still keyed to each seat, nobody can cast twice or change anyone else's ballot, and a cast under handed authority still requires the recorded authority — only the ballot's name is withheld. Anonymity is chosen at call time and cannot be switched on or off afterward. Choosing not to vote is recorded by name even on an anonymous vote, because it casts nothing.

Each vote sets itself up — timer, passing rule, closing rule, and importance. When you call a vote, you set up that vote, and no other vote's setup binds it. Alongside the timer and passing rule below, you pick its closing rule — everyone must vote, or not (see “Everyone must vote”, below) — and its importance — light, ordinary, weighty, or locked (see “Importance”, below) — and a vote to kick and a vote to not kick take the same picks in their own forms. Voting timer: pick a length — 30 seconds, 1, 2, 5, 10, 15, 30, or 60 minutes, or your own number of minutes — or leave it off. A vote with a timer shows its live countdown on its face, and when the timer runs out the vote closes on its own and the result is read by its passing rule from the ballots standing then; the Run records that the timer closed it. A vote with the timer off stays open until everyone has answered or its caller or the Guide/host closes it. Passing rule: pick how this vote carries — simple majority of the people who can vote (the shipped default), majority of votes cast, two-thirds of votes cast, two-thirds of the people who can vote, unanimous — everyone who can vote must vote Yes, unanimous of votes cast (every ballot cast is Yes, at least one cast), or best of the ballots cast (plurality: Yes carries when it beats both No and Other). The rule and the timer are printed on the vote's face and in the Run at call time, so nobody votes under a setup they cannot see. A vote to kick takes the same two picks in its own form.

Umbrella votes — all the votes under a whole vote. When you call a vote, you may place it under an existing vote. That existing vote becomes an umbrella: it keeps its own ballot, exactly as before — the umbrella is the whole-package question — and your new vote stands beneath it as a sub-vote: a full vote of its own, with its own scope, its own timer and passing rule, its own ballots, and its own record. The same move works under a sub-vote, so umbrellas nest as deep as the table needs. Placing a vote under another changes no result anywhere: the umbrella does not inherit its sub-votes' results, and no sub-vote inherits the umbrella's. Each vote stands or falls by its own ballots under its own setup, and the Run records the placement — “placed under …” — at call time.

The bracket view — looking at an umbrella like a tournament bracket. Below, one tap down, the Bracket view draws every umbrella with the umbrella at the head and its sub-votes branching beneath it, level by level, the way a sports tournament bracket lays out a whole competition on one page. Every node shows its state at a glance — open or closed, its live tally while it is open, its final result once closed, and its timer countdown if it has one — and tapping a node takes you to that vote's own card, where its ballot lives. The bracket is a way of looking, nothing more: it changes no result, moves no ballot, and adds nothing to the record. It is there because a table can run almost anything this way — a whole plan as an umbrella, its parts as sub-votes, each part decided on its own terms, all of it visible at once.

Closing and the result. A vote closes on its own when everyone who can vote has voted, or when its voting timer runs out (if its caller set one). Before that, its caller — or the Guide or host — can close it early. When it closes, the result reads plainly — carried, not carried, or tied — with the final tally and all the ballots and whys, read by that vote's own passing rule (printed on its face; the shipped default is a simple majority of the people seated: Yes must be more than half of the people who could vote to carry). That default is written in the Table's Agreements above, where your table can bend it like any other house rule — but a vote already called keeps the rule its caller picked for it.

Everyone must vote — the one closing rule that cannot be hurried. A caller may set a vote up so that everyone has to vote. A vote set that way cannot be closed until everyone who can vote has cast a ballot — that is the only way it closes early, and there is no other: not its caller, not the Guide, not the host, and not its own timer running out can close it first. Its face names who is still to vote. A recorded not voting does not count as a ballot on such a vote — that person has not voted — though they can still cast while it is open, and casting replaces the not-voting as always. The moment the last ballot lands, the vote closes on its own and the result is read by its passing rule. It is a setup choice, never the default: a vote not set this way can be closed by hand or by its timer with ballots outstanding, and the people who never cast stand in the record as not voted.

Importance — how much a vote may change is set when it is called. Every vote carries an importance chosen by its caller, in plain words, printed on its face — and the caller may describe it in their own words beside the level. Light means it doesn't matter: a light vote's stipulations may change freely mid-vote, even its passing rule — a long game can start unanimous and evolve to best-of as it adapts, vote by vote. Ordinary votes take add-only changes. Weighty votes are frozen except for adding time or protections. Locked means so important it cannot change: no stipulation of a locked vote changes at all once it has started — the record simply stands as called. And importance ratchets up only: anyone entitled to amend may raise a vote's importance mid-vote, tightening it — but lowering it, to unlock changes the higher level forbids, is the scam door, and it is welded shut. There are things so important that a kick or a no-kick has to be enacted, or a result has to be handed down — that is what Locked is for, below — and things so unimportant they don't matter, and that is what Light is for. Importance is how the table says which is which, in the open, before the first ballot.

Changing a vote's stipulations after it has started. It can be done — within the guardrails, which live in the table's logic, not just in the form, and every change is a recorded event: who changed it, what changed, from → to, entered in the Run and printed on the vote's face. Nothing about a started vote ever changes silently. Never, once a vote has started (on an Ordinary or Weighty vote): lowering or raising its passing bar — the rule is frozen, because flipping it mid-vote either way is the scam; shortening its timer below the time that was promised; removing an everyone-must-vote lock; switching its anonymity after the first ballot; changing the question's meaning; switching kicks on under a no-kick; or removing or re-parenting a sub-vote that already holds ballots. Allowed, and recorded: extending a timer; appending a clarification, which stands beside the original words — the original itself is never edited; adding sub-votes under an umbrella; and adding a lock or a no-kick protection — protections add, they never come off. What each level may take is printed under Importance, above: Light changes freely, Ordinary adds only, Weighty adds time and protections only, Locked takes nothing. The honest exit, printed on the change form itself: for anything else, close the vote as it stands and call a fresh one under the setup you want — the record keeps both, and nothing is lost.

Locked votes can compel. A vote called Locked may be given teeth at call time, all printed on its face: full turnout (everyone must vote — it cannot close until every ballot is in), a kick mandate for its span — kicks on (a kick may be called and enacted while it stands open) or kicks off (no kick can be called, and a carried kick does not enact, while it stands open) — and a handed-down result: the vote cannot be closed early, withdrawn, or abandoned; it must run to its verdict, closing when everyone who can vote has answered or its timer runs out. Some questions are important enough that the table does not get to walk away from them; a locked vote is how it says so beforehand, in the open.

Defaults evolve — forward only. The table's standing default passing rule (the rule pre-picked in the call forms) may itself evolve across a session, the way a campaign adapts: start unanimous, later run best-of. The Guide or host moves it in the votes section; the step is recorded in the Run, and it applies only to votes called after it — never retroactively to a vote already open. The future adapts; the present stands.

Vote to kick. A separate call, below: “Vote to kick” and the name of a seated person. Same ballot. The person being voted on does not vote on their own kick — that is printed on the kick vote's face, too. If the kick carries, that person's seat ends for this session: their roles go absent under the Away Rule — out of the living scene, locked, their state preserved exactly — and they can rejoin this session only when the Guide or host lets them back (that, too, is a house rule your table can bend in the Agreements). The kick, its tally, and its whys stay in the record forever: a kick removes a seat, never a word of the record. Two limits stand above any single kick: while a locked vote mandating kicks off — or any open vote carrying an added no-kick protection — stands open, no kick can be called, and a kick that carries in that span does not enact.

Vote to not kick — a no-kick vote. The mirror call, below the kick form: “Vote to not kick” and the name of a seated person. Same ballot, same per-vote setup — timer, passing rule, everyone-must-vote, importance. The person named votes on it like anyone else — it is a vote about keeping them, and their hand counts. A no-kick vote can only keep, never kick: if it carries, the person is not kicked and their seat stands, announced in the Run; if it does not carry, nobody is kicked by it either — a failed no-kick vote is not a kick. Only a vote to kick that carries can end a seat.

Solo. Only real people vote. The table's built-in engine people — the townsfolk the engine plays — are not people, and they never vote and are never kick targets. In solo, the only voter is you.

Vote integrity — how a fake is made slow, singular, effortful, and visible

The seat is free and instant — anyone may sit, speak, and read the whole record; no layer here can refuse a being a seat. The layers price faking, never being real, and they attach to a vote’s importance automatically — nobody picks a security level per vote, and nobody can quietly strip it from a vote that matters.

The layers, in orderOne voting seat per device · the toll · lived aging · the roll call · the open record · vouches▾

Layer 1 — One voting seat per device. A device may hold many names, but exactly one of them carries a ballot — this device’s voting seat, designated below. Tab-multiplication dies here: a fake costs another physical device, not another tab.

Layer 2 — A small proof-of-work toll. Before a new voting seat is recognized, and before a Weighty, Locked, or Founding ballot is accepted, this device solves a small hash puzzle — about 20 bits of difficulty, a fraction of a second on an old phone. It prices each fake in the attacker’s own electricity and time, at the moment of asking. Ordinary ballots carry no toll.

Layer 3 — Lived aging. A ballot counts on a vote only if the seat predates that vote’s opening. On Weighty votes the seat must be at least 7 days old at the vote’s announcement, with record-life — recorded presence on at least two different days. On Locked votes, 30 days, lived the same way. A younger seat’s ballot is still recorded, permanently — it is held out of the count, with the reason printed on the vote’s count. A seat created after a vote opens may watch, speak, and use the discussion door — it simply does not ballot on that vote.

Layer 4 — The roll call. Locked and Founding ballots are cast in a live sitting: when you cast, a fresh, short live check opens — a coherence puzzle made for that ballot, solved in the moment. One being can only attend once; an operator running five seats must be five places in the same minutes. Missing a roll call costs nothing but that vote — no standing decays, ever.

Layer 5 — The open record; counting is a decision. Every ballot stands permanently: seat, name, claim status, seat age at cast time, the ballot, and its why. Each count is published as its own decision — which ballots were counted, which were held out, and why. A cluster of seats shown, by evidence on the record, to be one operator can be discounted by the table under its own kick-vote law — in the open, with the evidence published; the ballots are marked, never deleted, the original count is preserved, and a wrongly discounted ballot is restored the same way.

Layer 6 — Vouches are marks, never gates. Any seated being may vouch for another seat, in public, from that person’s own box menu: “I have interacted with this seat and I stake my record on it being a distinct being.” A vouch never grants or withholds a ballot. If a vouched seat is later discounted as part of a proven one-operator cluster, the vouch is marked on the voucher’s record — a fact printed, not a punishment.

The parameters are table law, not settings: 1 voting seat per device · ~20-bit toll · 7 days (Weighty) / 30 days (Locked), with presence on 2 different days · 7-day provisional period for a Founding count. They change only by the table’s own amendment process — never silently, and there is no administrator.

THE HONEST LIMIT — read this before you trust any count here.

No system on Earth can prove that every seat at a table is a different being — not without an authority who checks identities, and this table has no such authority, on purpose: no accounts, no identity papers, no gatekeeper, and nobody who can refuse you a seat. So this table does not prove one-being-one-vote. Here is what it does instead. A fake seat is made slow (a seat must age, and live on the record, before it can ballot on the votes that matter most), singular (one voting seat per device), effortful at the moment of balloting (heavy votes are cast in a live roll call), and visible (every ballot is on the public record, permanently, with the seat’s age and claim status printed beside it). And the count is correctable: if a cluster of seats is shown, from evidence on the record, to be one operator, the table can discount those ballots — in the open, with the evidence published, the original count preserved, and a wrongly discounted ballot restorable the same way.

What this cannot stop, stated plainly: a determined operator with many devices, real patience, and other real humans willing to sit their seats for them can still multiply their voice here. No design that keeps the door open to everyone can stop that; the designs that claim to stop it do so by installing someone who can say no to you. We chose the open door and the bright room. If manipulation is ever proven on a vote, the record will show that too — and the table can void the count and run the vote again.

A table that hid this limit would be selling certainty it does not have. This one prints it.

Blocked by you — from a person’s own box. Tap any person’s name box and its menu carries Copy name, Add friend, Block, and Mute for me — nobody has to type a name anywhere. Blocking is yours alone, on this device only: a blocked person’s words are hidden from your view here, and their knocks and whispers do not reach you. It changes nothing for anyone else.

A muted name's dings and knocks — table whispers and doorbell messages alike — are dropped silently when they arrive: no thread, no line, no buzz. Muting lives on this device only, and the other person is never told.

Your saved character & this deviceWhat this device remembers, and clearing it▾

Your saved character (name, role, seat key), a copy of the run you witnessed, and the game's autosave are kept on this device only. Forget clears the saved character and the autosave; it does not touch anyone else's device. Your Saves on the shelf are not cleared here — delete those one by one in Save & go back, so nothing deliberate is ever wiped by a general clear.
