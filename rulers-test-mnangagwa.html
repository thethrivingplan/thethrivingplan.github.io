The Ruler's Test — Emmerson Mnangagwa, President of Zimbabwe

Subject: Emmerson Dambudzo Mnangagwa — President of Zimbabwe since 24 November 2017. The seat graded is the presidency, and the reason is stated plainly: in Zimbabwe the president is the executive — head of state and government, commander-in-chief, appointer of the cabinet, the service chiefs, the judicial leadership (through the Judicial Service Commission process), and the heads of the commissions. Mnangagwa, born 15 September 1942, came to the seat through the military-assisted transition of November 2017 ("Operation Restore Legacy"), in which the defence forces seized key installations, Robert Mugabe resigned on 21 November after 37 years, and ZANU-PF installed its former vice-president — whom Mugabe had fired a fortnight earlier — in his place. He won the July 2018 election with 50.8% (upheld by the Constitutional Court) and the August 2023 election with 52.6% against Nelson Chamisa's 44% (Zimbabwe Electoral Commission figures), in a poll the SADC observer mission took the unprecedented step of declaring short of Zimbabwe's own constitution, its Electoral Act, and regional standards. He is graded on the full tenure since 2017, weighted to recent years, because the seat's defining acts span the whole period. The brief's framing of a "term-limit question and 2030-extension movement" is corrected at the threshold: the extension is no longer a movement. It is law. On 7 July 2026 Mnangagwa signed the Constitution of Zimbabwe Amendment (No. 3) Act — Act No. 6 of 2026 — which lengthens the terms of the president, parliament and local authorities from five years to seven, extends his own current term from 2028 to 2030, postpones the next general election to 2030, abolishes the direct election of the president (parliament will choose instead), adds ten presidential appointees to the Senate, moves custody of the voters' roll from the electoral commission to the Registrar-General, and abolishes the National Peace and Reconciliation Commission. Five challenges to the Act are before the Constitutional Court: judgment has been reserved in one (Kawa and Machingura), two more were set down for 18 November 2026 after a postponement over the composition of the bench, and no ruling on the substance had been reported by 12 November 2026. Two other figures in the brief are dead: Blessed "Bombshell" Geza, the war veteran whose 2025 campaign against Mnangagwa opened the succession fight in public, died a fugitive in South Africa on 6 February 2026; and Wicknell Chivayo, the tenderpreneur at the centre of the corruption file the vice-president laid before the Politburo, died in a helicopter crash on 30 September 2026, mourned publicly by the President and conspicuously not by his vice-president. Context that frames everything below: ZANU-PF has governed Zimbabwe since independence in 1980; Mnangagwa was Mugabe's state security minister during the Gukurahundi massacres of 1983–87 (an estimated 20,000 people, mostly Ndebele civilians, killed by the Fifth Brigade — the toll is the commonly cited estimate of the Catholic Commission for Justice and Peace tradition, and his personal role is contested: he denies an active part, and the denial is printed where the record raises it); and he himself survived a Rhodesian death sentence in 1965, commuted on account of his age. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open. The ethic this Test applies is printed here in full: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." The mandate rule applies throughout: governing on the platform the voters elected is not inherently a failure and is never graded as one — Mnangagwa's elected platforms were the "new dispensation" of 2017–18 and continuity-plus-Vision 2030 in 2023, and delivering on them is credited where it is due, above all on Test 5. What is graded is the holding: whether the law is one law, whether the referees are gripped, whether everyone is counted, and whether the word survives contact with his own interest. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as two independent axes; neither launders the other, and the crossings are named where they occur.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The written law is even on its face, and some of its even-handed products are real. The Death Penalty Abolition Act, signed 31 December 2024, prohibits every court from imposing a death sentence and required the re-sentencing of the roughly 59 people on death row — a law with no partisan incidence at all, which Amnesty International called a "historic moment" while regretting the clause that would let the penalty return during a declared state of emergency (Death Penalty Abolition Act, 2024; Amnesty International, December 2024).
  • The courts have, at identifiable moments, ruled against the state's handling of its critics. When journalist Blessed Mhlanga was refused bail twice in 2025 — a magistrate reasoning that his release would itself cause unrest — High Court judge Justice Gibson Mandaza granted bail on 6 May 2025, holding that the lower courts had erred (Harare court reporting, May 2025). The five Constitutional Court challenges to Amendment No. 3 are being processed through the court's own procedures, with hearings held and judgment reserved rather than the petitions extinguished at the door (Afriprobe explainer, November 2026).
  • The anti-corruption machinery does sometimes move against figures inside the system: a former foreign minister, Walter Mzembi, was arrested on his return to the country in June 2025 and tried — and then acquitted in May 2026 after more than ten months in detention, the charges having collapsed (Southern Africa Litigation Centre, May 2026). The acquittal is recorded here as a court outcome; the ten months that preceded it are on the against shelf, because a process that detains first and acquits later has already applied its punishment unevenly.

Evidence against:

  • The eligibility law has been re-cut around one biography — the Djibouti pattern, in a constitution that was specifically written to prevent it. Section 328(7) of the 2013 Constitution provides that an amendment to a term-limit provision extending how long a person may hold office "does not apply to anyone who held that office before the amendment" — the no-benefit rule, drafted with Mugabe in living memory. Amendment No. 3 lengthens the presidential term from five years to seven and applies the lengthening to the sitting president, carrying Mnangagwa from 2028 to 2030 without a referendum; five sets of challengers, including six liberation war veterans, argue this is exactly what section 328(7) and the referendum requirement in section 328(6) forbid (Constitution of Zimbabwe, section 328; Afriprobe, November 2026; Madhuku-led petitions CCZ 271/26, 273/26, 277/26). ZANU-PF's stated position is that the changes alter the electoral cycle rather than the term limit and so need no referendum — the dispute is printed as a dispute; what is not disputed is that the one man who benefits from the reading is the man who signed it.
  • The law's incidence tracks alignment in the ordinary run of cases, through detention rather than verdict. Opposition leader Job Sikhala spent 595 days in pre-trial detention (June 2022 – January 2024) before receiving a suspended sentence shorter than the time already served. Journalist Blessed Mhlanga spent 72–73 days in pre-trial detention in 2025 for broadcasting interviews with a ruling-party veteran, twice denied bail, released only after international pressure, with his trial still dragging through 2026 (Human Rights Watch, March 2025; Mhlanga's Geneva Summit address, February 2026). The roughly 98 people arrested at the 31 March 2025 stay-away protests waited six weeks for bail (AFP, 14 May 2025). An activist arrested in October 2025 over protest fliers remained in prison while his co-accused were bailed (Southern Africa Litigation Centre, 2026). Bail, in the documented pattern, is a right for the unaligned and a negotiation for the critic.
  • The security forces stand outside the law that applies to protesters. Soldiers and police killed six people and injured 35 on 1 August 2018 (the Motlanthe Commission's own findings); local groups reported 17 killed and at least 17 women raped by uniformed soldiers in the January 2019 crackdown (Human Rights Watch, citing local groups). Eight years on, no member of the security forces has been arrested or prosecuted for either episode, and the Motlanthe Commission's recommendations — accountability and compensation — remain unimplemented (Human Rights Watch, August 2021 and August 2026). One law for the street; another for the uniform.
  • Proximity to the seat is a documented shelter. In September 2025 Vice-President Constantino Chiwenga laid a corruption dossier before the ruling party's presidium naming the businessmen around the President — Kudakwashe Tagwirei, Wicknell Chivayo, Pedzisayi Sakupwanya, Paul Tungwarara — as "zvigananda" looting public resources on an industrial scale and funding the 2030 extension campaign (NewsHawks investigative reporting, 26 September 2025). These are one faction's ALLEGATIONs against another, and are labeled as such. What is a matter of record is the sequel: no arrests followed; the party instead confirmed Tagwirei's co-option into its Central Committee (Politburo endorsement, 30 July 2025); and on 10 December 2025 the Zimbabwe Anti-Corruption Commission closed its probe into the alleged Chivayo–electoral commission tender — the allegation that a no-tender contract for 2023 election materials had been inflated by some 235% — with its chairperson stating that investigators found no documentation linking Chivayo to the commission and that the accusers had supplied no proof (ZACC chairperson Michael Reza, December 2025). Chivayo's position — that the allegations were baseless — is recorded; so is the shape of the outcome: the file on the President's friend closed for want of documents, while the files on the President's critics proceed on the strength of their words alone.
  • The family is inside the state. In September 2023 Mnangagwa appointed his son, David Kudakwashe Mnangagwa, deputy minister of finance, and his nephew, Tongai Mnangagwa, deputy minister of tourism — appointments the opposition CCC called the building of "an illegitimate and dangerous dynasty" and analysts called nepotism; his defenders reply, on the record, that the son holds an actuarial-science degree from Drake University and a law degree from the University of Zimbabwe (CNN, September 2023; The Africa Report, September 2023). The qualifications are printed; so is the fact that no competitive process stood between the President's signature and his son's office.
  • A second criminal law hangs over speech itself. The July 2023 amendment to the Criminal Law Code — the "Patriotic Act" — introduced heavy penalties for citizens at home and abroad convicted of "willfully injuring the sovereignty and national interest of Zimbabwe," a standard whose incidence is, by construction, the critic (US Department of State, 2024 Country Report).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the pattern repeats at every level the test looks at: the constitution's own anti-self-dealing clause read around by the one man it restrains; detention used as the punishment for critics while verdicts wait; killings by the state's forces unprosecuted for eight years while protesters are prosecuted for gathering; the corruption file on the palace circle closed by the state's own commission while the vice-president's dossier goes unanswered; and the ruler's son and nephew seated by his signature. The for-shelf items are genuine — an abolition that applies to everyone, judges who sometimes rule against the state, petitions heard — and they describe a legal system that still functions at its edges, not one law at its centre.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the presidential household and its commercial circle: David Kudakwashe Mnangagwa and Tongai Mnangagwa (seated by appointment); Kudakwashe Tagwirei (elevated into the party's Central Committee while named in the vice-president's dossier); Wicknell Chivayo (his file closed in his lifetime, December 2025); and every security-force member shielded by eight years of non-prosecution. Hurt — Job Sikhala (595 days pre-trial); Blessed Mhlanga (73 days, trial pending); Walter Mzembi (ten months detained, then acquitted); the families of the six killed on 1 August 2018 and the 17 reported killed in January 2019, still without a single prosecution or the compensation the state's own commission recommended; and the roughly 98 people of 31 March 2025, for whom bail took six weeks.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • One referee has been allowed to work, and the results are the strongest numbers of the tenure. The Reserve Bank under Governor John Mushayavanhu ran the tight-money regime behind the ZiG currency from April 2024: annual ZiG inflation fell from roughly 85% in April 2025 to 4.1% in January 2026 — the first single-digit local-currency inflation since 1997 — and to 2.9% by August 2026, with the official rate steady around ZiG 26–27 to the dollar (World Bank, Zimbabwe Growth & Jobs report, 2026; IMF staff assessment, September 2026). A central bank permitted to hold an unpopular line against a spending government is a referee ungripped in its lane, and the lane mattered.
  • The Constitutional Court has not simply waved Amendment No. 3 through. It heard the first challenge and reserved judgment; it postponed two further applications after concerns were raised about the composition of the bench — including the argument, advanced by Professor Lovemore Madhuku, that judges appointed under provisions the amendment itself created should not sit on the challenge to it (Afriprobe, November 2026). The process is slow and its outcome unknown; that it is running at all, in public, is a fact of this shelf.
  • Parliament's forms operated: the amendment was debated and voted, the opposition's 42 votes against in the National Assembly and four in the Senate are on the public record, and the two-thirds thresholds the constitution sets were the thresholds used (National Assembly, 18 June 2026; Senate, 24 June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Amendment No. 3 is, among other things, a referee-transfer act. It moves custody of the voters' roll from the Zimbabwe Electoral Commission — the independent body charged with elections — to the Registrar-General, a government department inside the executive's own house (Act No. 6 of 2026). The referee who kept the roll no longer keeps it; the player's ministry does.
  • The same Act enlarges the player's hand in both remaining arenas: ten additional senators appointed by the president (Senate 80 → 90), and — on the Mahlupeko petition's argument, contested and before the court — provisions increasing the size of the Constitutional Court bench that were not in the Bill as gazetted (Afriprobe, November 2026). A litigant in the pending challenges thus faces a bench whose composition the challenged Act may itself have altered; the court's own postponement of the 11 November hearings over bench composition records that the problem is visible from inside the building.
  • The electoral referee's 2023 performance is the baseline the transfer builds on. Voting was extended into a second day after ballot papers arrived up to twelve hours late, overwhelmingly in urban opposition strongholds; turnout fell from 85% (2018) to 68.9%; ZEC has never published polling-station-level results; and on election night police raided the data centres of the two citizen-observer groups running a parallel vote tabulation and arrested their staff (Carter Center preliminary statement, 31 August 2023; SADC Election Observation Mission preliminary statement, August 2023). The SADC mission — Zimbabwe's own regional body, chaired by Zambia's Nevers Mumba — concluded the process fell short of the constitution, the Electoral Act and SADC's own principles; the AU, EU, Commonwealth and Carter Center missions each recorded serious concerns of their own.
  • The elected opposition was then dismantled through the referees' forms. After the 2023 election, Sengezo Tshabangu — a man the CCC says was a self-appointed impostor with no office in the party — wrote to the Speaker claiming to be the CCC's interim secretary-general and recalling its MPs and councillors; the Speaker accepted the letters, the courts declined to stop the recalls, and dozens of elected opposition legislators lost their seats, restoring ZANU-PF's two-thirds majority — the majority that later passed Amendment No. 3 (Brill Africa Yearbook 2025; ARIES strategic intelligence report, May 2026). Whether Tshabangu acted at the state's direction is an ALLEGATION the record assembled here does not establish, and it is labeled as such; what is established is that every institution the letters passed through — Speaker, courts, electoral commission — processed them in the direction of the ruling party's interest, and none processed them back.
  • Civil society's registrar is the executive. The Private Voluntary Organisations Amendment Act, signed 11 April 2025, empowers the government to deregister organisations deemed "politically partisan," seize their assets, and suspend their leadership — the minister may act without first obtaining a court order — with penalties reported up to 35 years' imprisonment for offences including misrepresentation (PVO Amendment Act No. 1 of 2025; Human Rights Watch, 17 April 2025; International IDEA democracy tracker, April 2025). The government states the Act answers FATF concerns about terrorism financing through charities; the EU suspended its 2025 governance funding in response, citing the civic-space commitments Zimbabwe had given in its debt-resolution dialogue (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025).
  • The media referee works under arrest. Reporters Without Borders' ranking aside, the operating facts are case-level: a senior journalist jailed for 73 days for an interview (Test 1); a Czech tourist arrested in August 2024 for recording a video mentioning water and power cuts (US Department of State, 2024 Country Report); state ownership of the dominant broadcaster and the main daily papers, with campaign coverage the SADC mission itself found biased toward the ruling party (SADC EOM, 2023).

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the tenure's trajectory is a steady transfer of the referees into the player's house, capped by an Act that takes the voters' roll off the electoral commission, adds the president's own appointees to the Senate, and is credibly alleged — before the court itself — to have resized the very bench that must judge it. The for-shelf is real: the central bank was left alone to do unpopular, competent work, and the Constitutional Court is hearing the challenges in the open. Those are lanes left ungripped. The electoral and civic referees — the ones that decide who may challenge the seat — have been gripped, staffed, or rewritten.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — the presidency and ZANU-PF's parliamentary operation: a two-thirds majority recovered through recalls, a voters' roll in a ministry's custody, ten more appointed senators, and a registrar empowered to dissolve inconvenient organisations. Hurt — the recalled CCC legislators and the voters who elected them in 2023; citizen observer groups raided on election night; NGOs and community organisations now registrable, suspendable and seizable at ministerial discretion; journalists; and the Constitutional Court's own litigants, waiting on a judgment about the rules of every future election.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down? This test also grades whether, when another option became available, he at least considered it openly: considering an option and declining it with stated reasons is not against-evidence; refusing to look at it is.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He has, at least once, taken a bill back. The first PVO Amendment Bill was gazetted in November 2021 and passed by parliament; after sustained domestic and international objection — including from four UN Special Rapporteurs and public consultations marred by violence — Mnangagwa refused to sign it in September 2023 and returned it to parliament for reconsideration (FIDH/OMCT Observatory, June 2025). The refusal was an adjustment, made in the open, against his own government's legislative programme. That he signed a revised version in April 2025 is graded on Tests 1 and 2; the 2023 act of looking again belongs to this shelf.
  • After the August 2018 shootings he appointed a commission of inquiry chaired by a foreign former president, Kgalema Motlanthe, and published its report — an open answer to a wobble, whatever the sequel (Test 1 records that its recommendations were never implemented; the appointment and publication themselves are facts of this shelf).
  • The drought of 2023–24 — the worst El Niño drought in some forty years — was answered with adjustment rather than denial: he declared a state of national disaster on 3 April 2024, published the need (an appeal of roughly US$2 billion, later raised to about US$3 billion), and ran a joint food-deficit programme with the World Food Programme (government statements, April 2024; WFP reporting, 2024). The partisan capture of the distribution is graded on Tests 1 and 4; the declaration and the appeal themselves were the state admitting the size of the blow and asking for help. The no-good-choice clause is applied to this file: in the lean season of 2024 the options were to move food through the only nationwide logistics that existed — state and party structures — at speed, or to build neutral channels while people went hungry; every door carried harm, the constraint is recorded rather than counted against him, and the choice of speed is graded as the best possible available in the moment. The clause does not cover the further, unforced choice to have the ruling party publicly "superintend" distribution — that was an option among options, and it is graded where it belongs, on Tests 1 and 4.
  • On Gukurahundi he opened a door his predecessor kept welded for thirty years: from 2019 he permitted public discussion of the massacres, and on 14 July 2024 he personally launched the Gukurahundi Community Outreach Programme in Bulawayo, with chiefs-led hearings and a government pledge that victims' cases requiring compensation would be compensated, case by case (launch reporting, July 2024; National Council of Chiefs briefing, June 2025). The design's flaws are on the against shelf; that the subject may now be spoken aloud at all is a movement his predecessor never made.

Evidence against:

  • The sharpest regional pushback of the tenure was answered with attack, not adjustment. When the SADC observer mission's preliminary statement found the 2023 election short of constitutional and regional standards, the ruling party and state media turned on the mission's head personally — Nevers Mumba was accused of meddling beyond his mandate — and no electoral reform followed; ZEC's non-publication of polling-station results stands uncorrected to this day (SSRC Kujenga Amani analysis, February 2024; The Zimbabwean, September 2023). A referee's report was treated as an act of hostility.
  • Pushback from inside his own house was answered with silence and reshuffle. Chiwenga's September 2025 dossier demanded arrests of the named tenderpreneurs; the documented response was no arrests, the demotion of Chiwenga's ally Obert Mpofu from secretary-general, and the continuation of the 2030 programme the dossier said the tenderpreneurs were funding (NewsHawks, September 2025; Free Library reporting, September 2025). When retired generals met him in May 2026 to raise concerns about Amendment No. 3, his reported answer was "whoever wins, wins" (Zimbabwe Situation analysis, July 2026 — a reported remark, printed as such).
  • The war veterans' pushback was answered with the criminal law. Geza — a Central Committee member of the president's own party — demanded his resignation in January 2025; within weeks Geza was expelled (6 March 2025), his wife had been arrested, and he was a fugitive facing charges including undermining the president and incitement, later dying in exile (Brill Africa Yearbook 2025; Wikipedia, citing family confirmation, February 2026). Whatever the merits of Geza's own claims — his corruption accusations against named businessmen are ALLEGATIONs — the seat's answer to an insider's public challenge was prosecution, not engagement.
  • The pushback against Amendment No. 3 itself was policed rather than answered. In October 2025 a suspected arson attack gutted the seminar room of the Southern African Political Economy Series Trust hours before civil-society figures were due to speak against the amendment (the attack is documented; its authorship is not established, and no attribution is made here). In February 2026 police shut the inaugural meeting of the Constitutional Defenders Forum, citing gathering regulations the organisers say they had complied with. Human Rights Watch documented seven attacks on student leaders campaigning against the amendment, some abducted, tortured and handed to police (CIVICUS Lens, November 2026; IPS, 16 November 2026). Opposition figures Tendai Biti and Morgan Ncube were arrested in Mutare on 21 March 2026 under the public-order law while organising against the amendment; the charges were withdrawn on 19 October 2026 (CIVICUS/Constitutional Defenders Forum records).
  • On the largest wobble of all — his own succession — the other option was placed in front of him by name and never openly considered. A referendum on the term changes was proposed by his own vice-president, by the challengers' lawyers, and by the constitution's text as they read it; his camp's answer was that a referendum was unnecessary, and the option was declined without any public weighing of it by the president himself. Under this test's rule, declining with stated reasons is not against-evidence — but the reasons were stated by spokesmen about procedure, and the president's own recorded contribution to the question, after signing, was that the decision was "collective" (State House interview, July 2026). The look, if it happened, happened off the record.

Why: The grade lands on MIXED because the record genuinely splits. The adjustments are not nothing: a bill returned unsigned under pressure, a commission appointed and published, a disaster declared and aid appealed for, a forbidden subject opened. But every wobble that touched the seat itself — the region's verdict on his election, his deputy's corruption dossier, his party veterans' revolt, the country's argument over his extension — was answered with attack, prosecution, policing, or silence, and the one option his own constitution's guardians kept naming, a referendum, was never openly weighed by the man it would have constrained. He adjusts on policy. On power, he doubles down.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — drought-hit households reached by the 2024–25 food programme at the speed the state-party machine could move (the same machine's incidence is on Tests 1 and 4); Gukurahundi survivors given, for the first time, an official door to knock on; civil society, briefly, in September 2023. Hurt — Nevers Mumba and the SADC mission, answered with personal attack for a professional finding; Blessed Geza, dead in exile under charges; the student leaders attacked in 2026; Tendai Biti and Morgan Ncube, arrested and held to charges later withdrawn; and the Constitutional Defenders Forum, whose first meeting the police closed.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the opposition voter, the urban worker, Matabeleland, the protesters, and the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The disaster appeal was universal in its terms. Declaring the drought a national disaster in April 2024, Mnangagwa pledged that no citizen would starve, and the responsible minister stated on the record that political affiliation would not affect distribution (government statements, April 2024). The pledge is recorded here; its incidence is the against shelf.
  • The Gukurahundi outreach counts a community the state spent decades refusing to count at all: hearings across Matabeleland North and South from 26 June 2025, each victim heard by their chief with a fourteen-member panel, compensation pledged on the merits of each case (National Council of Chiefs, June 2025). For a region whose dead were officially unmentionable within living memory, being summoned to testify is itself a form of counting.
  • The abolition of the death penalty (Test 1) counted the condemned — some 59 people resentenced — and the macroeconomic stabilisation of 2025–26 counts everyone who buys food in ZiG: inflation at 4.1% in January 2026 against roughly 85% a year earlier is a benefit that does not check party cards (World Bank, 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The hungry were counted by affiliation, on a documented monitor's count. In April 2024 the ZANU-PF central committee resolved that the party would "superintend" all food distribution — its spokesman Christopher Mutsvangwa announcing that party structures would monitor allocation "to make sure that no one starves" (NewsDay, April 2024). The Zimbabwe Peace Project's monthly monitoring then recorded the predictable incidence: in January 2024, 45 people in Mount Darwin denied aid as perceived opposition supporters; through 2024, repeated documented cases of villagers told to join the ruling party or go without — including a serving minister and politburo member, Sithembiso Nyoni, recorded by ZPP in March 2024 warning at a distribution in Nkayi North that "non-ZANU PF members should join the ruling party or risk being left out in receiving food aid" (Zimbabwe Peace Project monthly reports, January–December 2024). The government denies partisan distribution outright — the social welfare ministry's permanent secretary called the allegations false, and the party's communications director called them an opposition fabrication (The Standard, April 2024). The dispute is printed as a dispute; the monitor's case-level record, the party's own superintendence resolution, and the minister's recorded words are the evidence weighed.
  • The scale of the need makes the incidence a counting failure of the first order, and the counts are kept separate as they were published: the World Food Programme's January 2024 hunger map put 4.1 million people food insecure; the government's own early estimate was 2.7 million requiring aid; by December 2024 humanitarian agencies were reporting over five million in urgent need (WFP, January 2024; government appeal, April 2024; Zimbabwe Peace Project year-end report, December 2024).
  • Urban voters were counted late, or not at all, in 2023: ballot papers up to twelve hours late in Harare and Bulawayo while rural areas voted on time, turnout down sixteen points, results never published at polling-station level (Carter Center, August 2023; SADC EOM, August 2023). The 2026 amendment then counts the urban voter out of the presidential question altogether: from 2030 no Zimbabwean will vote directly for a president.
  • Matabeleland is counted on the counter's terms. The Gukurahundi hearings are chaired by chiefs who report to the president — the process's own steering committee states that the National Council of Chiefs "reports to President Emmerson Mnangagwa. He is our principal" (Chief Fortune Charumbira, June 2025) — and they are held in private, one victim at a time, media barred, after an initial design of open testimony was abandoned (Lemkin Institute, 2025; Southern Eye, June 2025). ZAPU's leadership and the Ndebele king's spokesperson called for a boycott, describing the process as one in which the community that was targeted collectively is required to testify individually, in secret, before a panel answerable to a man who was state security minister when the Fifth Brigade deployed — Mnangagwa's denial of an active role is recorded, and no finding on his personal role is made here; the structural objection stands independent of it. Amendment No. 3 then abolished the National Peace and Reconciliation Commission, the constitutional body whose mandate was exactly this file, while the chiefs' process continues (Act No. 6 of 2026; CIVICUS Lens, November 2026).
  • The growth does not reach the counted. The World Bank's 2026 assessment records growth averaging almost 6% for 2021–25 and 7.5% in 2025 (the IMF's figure is 8.3% — both printed, not blended), alongside 45.2% of Zimbabweans below the international poverty line of US$3.00 a day in 2025, four in five jobs informal, and a median wage of US$130 a month (World Bank, Zimbabwe Growth & Jobs, 2026). A recovery whose median worker earns $130 is a recovery counted in aggregate and missed in person.
  • Dissenters are counted as a security category: more than 160 people arrested ahead of the August 2024 SADC summit — among them four activists taken off a domestic flight on 31 July 2024 and held incommunicado for eight hours, of whom teachers' union leader Robson Chere was tortured, on Amnesty's account, to the point of risking kidney failure — after which the ruling party's spokesman announced the detainees could be released because they no longer posed a threat to the summit (Amnesty International, 2024; US Department of State, 2024 Country Report). Release as a function of the guest list is the counting rule stated aloud.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the tenure's accounting repeatedly sorts people before it serves them: food by party card in a drought, on a monitor's case record the government disputes but has not answered case by case; the presidential vote itself withdrawn from every citizen from 2030; Matabeleland's dead counted in private, one at a time, before the accused era's own chain of command, while the constitutional reconciliation body is abolished; and a celebrated recovery under which nearly half the country remains below the poverty line. The for-shelf items — a universal pledge, a door opened in Matabeleland, an abolition, a stabilised currency — are real, and each is undercut by the incidence recorded against it.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — ruling-party households in the drought distribution, on the monitor's record; the roughly 59 resentenced prisoners; ZiG earners and savers, helped by single-digit inflation for the first time since 1997; Gukurahundi survivors willing to use the chiefs' process, given a hearing and a compensation pledge. Hurt — perceived opposition supporters denied food aid in documented cases across 2024; urban voters of 2023 and every future voter in a direct presidential election; Gukurahundi victims who will not testify in secret and the community organisations urging boycott; the more than 160 people arrested before the 2024 summit, Robson Chere named among the tortured; and the 45.2% below the poverty line in the middle of a reported boom.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 24 November 2017, with dates; the seat's word graded as one continuous account.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • The stabilisation word — substantially delivered. The economic programme he ran on in 2023 (the National Development Strategy and Vision 2030 framework) promised macroeconomic stability and growth. The outcome measures, from referees he does not control: growth averaging almost 6% across 2021–25 and 7.5% in 2025 on the World Bank's estimate (8.3% on the IMF's); ZiG inflation at 4.1% in January 2026, the first single-digit reading since 1997, and 2.9% by August 2026; an IMF Staff-Monitored Program agreed at staff level on 6 February 2026 and approved 16 April 2026 (World Bank, 2026; IMF, September 2026). Under the mandate rule this is credited in full: he said the economy would stabilise, and on the headline numbers it has — with the distribution of the gain graded on Test 4, where it belongs.
  • Re-engagement — partially delivered. "Zimbabwe is open for business" (Davos, January 2018) and the promise to normalise relations produced, on 4 March 2024, the termination of the entire US Zimbabwe sanctions programme by President Biden's executive order, replaced by targeted Global Magnitsky designations of eleven individuals — Mnangagwa himself among them, on Treasury's stated grounds of gold- and diamond-smuggling networks and security-force repression, which are ALLEGATIONs at the level of a designation rationale, denied by Harare, and printed as such (US Treasury press release, 4 March 2024). The programme-level door he promised to open did open; that his own name moved to the targeted list is the other half of the same document.
  • The death penalty — a word kept across a lifetime. Mnangagwa, himself sentenced to death by the Rhodesian regime in 1965 and spared for his age, had long stated his opposition to capital punishment; on 31 December 2024 he signed its abolition (Death Penalty Abolition Act, 2024). The emergency carve-out is recorded; the abolition is in force.
  • His defenders' point on the term limit, printed in full: Amendment No. 3 does not abolish the two-term limit — it lengthens each term to seven years, so the constitutional cap of two terms survives in form, and a president elected by parliament in 2030 could serve to 2037 and, re-elected, to 2044. His July 2026 defence was that the changes were "a collective decision," "a collective evolution of the political process," and that parliament had "done the correct thing" (State House interview, July 2026). This is his stated position, and Test 5 weighs it against his own earlier words, below.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The constitutionalist pledge — the signature broken word of the series. In September 2024, in China, Mnangagwa stated publicly that he would step down at the end of his second term in 2028 and urged those campaigning for an extension to stop. In February 2025, at State House, before editors, he said it without exits: "I'm very clear that I have two terms, and these terms are very definite, and I am so democratic. When they come to an end, I will step aside and my party will elect my successor" — and, asked whether he could be persuaded to stay: "I will persuade the persuaders not to persuade me, so that I remain a constitutionalist" (State House editors' briefing, February 2025, widely reported). The sequence that followed is documentary: his party's October 2024 conference had already resolved on 2030; his cabinet approved the amendment bill in February 2026; parliament passed it in June 2026; and on 7 July 2026 the man who would persuade the persuaders signed it, extending his own term to 2030. The word was not overtaken by events. It was reversed by its maker, in favour of its maker.
  • Free and fair elections — pledged in 2017, graded short by his own region in 2023. The inaugural address of 24 November 2017 promised elections that would be free and fair and a government serving all citizens "regardless of political affiliation" (inaugural address, 24 November 2017; CRS summary, 29 November 2017). The 2018 election was followed by soldiers shooting protesters in the capital. The 2023 election drew the SADC mission's unprecedented finding of constitutional shortfall (Test 2). The pledge's trajectory runs the wrong way across both polls he has conducted.
  • Anti-corruption — pledged as zero tolerance, answered with the palace file closed. The 2017 programme promised an end to corruption and a three-month amnesty for repatriated funds; nine years on, the documented position is that the vice-president's dossier naming the president's circle produced no arrests, the electoral-tender probe into the president's friend was closed for want of documentation, the US Treasury designates the president himself over smuggling networks (an ALLEGATION, denied), and the businessmen named in the dossier fund the ruling party openly — one of them carried into its Central Committee in July 2025 (Tests 1 and 3). Whatever the truth of any single allegation, the promised direction — toward accountability — is not the direction the record runs.
  • Serve all citizens regardless of affiliation — the food file. The inaugural pledge meets the Zimbabwe Peace Project's 2024 distribution record (Test 4). The pledge was universal; the incidence, on the monitor's count, was not.
  • The Motlanthe word. He appointed the commission, published its findings, and set up an inter-ministerial task force "to address issues arising from" them (January 2019). Eight years later its core recommendations — prosecute the responsible, compensate the victims — are unimplemented (Human Rights Watch, August 2026). A word performed in the appointment and abandoned in the execution.

Why: The grade lands on FAILS because the ledger divides exactly along the line this series keeps finding: the programme words that cost the seat nothing were kept, some of them impressively — the stabilisation numbers are real, the abolition is real, the sanctions programme's end is real. The words that limited the seat were broken, and the largest of them was broken in the largest way available: a public, repeated, quotable promise to leave in 2028, reversed by a constitutional amendment he signed applying to himself, defended afterwards as a collective decision by the one individual it individually benefits. His word over time is two words: the country's, kept; the constitution's limit on him, renegotiated.

Helped / Hurt: Helped — every Zimbabwean whose prices stopped spiralling in 2025–26; the resentenced prisoners of death row; the business community that gained a terminated sanctions programme and an IMF programme; and, by the extension itself, the president and the parliamentarians whose terms now run to 2030 without facing voters. Hurt — the voters of 2028, whose election no longer exists; the successor generation inside his own party, Chiwenga's camp named first among them, whose expected turn the amendment defers; the families still waiting on the Motlanthe recommendations; and every citizen who took the February 2025 briefing at face value.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: FAILS.

Emmerson Mnangagwa is the second liberation-era security chief in this series to inherit a state from the man he served, promising a new dispensation from the old one's methods. Nine years on, the record shows a tenure of genuine, measurable competence in the economic lane laid over an unbroken Mugabe-era grammar in the political one. Test 1 fails on incidence: a constitution re-cut around its own anti-self-dealing clause, detention doing the work of verdicts for critics from Job Sikhala (595 days) to Blessed Mhlanga (73 days), the August 2018 and January 2019 killings unprosecuted into their eighth year, the corruption file on the palace circle closed by the state's own commission while his son and nephew sit in cabinet by his signature. Test 2 fails on transfer: Amendment No. 3 takes the voters' roll from the electoral commission into a ministry, adds his appointees to the Senate, and stands credibly accused before the Constitutional Court of resizing that court's own bench — this after the elected opposition was recalled out of its seats through the referees' forms and civil society was placed under a registrar who can dissolve it. Test 3 is the file's one MIXED, and it is honestly mixed: he returned the first PVO bill unsigned, appointed and published the Motlanthe Commission, declared the drought and appealed for help — with the no-good-choice clause applied to the speed of the 2024 food response and expressly not to the party's capture of it — and opened the Gukurahundi subject his predecessor had sealed; but the SADC verdict on his election was answered with personal attack, his deputy's corruption dossier with silence and reshuffle, the veterans' revolt with charges, the anti-amendment campaign with policing, and the referendum option with a refusal to look at it in public. Test 4 fails on the counting: food sorted by party card in a drought on a monitor's case record, the direct presidential vote abolished for everyone from 2030, Matabeleland heard in secret before the president's own chiefs while the reconciliation commission is abolished, and 45.2% of the country below the poverty line inside a reported boom. Test 5 fails on the signature: the programme words were kept where they cost the seat nothing — stabilisation delivered (inflation in single digits for the first time since 1997), abolition delivered, the sanctions programme ended — and broken where they bound him, above all the February 2025 constitutionalist pledge, reversed seventeen months later by his own signature on his own extension.

The four crossings of the two axes all occur in this file, and are named: (1) Kept his word and it helped — the stabilisation programme and the death-penalty abolition: promised, delivered, and felt in ordinary lives. (2) Kept his word and it still hurt — the drought-relief machine: the pledge that no one would starve was pursued through the party-superintended distribution system he stood behind, and on the monitor's record the keeping itself sorted the hungry by affiliation. (3) Went against his word and it helped — September 2023: against his own government's legislative drive, he refused the first PVO bill and sent it back, and civil society breathed for nineteen months; the help is recorded in full, and so is its expiry when he signed the revised Act in April 2025. (4) Broke his word and it hurt — the 2028 pledge: broken by Amendment No. 3, hurting first the voters whose 2028 election was cancelled by the man who had promised to be gone before it. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: the Constitutional Court's judgment on Amendment No. 3, awaited as this is graded; whether the compensation pledged to Gukurahundi victims is ever paid; and whether the referees transferred in 2026 are ever transferred back.
