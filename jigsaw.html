Strategies — more than one honest way

There is no single correct way to work a jigsaw. These are the approaches experienced puzzlers actually use. Each one is strong somewhere and weak somewhere else; the trade-off is printed with it, because that is the part that lets you choose.

Edges first

Find every piece with a flat side, build the frame, then work inward. The frame gives every later piece a border to lean on and shrinks the search: an inside piece can only hide in the tray, never on an edge.

Strong Almost always worth it; corners are the four easiest pieces in the box. Weak A picture whose border is all one colour (a plain sky frame) can eat a long time for little progress.

Colour-sort

Sort the tray into piles by colour and pattern — all the red roof pieces together, all the deep water together — then finish one pile at a time. You stop hunting through the whole tray for every piece.

Strong Pictures with bold, distinct blocks of colour. Very fast. Weak Gradients — sky, water, shadow — where every piece is nearly the same colour and the piles blur together.

Section-hunting

Pick one striking thing in the picture — the lighthouse, the cabin door — and hunt only for its pieces. Build it as a floating island, then grow it until it joins the frame or another island.

Strong Motivating and quick early wins; a finished object is easy to place. Weak Islands are hard to join when the pieces between them are dull, and a tempting object can distract from a nearly-done frame.

The shape read (for the last pieces)

When few pieces remain, stop looking at the picture and read shapes: this hole needs a piece with a tab on the left and a blank on top. Match knob to hole. Shape never lies, even when colour does.

Strong Endgame, and anywhere colour is uniform. Weak Slow as an opening strategy — early on there are too many holes to read.

Most experienced puzzlers blend them: edges first, colour-sort the tray, hunt one good section, and read shapes at the end. The Coach in this game reads your actual board and suggests the blend that fits what is left.

About the jigsaw

The jigsaw puzzle begins, as far as the documented record shows, in the 1760s, when mapmakers mounted printed maps on wood and cut them into pieces along borders — “dissected maps,” sold as teaching tools for geography. Through the 1800s the cut pictures spread from maps to scenes and stories, still mostly wood, still often for teaching. Cardboard pieces and the familiar interlocking tab-and-blank cut arrived later, and by the early 1900s the jigsaw had become the table game most people know today: a picture, cut into interlocking pieces, rebuilt by hand.

This page keeps the documented table form as its standard: pieces start the right way up (no rotation), flat-sided pieces are the true edges of the picture, and every piece's tabs and blanks are cut to fit exactly one home. Rotation is offered only as a labelled variant. The three pictures are original scenes drawn for this page, and all the words are written for this page.

How placing works here: a piece snaps home only in its own square, the right way up. A wrong square is a miss, counted honestly in the status line and the record — never punished beyond the count.
