The Ruler's Test — Andrej Babiš, Prime Minister of Czechia

Subject: Andrej Babiš — Prime Minister of the Czech Republic since 9 December 2025 (appointed by President Petr Pavel after ANO won the 3–4 October 2025 election with 34.51% and 80 of 200 seats; his third cabinet, a coalition of ANO, the Freedom and Direct Democracy party (SPD) and Motorists for Themselves holding 108 seats, was sworn in on 15 December 2025 and won its confidence vote on 15 January 2026, 108–91). He previously served as prime minister from December 2017 to December 2021, and as finance minister (2014–2017) before that. Born in Bratislava, Slovakia, in 1954, he founded the Agrofert agrochemical conglomerate — one of Czechia's largest companies, with more than 200 subsidiaries — and the ANO movement, which he has led since founding it in 2011–2012. He is graded on his completed first premiership and on the first eleven months of his current term. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The process against him ran, for years, while he held or sought power. Police and the European Anti-Fraud Office (OLAF) investigated the Stork's Nest subsidy from 2015; parliament lifted his immunity in 2017 and again in 2018, and he was formally charged on 9 October 2017 — weeks before he first became prime minister — without the investigation being institutionally blocked (police and OLAF records; Chamber of Deputies votes, 2017–2018).
  • At first instance the courts twice ruled in his favor on the merits as they assessed them: Prague's Municipal Court acquitted him in the Stork's Nest case in January 2023 and again at retrial. The fraud charge itself therefore remains an ALLEGATION, not a finding — two trial courts declined to convict (Municipal Court verdicts, 2023 and at retrial).
  • In his current term, the state's own agricultural fund acted against his conglomerate: the State Agricultural Intervention Fund (SZIF) terminated eight Agrofert-group subsidy applications worth about 68 million crowns, holding that a conflict of interest existed in the interval between his appointment as prime minister (9 December 2025) and the transfer of Agrofert into the RSVP Trust (20 February 2026); the Agriculture Ministry's appeals committee upheld the terminations (SZIF statements, February–May 2026).
  • His co-defendant's case continues on the ordinary track: Jana Nagyová, the former associate who signed the Stork's Nest subsidy request, faces the retrial that his immunity blocks only for him (AP/Reuters reporting, March 2026). No prosecution of an opponent, and no shielding of an ally outside his own coalition's immunity votes (below), is documented in the record reviewed.

Evidence against:

  • The decisive fact is the vote of 5 March 2026. After the Prague High Court annulled his second acquittal and ordered a retrial, the Chamber of Deputies — his own coalition supplying the majority — voted 104–81 not to lift his parliamentary immunity, freezing the retrial until the parliament's term expires in 2029 (Reuters, 5 March 2026). Whether the prime minister stands trial is thus decided by the prime minister's own majority. In the same vote the coalition also refused to lift the immunity of its partner Tomio Okamura, the SPD leader and Chamber Speaker, who faces a hate-speech charge over his party's 2024 campaign posters; the charge is an ALLEGATION and Okamura denies wrongdoing. In his first term, by contrast, parliament twice voted the other way (above) — the standard applied to him changed when the majority became his.
  • The Stork's Nest record that the immunity vote keeps from a verdict, stated exactly: prosecutors allege that in 2008 the Stork's Nest farm was transferred out of Agrofert to his family members so that it qualified for a €2 million EU subsidy reserved for small and medium-sized enterprises, and later returned to Agrofert. He was charged in 2017; the Municipal Court acquitted him in January 2023; the Prague High Court annulled that acquittal in November 2023; the Municipal Court acquitted again at retrial; on 23 June 2025 the High Court annulled the second acquittal, holding that the evidence had not been properly assessed and binding the lower court to its legal assessment in any retrial (reported by AP as directing a guilty verdict). Babiš has pleaded not guilty throughout and calls the case politically motivated. The underlying fraud remains ALLEGATION — unproven in any final verdict — and it is the immunity vote, not a court, that now prevents the question from being answered.
  • The European Commission's final audit report of April 2021 (DG REGIO and DG EMPL) concluded that during his first premiership he remained the beneficial owner of Agrofert despite the 2017 transfer of his shares to the AB private trust funds — whose trustees included his wife and his lawyer, and whose shares were to return to him on leaving office — and that he was therefore in breach of the Czech Conflict of Interests Act and the EU Financial Regulation; EU payments to Agrofert made from 1 September 2017 were to be repaid, and the Commission had suspended the relevant grants from December 2018 (European Commission audit, April 2021). Babiš denied and continues to deny the finding, maintaining he complied with Czech law as written.
  • The pattern repeated on his return. His appointment in December 2025 was conditioned by President Pavel on a public resolution of the conflict; on 8 September 2026 the Commission stated that, after a thorough assessment, it considers him "in a potential situation of conflict of interest" in the management of cohesion and agricultural funds, that "the trust fund set up by Mr Babiš does not sufficiently mitigate this situation," and it instructed Czech authorities not to declare expenditure on operations selected since 9 December 2025 involving entities he controls or is linked to; reimbursements of about €3.18 million were interrupted (European Commission midday briefing, 8 September 2026; SZIF). His response, to the ČTK news agency: "I don't care, and I am not dealing with it… I do not own Agrofert, I will never own it again and I receive no benefit from it."
  • A related witness file, stated at its exact strength: his son, Andrej Babiš Jr. — originally also charged in the Stork's Nest case — told Seznam Zprávy in November 2018 that he had been taken involuntarily to Crimea and elsewhere in 2017 to keep him from testifying; the prime minister denied any kidnapping and said his son was mentally ill and had travelled voluntarily. Police closed the kidnapping investigation on 9 February 2021, finding no suspicion of a crime; Babiš Jr. maintained his account in a February 2021 interview. The kidnapping claim is an ALLEGATION that the police investigation did not sustain; both the allegation and the closure are on the record, and the son's fitness findings were the stated basis on which his own prosecution in the subsidy case did not proceed.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the central bank, public media, municipalities, and coalition partners keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The judiciary has ruled against him at every level without documented retaliation: two appellate annulments of his acquittals (November 2023, June 2025), delivered by courts whose judges he does not appoint and whose rulings he publicly criticizes but obeys; the Constitutional Court is untouched; no judge, prosecutor, or editor is documented as removed or punished for a decision against him in either term.
  • The central bank's autonomy is intact in practice. As prime minister in 2021 he publicly criticized the Czech National Bank's interest-rate rises as harmful; the bank raised rates anyway, its governor answered that the bank is independent, and no institutional move against the bank followed in either term (CNB and press record, 2021; no contrary documented instance since).
  • He no longer owns media. Agrofert bought the Mafra publishing house (Mladá fronta DNES, Lidové noviny, iDNES.cz, Rádio Impuls) in 2013, and he held it — through the trust funds — throughout his first premiership. After parliament tightened the media-ownership law in 2023 to bar MPs and ministers from owning such assets even through trusts (a law ANO opposed), Agrofert sold Mafra and the Londa radio group to Karel Pražák's Kaprain group, agreed September 2023 and completed 1 February 2024 (Reuters, September 2023; company records). The exit was legally compelled rather than volunteered, but it is complete: the sitting prime minister owns no news outlet.
  • Presidential pushback was absorbed, not overridden. When President Pavel refused on 7 January 2026 to appoint the Motorists' Filip Turek as environment minister — citing Turek's past statements praising or downplaying Nazi Germany and showing disrespect for the legal order — Babiš did not sue, did not force the appointment, and by February 2026 declared the matter closed; Pavel's refusal stood (President's letter to the prime minister, January 2026; statements after their February 2026 meeting). His October 2026 electoral losses (Test 3) likewise left opposition parties in control of the Senate and of Prague's city hall, unmolested.

Evidence against:

  • The public-media bill is a structural grip in preparation. The coalition's programme promised to abolish the Czech Television and Czech Radio licence fees and fund both broadcasters from the state budget instead — converting an independent revenue stream into an annual government appropriation. The bill's first reading was pushed through the Chamber in a fast-track procedure at 4:36 a.m. on 24 October 2026; ČT and ČRo staff struck on 3 November 2026; both broadcasters and media-freedom organizations warned on the record that budget funding exposes the broadcasters to exactly the political pressure the fee system was designed to prevent, and sought statutory safeguards (ČT24 and broadcaster statements, October–November 2026). The bill had not completed passage at grading; it is graded as a documented direction, not a completed capture.
  • The Turek affair also contains the counter-move: refused a ministry by the president, Turek was appointed on 12 January 2026 as government commissioner for climate policy and the Green Deal, seated at the Environment Ministry, announcing he would "indirectly" lead the ministry from that post, while the Motorists' leader covered the ministry in an acting capacity (government appointment, 12 January 2026; Turek's statements as reported by Romea.cz). The president's formal refusal was respected; its substance was partially routed around. Hospodářské noviny's commentary — that Babiš could not discipline his coalition partner because he needs the Motorists' and SPD's votes, including on his immunity — is an interested newspaper's characterization, recorded here as commentary, not as a finding.
  • The first-term media record stands. While he owned Mafra, its titles' coverage was a standing subject of documented criticism: the International Press Institute recorded that during his January 2023 presidential campaign, lifestyle magazines from his (by then trust-held) portfolio published series of articles presenting him as an ideal family man that "seemed like PR materials written on demand" while pretending to be editorial content; Freedom House reported that Mafra disproportionately benefited from state support during the COVID-19 pandemic (IPI, 2023; Freedom House, Nations in Transit). Babiš denied using his media for political advantage. The ownership is ended (above); the record of its use is part of this test's history.
  • Rhetorical pressure on independent media is continuous and documented in both terms — repeated public attacks on Czech Television and on critical outlets by Babiš and by Speaker Okamura — though, to keep the shelves honest, no outlet has been closed, raided, or stripped of a licence, and critical coverage of the government is extensive and unimpeded in fact.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the president, the EU, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He has accepted every electoral verdict against him. In October 2021, after SPOLU edged ANO in the popular vote (27.8% to 27.1%) and the opposition coalitions commanded a majority, he accepted the defeat and congratulated the winner; having first explored whether he might still form a government, he stood aside when SPOLU refused any coalition with him, and Petr Fiala's government took office in December 2021 (election record; Euractiv reporting, October 2021). In January 2023 he lost the presidential runoff to Petr Pavel and conceded. In October 2026, when the governing coalition won none of the 27 Senate seats at stake and ANO lost ground in the municipal elections, he publicly called ANO's Senate result "a catastrophic result" and the results stood unchallenged (OSW analysis, 20 October 2026; his statements as reported by ČT24 and Respekt).
  • He adjusted to the president's pre-condition for his own appointment: in early December 2025, before Pavel would appoint him, he publicly announced the Agrofert trust arrangement — a concession extracted by pushback and delivered before he took office, whatever its sufficiency (Test 1).
  • He adjusted on the ammunition initiative. In July 2025 he told Reuters he would scrap the Czech-led programme supplying artillery ammunition to Ukraine; in office, after the state security council's deliberations and with the president publicly backing the programme, he confirmed on 6 January 2026 that "the project will continue and the Czech Republic will act as coordinator," with no Czech state money invested (Reuters, 6 January 2026). The campaign position was moderated in the open, on the record.
  • The Turek refusal (Test 2) was ultimately accepted in terms — "the question of Filip Turek in the government is closed" — and a competence lawsuit against the president was expressly ruled out by both Babiš and the Motorists' leader.

Evidence against:

  • The largest protest of his current term was met with a flat refusal. On 15 November 2026 the Million Moments for Democracy movement filled Prague's Letná Plain — organizers put the crowd at 350,000 to 450,000 — demanding withdrawal of the public-media bill and the removal of Speaker Okamura. On 16 November Babiš stated he was not considering the demands and that the government would proceed with abolishing the fees (ČT24, 16 November 2026). The bill's fast-track first reading at 4:36 a.m. is itself part of the answer the government gave to scrutiny.
  • The pattern is the same as in his first term: in June 2019, when Million Moments brought roughly a quarter of a million people to Letná — the largest demonstration since 1989 — demanding his resignation over the Stork's Nest charge and the conflict-of-interest findings, he refused to resign and dismissed the protests as a political campaign; his government survived the ensuing no-confidence vote (press record, June 2019).
  • The European Commission's conflict-of-interest findings have been met with denial in both terms rather than adjustment: the 2021 audit's conclusions were rejected, and the September 2026 assessment was answered "I don't care, and I am not dealing with it" (ČTK, September 2026). Where the referee is Brussels, the documented response across nine years is dismissal.
  • On his own prosecution, the adjustment ran the other way: having submitted to the process when earlier parliaments lifted his immunity, he accepted — and his coalition delivered — the March 2026 vote that shields him from the retrial the courts had ordered (Test 1).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include Ukrainians under protection, the Roma, his opponents' voters, the poor — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • His opponents' voters are counted in full. Elections under and against him are free and their results executed: the 2021 transfer of power, the opposition's Senate majority and its October 2026 sweep of the contested Senate seats, opposition control of Prague — none of it disputed, obstructed, or punished (Tests 2–3). The October 2026 municipal elections again seated councils across the country, independents winning most seats in small municipalities, with ANO first in national votes but reduced (OSW, October 2026).
  • Ukrainians under temporary protection remain inside the legal count: roughly 395,000–400,000 people hold temporary protection in Czechia, the status runs under the EU framework to March 2027, and the government has not moved to end protection or expel the protected population; the ammunition initiative that supplies Ukraine's army also continues under Czech coordination (Interior Ministry figures as reported 2025–2026; Reuters, January 2026).
  • His domestic programme is aimed at, and materially serves, the lower-income and pensioner electorate that forms his base: the coalition programme caps the retirement age at 65, raises public-sector wages, restores family tax benefits, subsidizes mortgages for young families, and shifts renewable-energy subsidy costs from household bills to the state (coalition programme, November 2025). Whatever its fiscal cost (Test 5), the flock he counts first is the poorer half of his own country, and it is genuinely counted.

Evidence against:

  • Ukrainians are counted down at the margins, by policy. The government ended Czech state military aid to Ukraine, declined to guarantee the EU's €90 billion loan for Kyiv, and in July 2026 refused participation in NATO's €70 billion assistance package for 2026, offering only a small one-off contribution to a separate US-weapons package (Reuters, 8 July 2026). Domestically, the government is preparing to tighten support for Ukrainian refugees — reducing or cancelling payments for working-age people who are not employed, reserving full assistance for those unable to support themselves; the parameters were not yet law at grading (Czech media reporting via Novinky.cz, 2026). The direction is documented; its final legal form is not.
  • The Roma and minorities are counted through a coalition partner's record and a nomination he defended. His partner Okamura's SPD built its 2024 European campaign on posters the police and prosecutors treated as incitement to hatred — the charge now frozen by the same March 2026 immunity vote (Test 1). His other partner's nominee Turek carried a documented history of racist and anti-Roma posts — the stated ground of the president's refusal — and Babiš kept the nomination alive for weeks before accepting the refusal, then seated Turek as a government commissioner (Test 2). The anti-racist organization ROMEA's petition against the nomination, and the president's reasoning, are on the record; Babiš's position was that the coalition agreement left nominations to each party.
  • The counting-out is, however, rhetorical and budgetary rather than legal in the record to date: no minority has been stripped of rights, status, or franchise by his governments; temporary protection continues in law; no pushback or expulsion practice is documented against his name. In a series where this test has failed on origin-based statutes and systematic border violence, Czechia's file does not reach that shelf — which is why this test is MIXED and not FAILS.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record, 2014–2026, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Public-media fees — kept, in progress. ANO campaigned in 2025 on cancelling the Czech Television and Radio fees ("we will… cancel public television and radio fees," campaign launch, September 2025); the coalition programme adopted the pledge and the implementing bill is moving through parliament in autumn 2026 (Test 2). The promise is being executed on the schedule announced, over mass protest.
  • No Czech money for Ukraine's war — kept. He pledged in the 2025 campaign that the national budget would no longer fund military support for Ukraine; in office, state funding stopped, the EU loan guarantee was declined, and the NATO package refused (Test 4). The ammunition initiative's continuation without Czech money (Test 3) is the stated form of the pledge, not its breach — he had also promised to end the scheme outright, and that part was moderated.
  • Retirement age — committed in the governing programme. The pledge to cap the retirement age at 65, reversing the previous government's gradual rise toward 67, is written into the coalition programme of November 2025; implementing legislation was part of the government's first-year agenda at grading.
  • First-term signature stances held: he promised in 2017 to keep the crown and reject euro adoption, to reject EU migrant quotas, and to run the state "like a company"; the euro was not adopted, quotas were refused throughout his term, and both positions were maintained consistently into the 2025 programme (no euro, rejection of the EU migration pact and of ETS2 household carbon pricing — coalition programme, November 2025).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • The conflict-of-interest promise — not kept on the referee's reading. Before his appointment he publicly committed to settle Agrofert so as to comply with Czech and European law, telling the public the shares would rest in an independently administered trust until his death and then pass to his children. The Commission concluded in September 2026 that the RSVP Trust does not sufficiently mitigate the conflict and that he remains in a potential conflict of interest; Transparency International's Czech office, citing the Commission's reply to its inquiry, stated the conclusion flatly: "by placing the shares in a fund, Babiš did not resolve the conflict of interest," and its director called the outcome a broken promise to the public and the president. A further documented discrepancy: Seznam Zprávy's reporting on the RSVP Trust statutes indicated his children could regain control of Agrofert when he leaves office rather than only at his death, as he had stated; Babiš responded that he had done "much more than I had to." The gap between his December 2025 description and the 2026 findings is CONTESTED between him and the Commission; that a gap exists on the official record is not.
  • The deficit promise — receding. The coalition programme undertook to keep public finances "safely below the 3% of GDP" EU deficit limit. The government's revamped 2026 budget instead widened the deficit target to 310 billion crowns against the previous government's 286 billion, and its draft 2027 budget projects 386 billion crowns — the second-largest deficit in Czech history — with Babiš himself saying in March 2026 that the 2027 deficit may widen further (Reuters, March 2026; AP reporting on the November 2026 no-confidence debate, which the government survived 83 votes for dismissal against the 101 needed). President Pavel signed the 2026 budget while warning that its defence outlays did not match NATO commitments.
  • Defence — the 2% line missed. Czech law and NATO commitment set defence spending at 2% of GDP; Czechia fell short in 2025, and at the July 2026 NATO summit Babiš said the country was unlikely to meet the target in 2026 either, aiming for 2027 (Reuters, 8 July 2026). The coalition programme pointedly made no commitment to NATO's agreed path toward higher spending.
  • The first-term anti-corruption word. He entered national politics promising to fight corruption and "run the state like a firm"; the European Commission's final audit of April 2021 found that throughout that premiership he had himself been in breach of conflict-of-interest law while his conglomerate received EU funds (Test 1). Measured against his founding promise, the audit finding is the record's answer.
  • Pandora Papers — ALLEGATION, stated once. October 2021 reporting on the Pandora Papers documented his purchase of French Riviera property in 2009 through offshore companies, a structure not declared in his asset statements; he denied any illegality and said the funds were taxed and the purchase lawful. No Czech charge followed in the record reviewed; the item stands as contested reporting with his denial beside it, and it is part of why his transparency pledges are graded against a discount.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Andrej Babiš is the only subject in this series who is graded across two premierships separated by a full term in opposition — and the two records rhyme. The institutions around him are demonstrably alive: courts annulled his acquittals, a president refused his nominee and made it stick, a state fund cut his conglomerate's subsidies, voters emptied his coalition out of the Senate, and he has accepted every one of those verdicts in fact. Nothing in Czechia is captured, and the grip test's MIXED reflects real autonomy, not its absence. But at the single point where the law reaches the man himself, the record bends in both terms: a European Commission audit found him in breach of conflict-of-interest law while prime minister in 2021, and found his remedy insufficient again in 2026; and when the courts ordered the retrial that could finally settle the Stork's Nest allegation, his own parliamentary majority voted in March 2026 to keep his immunity until 2029 — in the same vote shielding his coalition partner from a hate-speech charge. A ruler who submits to every referee except the one holding his own case does not give by one law. The fraud allegation itself remains exactly that — an allegation two trial courts declined to sustain and no final verdict has settled — and this record is correctable in the open: a retrial, a Commission-accepted remedy, or the public-media bill's final form would each move grades.
