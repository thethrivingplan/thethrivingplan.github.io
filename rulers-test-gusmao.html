The Ruler's Test — Kay Rala Xanana Gusmão, Prime Minister of Timor-Leste

Subject: Kay Rala Xanana Gusmão — Prime Minister of Timor-Leste since 1 July 2023. The seat graded is the premiership, and the reason is stated plainly: Timor-Leste is a semi-presidential republic in which the prime minister heads the government and directs executive policy, the budget, and the administration, while the president is head of state with limited executive powers — chiefly promulgating or vetoing legislation (parliament can override a veto), appointing the prime minister after elections, dissolving parliament in defined circumstances, granting pardons, and serving as supreme commander of the defence force (Australian DFAT country brief, 2026; CIA World Factbook). The president, José Ramos-Horta (in office since 20 May 2022, elected in the April 2022 run-off with 62.1%), is therefore not graded as the subject, but his record is stated and graded where it bears directly on a test — his veto threat over criminal defamation and his promulgation of the lifetime-pension repeal in Test 2 and Test 3, and his rejection of a pardon request after victims' advocates pressed the case (La'o Hamutuk mid-year report, January–June 2026). Gusmão's mandate is electoral and current, not inherited or completed: his National Congress for Timorese Reconstruction (CNRT) won 41.6% of the vote and 31 of 65 seats in the parliamentary election of 21 May 2023, formed a coalition with the Democratic Party (PD, 6 seats) for 37 of 65, and he was sworn in on 1 July 2023 at the head of the IX Constitutional Government for the 2023–2028 term (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2024; EU Election Expert Mission final report, 2023). He remains in office at grading: he presented the proposed 2027 budget to parliament on 9 November 2026 and attended the 49th ASEAN Summit in Manila on 15–17 November 2026 as prime minister (Government of Timor-Leste, November 2026). No premise correction to the seat framing was needed; the correction the record does require is one of emphasis — this is his second time in the seat (prime minister 2007–2015, after serving as president 2002–2007), and he is graded here only on the tenure that began on 1 July 2023. His predecessor in this tenure, Taur Matan Ruak (prime minister 2018–2023), appears only as context, chiefly as the government whose "irregularities" the IX Government's programme promised to correct. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: "How you play the game matters. Play by the rules. Stay true to your word. And if another option becomes available, at least consider it." Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Governing on the platform the voters elected is not graded as a failure; carrying out what he ran on is evidence on the FOR shelf of Test 5, and marks are lost only for how it is done. Every test closes with a plain-words why and a named helped/hurt ledger. Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded as independent axes. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The clearest one-law act of the tenure came against his own state's insiders. The 2025 public recruitment for the National Police (PNTL) officer training course was suspended on 11 February 2026 after candidates and civil-society organisations denounced a lack of credibility; the government's own Monitoring and Oversight Commission then found, in the chair Paulo Assis Belo's account, that 71 children of police officers and 72 children of veterans had been placed in privileged positions on the vacancy list (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026; Government of Timor-Leste, Council of Ministers, 25 March 2026). On 25 March 2026 the Council of Ministers partially cancelled the process, ordered all candidates who had passed document screening to retake the cultural, psychological, and medical tests, ordered a new selection jury, and established independent monitoring with international partners; Portugal's GNR and Australian police subsequently agreed to support the rerun for 400 vacancies (Government of Timor-Leste, 25 March 2026; Lusa, 22 April 2026; International IDEA Global State of Democracy tracker, March 2026).
  • The anti-corruption referee that had been left headless before he took office was filled under his government, by parliament rather than by executive appointment: Rui Pereira was elected Commissioner of the Anti-Corruption Commission (CAC) by 41 votes in the National Parliament on 24 June 2024 for the 2024–2028 term and sworn in on 5 July 2024, after the previous commissioner's mandate had expired in April 2023 with no successor agreed (Government of Timor-Leste, July 2024; EU Election Expert Mission, 2023). The CAC presented its 2025 annual report to parliament on 21 April 2026 and signed a cooperation memorandum with Indonesia's KPK in January 2026 (CAC, 2026).
  • The government's first-120-days programme applied its audit machinery to the previous government's institutions and projects without a documented exemption for its own coalition's people: working teams were set up to audit and legally analyse public institutions and major projects of the previous government, the oil and minerals sector entities were restructured with new chairmanships, and the National Rehabilitation Centre was audited (Government of Timor-Leste, 120 Days of Commitments report, July–October 2023).
  • A large, long-connected foreign investor was held to the same contract terms the state publishes for everyone — on the government's account. On 23 March 2026 the government announced its intention to cancel the Pelican Paradise project, and on 12 August 2026 the Council of Ministers ordered the return of the allocated land to the state, stating the Special Investment Agreement was no longer in effect and the company had been notified (La'o Hamutuk statement, 2 April 2026; Government of Timor-Leste via Independente, 14 August 2026). This item is CONTESTED: Pelican Paradise disputes the cancellation, stating the delays were caused by the state's failure to provide the water and electricity the agreement required, that it had invested about US$15 million, and that the dispute is subject to arbitration at the Singapore International Arbitration Centre (Pelican Paradise statement, 8 April 2026; Business Times reporting via RTTL, August 2026). The dossier records the act and the dispute; it does not settle who breached first.
  • The outcome measure has not deteriorated on his watch: Timor-Leste scored 44 of 100 in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index for both 2024 and 2025 (73rd of 182 in the 2025 index, released 10 February 2026), up from 43 in 2023 and 28 in 2015 (Transparency International, CPI 2025; Government of Timor-Leste, 13 February 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The favouritism the government cancelled in March 2026 had happened inside a recruitment run on its own watch, in its own interior ministry's force. The privileged placements — 143 favoured candidates identified by the government's own commission chair — were not an inheritance from the previous government; the process was a 2025 process (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026; Government of Timor-Leste, 25 March 2026). Cancelling a rigged process is one-law behaviour; having run it is the opposite, and both facts belong to this tenure.
  • One category of Timorese is favoured at the source by design, and the favour grew on his budgets. In the 2026 budget, public transfers of US$820 million — 34.3% of all spending — are dominated by veterans' pensions and institutional subsidies rather than targeted household support, and the IMF's 2025 Article IV assessment records that transfers "do not consistently reach the most vulnerable people" (Rui Gomes, former finance minister, budget analysis for FONGTIL, 2 July 2026, citing Ministry of Finance 2026 figures and the IMF; IMF Executive Board, 2025 Article IV, 26 September 2025). Veterans are a named, honoured category in Timorese law and the favour is open rather than hidden; Test 1 grades the favour itself, not its visibility.
  • The insiders' car purchase sat in an approved budget until the street removed it. A purchase of 65 Toyota Prado vehicles for MPs at about US$4.2 million (US$61,500 per vehicle) was approved in the 2024 budget process for the 2025 budget — a budget of the IX Government's parliament, with a CNRT-led majority — and was cancelled only on 16 September 2025, after two days of student protests (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025; Al Jazeera, 26 September 2025). The cancellation is graded in Test 3; here the shelf records that the second law for insiders was written first and unwritten under pressure.
  • The Special Administrative Region of Oe-Cusse Ambeno (RAEOA) was restructured by government resolution rather than open process: on 24 January 2024 the Council of Ministers, on Gusmão's own proposal, appointed Rogério Tiago de Fátima Lobato as President of the RAEOA Authority for a five-year term (Government of Timor-Leste, Council of Ministers, 24 January 2024). Appointment by resolution is the lawful mechanism the government used; the dossier records the mechanism, and no documented open competition for the post appears in the record assembled here.

Why this grade: The strongest one-law evidence is real and unusually concrete — a government that publishes its own commission's finding that police and veterans' children were favoured, then cancels the process and imports foreign monitors, is applying one rule against its own insiders. But the same record shows the favouritism occurred under his administration in the first place, that the single largest transfer stream in his budgets flows to a privileged category ahead of the poorest, and that an insiders' car fleet was budgeted until protesters stopped it. Acts in both directions, on the same test, in the same tenure: MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — the non-connected police-recruitment candidates whose competition was rerun under a new jury with international monitoring (Government of Timor-Leste, March–April 2026); taxpayers and landholding communities around the Tasi Tolu–Tibar site, on the government's account of the Pelican Paradise cancellation (La'o Hamutuk, April 2026). Hurt — the candidates who had advanced through the flawed 2025 police process and were made to retake tests for a failure that was not theirs (Government of Timor-Leste, 25 March 2026); the poorest households, who receive a comparatively small share of a transfer budget dominated by veterans' pensions (IMF, 2025; Gomes analysis, July 2026); Pelican Paradise and its employees, if the company's contested account of state non-delivery is the true one — recorded as CONTESTED, not settled.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the electoral commission, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The aggregate referees' scores moved the right way on his watch. Freedom House rated Timor-Leste "Free" at 72 of 100 in Freedom in the World 2025 and 73 of 100 in the 2026 edition — the only "Free" country in Southeast Asia in the EuSEE country focus assessment — and Reporters Without Borders placed it 39th worldwide for press freedom in 2025 (Freedom House, 2025 and 2026; Government of Timor-Leste, 23 March 2026; EuSEE/Hivos country focus report, 2025). Freedom House's standing caveat is recorded with the score: institutions "remain fragile, and disputes among leaders from the independence struggle dominate political affairs" (Freedom House, Freedom in the World 2025).
  • The judiciary's missing top floor is being built by open competition rather than appointment. Timor-Leste has never established the constitutionally mandated Supreme Court of Justice, leaving the Court of Appeal to exercise its functions; on 1 July 2026 the first recruitment process opened for six Supreme Court judges and nine Court of Appeal positions, assessed by an international jury of retired Portuguese Supreme Court judges, under a government roadmap adopted in 2025, with the court expected to be established by November 2026 (International IDEA tracker, July 2026). At grading, establishment is documented as expected, not as completed.
  • When a referee was proposed for abolition, the proposal failed in the open. In January 2026 eight MPs proposed a law to eliminate the Petroleum Fund Consultative Council (KKFP), the advisory body that publicly warns on fund sustainability; after submissions and hearings the proposal failed and the Council's mandate remains in the Petroleum Fund Law (La'o Hamutuk mid-year report, January–June 2026). The proposal came from MPs, not from the government — the shelf records both that the attempt existed inside his parliament and that it did not pass.
  • The media referee was defended by the executive itself in 2026. When parliament tabled a draft seventh amendment to the Penal Code in May 2026 to reintroduce criminal defamation and insult — reversing the decriminalisation adopted in 2014 — Prime Minister Gusmão publicly expressed his disagreement, arguing it would burden the courts and multiply disputes, and President Ramos-Horta told parliament not to waste time debating it because he would veto it; on 13 July 2026 the President of the National Parliament announced the defamation provisions would be removed from the legislative agenda (CIVICUS Monitor, July 2026; La'o Hamutuk mid-year report, January–June 2026; Press Council of Timor-Leste via Lusa, 11 June 2026). On this file the president's veto power was used as a shield for the press, and it worked before any vote.
  • The electoral machinery is being widened, not narrowed, ahead of the 2027 presidential election: in October 2026 the Council of Ministers approved four draft bills amending the presidential, parliamentary, and municipal election laws and voter registration — accessible voting for people with disabilities, extended overseas voting hours, digitised and biometric registration — following a September 2026 decision to run an extended overseas registration period from October 2026 to January 2027, with more than 11,600 voters registered abroad by 5 November 2026 (International IDEA tracker, October 2026; Government of Timor-Leste, November 2026). Enactment by parliament is not yet documented; the direction of the proposals is what this shelf records.
  • The central bank's autonomy is visible in the fund's plumbing: the Petroleum Fund is held at the Central Bank of Timor-Leste (BCTL), which acts as operational manager under an investment policy set by the Ministry of Finance and advised by an Investment Advisory Board, and the fund's balance and the Estimated Sustainable Income are published figures that the government's critics use against the government in parliament (International Forum of Sovereign Wealth Funds profile; La'o Hamutuk budget submissions, 2025–2026). No documented act of Gusmão removing a central bank governor, a CAC commissioner, an electoral official, or a judge appears in the record assembled here.

Evidence against:

  • The judiciary-control charge exists, is contested, and is recorded with its disputants named. The Portuguese newspaper Expresso reported on 13 May 2025, under the headline "Financial scandal causes constitutional crisis, Xanana Gusmão's Government accused of wanting to control the judiciary," an alleged strategy of interference linked to the appointment of a new President of the Court of Appeal. The government exercised a formal right of reply on 14 May 2025, calling the headline misleading, stating that no judge was dismissed or removed, that the Court of Appeal presidency changed on the expiry of the previous president's term under the legal and constitutional terms in force, and that Deputy Prime Minister Francisco Kalbuadi Lay "has never been convicted of any crime" and so could not be the object of any pardon strategy (Government of Timor-Leste, right of reply, 14 May 2025). This is an ALLEGATION by Expresso, denied on the record by the government; the dossier states neither as a verdict. What is independently documented is that judicial discontent continued into 2026: the Judges' Association of Timor-Leste (AJTL) publicly called for Court of Appeal President Afonso Carmona to resign over a polemic about the transfer of four Dili first-instance judges, and fourteen judges who sought an audience with Gusmão in October 2026 left, in the AJTL president's account, with the prime minister having "only listened" and no solution produced (AJTL via Martin Hardie reporting, October 2026).
  • In July 2026 the government asked parliament for legislative authority to approve a cybersecurity and cybercrime package by decree-law — that is, to legislate in an area touching expression and criminal procedure without a full parliamentary law. The Council of Ministers approved the request on 22 July 2026 and sent it to parliament; civil-society organisations and press-freedom groups raised concerns about its potential effect on freedom of expression, and no report that parliament has granted the authority appears in the record assembled here (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026; Computer Crime Research Center legislation tracker, updated 14 November 2026). Asking is not gripping; the ask is nevertheless a request to move a referee-adjacent power into the executive's hands, and it is recorded as such.
  • The checks that work are largely checks inside one political family. The president who threatened the defamation veto is a CNRT figure and Gusmão's fellow independence leader; at the opening of the fourth legislative session on 15 September 2026 Ramos-Horta publicly congratulated Gusmão "for his wise leadership of the 9th Constitutional Government" (Government of Timor-Leste, 15 September 2026). Budgets pass with the opposition abstaining rather than prevailing — the 2026 budget in general terms by 42 votes to none with 23 abstentions, the 2027 proposal by 41 to none with 23 abstentions (Government of Timor-Leste, November 2025 and November 2026). Nothing in that is improper; it means parliament's grip on this executive has been exercised as scrutiny and abstention, not as defeat, throughout the tenure.

Why this grade: No documented purge, removal, or capture of a court, the electoral administration, the CAC, the central bank, or a media outlet appears in this record, and two referees — the Petroleum Fund Consultative Council and the press, via the defamation fight — visibly survived challenges during the tenure, with the prime minister himself on the press's side of the second one. Against that stand a contested, denied ALLEGATION of judicial interference from Expresso, a live internal judiciary dispute in which judges appealed to the prime minister personally, and an executive request to legislate on cybercrime by decree. That combination — autonomy intact in outcomes, pressure visible at the edges — is MIXED, not HOLDS.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — journalists and critics, who kept a decriminalised defamation regime after the executive and the president both opposed re-criminalisation (CIVICUS Monitor, July 2026); voters abroad and voters with disabilities, named beneficiaries of the October 2026 electoral bills if parliament enacts them (International IDEA, October 2026); future appellants, if the Supreme Court recruitment completes (International IDEA, July 2026). Hurt — the four transferred Dili judges and the fourteen who backed them, whose dispute with the Court of Appeal presidency remained unresolved after their audience with the prime minister (AJTL, October 2026); no other named hurt on this test is documented.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, parliament, the street, foreign partners, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The street's sharpest pushback of the tenure was answered in days, in the open, at the insiders' expense. Student-led protests of more than 1,000 people on 15 September 2025, growing to more than 2,000 on 16 September, demanded cancellation of the US$4.2 million MP car purchase and then abolition of lifetime pensions for former MPs. On 15 September the three governing parties — CNRT, PD, and KHUNTO — announced they would ask parliament to cancel the car purchase; parliament voted to cancel it on 16 September; on 17 September MPs agreed with protest leaders to scrap the lifetime pensions; on 26 September 62 MPs voted to abolish lifetime pensions for MPs, former presidents, prime ministers, and cabinet ministers; and President Ramos-Horta promulgated the law on 29 September 2025 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025; International Crisis Group CrisisWatch, September 2025; International IDEA tracker, September 2025; Al Jazeera, 26 September 2025). Gusmão's own public answer, on 25 September 2025, was to challenge university students to discuss the state's problems in order to strengthen "objective social control" — while also saying students could protest but should not destroy property (Lusa, 25 September 2025).
  • The police-recruitment wobble was answered the same way, twice. The government suspended the process itself on 11 February 2026 when its credibility was denounced, before the March protests; after young candidates protested in Dili on 16 March 2026, Gusmão announced a new recruitment process on 19 March, and the Council of Ministers formally cancelled parts of the old process on 25 March with a new jury and international monitoring (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026; Government of Timor-Leste, 25 March 2026).
  • On the tenure's largest deadlocked file, the test's question — if another option becomes available, does he at least consider it — was answered yes: he took the new option, without abandoning his stated end. Greater Sunrise had been stalled for years between Timor-Leste's insistence on onshore processing and operator Woodside's preference for Darwin. The government first signed a cooperation agreement with Woodside for studies on processing in Timor-Leste (authorised November 2025), then, in a joint statement with Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese in Dili on 28 January 2026, both governments pressed the joint venture to submit a development concept "as soon as possible," with Australia recognising Timor-Leste's commitment to onshore processing and the Tasi Mane south-coast project (Lusa, 24 November 2025 and 28 January 2026). When the venture still did not move, the option that became available was a buy-out: TIMOR GAP agreed on 2 September 2026 to acquire Osaka Gas's 10% interest, and on 27 October 2026 signed a sale and purchase agreement for Woodside's entire 33.44% interest for total consideration of up to US$200 million — US$13.71 million on completion, the rest contingent on a final investment decision and on first LNG — leaving TIMOR GAP to hold 100% on completion, targeted for the first half of 2027, subject to approvals (Woodside Energy announcement, 27 October 2026; TIMOR GAP, 27 October 2026; Government of Timor-Leste, 27 October 2026). On 11 November 2026 Gusmão launched the Detailed Marine Surveys for the Greater Sunrise and Bayu-Undan pipelines at Tibar port (Government of Timor-Leste, November 2026). Considering the new option and changing the means, openly and by signed agreement, is exactly what this part of the test grades; declining the Darwin option with stated reasons — onshore value retention, jobs, energy security, and the Wood Group conceptual study's finding that the Timor-Leste LNG option was feasible with lower operating costs — is recorded as a reasoned decline, not as a refusal to look (Lusa, 24 November 2025).
  • The defamation wobble (Test 2) was also answered by adjustment rather than doubling down: the proposal his own parliament had tabled was dropped from the agenda in July 2026 after civil-society, Press Council, and student opposition, with the prime minister on the record against it (CIVICUS Monitor, July 2026; Lusa, 11 June 2026).
  • On the fiscal wobble, the warning was owned in his own voice rather than denied. Presenting the 2027 budget on 9–10 November 2026, Gusmão warned parliament that "projections indicate that, if current trends continue, the Petroleum Fund could be exhausted around 2039. This constitutes a serious warning signal" — and the June 2026 rectifying budget increase of US$101.1 million was, on his statement in the debate, financed by reallocation, carried-over balances, dormant state accounts, and higher domestic revenue rather than by additional Petroleum Fund withdrawals (Government of Timor-Leste budget presentation via Asia Daily reporting, November 2026; Tatoli via La'o Hamutuk, June 2026).

Evidence against:

  • Both street wobbles were answered only after force was used against the people raising them. Police fired tear gas at the September 2025 student protesters, injuring at least four, and at least 12 protesters were arrested and detained for hours before release (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025; International Crisis Group, September 2025). In March 2026 police used tear gas against the police-recruitment protesters at the National University campus on 18 March, with at least seven people injured on CIVICUS's count (CIVICUS Monitor, 2026). The adjustment came; the first answer was gas.
  • The fiscal pushback — the IMF's, the Petroleum Fund Consultative Council's, La'o Hamutuk's, and the Finance Ministry's own — has been heard for three consecutive years and the envelope has not adjusted. The IMF's 2024 Article IV (December 2024) urged gradual consolidation, containing recurrent spending, and introducing VAT in 2026; its 2025 consultation (September 2025) repeated the call with VAT by 2027; its August 2026 mission still found, "without further reforms," deficits leading to "depletion of the Petroleum Fund in the late 2030s" and still urged the government to "accelerate preparations" for VAT (IMF, 17 December 2024; 26 September 2025; 28 August 2026). The budgets moved the other way: US$2.2 billion approved for 2025, US$2.291 billion consolidated for 2026 rising to US$2.392 billion after rectification, and US$2.569 billion proposed for 2027 (La'o Hamutuk annual report 2025; Government of Timor-Leste, November 2025 and June 2026; Government of Timor-Leste, 9 November 2026). Explanation and warning are documented; adjustment of the spending path is not.
  • Fundasaun Mahein, a Timorese security-sector NGO, assessed the September 2025 protests as "the result of profound failures by the elites to promote equitable development and political inclusion" — a critic's characterisation, recorded as such, that the wobble was of the government's own making before it was answered (Fundasaun Mahein via Lusa, 19 September 2025). The car purchase and the pension law that triggered the protests were both products of the political class he leads.

Why this grade: On political pushback — students in the street, candidates in the street, civil society on defamation — this is one of the stronger records in the series: demands were met in days, in public, by law, at the expense of the insiders who had written the perks. On the fiscal pushback, the same government has listened to the same warning from its own Finance Ministry, its own fund council, and three IMF consultations, repeated the warning in its own voice, and raised the budget anyway. A ruler who adjusts to the street and not to the spreadsheet earns MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — university students and young Timorese generally, whose two central demands became law within two weeks in September 2025 (Al Jazeera, 26 September 2025); police-recruitment candidates without connections, given a rerun process (Test 1); journalists, spared a criminal defamation law (Test 2). Hurt — the protesters injured by tear gas in September 2025 (at least four) and March 2026 (at least seven on CIVICUS's count) and the at least 12 detained in September 2025 (CIVICUS Monitor, 2025 and 2026); future taxpayers and fund beneficiaries, who bear the unadjusted spending path documented above (IMF, August 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the rural majority, the poor, women, people with disabilities, Oe-Cusse and Atauro, the diaspora, and those who did not vote for him — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The political count is formally broad and stayed broad. Parliament is 35.4% women (23 of 65), under a list rule requiring at least one woman in every three candidates, and its Speaker since 2023, Maria Fernanda Lay, is the first woman to hold the post (IPU Parline, 2026; International IDEA). Gusmão's government established the National Council for People with Disabilities in its first 120 days and audited the National Rehabilitation Centre (Government of Timor-Leste, 120 Days report, 2023), and its October 2026 electoral bills add accessible voting measures for people with disabilities (International IDEA, October 2026).
  • The diaspora was counted more fully on his watch: overseas registration was extended from October 2026 to January 2027 with additional resources and possible mobile posts, more than 11,600 voters abroad were registered by November 2026, and the electoral bills would extend overseas voting hours and allow registration committees in countries without Timorese diplomatic representation (Government of Timor-Leste, November 2026; International IDEA, October 2026).
  • The geographic count reaches beyond Dili in the documented works programme: in November 2026 alone Gusmão presided over groundbreaking for urban road rehabilitation in Liquiçá and Aileu (19 and 21 November) and the Letefoho–Atsabe–Lourba road in Ermera (30 October), and inaugurated classrooms in Dili and Baucau; the 2026 budget allocated US$60 million to the Oe-Cusse Ambeno Special Administrative Region (Government of Timor-Leste, November 2025 and November 2026).
  • The social floor was maintained and, on the World Bank's account, widened: growth of 4.5% in 2025 was sustained in part by "expanded social assistance" and veterans' transfers supporting household demand (World Bank, Timor-Leste Macro Poverty Outlook, 2026). The government's documented health programme includes a National Multisectoral Plan to Combat Stunting, purchase of specialised nutritious foods, HPV vaccination, and WHO-validated rubella elimination (Government of Timor-Leste, 2025 budget debate materials).
  • The September 2025 pension repeal (Test 3) is also a counting act: it removed a lifetime benefit enjoyed only by former office-holders — MPs, presidents, prime ministers, and ministers — in a country where the protesters' central fact was that more than 40% of the population lives below the poverty line (Al Jazeera, 26 September 2025; World Bank figure cited therein).

Evidence against:

  • The poor are counted in the speeches and under-counted in the money. La'o Hamutuk's analysis of the 2026 budget finds only 18% of expenditure allocated to the strategic sectors of education, health, agriculture, water, and sanitation combined (La'o Hamutuk annual report, January–December 2025). Former finance minister Rui Gomes's July 2026 analysis, from Ministry of Finance figures, puts transfers plus wages at 54.2% of the 2026 budget — over half spent before a service is delivered — with the transfer stream dominated by veterans' pensions and institutional subsidies rather than targeted support for the poorest (Gomes, FONGTIL analysis, 2 July 2026).
  • The underlying counts are severe and have not visibly moved under this government: 42% of the population below the national poverty line (World Bank, Transforming Public Spending, February 2025, cited in Gomes 2026), and about 47% of children under five stunted (Ministry of Health 2020 figures carried in UNICEF Timor-Leste's cash-transfer study) — separate sourced figures, printed separately and not blended. Less than a third of the working-age population is economically active, and national income per capita is the second lowest in the region (World Bank, Macro Poverty Outlook, 2026).
  • The municipal count is still appointed, not elected. Timor-Leste's municipalities are run by administrators appointed by the government; the constitution provides for elected local government and a decentralisation strategy for 2025–2028 has been adopted, but no municipal elections have been held yet (Electos country profile, 2026, summarising the constitutional and legal position). The only elected local bodies remain the suco (village) councils, last elected in October–November 2023. A government elected in 2023 on a programme that included restructuring RAEOA and the municipality of Atauro has, three years in, restructured both from the centre — the RAEOA president appointed by its own resolution (Test 1) — without yet holding the local elections that would let those populations count themselves.
  • The no-good-choice clause applies to this test's fiscal core, and its mechanics are recorded rather than used as a demerit. When Bayu-Undan, the last producing field, ceased production in June 2025, the options actually open were: cut spending toward the Estimated Sustainable Income (about US$543 million for 2026, against a US$2.3 billion budget) and collapse the salaries, transfers, and services that most households depend on the state for; or keep spending and draw the fund down toward depletion in the late 2030s, transferring the cost to the next generation (IMF, 2025 and 2026; La'o Hamutuk, 2025). No third door was documented as open at the time — Greater Sunrise revenue cannot arrive before the 2030s on any plan (Test 5), and domestic revenue is under 15% of state operations (Gomes, 2026). The trap was not built by Gusmão — excess withdrawals above sustainable income began in 2008–2009 under earlier governments, including his own first premiership, which is part of the record (Gomes, 2026) — and he has been honest about the trap while in it, warning parliament of exhaustion around 2039 in his own voice (November 2026). What remains gradable, and is graded in Test 5, is whether he took the least-harm door actually open: the documented record is that the envelope rose each year rather than gliding down, which is neither of the two hard doors but a deferral of both.

Why this grade: Everyone is in the formal count — women in parliament at 35.4%, disability and diaspora provisions expanding, Oe-Cusse funded, roads and classrooms reaching the municipalities — and one insider-only benefit was abolished outright. But the budget's centre of gravity still sits with wages, veterans' transfers, and the capital, while the sectors the poorest depend on share 18%, two in five Timorese remain below the poverty line, nearly half of young children are stunted, and no Timorese citizen has yet been allowed to elect a municipal government. Counted in the law, under-counted in the money: MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — former office-holders' would-be pension costs redirected: taxpayers generally, and specifically the students whose demand abolished the lifetime pension (September 2025); voters abroad, given an extended registration window (2026); people with disabilities, given a national council (2023) and proposed accessible voting (2026); residents of Liquiçá, Aileu, Ermera, and Baucau named in the 2026 works record. Hurt — the poorest households, who receive a small, untargeted share of the largest transfer budget in the state while education, health, agriculture, and water share 18% of spending (La'o Hamutuk, 2025; IMF, 2025); children under five, among whom stunting stands at about 47% on the last published survey figures (UNICEF/Ministry of Health); municipal populations, still governed by appointees with no election date documented.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Program pledges and public statements vs. the record since 1 July 2023, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • ASEAN membership — the programme's central external pledge, delivered on the date the bloc set. The IX Government's programme (presented 13–18 July 2023) made regional integration a pillar; Timor-Leste followed the 2023 Roadmap for Full Membership, acceded to the required legal instruments, and was admitted as ASEAN's 11th member state at the 47th ASEAN Summit in Kuala Lumpur on 26 October 2025, with Gusmão signing the Declaration of Admission and stating Timor-Leste entered "with full commitment to its Charter" (ASEAN Secretariat, 26 October 2025; Government of Timor-Leste, 26 October 2025). Parliament ratified the declaration unanimously on 11 November 2025, and at the 49th Summit in Manila in November 2026 ASEAN leaders confirmed Timor-Leste will assume the ASEAN chairmanship in 2029 (Government of Timor-Leste, November 2025 and November 2026).
  • WTO membership — delivered. Timor-Leste became the WTO's 166th member on 30 August 2024, after parliament approved the accession protocol on 3 June 2024 and the instrument was deposited in July 2024 under Gusmão's authority by Deputy Prime Minister Francisco Kalbuadi Lay (WTO, 30 August 2024; Government of Timor-Leste, 22 July 2024).
  • The 120-day commitments — substantially executed on the government's own published accounting, which is named as a self-report. The programme's first commitment was "restoring democratic normality": approval of the five-year programme, the government's organic law, and the 2023 amending budget all occurred in 2023; the amending budget removed the excise duty on sugar and restored the previous 2.5% customs rate; 158 compulsorily retired PNTL officers were reinstated; the RAEOA/ZEESM law amendments were approved in the Council of Ministers (Government of Timor-Leste, 120 Days of Commitments report, 2023). Independent confirmation of the full 18 commitments was not assembled for this dossier; the shelf records the government's accounting as its accounting.
  • Greater Sunrise onshore processing — the word held, at cost, for three years. The position stated throughout — gas "must be processed onshore in Timor-Leste" (Lusa, 24 November 2025) — never changed, and the means changed around it (Test 3): studies agreement (2025), the January 2026 joint statement with Australia, the Osaka Gas and Woodside buy-outs (September and October 2026), and the launch of Detailed Marine Surveys on 11 November 2026, with Gusmão telling parliament on 10 November 2026 that operations are targeted to commence in 2034 (Government of Timor-Leste, November 2026; Indiplomacy reporting of the budget debate, 12 November 2026).
  • The lifetime-pension reform promised in the programme — delivered, in a stronger form than promised, under pressure. The 2023 programme pledged to reform the Lifetime Pension Law by integrating it into the contributory social security scheme for new office-holders; the September 2025 law abolished lifetime pensions for MPs and senior office-holders outright (Programme of the IX Government, July 2023; International IDEA, September 2025). The crossing is named below.
  • A small, checkable word kept in 2026: presenting the June 2026 rectifying budget, Gusmão stated the US$101.1 million increase would not be financed by additional Petroleum Fund withdrawals but by reallocation, balances, dormant accounts, and higher domestic revenue; President Ramos-Horta promulgated the rectified budget — US$2.392 billion consolidated — on 8 June 2026, and no documented contradiction of the financing statement appears in the record assembled here (Tatoli via La'o Hamutuk, June 2026).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • Fiscal sustainability and reduced dependence on the Petroleum Fund — the programme's economic core, moving backwards. The programme and successive budget speeches commit the government to diversification, domestic revenue, and sustainable public finances. The documented path: in 2025, US$1.5 billion withdrawn from the fund paid for three-fourths of all state expenditure (La'o Hamutuk annual report, 2025); the 2026 budget's Estimated Sustainable Income was about US$543.3 million against budgeted withdrawals of about US$1.719 billion (Ministry of Finance figures in Immortalis Journal of Interdisciplinary Studies, November 2026, citing the 2026 budget report); the proposed 2027 budget withdraws US$1.891 billion — about 3.5 times the US$540.5 million sustainable benchmark — covering about 73.6% of a US$2.569 billion budget (Government of Timor-Leste budget presentation, 9 November 2026; La'o Hamutuk submission to parliament, 21 October 2026). Conflicting depletion figures are printed as sourced, never blended: the IMF projects depletion "by the end of the 2030s" / "in the late 2030s" (IMF, 2024, 2025, August 2026); a Finance Ministry estimate in a 2026 public-debt bill memorandum says exhaustion by 2037; Gusmão's own November 2026 figure is "around 2039"; the fund stood at about US$18.61 billion at the end of 2025 (Government of Timor-Leste figures via IJIS, November 2026).
  • VAT — promised in the programme's tax-law review, still not in force. The programme listed a tax law introducing Value Added Tax among its legislative reviews (Programme speech, 18 July 2023). The IMF's December 2024 consultation called VAT introduction in 2026 "a key reform priority" requiring "immediate progress"; by July 2025 the government was "implementing a Value Added Tax by January 2027"; by August 2026 the IMF was still urging it to "accelerate preparations for the implementation of a broad-based Value Added Tax" (IMF, December 2024; July 2025; August 2026). At grading, no enacted VAT appears in the record assembled here.
  • The Supreme Court — a constitutional promise older than this government, still unbuilt at grading. The programme's justice-sector reform, coordinated by former minister Lúcia Lobato, opened recruitment only on 1 July 2026, with establishment expected by November 2026 (Government of Timor-Leste, July 2024; International IDEA, July 2026). In motion, credibly; delivered, not documented.
  • Greater Sunrise revenue — the development promise behind the onshore word remains undelivered and undeliverable inside this term. No final investment decision exists; the buy-out completes, if approved, in the first half of 2027; Gusmão's own November 2026 target for operations commencing is 2034 — six years after this government's term ends, and inside the window in which every sourced projection has the Petroleum Fund running out (Test 5 above). The word about where the gas will be processed has been kept; the word that the project is "essential for the country's development" in time to replace the fund has not been, and on the government's own dates cannot now be.

The two axes, named: Word-keeping and helped/hurt are graded independently, and all four crossings are recorded for this tenure. (1) Kept his word AND it helped people: ASEAN and WTO accession — pledged in the programme, delivered in October 2025 and August 2024, opening a market of 680 million people on ASEAN's figures and a rules-based trade framework (ASEAN media kit, October 2025; WTO, 2024). (2) Kept his word AND it still hurt people: the onshore-processing word on Greater Sunrise was kept without deviation, and the keeping carried a documented cost — years of continued stalemate with the operator while the fund that must bridge the country to any Sunrise revenue declined, a cost ended only by the state buying the operator out in 2026 (La'o Hamutuk Greater Sunrise analysis, updated March 2026; Woodside announcement, October 2026). The keeping earns Test 5 credit; the hurt is recorded here in full. (3) Went against his word AND it helped people: the programme's word was to integrate lifetime pensions into the contributory scheme for new office-holders; what was enacted in September 2025 was outright abolition for MPs, former presidents, prime ministers, and ministers — beyond and against the letter of the programme's gradual formula — and the help is recorded in full: an insider-only lifetime benefit ended in a country with 42% poverty (Programme, July 2023; International IDEA, September 2025; World Bank, 2025). The break is recorded on this test; the help stands in the ledger. (4) Broke his word AND it hurt people: the commitment to sustainable public finances and reduced fund dependence, against a documented record of withdrawals at roughly three to three-and-a-half times the sustainable level and a rising budget envelope — the hurt falling on the next generation of Timorese, who inherit either the depleted fund or the adjustment (IMF, August 2026; budget figures above).

Why this grade: The two biggest external words of the programme — ASEAN and the WTO — were kept on schedule and in full, and the Sunrise word was kept at real cost and adapted in its means. The biggest internal word — a sustainable fiscal path that lets the Petroleum Fund outlive the transition — has been broken in the same direction every year, with the VAT that would start closing the gap still unbuilt and the fund's end dated, by his own statement, to around 2039. A record that keeps its foreign word and breaks its fiscal word is MIXED.

Helped / Hurt ledger: Helped — Timorese exporters, workers, and students who gain ASEAN and WTO frameworks (ASEAN, October 2025; WTO, August 2024); south-coast communities around the Tasi Mane project, the named intended beneficiaries of onshore processing and its promised jobs (Lusa, November 2025); all Timorese who would have paid lifetime pensions to former office-holders indefinitely (September 2025). Hurt — future Timorese taxpayers and service users, on every sourced depletion projection, if the withdrawal path is not changed (IMF, August 2026; Finance Ministry estimate of 2037; Gusmão's own 2039 figure); present-day businesses and households who would bear a VAT whenever it arrives, and who meanwhile fund under 15% of the state they use (Gomes, 2026). Not documented: any instance of Gusmão breaking a personal, dated public promise to a named individual or community — the breaks recorded here are programme-level, and the dossier does not invent smaller ones.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · His word: MIXED.

Kay Rala Xanana Gusmão returned to the premiership on 1 July 2023 with a real electoral mandate — CNRT's 41.6% and a 37-seat coalition — and the record of the three years since is genuinely two-sided on every test. On one law, his government exposed and cancelled its own police recruitment after its commission found 143 favoured candidates, yet the favouritism happened on its watch and veterans' transfers still take the largest single share of his budgets ahead of the poorest; helped: unconnected candidates given a rerun; hurt: the poorest households and the candidates made to retake tests for the state's failure. Without gripping, no referee was purged or captured — the press kept a decriminalised defamation law with his public support and President Ramos-Horta's veto threat, the fund's advisory council survived an abolition attempt, and the missing Supreme Court is being recruited by open competition — yet a denied ALLEGATION of judicial interference by Expresso, an unresolved judges' dispute in which he "only listened," and a request to legislate on cybercrime by decree keep the grade at MIXED; helped: journalists and future appellants; hurt: the transferred Dili judges, unresolved. On the wobble, he is two rulers in one: the street's demands in September 2025 and March 2026 were met in days, by law, against his own class's perks — after tear gas was fired at the people making them — while the fiscal warning, repeated by the IMF in 2024, 2025, and 2026 and owned in his own "around 2039" warning, has been answered with a larger budget every year; and on Greater Sunrise, if another option becomes available was the test, he met it — considering and taking the buy-out of Woodside and Osaka Gas rather than abandoning the onshore end he had stated. On counting everyone, the formal count widened — women at 35.4% of parliament, a disability council, an extended diaspora registration, roads and classrooms in the municipalities, an insider pension abolished — while the money still centres on wages and veterans, the poor's sectors share 18%, poverty stands at 42% and child stunting at about 47% on the last published figures, and no municipal election has yet been held. On his word, the foreign promises were kept whole — WTO in August 2024, ASEAN in October 2025, the 2029 chairmanship confirmed — and the fiscal promise was broken whole, with withdrawals at roughly three to three-and-a-half times the sustainable level, VAT unbuilt, and Sunrise revenue, on his own November 2026 target, not commencing until 2034. The open files that will move these grades are named in the dossier: whether the Supreme Court is actually established, whether VAT is enacted and the withdrawal path bends before the fund's projected late-2030s end, whether municipal elections are held, and whether the Greater Sunrise buy-out completes in the first half of 2027 and reaches a final investment decision.
