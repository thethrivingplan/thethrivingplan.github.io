The Ruler's Test — Ferdinand Marcos Jr., President of the Philippines

Subject: Ferdinand "Bongbong" Marcos Jr. — President of the Philippines since 30 June 2022; previously Senator (2010–2016), Governor and Congressman of Ilocos Norte, and vice-presidential candidate in 2016. He won the May 2022 election with 58.9% of the vote, in tandem with Vice President Sara Duterte, from whom he is now estranged. He is the son of Ferdinand Marcos Sr., who ruled from 1965 until his ouster in 1986, and of Imelda Marcos. He is graded on his own record in office; his family's record is set out separately, at exact strength, under Test 1, because it concerns the same question that test asks — whether the law reaches the source. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He opened the largest corruption case of his presidency against his own coalition, in public, himself. In his State of the Nation Address of 28 July 2025, Marcos told Congress that flood-control projects were "failures… or existing just in the imagination," named the kickback vocabulary of the system ("kickbacks, initiative, errata, SOP, 'for the boys'"), and said, in Filipino, mahiya naman kayo — shame on you — to the officials and contractors responsible, ordering the Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH) to publish three years of project records (SONA transcript; Presidential Communications Office, 28 July 2025). In August 2025 he personally named the 15 contractors that had taken roughly 20% of some ₱545 billion in flood-control spending since July 2022, and launched a public reporting portal ("Sumbong sa Pangulo").
  • The investigative machinery he created reached his own family. The Independent Commission for Infrastructure (ICI), created by his Executive Order No. 94 on 11 September 2025 and chaired by a retired Supreme Court justice, recommended charges against sitting senators of his own coalition; the National Bureau of Investigation recommended corruption charges against former House Speaker Martin Romualdez — the president's first cousin and closest legislative ally — and on 7 September 2026 the Ombudsman filed a ₱7.4-billion plunder case against Romualdez (Ombudsman filings as reported by GMA News, Philstar and the Daily Tribune, September 2026).
  • By the Ombudsman's own first-anniversary accounting (13 October 2026), 39 flood-control cases had been filed in court against 52 individuals — an incumbent senator, a former senator, four congressmen including the former Speaker, a former Cabinet secretary, 33 DPWH personnel and eight contractors (Office of the Ombudsman press release, 13 October 2026). Senator Jinggoy Estrada and former DPWH Secretary Manuel Bonoan were charged with plunder in May 2026 and surrendered when warrants issued on the non-bailable counts; former senator Ramon Revilla was charged in January 2026; contractor Sarah Discaya was charged in December 2025; bail was denied to nine former DPWH officials in the first case and the denial affirmed on 31 March 2026 (Sandiganbayan records as reported).
  • The law was also allowed to run against his predecessor's machinery and in favor of his predecessor's most prominent prisoner. Former senator Leila de Lima, jailed for nearly seven years on drug charges brought under President Duterte after she investigated his drug war, was granted bail in November 2023 and acquitted of the last of the three cases on 24 June 2024; his Justice Department's motion for reconsideration was denied and the acquittal stood (Muntinlupa RTC Branch 206, June–August 2024; Human Rights Watch, 25 June 2024).
  • A former president was arrested on an international warrant and surrendered to the International Criminal Court on 11 March 2025 (see Test 2 for the process dispute) — the law, whatever its defects of route, was applied upward to the most powerful man of the previous administration rather than suspended for him.

Evidence against:

  • The consequence gradient runs downhill. The first people charged, detained and denied bail were district engineers and contractors; the first Sandiganbayan filing came on 18 November 2025, nearly four months after the SONA promise that charges would be filed "in the following months," and after more than 100 days in which, as Asia Sentinel noted, nobody had been jailed at all (Asia Sentinel, November 2025). As of November 2026, thirteen-plus months after the exposure, the flood-control docket had produced zero convictions (case-status reviews, November 2026), and the nearly ₱25 billion in assets the president announced as "recovered, frozen, or preserved" in his 27 July 2026 SONA is, by the government's own reckoning, under 2% of the roughly ₱1.9–2 trillion in flood spending under scrutiny — with only about ₱800 million actually returned to the treasury (SONA 2026; World Bank and press tallies as reported).
  • The case against his cousin weakened in public view before it was filed. On 8 August 2026, four former Romualdez security aides who had testified to carrying cash on his behalf recanted their statements; the Ombudsman said the recantations bore the fingerprints of "Romualdez's camp" and asked the NBI to investigate (Philstar, August 2026). Romualdez himself remained uncharged for thirteen months after resigning the speakership, while the Ombudsman's assessment named him an alleged recipient of up to ₱56 billion in kickbacks — an ALLEGATION, which he denies, and which only the September 2026 plunder filing began to test in court.
  • The accusation that reaches the president himself is on the record and unanswered in substance. Former congressman Elizaldy "Zaldy" Co — the appropriations chairman who fled the country and was himself charged in November 2025 — released videos from hiding in November 2025 alleging that Marcos and Romualdez ordered some ₱100 billion in insertions into the 2025 budget and that ₱25 billion in kickbacks reached the president. This is an ALLEGATION by a fugitive co-accused, contested as to timeline and evidence by House leaders, and it is unproven; the documented fact graded here is the response: the Palace declined to "dignify" it, no independent body outside the president's own executive has jurisdiction over it in practice, and Co's passport was cancelled on 10 December 2025 while he remained abroad (contemporaneous reporting, November–December 2025).
  • The anti-graft prosecutor is his own man. In October 2025 Marcos appointed his sitting Justice Secretary, Jesus Crispin Remulla, as Ombudsman for a seven-year term extending beyond his presidency — an appointment the opposition and his own sister, Senator Imee Marcos, publicly questioned on independence grounds; Remulla's brother serves simultaneously as his Interior Secretary. Remulla's office has in fact filed against the president's cousin (above), which is credited on the for-shelf; the structural point stands that the official charged with prosecuting the president's circle owes the office to the president (appointment records; Senate statements, October 2025).
  • The Office of the President itself took ₱4.5 billion in confidential and intelligence funds in the 2026 budget — more than a third of the government's total pool — funds exempt from ordinary audit, in the same budget cycle in which confidential funds were the stated vice of the Vice President his allies impeached (2026 General Appropriations Act, as analyzed by BusinessWorld, November 2026).

The family record, stated exactly as documented. It belongs to this test because it asks whether the law reaches the ruler's own house.

  • Documented findings: Philippine courts, including the Supreme Court in 2003, 2012 and 2017, have ruled that the Marcoses hold ill-gotten wealth; the Supreme Court ordered some US$658 million in Swiss deposits forfeited to the state. The Presidential Commission on Good Government (PCGG) estimates the family and its associates amassed US$5–10 billion during Marcos Sr.'s rule, and reports having recovered about ₱280 billion in cash, shares, real estate and other assets as of 2023 (PCGG audit reports; Sandiganbayan and Supreme Court records). Imelda Marcos was convicted by the Sandiganbayan on seven counts of graft in November 2018; the conviction is on appeal and she has not been imprisoned.
  • The estate tax: The Bureau of Internal Revenue assessed the Marcos Sr. estate ₱23.29 billion in estate taxes on 26 July 1991. The assessment became final and unappealable when no protest was filed; the Supreme Court upheld the collection measures in Marcos II v. Court of Appeals on 5 June 1997, and the judgment became final and executory on 9 March 1999. With interest and penalties the liability is computed at about ₱203.8 billion. The BIR sent the heirs a written demand on 2 December 2021. As of November 2026 — with the BIR headed by the president's own appointees — the tax remains unpaid, and the coalition 1Sambayan was publicly urging the BIR to act before 2 December 2026, warning of a prescription deadline the coalition says is approaching (BIR and PCGG statements; Supreme Court records; Philippine Star reporting, November 2026).
  • The president's position, and its check: In a September 2022 interview, Marcos Jr. said he wanted the estate-tax case reopened because his family "wasn't given a chance to respond," being in exile. Fact-checkers rated the claim false: the Supreme Court's own decision records that levy notices were served on him personally in 1993 at his office in Congress, where he was then a sitting representative, and that he was himself the petitioner who litigated the case (VERA Files and Rappler fact checks of the Supreme Court record).
  • Cases dying of inaction on his watch: On 18 February 2025 the Sandiganbayan dismissed an ill-gotten-wealth suit against his parents because the PCGG — an agency within his executive — "has failed to prosecute the case for an unreasonable length of time," its inaction spanning "more than four decades" (Sandiganbayan Second Division, 18 February 2025). On 2 June 2026 the same court dismissed the remaining claims in Civil Case No. 0141, the 1991 forfeiture action, after the PCGG opted to present no further evidence; the dismissal leaves all earlier forfeiture judgments intact (Sandiganbayan Second Division, 2 June 2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the Ombudsman, media, the central bank, local governments, and the military keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The Supreme Court ruled against his House allies' central political project, unanimously, and was obeyed. On 25 July 2025 the Court en banc declared the Articles of Impeachment against Vice President Sara Duterte unconstitutional — barred by the Constitution's one-year rule and defective on due process — in a decision penned by Senior Associate Justice Leonen (G.R. Nos. 278353 and 278359). The House, led by his cousin, complied; the Court denied the House's motion for reconsideration with finality in January 2026. When a second impeachment was filed after the bar lapsed on 6 February 2026, the House impeached the Vice President again on 11 May 2026 (257–25–9) and the Senate trial opened on 6 July 2026 — a process the executive has not interrupted, and which, as of late November 2026, continues on a single remaining article (unexplained wealth) with no verdict (Senate impeachment court records).
  • The same Court, on 11 November 2026, dismissed as moot the habeas corpus petitions over the Duterte arrest — but expressly stated that dismissal "does not mean that Duterte's arrest and transfer were necessarily lawful or that his constitutional rights were not violated," and five justices wrote dissents arguing in whole or part that his rights were violated, one calling the transfer "an extraordinary rendition" (Supreme Court en banc, G.R. Nos. 278763, 278768, 278798, promulgated 11 November 2026). A judiciary willing to publish that against a sitting president's signature act is not a captured one.
  • Courts ruled for the government's critics in the open: De Lima's acquittal (Test 1); the Supreme Court's May 2024 ruling in Deduro v. Vinoya recognizing red-tagging as a threat to life, liberty and security; a Quezon City court's April 2024 award of ₱2 million in damages to journalist Atom Araullo over red-tagging by former officials.
  • No media outlet has been shut down by his government, and the measured climate improved: the Philippines rose from 134th (2024) to 116th (2025) and 114th (2026) in the RSF World Press Freedom Index, with RSF crediting in part an administration "less aggressive towards the media" than its predecessor and a year in the index period with no journalist killed (RSF, 2025–2026). The Senate ousted his ally Senate President Escudero on 8 September 2025 and his cousin resigned the speakership nine days later, both without executive interference; local governments run by opponents, including Duterte-held Davao, operate unmolested; the central bank's independence is not documented as interfered with — when the Bangko Sentral objected to contributing its dividends to the Maharlika sovereign fund during the 2023 legislative process, the provision was dropped from the final law.
  • The armed forces stayed in barracks through the largest protest season of his presidency and publicly affirmed constitutional loyalty; no officer is documented as purged for refusing a political order.

Evidence against:

  • The signature act of executive speed: on 11 March 2025 former President Duterte was arrested on arrival in Manila on an ICC warrant coursed through Interpol and flown to The Hague the same night, without being brought before any Philippine judge. The government's stated basis was Section 17 of Republic Act No. 9851 and its Interpol obligations. Former Justice Adolfo Azcuna Jr. testified to the Senate that the warrant was legal but the surrender violated the process the Rome Statute's Article 59 required — a judicial determination by the custodial state that was never held. The Supreme Court's majority ultimately resolved the challenge on mootness without deciding legality; the five dissenting justices' position is recorded on the for-shelf as evidence of judicial independence, and the underlying executive fact — a citizen transferred to a foreign tribunal within hours, faster than any court could act — is recorded here (Senate Foreign Relations Committee hearings, March–April 2025; Supreme Court decision and dissents, November 2026).
  • The executive shielded itself from legislative inquiry into that act: by letter of 31 March 2025, Executive Secretary Bersamin informed the Senate that Cabinet members would no longer attend Senator Imee Marcos's hearings on the arrest, invoking executive privilege after one appearance (Senate records, March–April 2025).
  • The flood-control investigation was built as an instrument of the presidency rather than independent of it: the ICI is an executive-order body whose members he appointed, which reports to him monthly, and whose subpoena and freeze powers are recommendatory to his own agencies. Its special adviser, Baguio City Mayor Benjamin Magalong, resigned within weeks in September 2025; commissioner Rogelio Singson resigned effective 15 December 2025 citing stress and "security risks"; commissioner Rossana Fajardo also stepped down by year-end; the body wound up its work and turned its files over to the Ombudsman and Justice Department with its core referrals against the most powerful figures still at the recommendation stage (ICI statements and contemporaneous reporting, September–December 2025).
  • The instruments of the previous administration's pressure on critics remain standing and funded under him: the NTF-ELCAC, the state's red-tagging apparatus, continues; monitors counted at least 184 attacks and threats against media workers from July 2022 to April 2025 — including 48 red-tagging cases — a higher count than the comparable Duterte-era tally, even as killings of journalists fell (Center for Media Freedom and Responsibility figures, as reported, May 2025). ABS-CBN, shuttered under Duterte, has never had its franchise restored by a House his family dominated; RSF has flagged as "even more worrying" the media holdings and ABS-CBN joint venture of his cousin Romualdez's Prime Media (RSF Philippines country file, 2026).
  • The Ombudsman's appointment (Test 1) is also a grip fact: the constitutional officer tasked with prosecuting presidential allies is the president's former Justice Secretary, appointed mid-scandal, over stated independence objections.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the Senate, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • He took the midterm rebuke on the record and reorganized around it. After his Alyansa slate won only six of twelve Senate seats on 12 May 2025 — the weakest showing for an administration ticket since 2007, with five seats going to Duterte-aligned candidates — Marcos publicly read the result as a verdict on his governance ("The people have spoken and they expect results — not politics, not excuses. We hear them and we will act") and on 22 May 2025 directed every Cabinet secretary and agency head to submit courtesy resignations for a performance reset (Palace statements, May 2025).
  • On the flood scandal, the adjustments were structural, not only rhetorical: he had already vetoed ₱16.7 billion of flood-control items in the 2025 budget he signed; after the SONA he accepted DPWH Secretary Bonoan's resignation (1 September 2025) and ordered a full organizational sweep under Secretary Vince Dizon; locally funded flood-control allocations were removed from the 2026 budget while the cleanup ran; the DPWH's proposed 2026 budget was cut by some ₱255 billion; and the department opened a public transparency portal listing 247,172 projects worth ₱6.359 trillion since 2016, searchable by any citizen (DPWH and budget records, 2025–2026).
  • He accepted the resignations — announced 17 November 2025 — of his Executive Secretary and Budget Secretary as the scandal's allegations reached their offices, and declared a one-year state of national calamity on 6 November 2025 after Typhoon Kalmaegi (Tino) killed at least 253 people, most heavily in Cebu, where flood infrastructure that should have been operational was not (Palace statements; disaster agency tallies, November 2025).
  • The protest movement was policed, not banned: the Trillion Peso March of 21 September 2025 and the Bonifacio Day rallies of 30 November 2025 — tens of thousands, some calling for his resignation — proceeded in the open, and the president publicly framed the anti-corruption anger as legitimate rather than seditious.

Evidence against:

  • The September 21 march ended with clashes in Manila in which over two hundred people were arrested and one person died (organizers' and police accounts as reported) — the state's answer to the largest protest of his term included mass arrest at its edge, and no police accountability for the death is documented in the record reviewed.
  • The adjustment on accountability lagged the harm and the promise alike: 100-plus days without a single jailing after the SONA; his own commission losing members to resignation and "security risks" while its mandate ran; and, in the Bersamin affair, a public account that did not hold — the Palace announced the Executive Secretary's resignation "out of delicadeza," and Bersamin, a former chief justice, then told reporters he "did not resign," that he had sent only a standard courtesy letter placing his post at the president's disposal, and that the announcement was made without consulting him (MNLToday and Palace statements, November 2025). Whether the exit was voluntary is CONTESTED between the two; the handling is documented.
  • On the ICC, the adjustment came as a reversal executed by fait accompli: from 2023 through November 2024 his stated position was flat — "We do not cooperate with the ICC. That is the position of this government" — before his officials developed the Interpol-comity route (Executive Secretary Bersamin, January 2025: Manila would "respond favorably" to an Interpol request) and then carried out the arrest and same-night transfer in March 2025 without prior public notice or domestic judicial process. The new position was never announced as a change of position; it was presented as continuity (presidential and Palace statements, 2023–2025).
  • On the drug war, the response to documented continued killings (Test 4) has been denial at the operational level: the sitting PNP chief publicly disputed that drug-related killings involving police or unidentified assailants have continued under Marcos at all — a claim contradicted by the government's own drug-enforcement agency figure of 271 people killed in anti-drug operations over three years, cited in the same exchange (PNP and PDEA figures as reported by VERA Files, 2025–2026). No review of the Duterte-era police memoranda governing the campaign — never rescinded, and thus still in force — has been ordered.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the poor, flood victims, drug-war victims' families, his opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The signature promise reached the poor in documented numbers, in subsidized form. The "Benteng Bigas, Meron Na!" program began selling ₱20-per-kilo rice on 2 May 2025 through government outlets to designated vulnerable sectors — indigent families, senior citizens, solo parents, persons with disabilities, minimum-wage earners, later transport workers, farmers, fisherfolk and teachers — capped at 30 kilos per household per month, with the state absorbing the difference through above-market purchases from Filipino farmers. The Agriculture Department reports 5.6 million beneficiaries from January to September 2026, a record 961,346 in September 2026 alone, and ₱18 billion allocated to expand toward a target of 60 million people (Department of Agriculture releases, 2025–2026).
  • Flood victims were answered with a national calamity declaration, a dedicated clearing operation (Oplan Kontra Baha, November 2025), and the accountability machinery of Tests 1–3 — an official admission, rare in the record of this series, that the state's own failure was the disaster's multiplier.
  • The families of the drug war's dead received the one act of accounting they had demanded for a decade: the arrest and surrender of the former president who ordered the campaign, now facing the ICC — alongside the release and acquittal of the senator jailed for investigating it (Tests 1–2).
  • His opponents' voters were counted in the ordinary way: the 2025 midterm results that cut his slate to six seats were proclaimed without dispute, Duterte-aligned senators took their seats, and the second Duterte impeachment proceeded through constitutional forms rather than around them.

Evidence against:

  • The drug war's population is still being killed, and the state declines to count it. The Dahas Project of the University of the Philippines Third World Studies Center — which compiles drug-related killings from news and police reports after the government stopped publishing its own figures — documented at least 1,008 drug-related killings from July 2022 to June 2025, 269 in calendar 2025 alone, and 1,291 by early July 2026, with state agents responsible for roughly 36% of the total and police for most of those. The victims are drawn from the same poor communities targeted under Duterte; three of those killed in 2025 were minors; and the highest number of victims with no known drug ties in the project's records was recorded in 2025 (Dahas Project tallies, 2025–2026; Human Rights Watch, June 2026). The president's own description of his campaign, from his July 2024 SONA, is "bloodless" — a characterization the project's founder calls "pure propaganda," and one the PNP chief defended by denying the killings occur, against his own enforcement agency's count of 271 dead in operations (VERA Files, 2026). A count that the state refuses to publish, of people it claims do not exist, is the definition of this test's failure.
  • The flood theft was taken from the most exposed. The Ombudsman states that more than ₱100 billion was paid for flood-control projects that were never constructed (Ombudsman press release, 13 October 2026); Senate investigators estimated losses on a far larger scale over fifteen years. The ghost projects were concentrated in flood-prone, low-income provinces — Bulacan above all — and the bill came due in the water: in November 2025 Typhoon Tino killed at least 253 people in a single storm, heaviest in Cebu, where 511 flood projects were on the books as completed or ongoing. A Sandiganbayan-ordered inspection of one indicted project in Pandi, Bulacan found no evidence the ₱92.8-million structure had ever existed (court-ordered inspection as reported, 2026).
  • The ₱20 promise, as campaigned, was for everyone; as delivered, it is a ration. Market rice retailed at ₱42–50 per kilo through 2025 (Agriculture Department monitoring) — the promise price exists only inside government outlets, only for listed categories of the poor, only up to 30 kilos a month. The near-poor who fall outside the beneficiary lists are counted out of the administration's flagship act of inclusion.
  • The protest dead and arrested of 21 September 2025 (Test 3) belong to this count as well: citizens who marched on the precise grievance the president himself had named were met, at the march's edge, with mass arrest and one death, unaccounted for in any finding in the record reviewed.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Campaign promises vs. the record since June 2022, with dates.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • ₱20 rice — delivered late, narrowed, but delivered and sustained. The 2022 campaign's signature pledge was dismissed by economists as impossible; a ₱20-per-kilo program has operated continuously since May 2025, is funded in the 2026 budget, and the president has committed to sustaining it to the end of his term in June 2028 (Agriculture Department records). Its narrowed, subsidized form is graded under Test 4; as a kept appointment with his own promise, it stands.
  • Flood accountability — the promise was his, and the docket exists. "In the following months, charges will be filed against all those found guilty" (SONA, 28 July 2025): the first case was filed on 18 November 2025, and by October 2026 the Ombudsman counted 39 cases against 52 accused, including — as Marcos forecast in his 27 July 2026 SONA, warning that his cousin "could soon be jailed" — a plunder case against Romualdez, filed 7 September 2026. The promise's completion (convictions) has not occurred; its machinery has.
  • Institutional repair items: the DPWH transparency portal, the removal of flood-control lines from the 2026 budget pending cleanup, and the prosecution of his own administration's officials are the follow-through of the 2025 SONA's specific undertakings, delivered in the forms he described.
  • He kept the forms of his democratic word: elections held on schedule and their adverse results accepted (Test 3); the constitutional bar on a third term nowhere tested; the second Duterte impeachment left to Congress and the courts after the Supreme Court voided the first — a ruling against his own allies' timetable that he did not contest.

Evidence against (promises not kept, or reversed):

  • "Bloodless." In his July 2024 SONA Marcos promised an anti-drug campaign that would be "bloodless" and focused on rehabilitation and prevention. The documented count since is in Test 4: at least 1,291 drug-related killings by July 2026 on the university monitors' tally, 271 of them acknowledged by the drug-enforcement agency itself as killed in operations. The word was not kept, and the administration's response has been to contest the counting rather than the fact.
  • The ICC reversal. Through 2023 and most of 2024 he stated, repeatedly and without qualification, that his government would not cooperate with the ICC — "We do not cooperate with the ICC. That is the position of this government" (November 2024). In March 2025 his government arrested and surrendered the ICC's principal accused. The Interpol-comity rationale was articulated by his officials from late 2024, and a president may change a policy; what the record shows is a flat public commitment reversed without ever being publicly withdrawn.
  • The estate tax. Whatever the family's legal theories, the president of a republic whose tax judgment against his own estate has been final since 1999 has now presided for over four years over a Bureau of Internal Revenue that has not collected it — while telling the public, inaccurately on the Supreme Court's own record, that his family was never heard (Test 1). His word on the subject and the record diverge at the exact point where his interest begins.
  • The general price of rice and the cost of the feud. The campaign implied ₱20 rice as a market condition achievable through production; four years in, the market price remains roughly double, the subsidy carries the difference for a listed minority, and the public verdict on the gap is measurable: his trust rating stood at 32% in May 2025 polling, and late-2025 surveys recorded "much trust" at 38% against "little trust" at 41%, with satisfaction at a record low — while the Vice President he broke with climbed to 56% trust over the same window (Pulse Asia and Social Weather Stations, as reported). A ruler's word is also graded by whether the flock still believes it; on the documented numbers, a majority no longer takes his at face value.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Ferdinand Marcos Jr. did the rarest thing in this series: he indicted his own government, by name and in its own vocabulary, from the podium — and then built machinery that has, in fact, reached his cousin, his cabinet secretaries, his coalition's senators and his predecessor, while courts ruled against his allies' impeachment and published dissents against his signature arrest, all unimpeded. That is a real record, and it is why no test here fails on grip or law outright. But the same record shows the gradient that runs through every file: consequence that lands first and hardest on district engineers, a cousin uncharged for thirteen months while witnesses recanted, a commission of his own creation expiring into referrals, zero convictions thirteen months on, and a tax judgment against his own family — final since 1999 — that his revenue service does not collect. Above all, the count fails where it is most literal: more than a thousand drug-related killings on the university monitors' tally, of the same poor communities as before, under a president who calls his campaign "bloodless" while his government has stopped publishing the numbers. He counts the poor at the rice outlet and the flood victim in the calamity declaration; he does not count the dead his police chief says do not exist. The tests measure the seat, not the intentions, and on the evidence assembled here the seat exposes corruption upward further than any in this series — and stops, each time, one ring short of the center.
