The Ruler's Test — Ahmed al-Sharaa, President of Syria

Subject: Ahmed al-Sharaa — President of Syria since 29 January 2025, when he was appointed by the Syrian General Command at the Revolution Victory Conference in Damascus; de facto leader of Syria from 8 December 2024, when the government he led as head of Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham (HTS) took Damascus and Bashar al-Assad fled to Russia, where he remains. Born Ahmed Hussein al-Sharaa on 29 October 1982 in Riyadh; known for two decades by the nom de guerre Abu Mohammad al-Julani. He remains in office in November 2026, presiding over a five-year transitional order of his own design. His international standing changed completely on his watch: the United States terminated its Syria sanctions program by executive order on 30 June 2025, revoked HTS's foreign-terrorist-organization designation effective 8 July 2025, and repealed the Caesar Act on 18 December 2025; UN Security Council Resolution 2799 (6 November 2025) removed al-Sharaa and his interior minister from the ISIL/al-Qaida sanctions list; he was received at the White House on 10 November 2025 — the first Syrian head of state ever to be — met President Putin in Moscow on 15 October 2025, and on 24 August 2026 the United States rescinded Syria's State Sponsor of Terrorism designation. Those are facts of diplomacy; the tests below grade the seat at home. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that he passes, then the strongest evidence that he fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The pledge was made in terms, at the moment it cost the most. On 10 March 2025, with the coastal killings still unfolding, al-Sharaa told Reuters: "Syria is a state of law. The law will take its course on all. We fought to defend the oppressed, and we won't accept that any blood be shed unjustly, or goes without punishment or accountability, even among those closest to us."
  • He built the instrument he pledged. By Presidential Decree No. 3 of 9 March 2025 he appointed a national fact-finding committee on the coastal violence, and its report of 22 July 2025 counted 1,426 dead including 90 women and named 298 alleged perpetrators within military factions and 265 linked to former-government groups, referring the files to the judiciary (committee summary as reported, 22 July 2025). The perpetrators named from his own side are ALLEGATIONs referred for trial, not verdicts; the referral itself is the documented act.
  • Trials followed on both sides of the line. The first public trial of the coastal events opened in Aleppo on 18 November 2025 with 14 defendants — seven described as former-regime "remnants" and seven pro-government combatants accused of murder and theft. In September–October 2026 verdicts included a death sentence, life imprisonment, a 20-year term, and an acquittal; on 15 October 2026 the Aleppo Military Criminal Court sentenced two former-regime men to death and convicted serving Ministry of Defence members — one soldier of the 82nd Division receiving 20 years for intentional killing, another three years for torture — with four ministry members sentenced in the October 2026 proceedings in total (trial reporting; CHS Doha paper, 16 November 2026).
  • The investigative door was opened to outsiders, not just to his own committee. In June 2025 his government granted the UN Commission of Inquiry unfettered access, including to mass-grave sites — access the Assad government had refused the Commission for fourteen years (UN Commission of Inquiry report A/HRC/59/CRP.4, 11 August 2025).
  • The transitional-justice architecture was built facing the old regime's crimes with real instruments: Presidential Decrees 19 and 20 of 17 May 2025 created the National Commission for Transitional Justice (NCTJ) and the National Commission for the Missing and Forcibly Disappeared; the Assad-era Counterterrorism Court was suspended in June 2025; and in April 2026 a Damascus criminal court opened the first domestic trial of a senior Assad-era official, invoking the Geneva Conventions and customary international law (Amnesty International and HRW submissions to the UN Universal Periodic Review, July 2026; Syria Justice and Accountability Centre trackers).

Evidence against:

  • The law stops at the level of the men who pulled triggers — and his own committee said so in structure if not in words. The fact-finding committee found no evidence that military leaders ordered the coastal attacks; the joint HRW / Syrians for Truth and Justice / Syrian Archive investigation "Are You Alawi?" (23 September 2025) found that the coastal operation was centrally coordinated by the Ministry of Defense — mobilization, zone assignments, and continued coordination after the abuses were public — while also finding no direct orders to commit atrocities. Between those two findings sits the unprosecuted space: coordination documented, command responsibility never charged. The committee's full report was never published; only a summary was released (Amnesty International UPR submission, July 2026).
  • The clearest favorite at the source has a name and a file. Mohammad al-Jassim, known as "Abu Amsha," was sanctioned by the US Treasury in August 2023 for documented abuses in Afrin — extortion, forced displacement of Kurds, ransom kidnappings (US Treasury statement). On 2 February 2025 — five weeks into the new order — al-Sharaa's government promoted him to brigadier general and gave him command of the new army's 62nd Division. His faction was then named by the UN Commission of Inquiry among the units whose members committed violations on the coast, and the European Union sanctioned him and his faction on 28 May 2025 for involvement in the coastal massacres. He has never been prosecuted. In August 2026 he was moved sideways to deputy commander of the Central Region Corps — a sidelining dressed as a promotion, in SOHR-linked reporting — his division taken from him but no charge laid. The pledge of 10 March 2025 named exactly this case: "even among those closest to us." The record shows promotion, then transfer, never law.
  • Amnesty by his own hand undid accountability where it touched his own forces. The UN Commission of Inquiry's Suwayda report (27 March 2026) documented that several suspects arrested over the July 2025 Suwayda killings were released under a February 2026 amnesty issued by al-Sharaa himself; a second amnesty wave followed by presidential decree in March 2026 (SJAC). For Suwayda, the only consequence any commander is documented to have received is a transfer: the province's internal security chief was moved to a similar post in Rif Dimashq (UN Commission of Inquiry, March 2026).
  • The other door of one law — clemency for the old regime's connected — opened without legal process. In June 2025 the Supreme Committee for Preserving Civil Peace released some 300 former military and security officers without clear legal procedure (SJAC). Reporting by Reuters and DW identified the releases as brokered with the involvement of Fadi Saqr, a former National Defense Forces commander in Tadamon who is US- and EU-blacklisted over wartime massacres and who sits on the Civil Peace Committee itself; Saqr denies any role in the 2013 Tadamon massacre and says he took command after it, and committee member Hassan Soufan defended the releases publicly on 10 June 2025 as preventing bloodshed. Victims' families and figures from the Caesar-files investigations protested in public. Whatever the security logic, it is not one law: connected officers walked out by committee decision while unconnected detainees waited for courts.
  • The transitional-justice mandate itself was drawn to exclude his own side's victims. HRW criticized the NCTJ's founding decree as "troublingly narrow" — framed around Assad-regime crimes in a way that excludes many victims, including victims of abuses by the authorities who replaced him (HRW, 2025). A law that by design reaches only the defeated is not one law.
  • The pattern continued after the massacres: the UN Commission of Inquiry documented ongoing Alawite abductions, arrests, and looting after March 2025 — the community the law was pledged to protect "even among those closest to us" continued to be preyed on by men the law did not reach.

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, the legislature, the media, the prosecution, and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The founding text promises the right things on paper. The Constitutional Declaration signed on 13 March 2025 guarantees the rights of all ethnic and religious groups, judicial independence, and freedoms of expression and the press (EUAA summary of the Declaration).
  • Expression space has genuinely widened against the baseline he inherited. The UK Home Office's country policy note (April 2026) assessed that space for expression in Syria has expanded significantly compared with the Assad era, that arrests of critics are selective and inconsistent, and that available information does not indicate a systematic policy of detaining critics.
  • The central bank's first governor under the new order was a landmark appointment — Maysaa Sabreen, the first woman to head the bank in its more than seventy years, named acting governor on 30 December 2024 — and she publicly foreshadowed draft amendments intended to foster the bank's independence (POMEPS). No such independence law has been enacted; the foreshadowing is recorded here at its exact weight.
  • The People's Assembly that finally convened on 12 July 2026 is not a monolith on paper: of its roughly 206 seated members, 22 are women (10.7%), and the elected portion includes Kurdish, Christian, and two Alawite representatives (IPU and election reporting).

Evidence against:

  • The grip is constitutional, not incidental. The Constitutional Declaration of 13 March 2025 establishes a presidential system with no prime minister; the president appoints all ministers, the vice presidents, and the judges of the Higher Constitutional Court; the president directly selects one-third of the People's Assembly and the remaining members are chosen through electoral colleges supervised by a committee the president forms; the legislature cannot question or remove the president; and the president may declare a state of emergency for up to three months with the approval of a National Security Council that he himself appoints (Constitutional Declaration, as summarized by the EUAA). Every referee in the system is appointed by, or answerable to, the seat being graded.
  • The legislature was built accordingly. The People's Assembly election of 5 October 2025 was indirect: 6,817 electoral-college members chose among 1,578 candidates for 140 seats, of which 119 were filled that day; voting was postponed in Suwayda, Raqqa, and Hasakah, and by-elections through May 2026 brought the elected total to 137. On 1 July 2026 al-Sharaa appointed the remaining 70 members personally, including 15 women. Suwayda's three seats remain vacant — the province is outside his government's control and unrepresented in his legislature. The Assembly's term is thirty months, renewable (IPU; election reporting, 2025–2026).
  • The central bank is a presidential appointment, exercised repeatedly. Sabreen resigned on 27 March 2025 after roughly three months and returned to her prior post as first deputy governor; her successor, appointed by the president and sworn in on 7 April 2025, served until 15 May 2026, when Mohammed Safwat Raslan was appointed governor by Presidential Decree 99/2026. Three governors in seventeen months, each holding office at the president's pleasure, and the independence amendments Sabreen foreshadowed never enacted.
  • The Assad era's speech law was kept and used. The Cybercrime Law No. 20 of 2022 — the old regime's instrument — remains in force under the new one. Amnesty International documented five journalists and activists detained between January and June 2026, held up to seven days, for online expression under provisions against "undermining the prestige of the state" and "electronic defamation"; two face prosecution. Content creator Hassan Akkad was arrested on 17 June 2026 on a defamation complaint and released after four days following public pressure; the Information Ministry then added six prohibited content categories, including "attacks on institutional reputations" (Al Jumhuriya, July 2026). The Justice Ministry announced a review of the law on 21 June 2026 with some limitations on its use; the law remains in force. During the March 2025 coastal violence, journalists were barred from the area and some who entered were arrested and forced to delete footage (UK Home Office CPIN, April 2026).
  • The prosecution and transitional-justice bodies exist by his decree and within mandates he set — including the NCTJ's narrow framing documented in Test 1 — and the fact-finding committee on the coast reported to the president, who released only its summary.

The subject's armed past, stated exactly as documented. It belongs to this test because the institutions above were built by the movement he led, and its commanders now staff them.

  • Documented: Al-Sharaa joined al-Qaeda in Iraq shortly before the 2003 US invasion (biographical record, Wikipedia and standard references). He founded Jabhat al-Nusra as al-Qaeda's Syrian affiliate and led it through the civil war's first years. In July 2016 he publicly broke with al-Qaeda, renaming the organization Jabhat Fateh al-Sham; in January 2017 it merged into Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham (HTS), which governed Idlib province through the Syrian Salvation Government until the fall of Assad. HTS was designated a terrorist organization by the United States and others during those years; the designations were revoked in 2025 as recorded above. On 29 January 2025, as he had promised in December 2024, HTS and all other armed factions were formally dissolved.
  • His own statements: From December 2024 onward he presented the break with al-Qaeda as genuine and complete, pledged that Syria under his leadership would protect its religious and ethnic minorities and threaten no one, and repeated those pledges through 2025 (AFP/Reuters reporting, December 2024 onward). The break itself is a documented organizational fact; its sincerity has been argued both ways by analysts and is not something this record can grade — what can be graded is what the institutions built by the movement's veterans do, which is the evidence above.

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, communities, the street, or events push back, does he adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The first wobble was answered with machinery within days. As the coastal violence burned, al-Sharaa created the fact-finding committee on 9 March 2025, made the accountability pledge to Reuters on 10 March, admitted the UN Commission of Inquiry with unfettered access in June 2025, received his committee's report with 563 names referred to the judiciary in July 2025, and saw public trials open that November (Tests 1 and 5). A ruler who intended only denial does not usually invite the UN's investigators to his mass graves.
  • On Suwayda, the second investigation was published whole. The Justice Ministry's investigative committee, established 31 July 2025, published its report in full on 17 March 2026, finding deliberate killings, torture, looting, and sectarian incitement by a range of actors including military and security forces, and sent its evidence to the public prosecutor (committee report, March 2026).
  • He signed the adjustment framework for Suwayda: the thirteen-point roadmap agreed by Syria, Jordan, and the United States on 17 September 2025, committing Damascus to the return of displaced residents, the release of detainees and abductees, an investigation of the July 2025 events, and prosecution of those responsible (roadmap text and US statements).
  • The street was, at least once, protected rather than dispersed. When Alawite protesters filled the streets of Latakia, Tartus, Baniyas, Jableh, and Homs on 25 November 2025 — called by Sheikh Ghazal Ghazal of the Supreme Alawite Islamic Council to demand federalism, the release of detainees, and an end to kidnappings — the Interior Ministry stated that security forces safeguarded the gatherings and upheld the right to express opinions within the law. Rival pro-government crowds gathered nearby and gunfire was reported in Latakia; officials said shots were fired into the air to separate the crowds, while Reuters-verified video showed a man motionless with a head wound and officials said unknown assailants had also fired — the sequence is CONTESTED between those accounts, and no mass arrest of the protesters is documented.
  • Pushed by the Kurdish file's collapse into war in January 2026, he adjusted beyond his own opening position: Presidential Decree No. 13 of 16 January 2026 (Test 4) conceded citizenship, language status, and a national holiday that the SDF had sought for decades, and the January agreements that followed ended the fighting and were carried through to the SDF's announced dissolution in August 2026.

Evidence against:

  • The first response to the coast was a frame, not an inquiry. In his 8 March 2025 speech — before any investigation — al-Sharaa described the violence as the fallen regime's remnants making an "unforgivable mistake" and declared "the riposte has come." The investigative machinery followed, but the verdict-shaped framing came first, and the committee's full report has never been published — only the summary the presidency chose to release (Amnesty International UPR submission, July 2026).
  • Suwayda's adjustment stopped at paper. The July 2025 operation itself — government forces entering the province on 15 July alongside tribal fighters, in what the UN Commission of Inquiry later found was a single joint operation — produced roughly 1,700 dead (Test 4). The roadmap he signed in September 2025 remains substantially unimplemented fourteen months later: on 16 November 2026 the UN Deputy Special Envoy, Claudio Cordone, told the Security Council that obstacles to the return of displaced Druze and Bedouin families remained, and US remarks the same day described the roadmap as a framework whose "progress now depends on implementation." Displaced Suwayda residents were still protesting in November 2026 to demand the return home the roadmap promised them. No Suwayda trial had begun as of July 2026 (Amnesty International), and his own February 2026 amnesty had already released arrested suspects (UN Commission of Inquiry).
  • The second Alawite street wobble was answered with force. After a mosque attack in Homs killed eight on 26 December 2025, protests in Latakia on 28 December 2025 turned violent; four people died, including a police officer, and at least 108 were injured, with security forces accusing some demonstrators of firing on police (state and local reporting, December 2025).
  • The church wobble drew a rebuke from the community itself. After the 22 June 2025 suicide bombing of the Mar Elias Greek Orthodox Church in Damascus (Test 4), Greek Orthodox Patriarch John X used the victims' funeral on 24 June to address the president publicly: the condolence phone call, he said, "is not enough for us," and "the government bears responsibility in full" (Reuters, June 2025). Al-Sharaa's own 23 June statement condemned the bombing as a crime against all Syrians without specifically naming the Christian victims or the church — a framing the Patriarchate's response made impossible to miss.
  • The Kurdish adjustment came after the doubling down. Through 2025 the March agreement stalled; in January 2026 his government answered with an offensive that took some 80% of SDF-held territory in about a day and displaced more than 170,000 people (European Parliament Research Service briefing, July 2026) — and only then issued Decree No. 13 and signed the ceasefires. The concession is real; so is the order in which force and concession arrived.

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Alawites, Druze, Kurds, Christians, former regime members, the displaced — or are some counted out?

GRADE: FAILS

Evidence for:

  • The Kurdish count-in is the strongest single inclusion act in this dossier. Presidential Decree No. 13 of 16 January 2026 recognized Syria's Kurds as an integral part of the Syrian people, granted citizenship to all Kurds residing in Syria — annulling the effects of the 1962 Hasakah census that had stripped generations of Kurds of nationality — established Kurdish as a national language alongside Arabic, guaranteed Kurdish-language education in Kurdish-majority areas, and made Newroz a national holiday. It built on the 10 March 2025 agreement with SDF commander Mazloum Abdi, which had affirmed Kurds as integral to Syria with citizenship and constitutional rights guaranteed; in March 2026 an SDF general, Sipan Hamo, was appointed Deputy Minister of Defence, and in August 2026 the integration the agreement promised was announced complete.
  • The transitional government formed on 29 March 2025 seated 23 ministers drawn from diverse backgrounds, including Alawite, Christian, Druze, and Kurdish representatives (EUAA). Its limits are also documented: Hind Kabawat, a Christian, was the sole woman in the cabinet — a widely reported criticism.
  • The elected portion of the People's Assembly includes minority representation — among the elected members, six women and ten minority representatives, including Kurds, Christians, and two Alawites — and fifteen of his seventy appointed members are women (election reporting, 2025–2026).
  • The Constitutional Declaration promises the rights of all ethnic and religious groups on its face (13 March 2025).

Evidence against:

  • The coast, March 2025. The UN Commission of Inquiry's report of 11 August 2025 (A/HRC/59/CRP.4) found that approximately 1,400 people, predominantly civilians, including some 100 women, were killed in the March violence; that the violence by interim-government forces was "widespread and systematic"; that men were identified as Alawi, separated, and shot; and that acts documented may amount to war crimes — by government forces and by pro-former-government fighters both, the Commission's findings running against each side at its own strength. The Commission found no evidence of a governmental policy or plan to attack civilians, and documented instances in which government forces stopped violations and protected civilians; both findings stand here as the Commission stated them. Other counts, kept separate as they must be: SOHR's toll stands at more than 1,659 civilians (1,682 in its one-year retrospective); the Syrian Network for Human Rights counted 639-plus civilians and disarmed fighters killed by government-aligned fighters against 213 security personnel and 231 civilians killed by Assadist militias; a Reuters investigation counted 1,479 Alawites killed on 7–9 March alone. The spark is also documented and belongs on the record: pro-Assad fighters' coordinated attacks of 6 March 2025 killed, by the government's own inquiry's count, 238 army and security personnel. A massacre that answers an atrocity is still a massacre of the counted-out: the victims were killed as Alawites, by forces answerable to this government, in the government's first hundred days.
  • Suwayda, July 2025. The UN Commission of Inquiry's Suwayda report (27 March 2026) counted approximately 1,700 people killed and some 200,000 displaced; found that government forces and tribal fighters operated together as part of the same operation, with the tribal fighters' acts attributable to the government for 14–16 July; and documented murder, torture, abductions, pillage, and sexual violence including rape, committed by all sides and falling predominantly on Druze and Bedouin communities. SOHR's separate toll for the July phase stands at more than 1,000 killed including 588 Druze civilians. Sectarian violence had already killed at least 100 people, including security forces, in a Druze suburb of Damascus on 29–30 April 2025 (UK House of Commons Library). Sixteen months after July 2025, Suwayda remains outside government control, roughly 155,000 of its people remain displaced, further violence was reported in March and May 2026 (UK Commons Library briefing, 2026), and the province's three seats in his People's Assembly sit empty.
  • Christians. On 22 June 2025 a suicide bomber opened fire and detonated his vest inside the Mar Elias Greek Orthodox Church in Damascus during the liturgy, killing at least 25 worshippers by the state's initial count (later reporting put the dead at 30) and injuring more than fifty. The Interior Ministry attributed the attack to the Islamic State; a group calling itself Saraya Ansar al-Sunnah claimed responsibility days later — the attribution is CONTESTED between those claims. The bombing was not his government's act; the count question is what followed — a presidential statement that did not name the victims as Christians, a patriarch's public rebuke, and, in July 2026, a deputy interior minister's statement that some 1,300 suspected ISIL members had been arrested and 34 cells dismantled in the year since. USCIRF's 2026 assessment is the summary judgment of the relevant referee: religious freedom in Syria "continued to worsen" since 2024.
  • Former regime members are counted in two directions at once — hunted in the aggregate, exempted in the particular. The SJAC tracker counts 350-plus former-government affiliates arrested since December 2024, with Justice Ministry statements suggesting the true figure may be several thousand; simultaneously, some 300 connected officers were released without clear legal procedure by the Civil Peace Committee (Test 1), and the NCTJ's mandate was drawn so that victims of the new authorities' abuses fall outside it (HRW). A person is counted, in this system, according to which list he is on.
  • Women are counted at roughly a tenth: one woman in a 23-minister cabinet; 22 women in an Assembly of about 206 (10.7%), fifteen of them present by his appointment rather than by election.

Test 5 — KEEPS HIS WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record since December 2024, with dates; his long public arc stated factually.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • HTS dissolved — kept, on the date implied. In December 2024 he promised that HTS would be dissolved; on 29 January 2025 HTS and all other armed factions were formally dissolved at the conference that made him president. The promise that most directly concerned his own power base is the one most cleanly kept.
  • The Kurdish agreement — kept late, and beyond its letter in one direction. The 10 March 2025 agreement with Mazloum Abdi promised the merger of all civil and military institutions in the northeast into the Syrian state and Kurdish citizenship and constitutional rights. The integration was announced complete on 21 August 2026 and the SDF's dissolution on 25–26 August 2026; Decree No. 13 (16 January 2026) delivered citizenship, language status, and the Newroz holiday in terms broader than the agreement's text. The deadline failure is on the other shelf; the destination was reached.
  • Accountability for the coast — partially performed, in public. The 10 March 2025 pledge ("even among those closest to us") produced a committee, 563 referrals, public trials from November 2025, and 2026 convictions that include serving Ministry of Defence soldiers (Test 1). Partial performance of a hard promise is still performance; the shortfall is graded where it belongs, under Test 1.
  • The stated timeline, restated consistently after he set it. At the Doha Forum on 6 December 2025, al-Sharaa told CNN's Christiane Amanpour that the Constitutional Declaration gave him a five-year mandate to legislate and draft a constitution, with elections scheduled four years later — the same architecture he had promulgated in March 2025, defended in public a year into it rather than quietly extended.

Evidence against (promises not kept, moved, or undone by his own hand):

  • The elections timeline moved once, in his favor, before it stabilized. On 29 December 2024 he told Al Arabiya that elections could take up to four years and a new constitution two to three years. The Constitutional Declaration he signed on 13 March 2025 set a five-year transitional period — elections after 2030 on its face, beyond the four-year horizon he had given the public ten weeks earlier. The December 2025 restatement (above) is consistency with the moved line, not with the original one.
  • The SDF deadline was missed by every measure he accepted. The 10 March 2025 agreement set merger by the end of 2025; US-brokered deadlines in August, October, and December 2025 all passed unmet, with Damascus accusing the SDF of stalling and the SDF insisting the details required time — both positions documented (Middle East Institute). The agreement was finally executed in August 2026, eight months late and only after the January 2026 offensive settled the territorial question by force (Test 3).
  • Minority protection — the pledge most repeated, the record most against it. From December 2024 onward he vowed to protect Syria's religious and ethnic minorities (AFP/Reuters). Within fifteen months, forces answerable to his government had killed some 1,400 mostly Alawite civilians on the coast and fought a joint operation in Suwayda in which some 1,700 died (UN Commission of Inquiry); the Mar Elias congregation was bombed in his capital; and USCIRF recorded religious freedom as having worsened since 2024. He did not order the massacres — the Commission found no evidence of a governmental policy or plan — but the promise was protection, and protection is measured by outcomes for the protected.
  • Accountability, undone at the edges by his own signature. The February 2026 amnesty he issued released arrested Suwayda suspects (UN Commission of Inquiry); a second amnesty wave followed in March 2026; the coastal report that grounded his accountability pledge was never published in full; and the commander whose faction the EU sanctioned for the coastal massacres was promoted before them and transferred after them, never charged (Test 1). The pledge of 10 March 2025 stands on the record in full; so does each of these.
  • Inclusive government — delivered in form, thinner in substance. The February–March 2025 transition produced a diverse-on-paper cabinet with one woman among 23 ministers and a legislature one-third of which he appoints personally, chosen the rest of the way by electoral colleges under a committee he forms (Tests 2 and 4). The promise of inclusion was kept as representation and not as power-sharing; no institution outside his appointment can check him, which is the subject of Test 2.
  • The long arc, factually: the man who co-founded al-Qaeda's Syrian affiliate, broke with it in 2016, ruled Idlib through a Salvation Government, and took Damascus in December 2024 has, in two years, dissolved his own movement on schedule, been delisted by the UN Security Council, sat in the Oval Office, signed a Kurdish settlement his predecessors never attempted, and presided over two sectarian mass-killing episodes by forces under his flag that his accountability machinery has answered at the level of riflemen and amnesties rather than commanders. His word has proven most reliable where it concerned the architecture of his own rule — dissolution dates, transition length, integration of territory — and least reliable where it concerned the safety of the communities he promised to protect. Both halves of that sentence are the documented record.

Overall Summary

One law: FAILS · Without gripping: FAILS · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: FAILS · His word: MIXED.

Ahmed al-Sharaa built Syria's transitional order as a single point of decision: a Constitutional Declaration that lets the president appoint the ministers, the constitutional judges, a third of the legislature, and the security council that would approve his emergencies, under a legislature that cannot question him. Within that order, the law he pledged — "even among those closest to us" — has reached privates and former-regime men in public trials, while the sanctioned commander whose division was named in the coastal massacres was promoted, then transferred, never charged; while connected officers of the old regime walked free by committee; and while his own amnesties released suspects from the second massacre. Twice in his first year, forces answerable to his government killed minorities in the thousands'-neighborhood — some 1,400 on the coast, some 1,700 around Suwayda, by the UN Commission of Inquiry's counts — and Suwayda remains outside his state and outside his Assembly. Against that record stand acts no honest grading can omit: his own movement dissolved on the promised date; the UN's investigators given the access Assad refused them for fourteen years; a Suwayda report published whole; a Kurdish citizenship decree that undid a sixty-year-old injustice and an integration carried through to the SDF's dissolution; and a diplomatic rehabilitation — sanctions lifted, delisting voted 14–0–1, the White House door opened — that the world granted on the strength of the promises this dossier grades. The tests measure the seat, not the rehabilitation. On the evidence assembled here, the seat grips by design, gives by favor at the top while giving by law at the bottom, adjusts when pushed but often only after force has spoken first, keeps its word best where the word built its own power — and counts everyone on paper while Alawites, Druze, and Christians have buried their dead in numbers the paper does not carry.

Free. Always free.
