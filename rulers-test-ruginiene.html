The Ruler's Test — Inga Ruginienė, Prime Minister of Lithuania (2025–2026)

Subject: Inga Ruginienė — Prime Minister of Lithuania from 25 September 2025 to 14 July 2026 (cabinet resigned 23 June 2026; she served in a caretaker capacity until her successor was sworn in). A former head of the Lithuanian Trade Union Confederation who entered national politics only in 2024, she served as Minister of Social Security and Labour under her predecessor and party colleague Gintautas Paluckas, and became the Social Democrats' (LSDP) nominee for prime minister after Paluckas resigned on 4 August 2025 amid law-enforcement investigations into his business dealings. The Seimas approved her appointment on 26 August 2025 (78 in favour, 35 against, 14 abstentions); her cabinet — LSDP with the populist Dawn of Nemunas (Nemuno Aušra), the Farmers and Greens Union (LVŽS) and the Electoral Action of Poles (LLRA-KŠS), 82 of 141 seats — was sworn in on 25 September 2025. She is graded on that completed tenure of just under ten months; since 14 July 2026 she has again served as Minister of Social Security and Labour, this time under Prime Minister Mindaugas Sinkevičius. Graded: November 2026, on the documented public record. Method: Each test is graded only on the evidence listed under it. Both shelves are shown — the strongest evidence that she passes, then the strongest evidence that she fails. Allegations are labeled ALLEGATION and never stated as verdicts. Contested items are named as contested, with who disputes them. Seat-holders and public figures are named; people below the public eye appear by role only, unless the documented record itself establishes their wrongdoing. Grades: HOLDS / FAILS / MIXED / NOT ENOUGH EVIDENCE. This record is correctable: new evidence changes grades, in the open.

Test 1 — GIVES BY ONE LAW

Same rules for allies and opponents, insiders and outsiders — no favorites at the source.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The law reached her own party's fallen leader, with her majority's votes. The criminal probes opened in 2025 into former prime minister Gintautas Paluckas — her predecessor and fellow Social Democrat — proceeded throughout her tenure: prosecutors investigated roughly €344,000 in allegedly unexplained wealth acquired between December 2010 and December 2024, his wife was criminally charged, and in May 2026 the Seimas voted 93 of 141 to lift Paluckas's parliamentary immunity at the Prosecutor General's request so he could be indicted for alleged abuse of office and unlawful enrichment. Paluckas denies any crime; the enrichment and abuse charges are ALLEGATIONs, unadjudicated, and he agreed to a simplified immunity-lifting procedure. The governing majority did not shield him in the chamber (OCCRP, May 2026; Prosecutor General's address to the Seimas, May 2026).
  • The courts reached her coalition partner's leader while he sat in her coalition. On 4 December 2025 the Vilnius district court convicted Remigijus Žemaitaitis, leader of Dawn of Nemunas, of inciting hatred against Jews and grossly minimising the Holocaust in 2023 statements, fining him €5,000 (prosecutors had sought €51,000). The LSDP stated that antisemitism and hate speech are "unacceptable and incompatible with our values" and that it respected the court's decision, while noting it was not final; Žemaitaitis denies wrongdoing, called the ruling politicised, and appealed (Associated Press, 4–5 December 2025).
  • The ethics machinery reached the prime minister herself, and she submitted to it. After an appeal by an opposition-aligned MP, the Chief Official Ethics Commission (VTEK) investigated Ruginienė's inclusion of her family in official delegations; her office said the government had itself asked the commission to assess the delegation-forming practice, and she publicly called the probe "a routine process that will allow for an objective answer" (Baltic Times/BNS, March 2026). The commission's adverse finding is on the against shelf; the point here is that the process ran against a sitting prime minister, in the open, and she neither blocked it nor dismissed the body.

Evidence against:

  • The ethics commission found that she herself broke the law at the source. In June 2026 the Chief Official Ethics Commission concluded that Ruginienė had grossly violated the Law on the Harmonisation of Public and Private Interests: by her own signed orders, her husband was made a member of the state delegation for the 6–8 March 2026 Vatican and Rome visit, her two children were granted accompanying-person status, and the Government Chancellery covered the family's travel costs from the state budget — decisions from which she did not recuse herself, as the law requires of a decision-maker whose close persons benefit. A parallel complaint covered her husband's state-funded travel with her to the Milan-Cortina Winter Olympics opening in February 2026. Her office's position is printed as she stated it: under the Law on the Government the prime minister forms delegations by decree, and in international practice family members are included where protocol requires it, for state representation rather than private interest (Baltic Times/BNS, March 2026; LRT, 23 June 2026). The finding stands as a documented instance of the law bending for the seat-holder's own household.
  • Coalition patronage put a ministry in unqualified hands as the price of a majority. Her September 2025 coalition agreement gave the Ministry of Culture to Dawn of Nemunas, whose nominee, Ignotas Adomavičius, had no background in culture; the cultural community protested from the day her government was sworn in (25 September 2025). He resigned on 3 October 2025 after failing, in a public interview, to affirm that Crimea is Ukraine's — a documented competence and loyalty failure in a portfolio allocated by coalition arithmetic rather than fitness (China-CEE Institute political briefing, November 2025; contemporaneous reporting).
  • Her majority declined the parliamentary track against Paluckas even as prosecutors proceeded: the Seimas voted down the opposition's proposal to open an impeachment inquiry into the former prime minister for allegedly running his companies while an MP. The criminal process continued regardless (Test 1, for shelf), but in the chamber the governing parties closed the political-accountability route against their own former leader (Baltic Times/BNS, 2025–2026).

Test 2 — HOLDS WITHOUT GRIPPING

Do the courts, media, the electoral authority, the prosecution, the Special Investigation Service (STT), and the central bank keep their autonomy — or is independence punished and control centralized?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • Every other referee in this dossier acted against the government's interests without documented punishment. The Prosecutor General's office pursued the former prime minister of her own party to an immunity vote (Test 1); the district court convicted her coalition partner's leader; the ethics commission found against the prime minister herself; the Constitutional Court accepted petitions from 37 MPs against her majority's broadcaster law and, earlier, a petition against its funding freeze (Constitutional Court petitions accepted 5 February and 23 September 2026). No judge, prosecutor, ethics commissioner, or STT officer is documented as removed, transferred, or threatened in retaliation.
  • The central bank and the electoral commission were left alone: the record of her tenure contains no documented interference with the Bank of Lithuania's decisions or with the Central Electoral Commission (VRK), and the fiercely critical private and investigative media — Siena and Laisvės TV, whose reporting had brought down her predecessor — continued publishing throughout.
  • The public broadcaster's director general was not removed. Monika Garbačiauskaitė-Budrienė remained in post through the whole fight over the LRT law, and the law finally adopted in June 2026 retained the two-thirds Council majority for early dismissal, on narrowed grounds — the core safeguard the original bill would have abolished (Böll Foundation analysis of Law No. XV-981, 2026).
  • Parliamentary resistance worked in the open: the December 2025 opposition filibuster — the first documented in modern Lithuanian parliamentary history — stalled the fast-tracked broadcaster bill, and the Presidency publicly checked the majority's method (below).

Evidence against:

  • Her coalition spent eight months trying to loosen the public broadcaster's safeguards, and partly succeeded. In late November 2025 Dawn of Nemunas, with coalition backing, proposed cutting the threshold for dismissing LRT's director general from two-thirds of the Council (8 of 12) by open ballot to a simple majority by secret ballot, removing the requirement that dismissal serve the public interest; the Seimas Legal Department stated the proposal contradicted Constitutional Court doctrine on LRT's independence. A revised coalition version (7 of 12, secret ballot) was pushed through committee and plenary stages in December 2025 under an expedited procedure (first reading 27 November, 60–45; after deliberation 17 December, 67–47), over a petition of more than 100,000 citizens, an LRT staff strike, and protests of roughly 10,000 people on 9 December 2025 (EBU statement, December 2025; State Media Monitor chronology).
  • The money was gripped even where the dismissal rule was saved. On 27 November 2025 her majority froze LRT's funding at €79.6 million a year for 2026–2028, overriding the statutory formula that would have provided about €88.2 million in 2026; the Venice Commission criticised the freeze's fast-track adoption, without a revised explanatory memorandum or impact assessment, as inconsistent with European good law-making practice and warned that freezing a public broadcaster's funding "places direct pressure on independent journalism" (Venice Commission report, March 2026, via the European Federation of Journalists). The State Media Monitor reclassified LRT from "Independent Public" to "Independent State Funded" in its 2026 review cycle as a direct result.
  • The end state still restructured the institution toward the majority. The LRT law adopted on 2 June 2026 (77–1, with the opposition not participating) and signed by President Nausėda ten days later enlarged the Council from 12 to 15 members, shortened terms from six years to four, created a new Management Board, and revised dismissal conditions; the Journalists' Professional Association had asked the President to veto it, and 37 MPs petitioned the Constitutional Court that the model does not sufficiently protect LRT from political influence — a question pending at grading (China-CEE Institute briefing, July 2026; Böll Foundation, 2026).
  • The pressure campaign drew formal external censure: the European Parliament adopted a resolution condemning the amendments as incompatible with the European Media Freedom Act and urging Lithuanian authorities to halt the process (European Parliament resolution, early 2026). Protests continued into the spring — over 10,000 people on 8 April 2026 and some 30,000 on 25 April 2026 under "Hands off free speech" (BNS; European Youth Portal report, July 2026).

Test 3 — ANSWERS THE WOBBLE

When courts, voters, the Seimas, the President, or events push back, does she adjust openly — or deny and double down?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • On the broadcaster law, the fast track was abandoned under pressure and the product genuinely softened. After the December 2025 filibuster, mass protests, and President Nausėda's public statement (22 December 2025) that such serious changes could not be made under an expedited procedure, the majority agreed to suspend the fast-tracked process and set up a cross-party working group; the law that emerged in June 2026 kept the two-thirds dismissal threshold, narrowed the dismissal grounds, and wrote editorial independence into the text — a partial victory the LRT director general herself credited to the protests (China-CEE Institute briefings, January and July 2026; Böll Foundation, 2026).
  • She answered the balloon crisis with the instruments the law provides, escalating by steps and through parliament. As contraband balloons from Belarus repeatedly closed Vilnius airport from October 2025 (623 balloon incursions recorded in 2025 to 8 December; over 350 flights and some 51,000 passengers disrupted), her government closed two Belarus border checkpoints for three weeks from late October, then on 9 December 2025 declared a nationwide emergency situation — a step below a state of emergency — and asked the Seimas to authorise military support to police and border guards rather than assuming the powers (Reuters/AP, 9 December 2025; European Parliament resolution B10-0571/2025). Belarus denies orchestrating the incursions and accuses Lithuania of provocations; that position is recorded.
  • When her majority ceased to exist, she handed over power on schedule and without a fight. After the LSDP announced the coalition's collapse on 6 June 2026 and Dawn of Nemunas formally withdrew, Ruginienė and her cabinet resigned on 23 June 2026, served as caretakers at the President's request, and left office when the Sinkevičius cabinet was sworn in on 14 July 2026 — saying publicly that Sinkevičius "should have become prime minister last year" (LRT, 23 June 2026).
  • On defence spending she absorbed a public internal revolt and still delivered the number: after Defence Minister Dovilė Šakalienė's dismissal/resignation on 22 October 2025 in the dispute over whether the 2026 draft truly reached 5% of GDP (below), the adopted 2026 budget earmarked €4.79 billion, 5.38% of GDP, for defence (Ministry of Finance, 11 December 2025).

Evidence against:

  • Her first answer to her defence minister's pushback was removal. Šakalienė had mobilised analysts and influencers after a 14 October 2025 ministry briefing left participants doubting the 5% pledge would be honoured; she published internal correspondence showing earlier Finance Ministry drafts at under 4% and then 4.87% of GDP. Ruginienė announced lost confidence, initiated the dismissal herself, and described the minister as dishonest and uncooperative; Šakalienė resigned the same morning, saying she had "no hope of working together with the current head of government." The final figure did reach 5.38% — but the minister who forced the question was gone within a month of the government's formation (Baltic Times/BNS; AFP, 22 October 2025). Whether Šakalienė's back-channel lobbying justified dismissal is CONTESTED between the two principals; the sequence is documented.
  • On the coalition's central liability she held on for nine months. Žemaitaitis's record was not new information: the Constitutional Court had ruled in April 2024 that his antisemitic statements broke his oath and grossly violated the Constitution; President Nausėda had called including his party in government a mistake; and after the December 2025 conviction the pressure only grew. Ruginienė nonetheless renewed and kept the partnership from September 2025 until June 2026, through the conviction, the culture-ministry fiasco, and the largest protest movement of her tenure, adjusting only when her own party ended the coalition (Associated Press, December 2025; FPRI Baltic Roundup, June 2026).
  • The Kapčiamiestis training-ground decision was announced to its host community by website post. Residents of the township learned on 15 December 2025 — "along with the rest of the nation," in their words — from a Defence Ministry webpage that a roughly 12,000–14,600-hectare training ground would be built beside their homes, taking 13 homesteads and some 2,000 private land plots. Officials who then met residents were, on the documented account, unprepared for their questions, and residents maintained through the spring votes that they had not been heard, even as the government offered above-market compensation, €52,000 additional payments per property, and five years to relocate (Baltic Worlds, 2026; LRT, 26 April 2026).
  • Her China opening overshot what her partners would carry (Test 5): after her February 2026 remarks, the President publicly cautioned that restoring relations requires goodwill from both sides and warned of the risks of being "overly close" with China, and her own Seimas Speaker said there was no need to rush — public corrections from her own camp (BNS/LRT, February 2026).

Test 4 — COUNTS EVERYONE

Does the accounting include the whole flock — Polish and Russian-speaking minorities, rural communities, the poor, the Jewish community, her opponents' voters — or are some counted out?

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for:

  • The 2026 budget she delivered was weighted to the bottom of the flock. Adopted on 11 December 2025, it raised the monthly minimum wage by 11.1% to €1,153 from 1 January 2026, raised the average old-age pension by 12% to €750 (€810 for those with the full contribution record), and increased social benefits including child payments by an average of 5.4% (Ministry of Finance, 11 December 2025; Swedbank Economic Outlook, November 2025).
  • The Polish minority was inside her government, not outside it. The Electoral Action of Poles (LLRA-KŠS) sat in her coalition from September 2025, giving the Polish-speaking community's party a share of governing power; the coalition arithmetic that critics attacked (Test 1) simultaneously included the country's largest ethnic minority in the majority.
  • The pension-flexibility reform her government implemented from 1 January 2026 treated savers as owners of their own money: the second pillar became voluntary, with a two-year window to withdraw accumulated savings; about 580,000 people — some 40% of members — had taken money out by April 2026, and roughly €3.2 billion reached participants in the first quarter (Ministry of Social Security and Labour; China-CEE Institute briefing, April 2026). The reform had been legislated under her predecessor; its implementation, and its macroeconomic risks, were hers.
  • Kapčiamiestis's displaced were compensated above the market: homestead owners were offered more than market value plus €52,000 per property and a €25,000 relocation incentive over five years, and some local businesses (kayak rentals, hunting clubs) may continue operating (LRT/Daily Baltic, March–April 2026).

Evidence against:

  • The Jewish community was counted out of the coalition's design for most of her tenure. The Lithuanian Jewish Community had formally urged that a party led by a man the Constitutional Court found to have breached his oath with antisemitic statements be kept out of government; her September 2025 coalition included that party anyway, and it remained in her government for six months after its leader's December 2025 conviction for inciting hatred against Jews — until her party, not the community's appeals, ended the arrangement in June 2026 (European Jewish Congress/Lithuanian Jewish Community statement; Associated Press, December 2025).
  • The cultural community's central demand was refused for the life of the coalition. Protesters from 25 September 2025 onward asked that Dawn of Nemunas not hold the Culture Ministry; when Adomavičius resigned, the replacement minister also came from that party's nomination, and the protests — the Culture Assembly movement, later joined with the LRT protests into the largest demonstrations since independence — continued to the end of her tenure (European Youth Portal, July 2026; China-CEE Institute, November 2025).
  • Kapčiamiestis's residents were decided about before they were consulted (Test 3): a rural border community of some 500 people learned of a training ground for 3,500–4,000 troops from a website announcement, and the Seimas approved the project in spring 2026 (102–10–5 at the March stage; 105 in favour at the final April vote) over their stated objections — with the votes against coming largely from her own coalition partner, not the opposition.
  • The border communities and hauliers absorbed the costs of the Belarus confrontation: the October–November 2025 checkpoint closures stranded Lithuanian trucks in Belarus, and the December emergency regime placed the affected border districts under expanded military powers — measures her government justified on security grounds, with the burden falling on the country's south-eastern, heavily Polish- and Russian-speaking districts (Reuters/AP, December 2025).

Test 5 — KEEPS HER WORD OVER TIME

Stated promises and positions vs. the record from her nomination (August 2025) to her resignation (June–July 2026), with dates; her party's inherited commitments noted as such.

GRADE: MIXED

Evidence for (promises kept or credibly in motion):

  • Defence 5–6% — kept. On taking office she pledged to spend 5–6% of GDP on defence over the following years and continuity with the pro-Ukraine, pro-defence course (Reuters, 25 September 2025). The 2026 budget adopted under her government allocated €4.79 billion — 5.38% of GDP — to defence, among NATO's highest shares, and the Kapčiamiestis training-ground legislation for the alliance's eastern flank was carried through her Seimas in spring 2026 (Ministry of Finance, December 2025; LRT, 2026).
  • Social figures — kept to the euro. The minimum wage (€1,153), average pension (€750/€810), and benefit increases in the adopted 2026 budget match the figures her government announced in October 2025 (Ministry of Finance, 16 October and 11 December 2025).
  • Ukraine — kept. Support for Ukraine, including hosting and assistance, continued without qualification throughout her tenure; her one coalition casualty on the point was a minister of her partner party who would not affirm Crimea as Ukraine's and was gone within a week of the remark (October 2025).

Evidence against (promises not kept, or receding):

  • "Restoring stability" — not delivered. She told the Seimas on 26 August 2025 that she would focus on "restoring stability." Her government lasted nine and a half months, was preceded by one collapsed cabinet and followed by a third government in the same parliamentary term, and spent much of its life managing protests, two ministerial resignations in its first month, and its coalition partner's scandals — instability she inherited in part, but which her coalition choices prolonged (AP, 26 August 2025; OSW analysis, 15 July 2026).
  • China normalisation — announced, not achieved, and walked toward unilaterally. Her government programme set the goal of "restoring diplomatic relations with China to the same diplomatic level as in other European Union countries." On 3 February 2026 she called the 2021 Taiwanese Representative Office name a "strategic mistake," saying Lithuania had "jumped in front of a train and lost," and on 11 February 2026 said she saw no reason the office could not be renamed for Taipei. Taiwan's foreign ministry responded that no name change was under discussion with Vilnius; Beijing replied that Lithuania should turn willingness into "concrete actions"; and by the end of her tenure there were still no accredited Chinese diplomats in Lithuania and no restoration — the goal unmet, the concession signalled before any negotiation (BNS/LRT, February 2026; CNA, 6 February 2026; Baltic Times, February 2026). The renaming demand later reported under her successor (September 2026) is his government's act, not hers, and is not graded to her.
  • The partnership her party said it would not make — renewed by her choice. The LSDP's pre-2024-election position, and the reason the Democrats "For Lithuania" quit the previous coalition in August 2025, was refusal to govern with Žemaitaitis's party. Forming her government in September 2025, Ruginienė had the option of rebuilding without Dawn of Nemunas; she instead signed a coalition that kept it, including its Culture Ministry nomination — the decision from which most of her tenure's crises followed. The party's earlier breach of that stance (December 2024) belongs to her predecessor's record; the renewal was hers (AP, August–September 2025; OSW, 26 September 2025).
  • Her own compliance word — overtaken by the ethics finding. A prime minister who took office after her predecessor fell to ethics and business-dealing investigations, and who presented her government as restoring standards, was herself found in June 2026 to have grossly violated the public-and-private-interests law over her family's state-funded travel (Test 1). She disputes the characterisation — her office maintains the delegation practice was lawful and protocol-based — and that position stands on the record beside the finding.

Overall Summary

One law: MIXED · Without gripping: MIXED · The wobble: MIXED · Counts everyone: MIXED · Her word: MIXED.

Inga Ruginienė governed for nine and a half months as a compromise candidate elevated by her predecessor's fall, and the record is genuinely two-sided. The institutions of the Lithuanian state worked on her watch at full strength and against her interests: prosecutors took her own party's former prime minister to an immunity vote with her majority's support, a court convicted her coalition partner's leader, the ethics commission found against her personally, and when her majority dissolved she resigned and handed over power on the constitutional schedule. She delivered what she had numbered — 5.38% of GDP for defence, the minimum wage and pensions as announced — and her social budget was real. Against that stand the choices that defined and ended her tenure: she renewed a coalition with a party whose leader the Constitutional Court had already found in breach of his oath, and kept it through his conviction and the largest protests since independence; her majority froze the public broadcaster's funding and spent eight months trying to loosen its independence safeguards before public resistance forced a softer law; her host community for the flagship defence project learned of it from a website; and the ethics standard she was elevated to restore was the one the ethics commission found her, personally, to have grossly violated. The seat neither captured the state nor held it cleanly — it gripped where the coalition demanded, yielded where the institutions pushed, and fell, in the end, by the same arithmetic that had raised it.
