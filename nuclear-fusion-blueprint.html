Nuclear FusionThe fire the sun runs on — light nuclei joining, and a little mass becoming energy.

Every star in the sky is a fusion reactor that works. Ours is the attempt to build a small one on Earth. The principle has been understood for a century and demonstrated in the laboratory — and no fusion machine has yet delivered net electricity to anyone's home. This sheet holds both halves of that sentence at the same size, because both are the documented record.

LAYERS

Three layers, one idea

What it is

Two light atomic nuclei, squeezed and heated until they join into one heavier nucleus. The joined nucleus weighs slightly less than the two that made it — and the missing mass leaves as energy. It is the reaction that powers the sun.

What it means

The fuel is some of the most ordinary stuff on Earth — forms of hydrogen found in seawater, plus lithium — and the energy locked in it is enormous per spoonful. That is why, after seventy years of trying, the world has not stopped trying.

What it could mean for you

Not a device you will build — an idea you will meet again and again: in headlines announcing "breakthroughs," in energy debates, in plans for ships to other planets. This sheet gives you the three questions that sort a real milestone from a relabelled one.

WHY → HOW → ACT

Why? → How? → Act.

WHY?

The sun has burned for about 4.6 billion years on this reaction, turning mass into light the whole time. If it can be held in a machine, one fuel source — drawn from water — could power civilisation without smoke and without running out on any human timescale.

→

HOW?

Heat light nuclei until they move fast enough to overcome their mutual repulsion, and hold them together long enough, densely enough, for joining to outrun the heat leaking away. Easy to state. The whole of this sheet is why it is hard.

→

ACT.

Learn to read the record like an engineer: every fusion claim names a gain — ask gain measured where? (At the target? In the plasma? At the wall plug?) That one question, asked every time, is the act this sheet teaches.

SHEET A

The principle, and why it is hard

Tap a panel to open it.

1 · The reaction — mass becomes energy Tap to close

Documented · the idea level

Atomic nuclei carry positive electric charge, and like charges repel — so two nuclei naturally push each other away, hard. Get them moving fast enough, in a place dense enough, and occasionally two get close enough for the strong nuclear force — which only reaches across the tiny width of a nucleus — to grab hold and bind them into one.

  • The sun's version: in the sun's core, hydrogen nuclei join in a slow chain that ends as helium. The helium weighs a little less than the hydrogen that made it. That missing sliver of mass is not lost — it leaves as energy, exactly as E = mc² says it must. Sunlight is that sliver, arriving.
  • The Earth version: the easiest fusion reaction known joins two heavy forms of hydrogen — deuterium and tritium — into helium, releasing energy and one fast neutron. "Easiest" is relative: it still needs conditions no ordinary material survives touching. Deuterium is plentiful in ordinary water; tritium is rare and must be made (see the Open Hows, below).
  • Why the energy is so large: nuclear binding is millions of times stronger, particle for particle, than the chemical binding burned in a fire. Fusion and its sibling, fission, live on that stronger shelf — which is also why this sheet's safety line is absolute.

This is the whole principle. Everything after it — the machines, the billions, the decades — is about creating and holding the conditions, not about the reaction itself. The reaction was never the mystery.

2 · Why it is hard — heat and holding Tap to close

Documented · temperature and confinement

Nuclei repel, so they must collide fast — and "fast," for a gas of particles, is what temperature means. The sun manages at about 15 million °C in its core because its gravity crushes the fuel to extraordinary density and holds it there for billions of years. A machine on Earth has no such gravity and no such patience, so it must compensate the only way left: run far hotter than the sun's core and hold the fuel — now a plasma, a gas stripped into charged particles — away from any wall, because no solid survives contact with it.

  • Confinement, road one — magnetic: charged particles spiral along magnetic field lines, so a strong enough field can hold plasma in a ring (a tokamak) or a twisted ring (a stellarator) without touching it. ITER takes this road.
  • Confinement, road two — inertial: don't hold it at all. Crush a tiny fuel pellet so fast and so evenly — with lasers, at the National Ignition Facility — that fusion happens in the instant before it blows apart. Gravity's squeeze, rented for a billionth of a second.
  • The Lawson criterion, as an idea: physicist John Lawson showed in the 1950s that three numbers multiply together — temperature × density × confinement time — and the product must pass a threshold before a fusion system can produce more than it consumes. Fall short on any one leg and the other two must make it up. The sun maxes out density and time. Machines must max out temperature — and fight for every fraction of a second of time.
SHEET B

The documented state of the art

Bounded honestly — what has actually happened, as of November 2026.

Documented · the December 2022 result, at full size

NIF ignition — a real first, with the boundary printed as large as the headline

On 5 December 2022, the National Ignition Facility (California) delivered 2.05 megajoules of laser energy to a tiny fuel target — and the target released 3.15 megajoules of fusion energy. For the first time in history, a controlled fusion reaction gave back more energy than was delivered to the target. (U.S. Department of Energy / Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory.)

That is net gain in the target — it is not net plant power. Making that 2.05 MJ laser pulse drew roughly 300 megajoules of electricity from the grid. Counted at the wall plug — the way a power station must be counted — the facility used about a hundred times more energy than the fusion released.

Both paragraphs are the same event, and any honest account needs both. The result has since been repeated and improved — in April 2025 a shot released 8.6 MJ from 2.08 MJ delivered, a target gain of about 4.1 (LLNL) — and the wall-plug boundary stands exactly where it stood. NIF is a research facility; it was never built to be a power plant, and it does not claim to be one.

ITER (France) The world's largest fusion experiment: a tokamak built by an international partnership, designed to produce 500 MW of fusion power from 50 MW of heating — a plasma gain of Q = 10. Under construction and assembly. Under its 2024 baseline plan, research operation begins in 2034 and deuterium–tritium operation in 2039 (ITER Organization). ITER is an experiment, not a power station: by design it will generate no electricity at all. Its dates have already moved once — the 2016 plan aimed at first plasma in 2025.
NIF (USA) The inertial road: 192 lasers crushing a fuel pellet, at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory. Ignition achieved December 2022 and repeated since — target gain above 1, up to about 4.1 in April 2025 (LLNL). Target gain only. At the wall plug the facility remains a large net consumer, and it fires rarely — a research instrument, not a prototype plant.
JET (UK) Europe's tokamak at Culham — for decades the largest operating fusion experiment and ITER's closest rehearsal. In its final deuterium–tritium experiments it set a world record of 69 megajoules of fusion energy in one pulse (announced February 2024, UKAEA), then ended operation after forty years. A record pulse, seconds long, that consumed far more power than it returned. JET proved plasma behaviour at scale; it never approached net power.
Private ventures Companies — ventures, named as ventures — racing the public programmes with newer magnet technology and private money (over $7 billion invested industry-wide by the Fusion Industry Association's 2024 count). Commonwealth Fusion Systems is building its SPARC tokamak in Massachusetts. Helion Energy has signed a power purchase agreement to supply Microsoft. TAE Technologies and others are building their own machines. Their timelines are claims, labelled as claims: CFS targets net energy gain (Q > 1) from SPARC in 2027; Helion's agreement targets electricity delivery from 2028. Neither has happened yet. A signed agreement is a documented fact; the delivery it promises is not.

The honest negatives

After some seventy years of work, no fusion machine has ever delivered net electricity. Not once, not anywhere, not for one second.

Say it plainly, because the record says it plainly: fusion power has been "about thirty years away" for most of the lifetimes of the people now building it. The flagship international project, ITER, was agreed in 2006, aimed at first plasma in 2025, and now plans to begin research operation in 2034 and real fusion fuel in 2039 — and even when it succeeds completely, it will produce no electricity, because it was designed as an experiment. The one genuine net-gain result, NIF's ignition, is a gain measured at a target the size of a peppercorn, inside a facility that consumed roughly a hundred times more electricity than the reaction released.

None of this makes fusion a fraud — the physics is real, the milestones above are real, and the people working on it publish their numbers where rivals can check them. It makes fusion unfinished: a technology whose principle is proven, whose laboratory milestones keep arriving, and whose power plant does not exist. Any page — or pitch — that blurs the distance between those two facts is selling something. This one doesn't.

OPEN HOWS

The open hows shelf

Each one labelled an open how — an unknown is not a no.

Open how · unknown, not a no

Sustained net gain at plant scale

Ignition has happened in instants, inside a target. A power plant needs gain that is sustained — minutes to months, not billionths of a second — and measured at the wall plug, after the magnets, lasers, pumps, and cooling have all taken their share. No machine is there. Whether the magnetic road, the inertial road, or a private venture's road gets there first is genuinely unknown.

Open how · unknown, not a no

Materials that survive the neutron flux

Deuterium–tritium fusion throws off very fast neutrons, and they carry most of the reaction's energy straight into the machine's walls — knocking atoms out of place, making steel brittle and radioactive over time. No material has yet been shown to survive years of that bombardment at power-plant intensity, and there is no operating test facility that fully reproduces it. The plasma may be solved before the wall is.

Open how · unknown, not a no

Breeding its own tritium

Deuterium is everywhere; tritium barely exists in nature and today's world supply — a by-product of certain fission reactors — is measured in kilograms. A fusion plant must therefore breed its own tritium from lithium in a blanket around the plasma, making at least one new tritium atom for every one it burns. The idea is sound and old. It has never been demonstrated as a working, closed fuel cycle at scale.

Concept · documented as a concept

Fusion propulsion — the starship door

A fusion rocket would not need to convert heat to electricity at all — the reaction's own products become the exhaust, giving thrust no chemical rocket can match. This exists today as documented concepts: NASA funded study of Princeton's Direct Fusion Drive, whose published concept papers describe delivering a 1,000 kg orbiter to Pluto in about four years, with design figures of a few newtons of thrust per megawatt and a specific impulse near 10,000 seconds (NASA / Princeton Satellite Systems). Label it exactly: those are design numbers from funded studies. The underlying experimental device has never produced a fusion reaction, and no fusion engine of any kind has ever flown.

ACT

Starter practice — reading the record like an engineer

There is no bench version of this sheet, by design. The act is understanding.

Fusion's Act section is not a project. It is a habit — the one this whole sheet has been practising — for every fusion headline you will ever meet:

Ask: gain measured where?

Target, plasma, or wall plug? NIF's 1.5 was at the target; at the wall plug the same shot was about 0.01. The number is rarely wrong — the boundary around it is where the truth hides.

Ask: for how long?

A billionth of a second (a laser shot), five seconds (JET's record pulse), or continuously (a power plant)? Duration is the quiet difference between a milestone and a machine.

Ask: electricity, or heat in a plasma?

Fusion power inside a plasma is not electricity out of a socket. ITER's complete success produces the first and none of the second. If a claim skips the conversion, it has skipped the plant.

Separate the fact from the claim

"Helion signed an agreement with Microsoft" is a documented fact. "Helion will deliver power in 2028" is a claim about the future. Both can be printed — never as the same kind of sentence.

Follow the primary record

The laboratories publish their own numbers: the ITER Organization, Lawrence Livermore (NIF), UKAEA (JET), NASA (propulsion concepts). Read the claim there first; read the celebration second.

Safety — plainly, and finallyThis sheet stops at principles, history, and why — deliberately and completely. It will not be extended with construction detail, and no reader should take any part of it as a step toward building anything. Fusion done for real lives in licensed laboratories with radiation shielding, tritium handling regimes, and national regulators — that is where the documented record lives too, and it is fascinating enough at the level of ideas.
