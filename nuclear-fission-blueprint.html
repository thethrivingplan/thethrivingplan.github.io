Nuclear FissionSplitting the heaviest nuclei to free the energy that binds them — the chain reaction, understood as an idea, with its whole documented record: the power, the accidents, and the waste.

The core line

One split can start the next. That is the whole invention — and the whole problem.

When a heavy nucleus splits, it throws out spare neutrons. If, on average, at least one of those neutrons goes on to split another nucleus, the reaction feeds itself: a chain reaction. Everything on this sheet — the power stations, the history, the danger, the waste — is that one sentence, unfolded.

LAYERS

Three layers, one idea

What it is

Fission is the splitting of a heavy atomic nucleus — uranium-235 is the one nature provides — into two lighter nuclei, spare neutrons, and energy. The energy was already in there, as the binding that held the nucleus together; splitting rearranges it, and a small part of the mass comes out as energy. That is Einstein's E = mc² doing ordinary bookkeeping.

What it means

Chemical burning rearranges electrons and frees a few electron-volts per atom. Fission rearranges the nucleus and frees about 200 million electron-volts per atom — millions of times more, atom for atom. That single ratio is why a kilogram of uranium carries the energy of thousands of tonnes of coal, and why this technology is simultaneously so useful and so unforgiving.

What it could mean for you

About one in every eleven light bulbs on Earth is lit by fission right now — roughly 9% of world electricity. Understanding the chain reaction as an idea — what “critical” actually means, why cooling can never be an afterthought, why the waste outlives us — is the difference between having an opinion about nuclear power and having a grounded one.

WHY?

The nucleus is the densest store of energy ordinary matter has. Splitting the heaviest nuclei is how we reach it — no fire we can build touches it.

→

HOW?

As an idea: keep the chain reaction exactly at replacement rate — each fission causing, on average, one more — and carry the heat away without pause. Hold those two, and you have power. Lose either, and you have the accidents in §④.

01 / SHEET A

The Principle — Chain Reaction & Criticality

Ideas only. This is the level at which the physics is taught in every textbook — and the level this sheet stays at.

In December 1938, in Berlin, chemists Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann bombarded uranium with neutrons and found barium — an element about half uranium's size — in the results. Their exiled colleague Lise Meitner, working in Sweden with her nephew Otto Frisch, supplied the explanation within weeks: the nucleus had not chipped; it had split in two, releasing about 200 MeV of energy. Frisch named it “fission,” after the splitting of a living cell. The papers appeared in January and February 1939, and the record has carried all four names ever since — even where the prizes did not (see §③).

Documented · the idea

Criticality is a headcount, not a substance

Physicists keep the headcount with one number: the multiplication factor, k — how many fissions, on average, one fission causes in the next generation.

  • k below 1 — subcritical. Each generation is smaller than the last. The chain dies out on its own.
  • k equal to 1 — critical. Each generation exactly replaces itself. The reaction runs steady. This — not an explosion — is the normal working state of every power reactor on Earth, every day.
  • k above 1 — supercritical. Each generation is larger. Power rises until something pushes k back down.

“Critical” sounds alarming because the word travelled; in the physics it simply means self-sustaining at a steady rate. What moves k is a balance of plain ideas: how much fuel, how it's arranged, how many neutrons leak away or get absorbed by something that isn't fuel. Control, as an idea, is anything that absorbs spare neutrons — which is why the first reactor in history was shut down by sliding in rods of a neutron-absorbing material, and why every reactor since keeps that same idea at its heart.

Documented · why control is possible

The lucky delay that makes control possible

Most neutrons leave a fission instantly — but a small fraction, well under one percent, arrive late, seconds to minutes after, from the unstable fragments. That sounds like a footnote. It is the reason controlled fission exists.

If every neutron were instant, a chain reaction would multiply in millionths of a second — far faster than any human or machine could respond. The delayed neutrons stretch the reaction's response time into seconds. Inside that borrowed time, instruments can measure, and control systems can act. A power reactor is steered in that narrow margin — one of the documented pieces of good fortune in this technology's physics, and worth knowing before any opinion about its safety.

There is a second, sterner fact in the same card: fission products keep generating heat after the chain stops. Shut the reaction down completely and the fragments' own decay still produces significant heat for days, then less for years. Cooling is therefore not part of running a reactor; it is a duty that outlasts the running. Remember this in §④ — every major accident is, at bottom, this paragraph coming due.

Try the headcount — a teaching aid

Tap a value of k and watch thirty generations of the chain. Pure arithmetic — the same exponential every textbook draws — with no device anywhere in it.

Why so much energy? The binding-energy idea Tap to open

Nuclei are held together by the strong nuclear force, and it takes energy to pull one apart — that stored energy is called binding energy. Measured per particle, binding is weakest for the lightest nuclei, strongest around iron, and slightly weaker again for the very heaviest, like uranium. So a heavy nucleus that splits into two medium ones moves toward the stronger-bound middle — and the difference has to go somewhere. It comes out as motion of the fragments, which becomes heat. About 200 MeV per split, from a mass difference of roughly a tenth of one percent of the atom: E = mc², with c² being an enormous exchange rate, is why a tenth of a percent is still a fortune.

The same curve explains the neighbouring sheet: pushing the lightest nuclei together — fusion — also moves toward iron, and also pays out. Fission comes down the hill from the heavy side; fusion climbs it from the light side. Two doors, one valley.

Documented · the same fact, in your hand

Why the fuel is small and the plant is not

At these densities, the fuel a large power station consumes in a year is measured in truckloads, not trainloads — while a coal station of the same output burns a trainload a day class of supply. That is the practical meaning of energy density: the mining, transport, and ash problems shrink by the same millions-fold factor as the fuel pile.

But the density cuts both ways, and this sheet prints both edges: the same concentration that shrinks the fuel pile concentrates the hazard. The energy in a small mass is exactly why cooling, containment, and the waste in §⑤ command so much respect. Density is the gift and the bill, in one number.

Documented · where uranium comes from

It starts at a mine mouth, like every metal age

Uranium is not rare. It is roughly as common in the Earth's crust as tin, it is found on every continent, and it is mined — in open pits and underground workings, under the same first law as every other mine: the ground's wealth is real, and so is its danger. The largest producers in recent years have been Kazakhstan, Canada, Namibia, and Australia.

Natural uranium is mostly U-238; the fissile U-235 is about 0.7% of it. What happens between the ore and the reactor is industry this sheet deliberately does not detail — the line drawn at the top of the page holds here too. The kin sheet for the digging itself is Mining — Blueprint.

03 / THE DOCUMENTED RECORD

From a Berlin Bench to a Ninth of the World's Lights

Eighty-eight years from discovery to the present grid. The record below is the documented one — including the part that comes first in every honest telling: this technology's earliest large use was not power.

DEC 1938 – FEB 1939

The split is found — and explained by an exile

Hahn and Strassmann find barium in neutron-bombarded uranium in Berlin. Meitner — who had fled Nazi Germany months earlier because of her Jewish ancestry — and Frisch explain it from Sweden: the nucleus split, ~200 MeV per split. Frisch confirms it experimentally in January 1939 and names it fission. Within months, laboratories worldwide confirm that fission releases spare neutrons — the chain reaction is possible.

DEC 2, 1942

Chicago Pile-1 — the first controlled chain reaction

Under the abandoned stands of the University of Chicago's Stagg Field, Enrico Fermi's team of 49 coaxes a stacked pile of graphite and uranium to criticality — the first self-sustaining, controlled nuclear chain reaction in history. It runs for 28 minutes at about half a watt, then the team shuts it down with a neutron-absorbing rod. The plaque on the site today reads: “On December 2, 1942, man achieved here the first self-sustaining chain reaction and thereby initiated the controlled release of nuclear energy.” Half a watt proved the idea; everything since is scale.

1945 — PRINTED AT FULL SIZE

The first large use was a weapon, in wartime

The honest negative belongs in the timeline, not a footnote. The wartime programme that funded Pile-1 was aimed at a bomb, and in August 1945 fission weapons destroyed Hiroshima and Nagasaki, killing on the order of a hundred thousand people or more, most of them civilians — estimates vary and remain documented, debated history. This sheet teaches nothing about how those weapons were made; that line does not move. But no sheet that skips this entry has told the truth about fission, and the scientists' own reckoning with it — many of the same names as 1942 — is part of why civilian power was later built inside treaties, inspections, and an international agency (the IAEA, founded 1957) rather than in the open market.

1951 – 1957

The turn to power — and to the sea

1951: the Experimental Breeder Reactor I in Idaho generates the first electricity from fission, lighting four light bulbs. 1954: Obninsk, in the USSR, becomes the first reactor to feed a power grid. January 17, 1955: the submarine USS Nautilus signals “Underway on nuclear power” — a reactor's endurance, needing no air and no refuelling for years, remakes naval propulsion; naval reactors remain documented, operating fact today, and the kin sheet is Submarines — Blueprint. 1956–57: Calder Hall (UK) and Shippingport (USA) open the era of full-scale civilian stations.

1960s – 1990s

The build-out

Reactor orders surge through the 1960s and 70s, especially after the 1973 oil shock; France, most decisively, rebuilds its electricity around fission and still draws the majority of its power from it. The fleet's share of world electricity peaks at roughly 17% in the mid-1990s. Growth then slows in the West — cost, public opposition after the accidents in §④, and cheaper alternatives — while continuing in Asia.

2024 – TODAY

Where it stands

Per the World Nuclear Association's and IAEA's documented figures: in 2024, reactors generated a record 2,667 TWh — about 9% of the world's electricity — from a fleet of roughly 440 reactors in about 30 countries. The absolute output is the highest ever; the share has halved since the 1990s because total electricity demand grew faster. Fission remains one of the largest sources of low-carbon electricity in the world, second among low-carbon sources only to hydropower. Those two sentences are both true at once, and most arguments about nuclear power come from quoting only one of them.

The credit problem — Meitner's name Tap to open

Contested at the time · settled in the history books, mostly

The 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of fission went to Otto Hahn alone. Strassmann was not included; Meitner and Frisch — whose paper supplied the physical explanation and the name — were not included. Historians of science have documented the circumstances for decades: Meitner was a refugee, her role was easy for institutions of the time to minimise, and Hahn himself was slow to credit the collaboration publicly. The judgment of most modern histories is plain: the exclusion is widely regarded as one of the Nobel system's clearest injustices, and element 109 — meitnerium — carries her name in the permanent record of the periodic table. This sheet follows the fuller record: four names, one discovery.

04 / BOTH SHELVES, FULL SIZE

Safety — The Ordinary Record and the Accidents

Both shelves are the record. Shelf one is decades long and mostly uneventful — that is a fact, and it matters. Shelf two has three names on it that everyone knows — those are facts too, and this sheet prints what kind of failure each one was, at principle level, without softening any of them.

Shelf one · documented

The long ordinary record

Civilian fission power has now operated for over sixty years across roughly 440 reactors. The overwhelming majority of that history is unremarkable in the literal sense: stations that ran for 40, 50, 60 years, refuelled on schedule, and shut down without incident. In that entire record there have been three severe accidents — the three on the shelf opposite.

Two measured consequences belong on this shelf. First: at Three Mile Island, the worst of the three in the West, the containment building did the job it existed for, and no member of the public was harmed by radiation. Second: measured per unit of electricity generated, the documented death toll of nuclear power — accidents and mining included — sits among the lowest of all major energy sources in the comparative studies published by bodies such as the IEA and reviewed in The Lancet's energy-and-health literature, far below coal's toll from air pollution. That comparison is itself debated — how to count long-tail risks is a genuine argument — but the air-pollution side of the ledger is not seriously disputed.

Shelf two · documented

Why the tail risk is real anyway

The honest counterweight, printed at the same size: fission's failures are not like other industries' failures. They are rare but consequential in kind — land excluded for decades, populations evacuated, trust destroyed for a generation — because the inventory inside a reactor core is radioactive in a way no chemical plant's is. §①'s stern fact governs everything: decay heat continues after shutdown, so cooling can never fail safely for long. Every severe accident below is that sentence, arriving by a different road:

  • Three Mile Island — cooling was lost through a stuck valve and misread instruments.
  • Chernobyl — the reactor's own power ran away first; cooling never got a vote.
  • Fukushima — the sea took the power that ran the cooling.

Three different roads, one destination: a core that could not shed its heat. Read them that way and the whole safety philosophy of the technology — multiple independent barriers, power-independent cooling, containment — stops being a list and becomes a story you already understand.

INES 5 · 1979 · USA

Three Mile Island — the contained meltdown

What kind of failure: equipment and understanding. A relief valve stuck open and drained coolant; the instruments, as presented, misled the operators about how much water was left; the core overheated and partially melted.

What held: the steel containment building — the final barrier — held. Releases to the public were small; the documented health record shows no deaths and no injuries from radiation. The reactor was destroyed; the public was not harmed.

What it changed: operator training, control-room design, and industry self-policing were rebuilt around its lessons. It remains the most serious accident in US commercial nuclear history — and the standing demonstration that containment is not decoration.

INES 7 · 1986 · Ukraine (then USSR)

Chernobyl — the design that could run away

What kind of failure: design, first — then procedure. The RBMK reactor had a documented flaw: under the conditions of a badly run safety test, boiling in its core could increase its power instead of damping it. With safety systems deliberately switched off for the test, power surged beyond any control; steam explosions destroyed the reactor, and its graphite moderator burned for days. There was no containment building to catch any of it.

The cost, plainly: two workers died in the explosions; 28 firefighters and plant staff died of acute radiation sickness in the following weeks. Thousands of thyroid cancers followed among people exposed as children — a treatable cancer, and the clearest documented public-health effect. A zone of roughly 30 km remains excluded. Longer-term, wider cancer estimates are contested: the WHO projected up to about 4,000 eventual cancer deaths among the most-exposed groups, while UNSCEAR has repeatedly reported no evidence of a major wider public-health impact beyond the thyroid cases — and campaigning estimates run far higher than either. This sheet prints the disagreement rather than picking a winner.

What it changed: the RBMK's flaws were corrected across its remaining fleet, and an international safety-reporting culture (INES ratings, peer reviews) exists largely because of this night.

INES 7 · 2011 · Japan

Fukushima Daiichi — the sea took the cooling

What kind of failure: a natural disaster beyond the design basis. The magnitude-9.0 earthquake shut the reactors down correctly — the chain reaction stopped as designed. The tsunami that followed, far taller than the sea wall, flooded the backup generators. With all electrical power lost, cooling stopped; three cores melted, and hydrogen from overheated fuel cladding exploded in the reactor buildings.

The cost, plainly: no deaths from radiation have been confirmed, and UNSCEAR projects no discernible future health effects from the releases. But more than 150,000 people were evacuated, many for years — and Japan's official counts record more than two thousand “disaster-related” deaths in Fukushima prefecture, overwhelmingly elderly people, from the evacuation and displacement itself. The evacuation's toll exceeding radiation's toll is part of this accident's honest record, and it cuts in a direction neither camp quotes often.

What it changed: worldwide stress tests, higher flood defences, and portable, power-independent backup cooling became standard expectations.

The sentence both camps have to carry

Fission power's ordinary record is, by the documented numbers, one of the safest ways humanity has ever made electricity — and its worst days are among the worst industrial days on record, with consequences measured in decades and in land. Anyone who quotes only the first half is selling. Anyone who quotes only the second half is selling too. The technology's real safety question was never “is it safe?” but “is the engineering, the regulation, and the operating culture worthy of the physics?” — and that question has to be re-earned at every plant, in every country, in every decade. This sheet's judgment, labelled as ours: the physics is unforgiving, the engineering answers to it exist, and the record shows they work exactly when — and only when — they are actually maintained.

05 / THE STANDING OPEN PROBLEM

Waste — An Open How

Every blueprint on this tree names where it ends. Fission's honest ending is here: the waste is the part of the problem the field has not finished solving — technically or politically. It is labelled the way this tree labels every unfinished thing: an OPEN HOW. An unknown is not a no.

Documented · what the waste is

Small volume, very long clock

Spent fuel is the used core of the reaction: the leftover uranium, the fission fragments, and heavier elements built up inside. It comes out hot — decay heat again — and radioactive, and it stays hazardous for thousands of years, with its heat and radioactivity falling steeply in the first decades and then very slowly. All of the high-level waste ever produced by the world's civilian fleet would fit, by volume, in a building the size of a large supermarket — and no honest sentence about it can stop at the volume, because the clock is the problem.

The standing practice, documented worldwide: cool the fuel in water pools for years, then seal it in massive steel-and-concrete dry casks that need no power and no moving parts. The US Nuclear Regulatory Commission's record for dry casks, loaded since 1986, shows no known radiation release affecting the public. But casks are storage, designed for decades — a waiting room, not a destination. The United States alone holds more than 90,000 tonnes of spent fuel at dozens of reactor sites it was never meant to stay at, and its designated repository, Yucca Mountain in Nevada, has been stalled by politics and consent fights for decades.

Open how · the doors being tried

What the record shows so far

  • Deep geological disposal — the consensus answer, one door built. The international scientific consensus (IAEA, national academies) holds that burying sealed waste deep in stable rock is the sound permanent route. Finland's Onkalo repository — copper canisters in clay, 400-plus metres into bedrock nearly two billion years old — is the world's first, built for Finland's own ~6,500 tonnes and moving through its final licensing and first disposals in the mid-2020s. One small country has walked the whole road: chosen a site with its host community, built it, and licensed it. That is an existence proof, not a world solution.
  • Reprocessing — partial, practised, not a disappearance. France and others chemically separate reusable material from spent fuel and burn it again. It shrinks the long-lived fraction and recovers energy; it does not eliminate the need for a repository, and it raises its own safeguarding questions. Documented practice, honestly bounded.
  • Burning the long-lived part — a possibility, labelled as one. Reactor designs exist, demonstrated at research scale, that can fission some of the longest-lived components of the waste, shortening its hazardous clock from hundreds of thousands of years toward hundreds. None runs as a commercial waste-burning fleet today. Possibility in full: real physics, unbuilt industry.
  • Deep boreholes — a concept. Sealing waste kilometres down in narrow drilled holes. Studied, never yet demonstrated at scale. Labelled: concept.

Why this stays open: the engineering is arguably the easier half. The harder half is consent — asking a community to host a facility whose duty outlasts every institution now alive, and meaning it. Finland's road took forty years of that work. The open how is social as much as technical, and this sheet prints that at full size too.

06 / ACT

Starter Practice — Learn It Like a Citizen of the Atomic Age

The small, honest version of this technology is not a device — it is literacy. Four practices, each one real, each one completable from where you sit.

  1. Run the comparator until the ratio is in your hands

    Go back to §② and set the slider to 1 gram. Three and a half tonnes of coal, from a gram. Then ask the sheet's question: what follows, for mining, for transport, for waste, from a ratio that size? You have just done the first real piece of nuclear reasoning — consequences from density.

  2. Find fission in your own electricity

    Look up your grid's generation mix — your utility, your national energy agency, or the IAEA's country profiles publish it. Is your home lit 5%, 20%, or 70% by fission tonight? The number differs enormously by place, and knowing yours turns an abstract argument into a local fact.

  3. Read one accident from the primary record

    Pick one name from §④ and read its official investigation summary — the Kemeny Commission (Three Mile Island), the IAEA's Chernobyl and Fukushima reports are all public. Notice how different the documented story is from the version carried by headlines, in whichever direction you expected. That gap is the lesson.

  4. Stand where it started, if you're ever in Chicago

    The Pile-1 site at the University of Chicago is marked by Henry Moore's sculpture Nuclear Energy and the 1942 plaque quoted in §③. No fence, no ticket. The first chain reaction happened under a football stand, at half a watt — worth standing in front of, if the road takes you there.
